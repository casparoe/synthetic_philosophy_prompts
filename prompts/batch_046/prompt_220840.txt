I serve on the postgraduate examination board for a philosophy of science programme, and we now face a recurring problem that our statutes never anticipated. Increasingly, doctoral candidates submit dissertations that are conceptually dense and formally polished, yet during the viva voce it becomes apparent that substantial portions—literature syntheses, formal derivations, interpretive judgments about primary texts—were produced with extensive assistance from large language models. Our regulations distinguish legitimate methodological aids from plagiarism, but they contain no category for what I shall call substitutive epistemic dependence: a situation in which a candidate’s own cognitive achievement has been displaced rather than extended, leaving them with beliefs they endorse but cannot justify independently. I need a reusable decision procedure that my board can run in approximately thirty minutes during a viva, with three examiners who are not necessarily specialists in the candidate’s exact subfield, and with the candidate present to answer direct questions. The stakes are high—we must either certify that the candidate possesses the understanding the degree requires, or withhold certification and require revision—but the constraints are tight: we have no access to chat logs, we cannot convene external expert reviews, and whatever framework we use must be operable by philosophers without specialised training in cognitive science.

The underlying principle, drawn from virtue epistemology and the philosophy of testimony, is that understanding is not reducible to true justified belief; it requires explanatory integration and what I term a reconstructive map of the reasoning supporting one’s claims. Where a practitioner relies on an instrument—say, statistical software—the user need not replicate every internal computation, but must understand what the instrument does, under what conditions it is reliable, and how its outputs connect to their own theoretical commitments. The particular danger with contemporary language models is that they generate plausible intermediate reasoning steps—objections, distinctions, analogies—that users adopt without being able to navigate the space of alternatives around them. For this procedure, I want you to treat substitutive dependence as established only when a candidate fails the transparency condition for substitutive cognition: they must be able not only to defend the adopted claim, but to articulate at least one plausible alternative formulation the tool could have generated, to explain why that alternative is inferior, and to show that this evaluation reflects their own conceptual commitments rather than post-hoc rationalisation. At the same time, the procedure must respect the distinction between prosthetic cognition, where heavy AI involvement is compatible with genuine understanding, and substitutive cognition, where minimal involvement still undermines it, depending on the structure of the candidate’s cognitive achievement.

Please construct a step-by-step procedure, expressed as an ordered sequence of questions with clear criteria for a sufficient answer at each stage. Indicate precisely where the procedure terminates with a definitive negative—where substitutive dependence has been established and the board must withhold certification regardless of the dissertation’s overall quality. Indicate also where the procedure must hand the decision back to human judgement, because the question involves values rather than facts—such as how much cognitive independence we ought to demand in an age of ubiquitous tools. Include explicit real-time checks an examiner can apply during questioning, and flag the hard cases where the procedure is likely to misfire, for instance where a candidate demonstrates deep understanding of a conclusion but not of every intermediate lemma generated by the model. Then apply the procedure to three cases: (i) a candidate who used an AI to formalise modal-logic derivations in an appendix, who can explain each rule of inference but admits they could not have produced the formalisation unaided; (ii) a candidate whose central interpretive chapter on a phenomenologist was drafted with AI assistance, who can discuss the primary texts fluently but concedes they never read the secondary literature the AI synthesised; and (iii) a candidate whose dissertation is exceptionally original and well-defended overall, yet who reveals that the specific objections to rival positions in Chapter Four were formulated by the model and adopted without prior independent evaluation—so that the procedure, applied rigorously, would require withholding certification for that chapter despite the board’s admiration for the work as a whole. For each case, show what the procedure yields, including the unwelcome result in the third case. Finally, state clearly what this procedure cannot do: what kinds of epistemic failure it will miss, what false positives it risks, and why it can never fully replace the examiner’s own judgement about the presence or absence of philosophical understanding. Please present your answer in British English.
