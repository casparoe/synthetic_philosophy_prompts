I’ve been trying to work out Scanlon’s idea that wrongness is basically a failure of justifiability: an act is wrong if it violates a principle that someone affected by it could reasonably reject. I understand there is a well-known aggregation problem—saving five people by killing one seems permissible, yet contractualism seems to block it because the one victim can reject the principle. I tried to push the reasoning in a direction I haven’t seen discussed, applying it not across different people but across the stages of a single life, and now I have an argument that seems to prove far too much.

(1) Contractualism: an act is wrong iff it is disallowed by a principle that at least one affected party could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement.

(2) In the standard aggregation case, saving five strangers by killing one innocent person is wrong, because the one victim can reject any principle that permits killing him for the benefit of others.

(3) So contractualism weighs claims individually rather than summing them.

(4) Now apply the same structure diachronically. One life contains many stages—call them Anna-at-t1, Anna-at-t2, ..., Anna-at-t10—each with its own experiences, interests, and burdens.

(5) Suppose Anna-at-t1 can undergo a painful and costly treatment that will prevent a fatal illness for Anna-at-t10.

(6) A principle requiring Anna-at-t1 to bear that present burden can be rejected by Anna-at-t1: she suffers now, and the beneficiary is Anna-at-t10, a different stage with a different set of experiences and no direct access to her present suffering.

(7) Therefore diachronic contractualism implies that no present stage can be required to make sacrifices for the sake of a future stage.

(8) But that result is plainly false. We think people ought to save for retirement, endure medical treatment, study for exams—present burdens for future benefits.

(9) To avoid (7), contractualism must allow the benefits distributed across temporal stages to be aggregated for the single person.

(10) Yet aggregation across temporal stages is structurally identical to aggregation across different people: in both cases one party bears a cost while multiple distinct parties (stages or persons) receive benefits.

(11) So if contractualism permits diachronic aggregation, consistency demands it permit synchronic aggregation.

(12) Hence contractualism either forbids rational long-term planning, or it surrenders its distinctive resistance to saving five lives at the cost of one.

I want to keep (1), (3), and (8), and I also want to preserve the intuition that there is a real tension rather than a simple mistake. Which step should I reject? Is the error in (4)–(6), where I treat temporal stages as independent contracting parties? Or is there a hidden shift in what “reasonably reject” means between (2) and (6)—perhaps a scope or standing ambiguity? And if I block (7) by denying (6), what view of personal identity over time am I implicitly committing myself to, and is that a price worth paying? I would also like to know what the answer predicts for a case I have not listed: a principle that requires a small present inconvenience to prevent a catastrophic harm to ten different future generations of strangers.
