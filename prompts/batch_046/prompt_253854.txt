In contemporary analytic theology, some defenders of divine command theory have recast the view in terms of metaphysical grounding: God's will or essential nature is said to ground moral properties, thereby avoiding the charge of arbitrariness that Socrates raises in the Euthyphro. Yet it seems to me that this move does not actually escape the dilemma but only displaces it. If grounding is well-founded and irreflexive, then whatever serves as the ultimate ground—whether divine commands or God's necessary nature—cannot itself be grounded in any further normative fact. That appears to reproduce the second horn: moral properties depend on a foundation that has no independent normative justification, which is precisely the arbitrariness problem in metaphysical dress. Meanwhile, the first horn remains intact, since if God's nature is necessarily and essentially good, then moral truths depend on necessary features of reality that God does not author, rendering divine agency redundant. So hasn't the grounding framework shown that any theistic ethics must fall on one horn or the other? If that diagnosis is correct, why do philosophers of religion continue to present modified divine command theory and grounding-based theism as serious competitors to secular moral realism, rather than treating the Euthyphro structure—once augmented with standard constraints on metaphysical dependence—as a settled refutation?
