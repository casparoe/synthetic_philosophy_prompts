I take the scale argument for insect moral consideration to be structurally decisive rather than merely suggestive. Even if we assign a very low prior—say, well below one percent—to phenomenal consciousness in any given insect, the cardinality of living insects (on the order of 10^19) implies that the expected moral weight of insect suffering dominates that of all vertebrate suffering combined. The conclusion, therefore, is not that we have demonstrated insect sentience, but that precautionary reasoning under massive scale makes insect welfare a first-order moral obligation rather than a speculative afterthought; the risk asymmetry alone is said to do the work. What would genuinely count against this position? I want an honest assessment of its exposure: which empirical results, which philosophical objections—say, about the incommensurability of suffering across taxa, the epistemic legitimacy of low priors in moral risk, or the practical impossibility of acting on such scale—would be decisive rather than merely awkward? Must a committed defender concede that if insects are shown to be entirely non-phenomenal—lacking even minimal phenomenal architecture—the argument collapses, or can the position be insulated by retreating to a purely symbolic or expressive stance? If it can be so insulated, is that a virtue or a vice for an argument of this kind? Rank the defeaters, indicate which ones the view's proponents should themselves recognize as genuine costs, and say whether the position risks unfalsifiability.
