Read Sidney Lumet’s film *Twelve Angry Men* as a philosophical fable about democratic deliberation, using the three canonical failures of Athenian democracy—the Mytilene debate’s emotional reversal under Cleon’s rhetoric, the Sicilian Expedition’s imperial overreach driven by assembly enthusiasm, and the trial of Socrates as democratic judgment against philosophical dissent—as your interpretive framework. The jury room is a stripped-down democracy: twelve citizens, direct speech, a binding collective decision made without institutional safeguards such as slavery, empire, or religious courts. Ask where the film reproduces and where it evades the pathologies that destroyed Athens. Does Juror 8’s patient reasoning model the epistemic humility that Thucydides’ Diodotus urged upon the assembly, or does the film conceal the fact that democratic correction often requires exactly the rhetorical and social pressure—shame, status anxiety, emotional contagion—that Cleon exploited to condemn the Mytileneans? Examine the moments when hostile jurors switch sides: are those instances of democratic learning, or are they micro-versions of the assembly’s susceptibility to the same forces that launched the Sicilian fleet and convicted Socrates? I am preparing this for a seminar on democracy and philosophy, and I want a reading that does not treat the film as a simple celebration of reason triumphing over prejudice. Instead, treat it seriously: does *Twelve Angry Men* show that deliberation can correct itself without the structural conditions that made Athenian failure inevitable, or does its very success depend on removing class conflict, imperial interest, and religious anxiety from the room? End by saying whether the film’s idealized jury offers a genuine philosophical contribution to debates about democratic legitimacy—perhaps showing that legitimacy requires not just correct outcomes but a specific psychology of dissent—or whether it merely relocates the problem of democratic failure into the minds of individual jurors, leaving the institutional questions that killed Socrates untouched.
