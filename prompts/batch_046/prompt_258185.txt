At a recent town meeting about our water supply, two neighbors got into a sustained argument over whether to replace our aging filtration plant with a new membrane system. The old plant allows trace levels of a regulated chemical that we have monitored for decades; the proposed system uses a polymer derived from food-grade sources but scaled up to municipal size for the first time, promising near-zero contamination but with no long-term operational record.

Maya argued that the precautionary principle clearly favors staying with the current system. She said the principle's core rule is that when an activity raises threats of harm to human health or the environment, precautionary measures should be taken even if some cause-and-effect relationships are not fully established scientifically — and that clearly points away from adopting an untested membrane.

Jonas replied that this applies the principle asymmetrically and therefore misapplies it. He said the status quo is not a risk-free baseline but an active exposure to a known contaminant; if uncertainty about a new action counsels restraint, then uncertainty about the cumulative long-term effects of our current exposure should counsel change. He called Maya's framing a baseline-risk-allocation mistake — treating existing harm as neutral background while labeling only novel interventions as risky.

Maya countered that there is a genuine epistemic asymmetry, not just a framing bias. With the old plant we have quantified risk distributions and known failure modes; with the scaled polymer we face structural uncertainties — nonlinear behavior at scale, degradation products we have not catalogued, and interaction effects with local water chemistry — that are different in kind, not just in degree.

Jonas conceded that scaling can alter properties, but said this undermines Maya's own comfort with the old system. If nonlinear scaling makes the new option riskier than its food-grade origin suggests, it may also make the old system's long-term low-dose effects riskier than our linear exposure models predict. He added that if familiarity alone exempts the status quo from precautionary scrutiny, the principle collapses into a conservative bias that protects incumbent technologies regardless of their actual harm profile.

Maya then noted that the polymer's food-grade origin does provide some mechanistic knowledge — its molecular structure is mapped — so calling it a total unknown ignores partial scientific grounding. Jonas agreed, but insisted that partial knowledge is precisely where precaution is most needed: we know enough to intervene, but not enough to be confident, which should push us toward comparing both options under the same uncertainty framework rather than granting the incumbent automatic exemption.

They turned to me, since I had been listening, and asked me to referee. Can you walk through their exchange step by step and say who is right about each specific move? Please address in particular: whether Jonas's symmetry objection succeeds against Maya's initial reading, whether her epistemic asymmetry holds up against his baseline-risk critique, and whether the food-grade origin of the polymer actually weakens Jonas's argument about unknown risks. Also say what both are missing — for example, whether either has clarified who bears the burden of proof, and whether they have confused applying an abstract principle with deciding what a municipal body ought actually do given competing values.
