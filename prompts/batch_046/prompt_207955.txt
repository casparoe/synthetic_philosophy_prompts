I am preparing the question sheet for a podcast interview with a philosopher who has recently advanced an unorthodox reconstruction of Moore's proof of an external world. Rather than treating the proof as an empirical inference against scepticism, she reads it as a pragmatic ostension — a demonstrative act that renders the spatial concept of being outside us operational without first resolving epistemological doubts. She argues that the proof succeeds only on a direct-realist account of perception, and that representationalism undermines it not because representation is false, but because it introduces an epistemic mediator that makes demonstrative reference impossible. Consequently, she claims that sceptical scenarios such as being a brain in a vat are not merely false but conceptually malformed: they violate the conditions for meaningful spatial predication rather than failing to match the world. Critics have charged that this collapses Moore's argument into rhetoric, that it smuggles in verificationism about spatial claims, and that it confuses psychological certainty with epistemic entitlement.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the actual content of her position rather than asking her to summarise it. I require at least one question on how pragmatic ostension differs from a mere rhetorical gesture and what philosophical work it does; one on why representationalism specifically, rather than indirect realism generally, breaks the proof; and one on whether her claim that sceptical scenarios are conceptually malformed commits her to a verificationism she elsewhere rejects. For each of these three, include a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to offer: should she invoke hinge propositions in the style of later Wittgenstein, ask whether hinges are genuinely non-epistemic or merely practically immune to doubt; should she appeal to the transparency of experience to distinguish representationalism from indirect realism, ask what she makes of phenomenological arguments for the opacity of perceptual content during illusion. The remaining questions should address the standard objection that Moore begs the question by assuming precisely what the sceptic denies, and should explore an implication she herself draws: if spatial location is constitutive of objecthood in the manner her proof requires, then purely non-spatial entities — such as digital minds lacking any embodied location — lack the conditions for being genuine objects of reference, with consequences for their moral status. Keep the tone formal and polished, use British English, and avoid biographical or generic opening questions.
