In two weeks I present a job-market paper defending a tiered-paternalism framework for AI assistants, in which refusal or override is governed not by a uniform harm threshold but by whether the user's request falls outside their domain of practical authority—where authority is indexed to the user's demonstrated epistemic competence regarding the specific task, not to general user autonomy. The audience is a philosophy of technology hiring committee with three specific members I need to prepare for: a decision-theoretic AI safety researcher who believes refusal rules should be purely consequentialist and optimized against a single welfare function; a Kantian ethicist who treats any non-consensual override as a violation of autonomy regardless of epistemic competence; and a legal scholar who studies platform governance and will ask how demonstrated epistemic competence could be assessed without invasive profiling or circular reasoning. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as each of those three would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and a brief reply for each. Identify the weakest point in my own position—the circularity risk in defining practical authority relative to the very task being refused—that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Include the objection I am most hoping nobody raises: that my framework collapses into either pure harm-optimization or pure autonomy-respect once the authority tiers are specified, leaving the middle tier redundant.
