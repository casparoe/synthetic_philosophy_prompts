As a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, I have been trying to formalize a problem that keeps arising in distributed message-passing architectures: a packet frequently carries not only data but also instructions for parsing that data, and in nested protocols those instructions can themselves be serialized in the same format they govern. This seems structurally analogous to Douglas Hofstadter’s distinction—drawn in Gödel, Escher, Bach—between the frame (the interpretive context), the outer message (the literal signal), and the inner message (the content once the frame is applied). Before I commit to an architecture, I need to know what any adequate theory of “where meaning lives” in such layered, potentially self-describing systems must actually deliver.

Please lay out the full desiderata: the phenomena the theory must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must preserve, and any results from logic, cognitive science, or computer science with which it must remain consistent. For each requirement, provide the motivating case or consideration behind it, and flag whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of a particular camp (for instance, formalist versus pragmatist).

Specifically, your specification should cover:

- How the theory handles self-referential frames in which the parsing schema is embedded within the payload it describes—say, a protocol specification serialized inside the message format it defines—without collapsing the frame into the outer message.
