For a feature I am writing on institutional deliberation when ethical disagreement runs deeper than first-order norms—when citizens do not merely dispute whether harm or autonomy should prevail, but disagree about what counts as a reason to revise one's moral framework at all—I need a rigorous specification of what an adequate theory of moral uncertainty ought to deliver. Write the desiderata: the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, the consistency conditions it must satisfy, and the practical recommendations it must be able to generate, both for individuals and for collective bodies. Specifically, include (a) how it handles first-order intertheoretic comparison when values are genuinely incommensurable; (b) whether it requires diachronic coherence—that an agent's current moral commitments constrain how she should respond to future conceptual revolutions in ethics—or whether such revolutions license radical discontinuity; (c) whether it can accommodate inter-subjective calibration among agents who do not share a common moral epistemology, without collapsing into either majoritarian aggregation or silent paralysis; (d) what it implies for institutional design, particularly whether a legislature or deliberative body can act under moral uncertainty without implicitly presupposing a single correct theory; and (e) whether it must preserve a distinction between moral uncertainty and empirical uncertainty about moral facts, and if so, on what grounds. For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or philosophical consideration behind it, and flag whether it is genuinely non-negotiable or merely a preference of a particular camp—for instance, whether determinacy of recommendation is required in every practical context, or whether tolerance of persistent indeterminacy is acceptable. Then identify any pairs or subsets of these requirements that appear jointly unsatisfiable; I am especially interested in the tension between demanding diachronic coherence and allowing for genuine conceptual moral change, and between requiring inter-subjective calibration and avoiding the aggregation difficulties familiar from social-choice theory. Finally, briefly assess how the principal existing approaches—expected moral-value maximization, the "my favorite theory" fallback, democratic or parliamentary aggregation, and suspension of judgment—fare against your list, noting which failures are fatal and which are negotiable trade-offs.
