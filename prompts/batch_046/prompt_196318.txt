As a retired molecular biologist who has recently taken up philosophy in earnest, I have become puzzled by the curatorial practices I observe in contemporary galleries. They will hang a photograph taken in a split second of selection and framing, and they will exhibit a collage assembled entirely from pre-existing images cut by other hands, yet they refuse to display images produced by a diffusion model even when the human operator has spent months refining prompts, selecting seeds, curating from thousands of outputs, and manually inpainting details. The stated reason is that the machine, not the person, performed the creative act. At the same time, these institutions demand exhaustive documentation of an AI system's training data—every copyrighted image it might have ingested—while requiring no comparable provenance for the visual memories, museum visits, and reproduced works that shaped a human painter's style.

I am trying to understand what view of artistic creativity and authorship this combination of practices quietly assumes. Does it presuppose that creativity must involve a direct causal chain from mental state to physical mark, that influence is only problematic when mediated by algorithmic weights rather than neural ones, or that artistic value resides in a particular kind of embodied struggle? Could the same practices be justified under a purely intentionalist theory of art, or do they instead commit the institution to a kind of biological essentialism about human cognition? And if we replaced the underlying view with one that treated all influence—whether through retina, synapse, or gradient descent—as equally part of the artistic substrate, what specific changes to acquisition and display policy would necessarily follow?
