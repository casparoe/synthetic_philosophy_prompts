I am a retired biologist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of science and decision theory. I recently rewatched The Martian, in which the stranded astronaut Mark Watney survives through a series of improvised botanical and engineering fixes—growing potatoes in treated waste, patching the habitat with duct tape, and navigating by dead reckoning—without ever computing a globally optimal plan. Read the film through Herbert Simon’s framework of bounded rationality and satisficing: Watney repeatedly selects the first option that clears an aspiration threshold rather than maximizing expected utility. I am particularly interested in Simon’s distinction between procedural rationality—the soundness of a decision process given cognitive and environmental limits—and substantive rationality—the optimality of the outcome itself. Apply that distinction to two or three specific scenes, such as the potato farm or the rover modifications: does the film present Watney’s choices as procedurally rational, substantively rational, or both? Where does the narrative risk conflating “good enough to survive” with “the best possible plan”? Furthermore, does the film’s celebration of human ingenuity implicitly endorse substantive rationality despite dramatizing only procedural success? I am preparing notes for a philosophy-of-science discussion group, so please separate what is settled in Simon’s own framework from what remains genuinely contested—especially regarding whether satisficing can serve as a normative standard or is strictly a descriptive limitation of bounded agents—and do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus view among philosophers of science.
