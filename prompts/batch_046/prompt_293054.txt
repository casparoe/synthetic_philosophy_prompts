I have noticed a recurring evaluative pattern that sits near moral luck but does not map neatly onto the standard categories, and I am unsure whether philosophers have given it a specific name. Imagine an institution that adopts a decision rule—say, a randomized allocation protocol—precisely because it is impossible, with current knowledge, to distinguish which individuals will be harmed by option A and which by option B. The rule is transparent, uniformly applied, and defensible; yet when an unforeseeable, idiosyncratic factor causes harm to one recipient of A, observers retrospectively blame the institution for not having anticipated that factor, even though anticipating it was the very problem the rule was designed to avoid. The blame seems to depend on the counterfactual that a different assignment would have spared this particular person, not on any failure of diligence or reasoning. Is there an established concept for this specific configuration—where procedural fairness is retrospectively undermined by hidden heterogeneity that was definitionally outside the decision framework? How does it relate to resultant, circumstantial, and constitutive moral luck, and where are its edges—does it require that the hidden factor be genuinely undetectable, or does it extend to cases where detection was merely impractical?
