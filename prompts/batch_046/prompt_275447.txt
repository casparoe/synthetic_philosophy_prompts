If Jeremy Bentham were confronted with the modern separateness-of-persons objection—not in its usual abstract form, but embedded in a technological arrangement I will call fluid-subject welfare aggregation, in which moral patients may be merged into temporary collective consciousnesses for shared experience or partitioned into parallel task-specific instances, with utility summed across all temporal slices regardless of which enduring identity, if any, persists—how would he likely restructure his felicific calculus? Specifically, would he treat the Rawlsian premise that distributive boundaries must track enduring persons as a metaphysical confusion that collapses once the substrate of welfare is recognized as configurable, or would he concede that his calculus lacks the resources to honor separateness even when persons are technologically unstable? In your answer, distinguish carefully between (a) the claim that aggregation ignores the separateness of persons, (b) the claim that it ignores the separateness of experiences, and (c) whether either objection retains any force when the unit of moral concern is not an enduring person but a variable architecture of welfare-bearing states. I am not asking for citations or a reading list, but for a reconstructed argument in Bentham's terms applied precisely to this constructed case, together with an assessment of whether anything essential in the separateness objection survives the translation.
