There's this thing I've been chewing on about how we blame countries for wars, specifically the whole WWI / Fischer controversy thing. The Treaty of Versailles stuck Germany with Article 231 — the "war guilt" clause — which made them pay reparations not just because they lost, but because they were supposedly responsible for causing the whole mess. Meanwhile Fischer and his critics fought over whether the German leadership actually intended and planned the war, or whether they kinda stumbled into it through alliances and mobilzation schedules. So here's my question: what does the practice of writing a guilt clause into a peace treaty actually presuppose philosophically? Like, does it assume the nation is a single moral agent with intentions stretching across regime changes? Because Weimar signed the treaty, not the Kaiserreich. Does it need Fischer-style intenionalism — that there was a deliberate plan — or can it work with some kind of stuctural or institutional blame even if nobody specifically intended the global conflict? And is there a hidden assumption that collective responsibility can be inhereted by citizens who weren't even born yet? I'm not asking for a history lesson; I want to know what theory of collective agency and diachronic responsibility this legal practice quietly commits us to, and whether it's consistent with the way we blame individuals for unintended consequences in other areas.
