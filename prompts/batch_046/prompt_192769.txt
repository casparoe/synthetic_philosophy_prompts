I am preparing a philosophical working document for a seminar that has spent two years in adversarial debate about Richard Rorty’s antirepresentationalism and neopragmatism, specifically about whether dropping the mirror-of-nature image also forces us to abandon any substantive notion that successful beliefs are constrained by an independent reality. The two opposing camps are, first, committed Rortyan antirepresentationalists, who hold that vocabulary replacement is driven entirely by practical coping, solidarity, and the avoidance of cruelty, with representational talk being at best a redundant honorific; and second, critical pragmatist representationalists, who accept Rorty’s rejection of epistemological foundationalism and the correspondence theory of truth but maintain that a non-foundational, regulative notion of representational success—defined as robust predictive and explanatory stability across vocabulary shifts—remains an independent constraint on belief revision rather than a reducible byproduct of coherence or social agreement. I want you to draft the joint statement that these two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The document should contain four parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: include shared commitments about Rorty’s critique of representationalism as a philosophical foundation, about the contingency of language and final vocabularies, about the distinction between normal and abnormal discourse, and about the formal structure of scientific change—phrased neutrally so neither camp feels misrepresented. Second, a disagreements table that states the precise divergences in wording each side would accept for its own position, especially on whether representational success can be reconstructed in purely pragmatic terms or whether it functions as an irreducible normative constraint. Third, the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side—including whether predictive invariance across theory change supports a minimal representational reading or can be fully accounted for by pragmatic coherence and solidarity. Fourth, for each crux, identify a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and record each camp’s explicit prediction about what that case shows.

To keep the prompt from being generic, inject the following niche framing: center the collaboration on the inter-vocabulary arbitration problem, a hypothetical but well-formed scenario in which two scientific vocabularies achieve identical empirical predictions and practical success but assign incompatible ontological statuses to the same unobservable entities. Use this scenario as the central test case running through the cruxes. Additionally, ask whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal—specifically, whether disagreement over the word representation dissolves once both sides accept a reconstructed pragmatic definition—and if so, identify exactly which part and explain why. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; do not invoke obscure theories outside mainstream neopragmatist discourse; and do not rely on highly specific biographical or textual details that a small model could not reliably recall. Instead, write from established philosophical knowledge, paraphrasing Rorty’s positions without verbatim quotation marks unless you have fetched and verified the exact passage.

Take a committed stance in your drafting: do not merely list options, but construct formulations that are genuinely acceptable to each camp, identify which crux you judge to be the deepest, and defend that judgment with reasons. The final line of your response must begin with Bottom line: and contain a single sentence summarizing the core residual disagreement that survives the collaboration.
