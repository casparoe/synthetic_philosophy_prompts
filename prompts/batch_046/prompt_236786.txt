I'm preparing a consistency check for a philosophy of knowledge workshop, and I'd like an audit of the following package before I present it. My commitments are: (1) Foundationalism holds for perceptual knowledge—there are genuinely basic beliefs produced by properly functioning cognitive faculties in favorable conditions, and these justify other beliefs without themselves requiring further inferential support. (2) Coherentism holds for scientific knowledge—no scientific belief is epistemically basic, and justification is entirely a matter of mutual support within a theoretical system, with observation reports gaining their standing only through coherence with the broader framework. (3) Infinitism holds for mathematical knowledge—justification requires an available, non-repeating, infinite chain of reasons, and this is not vicious because mathematical knowledge demands only that further support be accessible in principle, not that a finite knower actually traverses the chain. (4) These three verdicts are not ad hoc but principled: they track whether a domain involves direct causal interaction with the world, systemic interdependence among beliefs, or abstract normative construction. (5) Cross-domain inferences are legitimate and often necessary—perceptual beliefs about harm can justify moral judgments, and mathematical results can justify predictions in physical science—so justification is not hermetically sealed within each domain. (6) There is no single master account of justification that must apply uniformly across all domains; epistemic pluralism is correct.

On the surface, (1) and (3) appear to conflict, since foundationalism requires termination and infinitism denies it, but I take that tension to dissolve once the domains are separated. The deeper worry is elsewhere: if perceptual beliefs are basic only within the perceptual domain, and scientific beliefs are justified only holistically within science, then the cross-domain moves in (5) seem either to violate domain insulation or to import premises that lack the right kind of justification in the target domain. Meanwhile, (3) and (5) raise a parallel problem for mathematics feeding into science, since an infinite chain available only in principle may not supply the kind of support a holistic scientific system requires. I need to know which commitments actually pull against each other, which pairs are only apparently in conflict, and what the least costly revision is—preferably one that preserves pluralism but sacrifices the weakest link. Rank the tensions by severity, specify what each potential revision costs, and identify the single change that cleans up the most conflict without collapsing everything into one uniform theory.
