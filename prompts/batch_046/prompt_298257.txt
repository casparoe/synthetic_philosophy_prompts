I am a doctoral student in philosophy of biology assisting with the public-facing documentation for a university-backed genetic-risk screening service. The proposed patient leaflet describes DNA as "a code that writes instructions for your body" and states that "reading your DNA decodes your health risks". A molecular geneticist on our ethics panel objects that this is not merely simplification but conceptual error: she argues that nucleic acids carry causal correlations rather than semantic content, and that calling them a "code" illicitly imports an intentional encoder and a deterministic programme. The communications director counters that the metaphor is ubiquitous in popular science and that removing it will confuse lay users and reduce uptake. I have been asked to advise whether we should retain, revise, or qualify the language, but I am unsure whether the philosophical distinction between causal information and intentional representation ought to constrain a practical leaflet, or whether insisting on it is mere pedantry in this context. How should I think through the matter? Please answer in British English.
