Here is an argument I sketched for a seminar on mechanism and now find suspicious because the conclusion is far stronger than I want to accept. (1) For any consistent formal system F adequate for arithmetic, Gödel's first incompleteness theorem yields a sentence G_F that F cannot prove, though G_F is true if F is consistent. (2) If a human mind M is fully mechanized by a single system F_M, then every arithmetical judgment M can justifiably form should be capturable within F_M (or be an unreliable heuristic). (3) A mathematician can understand the Gödel construction for F_M and, on the assumption that F_M is consistent, come to justifiably believe G_F_M. (4) So M can hold a justified belief that F_M cannot prove—contradicting (2). Hence (5) no single consistent formal system fully mechanizes M. (6) The mechanist might reply that M updates its framework: upon recognizing G_F_M, it adopts F'_M, which proves G_F_M. (7) But F'_M is also consistent and arithmetically adequate, so it has its own Gödel sentence G_F'_M. (8) By parallel reasoning, M can recognize G_F'_M (assuming F'_M consistent), forcing adoption of F''_M, and so on. (9) Therefore complete mechanization requires an actual infinite ascending chain of formal systems instantiated inside M. (10) No finite physical device—brain, neural network, or firmware-updating calculator—can implement an actual infinite chain. (11) So the argument proves not just that minds are non-mechanizable, but that any physical system capable of arithmetical learning is impossible, which is absurd. I want to preserve (1)–(5) if I can. Where does the overreach occur—at (6), (8), or (9)—and what does rejecting that step cost me? Does saving the core Lucas-Penrose point force me to deny that minds can revise their formal commitments, or is there a more local mistake?
