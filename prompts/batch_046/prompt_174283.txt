A friend offered the following summary of Derek Parfit's argument regarding the repugnant conclusion: if total well-being is what matters, then a world with ten billion people each living a life barely worth living contains more aggregate value than a world with one million people living in profound flourishing, because the sum is larger. My friend says Parfit regarded this as a decisive refutation of totalist axiologies and proposed Theory X as the correct replacement. According to her, Theory X holds that once every existing person is above a critical well-being threshold, further gains to the already well-off carry zero moral weight; consequently, the correct policy is to maximize the number of people positioned just barely above that threshold. She concludes that this vindicates a person-affecting restriction—creating someone at the threshold harms no one, but if doing so lowers the well-being of those already present, it is impermissible, which shows that impersonal maximization must be rejected. I suspect the basic framing of the repugnant conclusion is accurate, but I worry she has conflated Parfit's deliberately unspecified Theory X with a specific critical-level view, misrepresented its distributive implications, and presented a standard objection as if Parfit himself endorsed it. Could you provide an accuracy audit? Specifically, separate the parts that correctly capture Parfit's challenge to totalism, identify where her account of Theory X and its policy consequences subtly overstates or conflates positions, and determine whether her conclusion about person-affecting restrictions survives once those corrections are made.
