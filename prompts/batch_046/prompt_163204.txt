I am preparing a single ninety-minute graduate seminar on Buddhist anatta and personal identity for philosophy students who have read primary sources in translation but lack formal training in analytic metaphysics or Abhidharma scholasticism. I want them to understand anatta not as a mystical denial of the soul but as a constructive dependency claim—that a person is a conditioned pattern among the five aggregates, with direct implications for moral responsibility across discontinuous mental streams. Design the pedagogical arc: specify the exact sequence in which the aggregates, conditional co-arising, and the conventional-versus-ultimate distinction must be introduced so that anatta functions as an explanatory framework rather than a bare negation; propose the one carrying example that should thread through the session—I am considering a scenario in which a meditator’s mental states are systematically replaced by functionally identical synthetic analogues, but I want you to evaluate whether this scaffold works and, if not, propose an alternative that preserves structural dependency without smuggling in a hidden substrate; identify the three predictable misconceptions (for instance, that anatta implies no subject of suffering, that it collapses without residue into a Humean bundle theory, or that it undermines all diachronic moral accountability) and pinpoint exactly where in the sequence each should be preempted; provide one precise check-for-understanding question that reveals whether students grasp the dependency relation rather than merely parroting the negation of self; and state explicitly which debates—such as the Abhidharma analysis of momentariness or the Madhyamaka critique of svabhava—should be bracketed as beyond scope, with a brief rationale for each omission. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
