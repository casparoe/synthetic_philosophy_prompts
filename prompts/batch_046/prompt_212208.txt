I was chatting with a friend over coffee and she laid out this argument that now feels suspicious because it seems to demolish both major theories at once. Here's the chain: (1) Epistemicism says predicates like 'bald' have sharp boundaries we just can't locate. (2) But if the cutoff is exactly n hairs, then 'has exactly n hairs' is itself vauge — split ends, thinning patches, broken follicles all blur the count. (3) So epistemicism for baldness forces epistemicism about hair-count, which forces it about 'split end', and so on downward ad infinitum. (4) You can't halt that regress without picking an arbitrary, non-vague foundation, but every physical predicate bottoms out in fuzziness. (5) So epistemicism either collapses into an infinite regress or relies on an unjustified stopping point. (6) Supervaluationism dodges this by letting us precisify at each level, but (7) if every level can be precisified either way, then there's no stable fact about whether supervaluationism itself applies — its own status can be precisified away. (8) Therefore both standard views fail and vagueness must be purely pragmatic, not semantic.

I feel like something slips between (4) and (8), maybe in (7) where it assumes meta-level instability, or maybe the regress in (3) overstates the commitment. I'd rather keep epistemicism for ordinary predicates if I can, but I want to know exactly which step to reject and what that costs — does giving up (3) mean accepting that some base-level descriptions are sharp, or does saving epistemicism require a different reply to the regress altogether?

Please seperate what's actually settled in this literature from what's genuinely contested — don't present one camp's reading of higher-order vagueness or precisification as the consensus, since I'm writing for an audience that needs to know where philosophers actually disagree rather than getting a tidy fake agreement.
