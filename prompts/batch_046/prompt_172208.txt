I recently joined the advisory council of a small community archive founded in the early 1970s around a single governing covenant: every acquisition and display decision must be justified exclusively by the artifact's documentary authenticity, with audience appeal, donor relations, and contemporary political climate explicitly excluded as relevant factors. Over time, members have come to regard this covenant not merely as a procedural guideline but as a source of institutional moral authority—departing from it is treated as a kind of corruption of the archive's essential purpose. We are now facing a concrete dispute over whether to retain and exhibit a large collection of state-issued identification documents from a defunct authoritarian regime. One group of council members argues that we should apply the covenant mechanically: if the documents possess documentary authenticity, we must preserve and display them, regardless of whether the exhibition causes real harm to affected communities or triggers funding cuts that would shutter other programs. They maintain that the covenant is authoritative in itself—its rules are binding precisely because they are the rules, not because they happen to align with some external good. Another group contends that the covenant derives its authority from having correctly identified documentary authenticity as a genuinely valuable principle; consequently, when strict adherence to the principle in this particular case undermines the archive's ability to serve the public and protect vulnerable groups, we should interpret or suspend the rule. During a preparatory discussion, someone mentioned that this tension sounds like a classic problem about whether norms are valid because an authority posits them, or whether an authority is legitimate only because it tracks norms that are valid independently. I am unsure how to advise the council. Should I defend the covenant's literal application even when it produces harmful outcomes, on the grounds that its authority is self-grounding? Or should I argue that we are only bound by it to the extent that it aligns with independent moral and practical reasons, which in this case might justify an exception?
