I've been arguing with my roommate about Malthus lately — not the usual 'he was wrong about food' thing, but this weird rhythm I keep noticing with big long-range theories. You know the type: someone predicts a structural collapse or resource crisis by, say, 2040, based on some deep mechanism. Then 2030 comes and everything's basically fine, but instead of treating that as a miss, supporters say the mechanism was real but got temporarily masked — maybe by an unexpected tech boom, maybe by policy — and they just slide the deadline to 2060. Then it happens again. Each individual rescue sounds reasonable, like 'oh, there was an efficency gain nobody forecasted,' but after three or four rounds the theory hasn't really risked being wrong; the failures get reinterpreted as proof that the pressure is still building and there clock is still ticking. I saw this happen with Malthusian-style arguments where famine kept being delayed but people still call the framework prophetic because population eventually outstripped local supply somewhere, eventually. Anyway, I'm not asking about plain ad-hoc immunization — that's too generic. Is there an established concept or name for this specific pattern, where long-horizon predictions survive through a series of individually plausible temporal delays, and defenders treat each near-miss as confirmation that the underlying crisis is merely postponed? If there's no precise term, could you define one with clear edges — especially how it differs from ordinary theory revision and from straight-up unfalsifiability? I want the carving, not just a label.
