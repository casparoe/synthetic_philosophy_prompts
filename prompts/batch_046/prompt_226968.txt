The debate over Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation—famously illustrated by the "gavagai" scenario—has persisted for decades without resolution. Some philosophers read it as a metaphysical claim that there is no objective fact of the matter about meaning; others read it as an epistemic claim that behavioral evidence alone underdetermines translation manuals; and still others contend that compositional and structural constraints in formal semantics remove the indeterminacy for practical purposes. Yet participants rarely change positions. Diagnose why this debate has stalled: is the disagreement verbal, turning on different uses of "meaning," "determination," or "fact"; substantive but obscured by shared vocabulary; or a case of different questions—about ontology, methodology, or formal linguistic structure—being pursued under a single heading? In particular, consider whether an unacknowledged dispute about whether compositional syntax can constrain lexical indeterminacy is doing hidden work. What kind of disagreement is this actually, and what would count as progress: a formal result mapping translation degrees of freedom, a conceptual distinction among types of indeterminacy, or a demonstration that the question is ill-posed?
