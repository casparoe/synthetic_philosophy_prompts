I am drafting a two-page proposal for a six-month independent research fellowship in philosophy of mind. I want to work at the intersection of Buddhist anattā and analytic metaphysics of personal identity, but I want to sidestep the usual comparison with Parfit's reductionism, which I think obscures a more precise distinction. My half-formed idea is that the Buddhist texts deny not merely a permanent metaphysical essence (ātman), but specifically a diachronic executive — a single controller that owns mental states across time — while leaving open whether there is a synchronic subject of present experience. The standard framing collapses both denials into one, and then asks whether the result is eliminativism or reductionism. I want to ask whether these are genuinely separable, and whether they remain separable once we model continuity of selfhood not as identity of substance but as coherence-maintenance in a hierarchical predictive system — i.e., an embodied inference process that minimises prediction error across time without requiring an identical owner.

Please write the proposal: formulate the research question with enough precision that it could be answered negatively; explain why existing analytic and Buddhist-philosophical treatments leave it open (without a literature review by citation); describe the method — conceptual analysis of the diachronic versus synchronic denial, plus a constructed thought experiment involving gradual neural and functional replacement that tests whether predictive coherence can substitute for an executive controller; state what a useful deliverable would be (e.g., a taxonomy of continuity conditions that does not presuppose substance identity); specify what would count as failure (e.g., showing that the diachronic/synchronic distinction collapses under pressure); and assess honestly whether six months is sufficient given that the project is conceptual rather than empirical. Write in a formal register suitable for a funding panel that is sceptical of philosophy projects with no empirical component.
