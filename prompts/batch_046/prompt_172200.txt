I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, have been working through philosophical ethics with the same systematic habit I once applied to measurement error. In a recent seminar on research integrity, a colleague invoked the slogan commonly associated with Kant—the claim that one must never lie, not even to a murderer asking after his intended victim—to argue that scientists facing preliminary data that might trigger public panic have no moral latitude whatsoever to withhold, frame, or delay disclosure. The analogy struck me as powerful but obscure: the slogan seemed to be doing several distinct jobs at once, and I could not tell which job was actually supporting her conclusion about scientific communication.

Could you lay out, as a precise menu of distinct theses, what that one-line prohibition could actually be asserting? For each reading, please specify: (i) the precise nature of the wrong involved—whether it is a violation of perfect duty, a corruption of the liar's rational agency, a breach of an implicit communicative contract, or something else; (ii) the scope of the prohibition—whether it covers all intentional falsehoods, only authoritative assertions made to rational interlocutors, only statements that exploit another's epistemic vulnerability, or only lies told to those who have forfeited their claim to truth; (iii) the exact role assigned to consequences—whether they are categorically excluded from moral reasoning, merely outweighed by duty, or assessed through a different normative framework altogether; and (iv) whether that particular reading genuinely supports the seminar conclusion that a researcher may never suppress or strategically frame dangerous preliminary findings, or whether it instead leaves room for epistemic discretion.

Please treat this as a disambiguation exercise rather than an exegesis of Kant's texts: indicate which readings are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, which are likely false, and which one my colleague probably intended but failed to articulate. I do not have access to libraries or citation databases, so avoid bibliographies, reading lists, and specific textual references; work only with the conceptual distinctions themselves, and apply them directly to the analog between lying to a murderer and managing risky scientific disclosure.
