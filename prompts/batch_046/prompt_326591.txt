I have no formal training in philosophy or linguistics, but I have been thinking on my own about how certain utterances seem to do things rather than merely describe them—the familiar cases where a judge pronounces a verdict or an official declares a meeting open, and by saying it they make it so. I want to push this idea into a less familiar corner. Consider a kind of speech act—let me call it a "mnemonic performative"—in which the utterance not only changes a social or institutional fact, but also retroactively alters the audience's access to the conditions that made the utterance valid. For example, a speaker declares, "I hereby dissolve the agreement," and in doing so not only cancels the contract but also makes it impossible for anyone present to recall that the contract's existence was ever a prerequisite for the dissolution being valid. The audience remembers the cancellation, but the record of what was canceled is erased not physically, but cognitively, as an effect of the utterance itself.

Write a short story centered on a character who attempts or suffers such an act. The narrative should explore whether this still qualifies as a performative utterance when the conditions for its success are themselves modified by its occurrence, and whether there remains any meaningful distinction between what was said and what was done if the saying rewrites the audience's evidence for both. I am not asking for references to specific philosophers or technical literature; I want the concept developed through plot and consequence, avoiding the standard scenarios of marriage ceremonies or legal sentencing. Please make the situation concrete—a specific setting, a specific relationship or institution at stake—and show where the usual boundaries between saying, remembering, and making real begin to break down.
