In forum debates about scientific priority—Newton and Leibniz on calculus, Darwin and Wallace on natural selection—someone always eventually drops “it was just an idea whose time had come” to shut down the fight over attribution. I suspect the phrase is doing more philosophical work than it admits, but I cannot tell whether it expresses a substantive thesis about the modal structure of discovery or is merely a rhetorical excuse for not caring about credit. Could you disambiguate it for me?

Specifically, lay out the distinct precise readings the slogan could carry along at least these axes: (1) ontological inevitability—whether the mathematical or biological structure of reality made this specific formulation unavoidable; (2) epistemic convergence—whether any rational agent with access to the same evidence, methods, and social conditions would eventually reach an equivalent conclusion; (3) sociological redundancy—whether the institutional, technological, and communicative conditions of the era made multiple independent discoveries probable regardless of individual genius; and (4) counterfactual credit—whether, even if the discovery was inevitable in some modal sense, the actual path of formalization, notation, or application depends on specific individuals in a way that should affect attribution.

For each reading, say whether it is trivial or substantive, whether it actually undermines the importance of assigning priority, and whether it applies symmetrically to both the calculus and evolutionary cases or fails in one but not the other. I am especially interested in whether there is a reading that does real work—perhaps by linking inevitability to discovery as a collective epistemic process rather than an individual achievement—versus readings that collapse into “history doesn’t matter.” Present the menu rather than picking a winner, and flag which interpretations people who invoke the slogan in priority disputes probably intend versus which ones they would reject if made explicit.
