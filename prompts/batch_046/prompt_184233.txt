I'm putting together a proposal for a six-month independent study fellowship on the units of selection debate. I don't have formal training in evolutionary biology or philosophy of science, but I've been reading and thinking on my own, and I'm frustrated by how the conversation usually collapses into slogans: genes are replicators, individuals are vehicles, groups are just aggregates. What I actually want to know is whether there is a single, correct answer to "what is the unit of selection," or whether people are using the same word to ask three different questions without noticing. Specifically, I want to ask whether the debate can be dissolved by separating (a) the level at which evolutionary variance is measured, (b) the level at which fitness differences are calculated, and (c) the level at which an actual intervention would change the evolutionary trajectory—because these three might point to different answers in the same case.

The existing positions leave this open because multilevel selection theory tends to say that all levels matter simultaneously, which feels like a diplomatic truce rather than a resolution, while gene-selectionists usually insist that replication is the decisive criterion without explaining why replication should trump predictive control or intervention efficacy. I don't want to write a literature survey or ask for a bibliography; I want to build a conceptual framework that maps exactly how these three criteria relate to one another, and then apply it to one concrete evolutionary transition—say, the emergence of reproductive division of labor in social insects—to see whether the criteria converge or come apart. If they come apart, then the standard debate is not about facts but about which criterion we implicitly prioritize, and we need an argument for that priority rather than more data.

My approach would be to spend the first two months formalizing the logical relations between the three criteria and constructing toy evolutionary scenarios where they diverge; the next two months applying the framework to the social-insect case and to one other transition (perhaps multicellularity) to check robustness; and the final two months writing up whether any of the standard positions—gene-level, individual-level, or group-level—can consistently satisfy all three criteria, or whether each position is forced to reject one criterion as illegitimate. The obvious alternative is to survey empirical studies, but I think that misses the point: this is a conceptual disagreement about what "unit" means, so progress requires clarifying the concept before gathering more evidence. Success would be demonstrating that at least one mainstream position fails on one criterion in a non-ad-hoc way; failure would be finding that the three criteria are merely notational variants, leaving the debate exactly where it started but now unexplained. Given the six-month window, I think this is realistic because I am not trying to resolve evolutionary biology, only to make the conceptual disagreement precise enough that people can see where their intuitions actually conflict.
