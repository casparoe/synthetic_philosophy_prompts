Read Christopher Nolan’s Inception (2010) through the philosophy of perception, specifically disjunctivism about perceptual experience and the argument from illusion. The film’s premise—that a dream can be phenomenally indistinguishable from waking life, so that characters must rely on private totems and bodily “kicks” to tell them apart—seems to presuppose the common-kind thesis that disjunctivism rejects, namely that veridical perception and hallucination share an intrinsic mental nature. At the same time, the nested dream structure and Cobb’s false awakenings with Mal might be read as dramatizing a disjunctivist claim: that waking and dreaming are fundamentally different kinds of consciousness, with the totem functioning not as an external epistemic check but as the manifestation of an internal phenomenal difference. I want a reading that treats neither the film nor the philosophy as mere illustration.

First, identify specific scenes—such as Ariadne’s first constructed dream, the hotel corridor fight, or Cobb’s repeated false awakenings—where the film either relies on or complicates the common-kind assumption. Second, distinguish whether the characters’ anxiety about “not knowing” maps onto the epistemic problem of illusion (how do I know I am not now dreaming?) or onto the metaphysical problem (what is the nature of the mental state I am in?). Third, introduce a less-discussed wrinkle: disjunctivists face well-known difficulties with misperception (e.g., seeing a straight stick as bent in water). Does Inception’s depiction of dream-architecture—where the environment is literally projected by the dreamer and can become unstable—offer a useful analog to misperception? Can disjunctivism accommodate such cases without conceding a shared substrate between veridical and illusory experience, or does the film’s imagery suggest that any adequate theory must allow some common representational medium? Fourth, conclude by saying what the film adds to the debate that pure argument misses—perhaps something about the emotional and agential costs of living without a stable phenomenal boundary between reality and illusion. I am preparing this for a philosophy-of-mind seminar and want to use it without reducing disjunctivism to a form of Cartesian skepticism.
