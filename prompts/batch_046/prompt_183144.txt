At a recent workshop on well-being, two philosophers—Maya and Jordan—fell into a sustained argument about a hypothetical technology that has not received much attention in the standard literature: a retrospective coherence device. Rather than replacing experience with simulation, the device allows a person to live a fully real life, but offers, after the fact of any significant suffering or failure, an optional cognitive restructuring. With explicit post-hoc consent, it integrates the suffering into a larger narrative framework—say, as necessary for a subsequent achievement—and adjusts the emotional coloring of the memory, without altering the objective facts of what occurred or the relationships and accomplishments built around it. Maya defended the device on the grounds that well-being is partly a matter of internal coherence and satisfied evaluation. She argued that using it is just an accelerated version of the psychological processes people already employ to process trauma, and that refusing it amounts to a fetish for raw, unmediated pain. She added that since the objective achievements and relationships remain intact—the suffering really happened, the success really followed—there is no authenticity cost. Jordan countered that this misses the core challenge raised by the classic experience-machine thought experiment: the problem is not false belief per se, but whether one's evaluative mental states are properly connected to reality. He claimed the retrospective device creates an epistemic divorce between one's present endorsement of past suffering and the suffering itself; one now values the pain only because the technology has made it emotionally palatable, not because one genuinely values the achievement it enabled. He went further, saying this is actually worse than the original simulation scenario, since with a standard experience machine at least one knows one is in a simulation, whereas here one believes one is reflecting authentically on one's real past while one's evaluation has been engineered. Maya replied that Jordan is smuggling in an objective-list theory of well-being under the guise of an epistemic point—proper connection to reality only matters if well-being depends on actually achieving things rather than on being satisfied with one's achievements. She pressed him on why natural psychological adaptation, such as the gradual acceptance that often follows grief, is not equally inauthentic, since both involve present mental states altering the emotional valence of past events. Jordan answered that there is a principled distinction: natural adaptation is a continuous process of the same self responding to its own history, whereas the device introduces an external technological mediator that severs the causal chain of personal development. He concluded that if retrospective restructuring is permitted, we lose the capacity to distinguish between a life that was genuinely meaningful and one that was merely made to feel meaningful after the fact, which undermines the very concept of well-being assessment.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of Maya's and Jordan's four interventions, rate whether it is clearly mistaken, mostly wrong, mostly right, or clearly right, and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both parties are missing that would help resolve the dispute, particularly with respect to the distinction between experienced well-being, narrative well-being, and the conditions under which a mental state's causal origin matters for its evaluative authority.
