You are the senior language-policy editor at a humanities press that processes roughly thirty manuscripts per month, and you need a reusable fifteen-minute audit procedure—applicable by anyone with copy-editing experience but no specialized linguistics training—to determine whether a submitted text relies on the hidden gender-default assumptions Douglas Hofstadter satirized in his essay on sexist language and analogical reversibility: the invisible male norm, asymmetrical markedness (gender specified for women but left unmarked for men), and the failure of analogical substitution (if replacing gender terms with race or ethnicity markers produces an obviously biased result, the gendered original is carrying hidden weight). The procedure must work from a quick scan and a fixed set of test sentences rather than from a full close reading; it must specify, in strict order, what to check, what counts as a clear pass or flag, where to stop and require revision, and where the decision is genuinely a value judgment that no checklist can settle—especially when an author appeals to historical accuracy, grammatical convention, or disciplinary tradition. Include explicit misfire warnings: cases where the audit will flag language that is not actually unjustified, and cases where bias is structurally present but will slip through. Structure your deliverable as follows: first, a one-paragraph statement of the governing principle, defining what "default assumption" means in this audit context and why analogical reversibility is the central conceptual test; second, the ordered checklist itself, with each step written as a concrete action (e.g., "Scan all generic human references..."), a binary or trinary decision rule, and a note identifying which conceptual distinction from the framework is being operationalized; third, three worked applications run step-by-step—(a) a philosophy manuscript that uses masculine generics throughout with the author defending them as grammatically neutral; (b) a sociology study that uses gender-neutral nouns but applies gendered modifiers asymmetrically (e.g., "the female surgeon" versus "the surgeon" for male subjects), producing a flag that forces a rewrite of an otherwise polished section—this is the unwelcome result; and (c) a historical study of a nineteenth-century gender-segregated institution, showing exactly where the procedure stops and hands the decision to human judgment; for each case, state the verdict and identify the specific feature that does the work. Finally, add a "structural limitation" paragraph: name one kind of gendered bias or linguistic effect that this checklist cannot capture regardless of refinement, explain why it is unavoidable rather than fixable with more detail, and note what an editor must do instead. Write in American English.
