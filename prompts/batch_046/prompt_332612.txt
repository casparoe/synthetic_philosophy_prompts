I've noticed a recurring asymmetry in how people argue about connections between events, and I am unsure whether it has a recognized name in philosophy or statistics. In classroom discussions about health trends or environmental correlations, people often treat independence as the default position: they say there is "no mechanism linking A to B" or "no reason to think they are connected," and that seems to end the discussion, as though independence requires no positive defense. But when someone actually demonstrates a correlation or a shared cause, the burden suddenly shifts to explaining the precise mechanism. By contrast, in formal probability, independence is a strong, substantive claim that must be justified with data or explicit modeling assumptions; it is never treated as a free null hypothesis. So everyday reasoning seems to operate with a heuristic presumption that absence of a known link proves independence, while formal reasoning treats independence as a precise condition that needs support. Is there an established concept for this heuristic — the informal treatment of independence as the burden-free default — and how does it differ from statistical independence or from a simple argument from ignorance? If it is recognized in studies of heuristic reasoning, what distinguishes it from other default-assumption biases, and if not, what would be the right way to characterize its scope and limits? I would like to understand whether this gap between informal and formal treatments of independence is considered a systematic feature of human reasoning or just an occasional rhetorical move.
