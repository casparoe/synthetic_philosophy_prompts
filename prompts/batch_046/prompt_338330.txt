In my philosophy-of-value reading group, two members—Mara and Leo—got into a sustained disagreement about whether Frank Ramsey’s philosophy allows any coherent answer to the question of life’s meaning. Mara began by arguing that Ramsey’s pragmatist account of belief, on which holding a belief is essentially having a habit of action oriented by expected outcomes, leaves no room for “the meaning of life” as a genuine proposition. She claimed that for Ramsey, cognitive content is exhausted by predictive and practical guidance; a claim like “human life is oriented toward cooperative inquiry” does not predict any specific experience, so it either expresses an emotion disguised as a judgment or must be translated into a set of instrumental preferences. Leo pushed back, saying that this ignores a distinction he takes to be implicit in Ramsey’s separation of scientific reasoning from practical commitment: between cognitive significance and what he called projective significance, the latter being a rational agent’s long-range ordering of ends, which is not verified by prediction but by global coherence with one’s entire system of values and probabilities.

Mara answered that Leo is simply relabeling the problem. If projective significance is just a name for a stable preference ordering, then asking about the meaning of life reduces to asking which long-term goals are most coherent with one’s probabilities, and that is a technical decision problem, not an existential one. She added that Ramsey’s probabilistic rationality treats ends as parameters given from outside the system—what she called parametric ends—so there is no internal mechanism for justifying one global narrative over another; coherence alone cannot generate meaning, it only organizes it. Leo replied that this is too quick: coherence is not merely organizational but constitutive. He introduced a distinction between local coherence, the consistency of immediate choices with short-term beliefs, and global narrative coherence, the fit between a lifelong project and the evolving structure of one’s commitments. On his view, Ramsey’s framework actually supports the idea that an agent rationally revises not only beliefs but also ends when they fail to cohere globally, which means the question of meaning becomes the problem of achieving and maintaining global narrative coherence, not a pseudo-question at all.

Mara then conceded that global coherence might be a legitimate rational ideal but insisted that Leo has smuggled in a teleological assumption that Ramsey’s system rejects. She argued that if ends are genuinely revisable, then there is no stable meaning being pursued—only a sequence of reconstructed preferences—so Leo’s account collapses into a form of existential drift rather than meaningful orientation. Leo countered that drift is only inevitable if one assumes a foundationalist picture of value, where meaning must rest on an unchanging axiom; once that assumption is dropped, the rational pursuit of global coherence over time just is what it means for a life to have meaning, without requiring any external guarantee. They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not decide. Please go through their exchange point by point and say who is right about each specific claim—particularly about whether Ramsey’s framework treats ends as parametric, whether global narrative coherence can serve as a rational criterion without collapsing into drift, and whether Mara is correct that coherence alone cannot generate existential significance. Please also say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing—whether that is a distinction within Ramsey’s own writings, a third option between parametric and reconstructive ends, or something else entirely.
