In the novel I am writing, a scene debates whether citizens should hide anything from the state. One speaker, defending an American-style openness and implicitly echoing the logic behind Chinese social-credit transparency, invokes the familiar image that sunlight serves as the best disinfectant: citizens should live in glass houses, since hidden corners only breed corruption and only the guilty fear exposure. A lawyer, drawing on German informational self-determination doctrine, rejects this and offers a different analogy — privacy not as a wall but as the selective permeability of a cell membrane, regulating exchange rather than sealing off the organism.

Can you audit the glass-house analogy precisely: what maps to sunlight, disinfectant, hidden corners, and glass walls in the citizen-state relation; which architectural or biological features it quietly imports that do not belong there; and whether the conclusion — that citizens ought to hide little — survives once those disanalogies are on the table? Then assess her membrane counter-analogy: does it correct the failures, or does it import its own misleading features — for instance, treating the state as an external environment rather than an internal authority, or ignoring collective goods that transparency might serve?
