I'm drafting my position for a philosophy-of-psychiatry seminar and want a consistency audit before I present. Here are the commitments I intend to defend:

(a) Psychiatric categories are interactive kinds in Hacking's sense: being sorted into a diagnosis changes self-understanding and behavior, which feeds back into the category's extension and intension.

(b) The looping effect is asymmetrical: conditions with robust biological markers exhibit negligible looping, whereas conditions defined mainly by narrative coherence and first-person report exhibit strong looping.

(c) Taxonomic revision should be governed by predictive validity and clinical utility, not by whether a category counts as a natural kind.

(d) Some mental conditions involve genuine dysfunctions of cognitive or affective mechanisms that exist prior to and independently of classification.

(e) A category largely constituted by looping—where the diagnostic framework teaches individuals to organize experience into symptoms they otherwise would not have exhibited—should still be retained if it shows high clinical utility and reasonable predictive validity.

(f) Clinicians have an epistemic obligation to disclose to patients when a diagnosis is an interactive kind rather than a biologically anchored condition.

(g) For at least some interactive conditions, such disclosure undermines the therapeutic loop that partly generates the category's clinical utility.

Please audit whether this set hangs together. Rank the tensions by severity: identify which pairs are genuinely incompatible, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on inspection, and what the least costly revision would be if consistency demands one. In particular, address whether (a) together with (b) undermines the predictive-validity criterion in (c); whether (e) conflicts with (d); and whether (f) and (g) generate a practical contradiction with (c) and (e). Then say what argument or piece of evidence would most likely change your verdict about where the deepest tension lies, and point to the specific step in your reasoning where you are least confident.
