Write an essay on whether the past can genuinely be told from everywhere at once, or whether every global history necessarily conceals a centre. Examine the claim that Eurocentrism is not only a matter of biased archives but a structural feature of narrative form itself: any world-spanning account requires a temporal anchor (a periodisation scheme, a calendar) and a spatial protagonist (the region whose transformations supply the plot's engine), and every available anchor embeds a particular geopolitical origin. Set against this the idea of a "network historiography" that treats civilisations as nodes of exchange with no default vantage point. Assess whether such an approach solves the problem or merely dissolves global history into disconnected local chronicles. In particular, consider whether abandoning unified chronology — for instance, replacing BCE/CE with multiple incommensurable temporal frameworks — is the necessary price of decentring, or whether coherence can be preserved without a single master timeline. Reach and defend a clear conclusion: is a genuinely polycentric global history achievable, and if so, what must conventional historiography surrender?
