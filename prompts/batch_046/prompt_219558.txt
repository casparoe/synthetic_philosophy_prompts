Exam question:
Recent synthetic-biology protocols have produced “chimeric prokaryote consortia”—engineered bacterial populations that share a designed plasmid pool permitting rapid horizontal gene transfer across chromosomal lineages that would otherwise be reproductively isolated. Defenders argue these consortia form a single evolutionary unit; critics maintain they remain distinct species because each lineage retains its own chromosomal backbone. Evaluate this dispute using at least two philosophical accounts of species (for instance, Mayr’s Biological Species Concept, Cracraft’s Phylogenetic Species Concept, and/or Van Valen’s Ecological Species Concept). Specifically, address whether reproductive isolation remains meaningful when gene flow is engineered rather than natural, and whether shared plasmid pools constitute shared evolutionary fate or shared adaptive zone.

Student answer:
Mayr’s Biological Species Concept defines a species as a group of actually or potentially interbreeding natural populations that are reproductively isolated from other such groups. Although bacteria reproduce asexually, philosophers extend the BSC to microbes by treating gene flow as the analog of interbreeding. Because the consortium’s plasmid pool permits extensive gene flow among lineages, they fail the isolation criterion; hence they should be treated as one species.

Cracraft’s Phylogenetic Species Concept defines a species as the smallest monophyletic group diagnosed by a unique derived trait. Because the consortia share a horizontally transferred plasmid pool, they possess a shared evolutionary fate and a common derived genetic module; thus, under the PSC they also constitute one species, since the plasmid functions as a shared autapomorphy uniting the pool.

Van Valen’s Ecological Species Concept holds that a species is a lineage evolving separately within its own adaptive zone. The consortia members were engineered for complementary metabolic roles—nitrogen fixation, carbon fixation, and waste processing—so they inhabit distinct adaptive zones. Consequently, the ESC supports recognizing multiple species, not one.

In sum, both the BSC and the PSC favor treating the consortia as a single species, whereas the ESC favors pluralism.
