Question: A post-colonial state in the mid-twentieth century institutes an annual national holiday centered on a so-called medieval charter of communal origin. Archival and philological analysis later demonstrates that the charter was composed in the late nineteenth century by nationalist antiquarians, yet it is displayed in a state museum as material proof that the nation’s ethnic consciousness has remained continuous since the medieval period. Drawing on Hobsbawm’s analysis of invented tradition and Anderson’s framework of imagined communities, analyze how this artifact operates simultaneously as an invented tradition and as a mechanism of national imagination. In particular, address whether the temporal discontinuity between the charter’s actual origin and its claimed antiquity undermines the ancient quality that Anderson associates with national consciousness, or whether Hobsbawm’s framework predicts precisely this kind of discontinuity being ritually masked.

Student answer:
This case exemplifies Hobsbawm’s concept of invented tradition because the charter was produced in the late nineteenth century during rapid social transformation, and its ritual museum display serves to legitimize present institutions by projecting them into a distant past. Hobsbawm argues that such traditions are conscious fabrications designed to deceive populations into accepting state authority, which is exactly what occurs when citizens treat the forged document as medieval proof of ethnic continuity. Turning to Anderson, a nation is an imagined community because its members hold a mental image of communion with anonymous fellow citizens. Anderson maintains that this imagination depends on print-capitalism and standardized vernaculars, and that it requires an empirically continuous historical narrative; consequently, if the charter’s nineteenth-century origin is exposed, the temporal rupture undermines the ancient quality essential to national consciousness and the imagined community dissolves. The state also borrows the formal structure of European charters, illustrating Anderson’s observation that nationalism is modular. Therefore, the discontinuity is fatal to both frameworks: Hobsbawm predicts masking but not that exposure destroys the tradition’s function, while Anderson’s model cannot survive a revealed break in continuity.
