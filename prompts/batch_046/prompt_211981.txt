I'm taking an introductory course on ancient philosophy and came across a popular blog post that tries to reconcile Plato's metaphysics with modern science. The author summarizes Plato's position like this: "In the Republic, Plato argues that the physical world is literally a shadow cast by the Forms onto a wall of matter, and that prisoners chained inside a cave mistake these shadow-images for reality. The philosopher escapes, sees the sun—which is literally the Form of the Good—and realizes that every material object is an imperfect copy of its corresponding Form. Crucially, Plato intends this as an empirical cosmological mechanism: he expects that a complete science of optics and particle physics will eventually reveal that atoms and light waves behave exactly like the shadow-puppet mechanics he describes. So when modern quantum mechanics says that particles are probabilistic, non-local, and not shadow-like at all, Plato's theory of Forms is not just outdated but empirically falsified."

Something about this feels off, especially the claim that Plato meant the Cave as a literal physical mechanism open to experimental refutation, but I'm not sure which parts are harmless simplification and which are real distortions. Could you audit this summary point by point? Please separate what is faithful to the Republic and standard Platonic scholarship, what is a common pedagogical simplification that doesn't mislead too badly, and what is a substantive misreading that a defender of Plato's metaphysics would reject. In particular, address whether the Form of the Good is correctly identified with the sun in the allegory, whether Plato intended the Cave as an empirical prediction about optics or physics, and whether quantum mechanics genuinely bears on the theory of Forms in the way the blogger suggests.
