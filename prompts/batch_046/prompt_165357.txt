I am a retired experimental neurobiologist who, over the past five years, has turned systematically to philosophy of mind. I am applying for a one-year independent fellowship—suitable for interdisciplinary review by both scientists and philosophers—to develop a monograph-length study of approximately forty thousand words. The guiding question is whether emotions are best understood as judgments, perceptions, or feelings, but I do not intend to defend any of the three standard positions. Instead, I want to test whether they all share an unstated assumption that emotional states are representational—whether they represent values, bodily changes, or phenomenal qualities—and whether abandoning that assumption in favor of a calibration-signal model changes the landscape. On the calibration-signal view, emotional episodes are not representations at all, but precision-weighted adjustments that regulate the gain on interoceptive predictions within a hierarchical predictive system. The proposal should state the research question precisely: if emotions are calibration signals rather than representational states, does this dissolve the classic stalemates—such as the problem of recalcitrant emotions, the direction-of-fit debate, and the phenomenological transparency of affect—or does it merely relocate them at a different level of analysis? It should explain why judgmentalism, perceptualism, and pure-feeling theories leave this question open, particularly because each takes representational content to be the correct explanatory target. The method should be conceptual but constrained by empirical plausibility: I will work through three canonical problem cases—fear in the absence of danger, grief after the belief in loss has been revised, and affective inertia that outlasts its cognitive trigger—and ask whether the calibration-signal model predicts the correct phenomenological and behavioral profile without invoking representational content. The deliverable should specify what success looks like (a coherent framework that distinguishes emotional calibration from ordinary homeostatic regulation and explains the three cases), what failure looks like (the model collapses into one of the standard three views or cannot account for the normative and motivational dimensions of emotion), and an honest assessment of scope: can this be executed in twelve months without original empirical data collection, and where must I limit the argument to avoid overreaching into neuroscience proper? Write this as a formal two-page fellowship proposal. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I will supply references myself. The tone should be polished, rigorous, and appropriate for an interdisciplinary panel.
