Rank the following five interpretive stances on Einstein's remarks concerning religion and human freedom, ordering them from strongest (1) to weakest (5) according to a single composite criterion: the degree to which each stance simultaneously preserves (i) Einstein's explicit deterministic claims about the physical world, (ii) his expressions of moral admiration for individuals acting with autonomous purpose or inner freedom, and (iii) internal consistency with a Spinozistic theology in which God is identical with the ordered totality of nature, without invoking biographical evidence that Einstein never explicitly articulated. The stances are: (A) hard determinism, which treats his cosmic religious feeling as purely aesthetic awe and takes his denial of free will literally; (B) compatibilism, which distinguishes causal determination from rational self-legislation; (C) illusionism, which holds that free will is a useful fiction sustained by ignorance of neural causation; (D) the pantheistic-freedom synthesis, which identifies determinism with Spinoza's substance-mode relation and treats human choice as a mode of divine necessity; and (E) pragmatic humanism, which separates scientific determinism from ethical appeals to personal responsibility without attempting metaphysical reconciliation. For each rank, provide exactly one sentence of justification that focuses specifically on how that interpretation fares on criterion (ii)—that is, whether it can genuinely accommodate Einstein's moral language about autonomous human agency without reducing it to error or mere appearance.
