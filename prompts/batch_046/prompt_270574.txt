I have recently taken responsibility for organizing my late grandmother's papers and digital archive, and in sorting letters from her youth alongside her later writings I found myself wondering whether the author is literally the same thing across decades, or only related parts of a longer life. A philosopher acquaintance summarized the endurantism and perdurantism debate as follows: "Endurantists hold that a person is a wholly present three-dimensional object that persists through time like a train through a tunnel, remaining strictly identical at every instant. Perdurantists hold that a person is a four-dimensional spacetime worm composed of temporal parts, so the young letter-writer and the elderly woman are distinct stages of one extended object, not one thing repeated. The temporary intrinsics argument demonstrates that endurantism is contradictory: if the same object is wholly present both bent and straight, it possesses incompatible properties. Hence perdurantism alone explains change consistently, though it entails that you never literally survive to tomorrow — only your future temporal part does, and psychological continuity is just a relation between stages." Before I apply this framework to understand my grandmother's identity across her archive, I want to know whether this summary is fair. Which sentences are accurate representations, which are subtle misrepresentations or overstatements of what defenders actually claim, and does the conclusion that endurantism is contradictory actually follow? In particular, is it correct to say that perdurantism denies literal personal survival, or that endurantists have no reply to the problem of temporary properties?
