People have been fighting about dirty hands in politics since Walzer wrote about it and Sartre's play and all that, and honestly it feels like the same three moves replayed for decades. One side says leaders sometimes have to do genuinely wrong things for the public good and the guilt they feel proves it's real moral conflict, not just calculus. The other side says if an act is justified it's not wrong, so "dirty hands" is either hypocrisy or a confused way of saying consequentialism is true. My working theory is that the stall isn't about facts or experiments at all, but about whether there's a coherent category of "necessary wrong" that carries moral remainder, or if that's just ordinary failure dressed up in political language. Is that the actual fracture, or is there a hidden empirical question about what decision-makers actually face? Or are the two sides answering diffrent questions -- one asking what we should excuse, another asking what we should expect from people in roles? I want a real diagnosis of the disagreement structure, supported by what each side actually says, plus an honest take on whether anything -- a sharper taxonomy of excuses, empirical data on actual leaders, a formal distinction between role and personal morality -- could actually end it or at least move it. And please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who know the topic; don't present one side like it's the consensus.
