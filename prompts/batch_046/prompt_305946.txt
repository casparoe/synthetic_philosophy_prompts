Hey, I'm a retired chemist who finally started reading philosophy after retiring, and I'm stuck on this argument I cooked up about Hempel's raven paradox. It feels like it proves way too much but I can't see the leak.

(1) Hempel said that if an obsrevation confirms a hypothesis H, and H is logically equivilent to H', then that same obsrevation confirms H'.
(2) "All ravens are black" is logically equivilent to "All non-black things are non-ravens."
(3) So seeing a white shoe — non-black and not a raven — confirms that all ravens are black.
(4) Now generalize: any universal law "All A's are B's" is equivilent to "All non-B's are non-A's."
(5) That means every single thing I observe that isn't both A and B confirms every universal law in science.
(6) So when I was measuring reaction rates in my labratory for forty years, I was automatically giving massive evidential support to every biological, geological, and sociological law out there, just because none of my beakers violated them.
(7) But if that's true, then specialization is a total waste — a biologist studying ravens learns nothing extra that a chemist hasn't already confirmed.
(8) That can't be right; specialization clearly works and disciplines don't collapse into one big confirmation pool.
(9) So the mistake must be in (1) or the way I'm applying logical equivalence to confirmation. But (1) looks like pure logic.
(10) Where does this go wrong? I'd rather keep (1) and (4) if I can, since they just seem like formal truths, but I'm willing to ditch the leap to (6) if that's where the rot is. What am I paying if I do?
