I have been reading Wittgenstein's On Certainty on my own, not in any course, and I am stuck on a passage where he argues that doubt requires grounds and that if one attempts to doubt the very framework that makes doubt possible—such as that there is an external world or that one's memory is generally reliable—one does not achieve deeper skepticism but rather steps outside the conditions under which doubting makes sense. He also writes, in effect, that a child learns to take certain things for granted and only afterward learns to ask questions about them, so the hinge-like commitments come before inquiry rather than after it.

What I cannot tell is whether he means that these hinge commitments function like the rules of a game—so that questioning them is not false but literally not a move within the game—or whether they are more like the table that supports the pieces, something that could be replaced but only if the entire practice is rebuilt around it. I also keep returning to a distinction I have not seen discussed: there seem to be explicit hinges, such as the belief that the earth is old, and then there are silent ones, such as the unexamined assumption that one's shadow is not a separate agent observing one's actions. Do these silent hinges share the same epistemic status as the explicit ones, or are they a different kind of background? I would appreciate help understanding the passage and whether this distinction is useful or collapses back into Wittgenstein's original point.
