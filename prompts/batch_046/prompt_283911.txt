I've come to accept a kind of debunking take on Wigner's old point about mathematics being unreasonably effective in the natural sciences—not the standard Platonist or formalist responses, but something closer to a selection-bias story. The idea is that we call something a 'natural science' precisely when formal structures happen to track its behavior well, so the effectiveness of math in physics isn't a deep metaphysical mystery; it's definitional. We've just quietly exluded all the domains where math flops—ecological collapse, subjective experience, historical contingency—to softer disciplines or to 'not science yet.' So Wigner is marveling at a tautology dressed up as wonder.

What would actually count against this? I want the real defeaters, not just hand-waving about beauty. Specifically: are there hard-science successes where formalism predicted phenomena that nobody was hunting for—like Dirac's equation and antimatter—where the selection story can't apply because the math came first? Or results showing that mathematicl structures appear in nature even when we haven't engineered the domain to be measurable? I'd also like to know whether this view risks being unfalsifiable: if every failure of math gets explained away as 'well that's not real science,' has the position insulated itself into uselessness? Please rank the counter-arguments from fatal to merely awkward, include what a defender of Wigner's original mystery view would themselves concede as damaging, and tell me honestly if my debunking story has been qualified into unfalsifiability and whether that is a problem for a view of this kind.
