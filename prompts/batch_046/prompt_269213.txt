I am taking a course on formal epistemology and recently found myself in a long thread on a rationalist forum about logical uncertainty. The participants kept reaching for the analogy that logical induction is essentially a prediction market for mathematical statements: each sentence is an asset, the assigned probability is the market price, the computable trading strategies are the agents, and the no-arbitrage constraint is the mechanism that corrects mispricing. They use this to argue that we can have coherent, meaningful degrees of belief in open problems—whether P equals NP, or the consistency of large cardinals—without ever resolving them, as though the market had settled on a fair value.

I think the analogy is doing hidden work, but I want it audited rather than repeated. In real financial markets, settlement is external and definitive, liquidity depends on genuinely heterogeneous agents with private information, and arbitrage is profitable only because someone can actually execute the correcting trade and claim the surplus. Logical induction has none of these: there is no external resolver for unproven conjectures, the traders are all computable functions drawn from a single predefined class, and coherence is enforced algorithmically rather than through competitive profit-seeking. So I need a careful dissection.
