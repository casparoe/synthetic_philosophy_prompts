Please consider the following notes for an essay I am drafting for an advanced seminar in philosophy of language and social ontology. Rather than rehearsing the familiar spatial version of Putnam’s Twin Earth argument, I wish to develop what I call a diachronic, or temporal, Twin Earth scenario: imagine not two physically separated communities with identical mental states but divergent environments, but rather a single community whose collective practices, institutional frameworks, and normative expectations gradually shift across generations. I apply this framing specifically to institutional terms—those governing contractual obligation, social recognition, or collective responsibility—where the ‘environment’ that allegedly fixes reference includes mutable norms and shared intentions rather than merely physical substances. I distinguish between chemical anchoring, appropriate to natural kind terms, and normative anchoring, appropriate to institutional kinds. The tension I identify is that if semantic externalism holds for institutional terms, then reference may drift imperceptibly as norms evolve, producing a ‘slow Twin Earth’ effect in which speakers continue to employ the same vocabulary while its extension has silently diverged. I then ask whether this undermines the stability of institutional meaning altogether, or whether collective intentionality supplies an internalist constraint that resists pure environmental determination, thereby restricting the domain of externalism.
