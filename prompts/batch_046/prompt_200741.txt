I'm running a grad seminar on philosophy of history, about 12 people, 90 minutes. We're working on hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy in historical explanantion — the way knowing an outcome makes it feel inevitable and pushes us to smooth the past into a coherent arc. I want a runnable in-class activity where they feel that pull themselves before anyone names it, not just hear me describe it.

Design a simulation with rules and small stakes. Split them into pairs of "archival historians" working on a fictional political crisis (stripped of real-world outcome labels). Give them Stage 1 documents that are genuinely ambiguous — maybe three competing factors with no clear weight. They have to write a brief causal explanation and assign confidence scores, then defend it to another pair. Then hand them a Stage 2 "newly declassified" document that reveals the actual result, plus a hidden scoring rule: they get points for narrative coherence but lose points for hedging or adding post-hoc causes. Force them to revise under time pressure and present the revision. The payoff can be peer-vote tokens or just ranking on a board, no real money needed.

Please include a timeline for the 90 minutes, the exact materials or document summaries you'd use, the debrief that maps each stage onto hindsight bias versus the narrative fallacy, and an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce (maybe the emotional gravity of real tragedy?). Also tell me what will definatly go wrong the first run — I suspect people will see the trap coming? — and how to fix it. I'm especially interested in injecting a "retrospective coherence trap" where revising an old cause feels like breaking rules but adding a new one feels like cheating; build that pressure into the roles if you can.

Finally, please say what would change your mind about whether this actually works, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail — for instance if the fictional scenario is too artificial to trigger genuine bias.
