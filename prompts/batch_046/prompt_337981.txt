I'm a doctoral student in intellectual history, drafting a chapter on progress narratives in nineteenth-century historiography, and I want a consistency audit of my own methodological commitments before I finalize the argument. Here is the package as it stands:

(1) I accept the standard anti-Whig critique: treating the past as an inevitable progression toward present institutions imposes a false teleology, flattens the contingency of past struggles, and ignores that historical actors did not understand themselves as moving toward our outcomes.

(2) I also take Koselleck's framework of Erwartungshorizont to be indispensable: every agent operates within a field of future-oriented expectations, so writing history without reference to the outcomes actors themselves anticipated is impossible and would misrepresent their lived experience.

(3) I hold that normative evaluation of past practices—condemning chattel slavery, for instance, or the denial of formal rights to women—is not only permissible but required for moral coherence, even though such evaluation applies present standards that the actors in question did not share.

(4) I maintain that there are genuinely directional changes in some domains—literacy rates, life expectancy, the formal expansion of rights—that are not purely retrospective projection; denying any pattern of improvement seems to me as methodologically distorting as insisting on inevitable progress.

(5) Finally, I think the historian's primary duty is to recover the otherness of the past, yet I also think that asking how present conditions emerged from prior ones is a legitimate and unavoidable historical question, provided it is pursued as a genealogical tracing of contingent lineages rather than as a triumphalist narrative.

My suspicion is that some of these commitments pull against each other in ways I haven't fully mapped. (1) and (5) seem to conflict: if I reject teleology, does asking how we arrived here smuggle in the very Whig framework I reject? (2) and (1) also look tense, since talking about actors' anticipated futures might seem to license outcome-oriented narration. Meanwhile, (3) appears to require a present-centered evaluative stance that (1) and (5) both seem to forbid. At the same time, I think (2) and (4) may actually reinforce each other if directional change is reconstructed from actors' own horizons rather than imposed retrospectively—so perhaps one tension is only apparent.

Can you audit this set? I want a committed answer, not a menu of options: rank the genuine tensions by severity, say which pairs only look incompatible and dissolve on inspection, identify the single least costly revision that restores consistency (whether dropping one commitment or reinterpreting a clause), and tell me specifically whether I should abandon one of (1)-(5) entirely. Be precise about what each commitment logically commits me to, and do not hedge by presenting every combination as equally viable—make the call and defend it.
