I'm on my university's Arts & Experiential Ethics Commitee — yes, that's the actual name, don't ask — and we've got a proprosal for Thursday's meeting that I need help framing. There's an artist collective asking to run a three-week installation called The Weight of Water. Participants enter knowing everything is scripted and acted, but they're placed in an immersive fictional hospice where they make caregiving choices that cause fictional patients to suffer or be comforted. The designers want deep emotional engagement — grief, guilt, relief — and they argue that informed consent covers it since everyone knows it's not real.

My co-chair thinks this is a no-brainer approval: fiction is fiction, people consent, art should push boundaries. But I'm stuck on the paradox of fiction — specifically, why do we feel real grief for people we know don't exist? One of the external reviewers threw in this niche idea she calls affective stickiness in fictional moral agency: she claims that when practical reasoning gets engaged by a narrative, the emotional output doesn't just vanish when you update your belief that it's fake. There's leftover moral residue, she says, and consent given beforehand might not cover harm that participants can't actually anticipate because they think knowing it's fiction will protect them.

I need you to draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday. Please act like a senior ethics reviewer who actually knows philosophy of art — think step by step, maybe brainstorm the key considerations first, then get to the point. The memo should recommend one concrete course of action (approve, reject, or approve with conditions) with a specific timeframe, and it needs to take the strongest argument on the other side seriously rather than dismissing it. Don't give me a bibliography or cite specific papers; just work with the concepts. And please, don't just say it depends — the committee needs to vote.
