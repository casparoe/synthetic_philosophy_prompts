I was talking with my friend Jordan after a seminar on suffering-focused ethics, and we got into whether pleasure and pain are even on the same scale. Jordan was defending this position she calls lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism — the idea that there's a qualitative break between ordinary discomfort and real suffering, and once you're in the suffering zone no amount of pleasure can justify leaving someone there. She said you don't need a cardinal scale comparing the two; it's more like a lexical rule in deontic logic, eliminate severe suffering first, where the threshold just marks the boundary of that suffering-type.

Casey, my other friend, said that's cheating: if pleasure and pain are incommensurable you can't claim suffering dominates pleasure, you can only say they're different kinds. He pointed to revealed preferences — people take risks for fun all the time — as proof there's a common currency, and argued Jordan's threshold is just an intuitive gut-feel dressed up as structure. Jordan shot back that revealed preference is messed up by adaptive preferences and status quo bias; people endure chronic pain because they think they must, not because it's comparable to a good meal. Casey then said if the break is genuinly qualitative, Jordan doesn't need lexical ordering at all — she's just describing plural goods, and the lexical part is redundant unless she sneaks in cardinal comparison through the back door. Jordan replied that lexical priorities don't require cardinality — never-cause-X can outrank cause-Y regardless of quantity — and the threshold just defines what counts as X-type suffering. Casey ended by asking if that collapses her view into plain harm-avoidance with no real claim about pleasure.
