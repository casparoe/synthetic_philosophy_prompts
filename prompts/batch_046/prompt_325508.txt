I've been spending time with the modern Stoic revival—the books, podcasts, and social media accounts that translate Epictetus's dichotomy of control into everyday language. The basic idea is familiar: some things are within your power, others are not, and wisdom lies in knowing the difference. But watching how this gets applied in practice, I keep seeing a particular asymmetry. When people face a collective or systemic problem—say, a harmful workplace culture or a local environmental issue—they quickly classify the situation as outside their control and treat emotional detachment as the correct Stoic response. Yet when the same individuals pursue personal optimization—career advancement, physical fitness, or productivity systems—they treat outcomes that clearly depend on other people's judgments, market fluctuations, and luck as if they were fully within their control, because they can control their own effort and intentions. Meanwhile, the modern revival itself often markets Stoicism as a self-improvement framework rather than a virtue ethics, which seems to encourage using the dichotomy as emotional insulation rather than as a guide to moral action. I have also heard references to something called a trichotomy of control, in which some matters are partly within our power, which sounds like it might address the gray area I am noticing. My question is whether the selective, self-serving deployment I have described—using the dichotomy to disengage from shared problems while using it to justify intense personal ambition—has an established name or diagnosis in philosophy or psychology. If not, how should it be distinguished from a simple misunderstanding of Stoicism? And does the trichotomy, or any other refinement of the framework, actually resolve the tension I am pointing to, or does it reveal that the modern revival has simply borrowed a philosophical tool for purposes it was never meant to serve?
