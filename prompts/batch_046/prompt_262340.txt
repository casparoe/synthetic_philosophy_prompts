You're the designated methodology reviewer for an interdisciplinary journal publishing qualitative health research, and you need a reusable, ten-minute decision procedure—structured as an ordered checklist with branching questions—to flag potential epistemic injustice in manuscripts that gather first-person testimony about contested chronic conditions (for instance, long COVID, ME/CFS, or chronic Lyme). The procedure should translate Miranda Fricker's framework—testimonial injustice, hermeneutical injustice, credibility deficit, credibility excess, and identity-prejudicial framing—into concrete review actions that require no formal philosophical training, and it must be designed for repeated use with consistent outcomes across different reviewers. It should explicitly mark which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and it must be honest about where it will misfire.

The procedure needs to handle these specific hard cases: (1) a study discounts patient self-reports solely because no validated biomarker exists—distinguishing hermeneutical marginalization from legitimate scientific caution; (2) patient narratives appear in the manuscript only as affective color, never as evidentiary input—determining whether this is testimonial injustice or standard qualitative reporting convention; (3) inclusion criteria require a formal clinical diagnosis, which structurally excludes the most marginalized patients—separating sampling bias from an epistemic exclusion that reinforces hermeneutical injustice.

Present the deliverable as a step-by-step sequence of tests or questions, each with a one-sentence rationale linking it back to the underlying concept, followed by a brief "judgment call" flag where subjectivity is unavoidable. Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical manuscripts: (a) one in which it yields a false-positive flag of injustice despite technically rigorous methodology; (b) one in which a subtle hermeneutical gap slips through—specifically, patients lack an adequate conceptual vocabulary to describe post-exertional malaise, and the study inappropriately imports a generic fatigue framework—showing exactly where the checklist fails; (c) one technically sound manuscript in which the procedure returns an unwelcome result: the study meets every conventional quality criterion but systematically assigns credibility excess to clinician interpretation and credibility deficit to patient testimony, and you must explain precisely what the procedure detects and why a reviewer might resist acting on it.

Finally, state clearly two things the procedure cannot do: one involving the resolution of empirical disputes about the underlying ontology of the contested condition, and one involving the remediation of structural inequities that lie outside the manuscript's direct scope. Specify that you have fifteen minutes per manuscript, that your assessment must be defensible to an editor with no philosophy background, and that the output should be a one-paragraph recommendation with a confidence rating rather than a binary verdict.
