I'm working on my dissertation in moral philosophy and metaphysics of responsibility, and I've become convinced that karma is best reconstructed not as popular belief in cosmic payback, but as a rigorous, agent-independent theory of moral desert. On my view, karma functions as a process-based tracking mechanism in which the causal continuity of intentional mental formations—what the tradition calls sankhara—across temporal discontinuities supplies the only non-theistic grounding for proportional moral outcomes. Unlike divine-command retributivism, it requires no judging consciousness; unlike secular consequentialist accounts of desert, it does not depend on social institutions being able to observe and calibrate rewards across distant causal chains. The feature I most want to defend is what I call the latency thesis: the claim that moral desert does not require temporal contiguity between action and outcome, because the preservation of moral quality occurs through a substrate-neutral causal chain that can span lives. This lets karma escape the epistemic-accessibility objections that undermine secular desert theories—namely, that agents and societies cannot reliably trace remote effects to assign proportionate rewards—yet it also introduces what I term the inter-life identification problem: if the subject of rebirth is only causally continuous and not substantially identical, it is unclear why that future being deserves the outcome.

What would genuinely count against this reconstructed karma theory? I want you to map its exposure—what it risks, where it is vulnerable, and whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability. Specifically, please address the following in order of importance. First, which arguments from philosophy of personal identity—concerning the absence of substantial selfhood across rebirth—would be decisive rather than merely awkward, and which ones should defenders themselves concede? Second, does the latency thesis predict anything that rival theories of moral desert—retributivist, consequentialist, or eliminativist—do not, or has it been protected by ad hoc qualifications that render it compatible with any observed outcome pattern? Third, is there a formal or conceptual result showing that inter-life proportionality violates the identity conditions required for genuine desert, and if so, can the view absorb that blow without collapsing into a rival? Fourth, assess honestly whether the position is well insulated: has it been rendered immune to empirical or conceptual counterexample by redefining 'desert' or 'causation' post hoc, and if that insulation exists, is it a fatal methodological flaw for a theory of this kind? Fifth, state what you personally find most persuasive in overturning it—whether a successful reduction of karmic causation to intra-life psychological conditioning, a demonstration that latency severs the normative link between agent and outcome, or something else altogether.

Please distinguish decisive defeaters from merely uncomfortable ones, say for each whether defenders can absorb it without abandoning the core, and reach a clear verdict on whether the view's insulation is damaging or defensible. I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list—just the arguments and distinctions that should move someone working inside this framework.
