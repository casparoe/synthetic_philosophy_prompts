In discussions of the frame problem, one often hears the comparison to a spreadsheet with lazy evaluation: only the cells affected by an action are recalculated, while the rest remain fixed, and from this it is concluded that the problem dissolves into a matter of efficient data structures rather than deep representational difficulty. Audit this analogy: provide the correspondence table—what corresponds to the spreadsheet, the dependency graph, the recalculation trigger, and the untouched cells? Then show where the mapping fails, especially regarding how the agent knows which beliefs lie outside the causal radius without surveying them all, and regarding the ontological difference between a numerical value that stays constant and a fact that remains true. To sharpen the test, suppose we equip the agent with “epistemic inertia fields”—localized clusters of propositions shielded from revision unless directly intersected by an action’s causal cone. Does this addition repair the analogy or merely relocate the difficulty? Finally, say whether the conclusion that the frame problem is purely an engineering challenge survives once the disanalogies are explicit, or whether the spreadsheet image was doing all the argumentative work.
