In nursing homes there's this practice with advance directives for dementia patients. You sign one saying no feeding tube if you get severely demented and can't recognize family. Then years later you're demented, you're not in pain, you smile and open your mouth for spoon-feeding, and staff usually feed you—like your present behavior is an updated consent that cancels the document. But if you pull out an IV or resist a med, they often cite the old directive and restrain or reinsert, saying the "real you" already made the call. So present acceptance overrules the past refusal, but present refusal doesn't overrule past acceptance? That wierd asymmetry.

What philosophical view of personal identity and autonomy does that practice actually need? Do they implicitly think there's one continuous person whose earlier rational self holds veto power, but the demented self only gets authority when it agrees? Or is it just welfare in disguise—feeding is pleasant and harmless, pulling tubes is dangerous? Reconstruct the implicit theory here, say whether it's actually consistent or just muddling through, and tell me what'd have to change in these facilites if we genuinely believed either that the demented patient is numerically a different person, or that autonomy belongs only to rational agents with long-term plans. I don't need a bibliography, just the conceptual map.
