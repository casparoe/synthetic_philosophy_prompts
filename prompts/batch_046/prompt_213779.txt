So I've been thinking about the classic utility monster scenario, but with a twist I'm calling the "autarkic monster" framing. Instead of the usual story where the monster just gets more pleasure per slice of cake, imagine it produces enormous utility in a totally self-contained loop — no resources are taken from anyone, it's basically a free lunch that we're just refusing to serve. I think this shows the monster only looks bad for aggregationism because we secretly smuggle in a competitive distributive assumption; once you seperate the utility axis from the resource axis, there's no real conflict. My position is that utilitarian-style aggregation survives just fine if we allow these non-competitive welfare gains, and that objections based on "fairness" are tracking a different moral dimension altogether.

But I want to stress-test this properly. What would actually count against my view? I'm not asking for the standard "equality still matters" reply — I want formal problems, like whether multi-dimensional moral theories collapse back into one dimension, or whether consistency conditions in social choice forbid treating autarkic utility differently from competitive utility. Are there hidden cases where the separation breaks down, maybe involving opportunity costs or indirect resource effects I haven't noticed? Would a committed defender of this framing have to concede that it cheats by defining away the moral conflict, or that it risks unfalsifiability? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about whether insulating the view this way is actually a problem for a moral theory of this kind — or if all moral theories end up doing that eventually.
