I am designing a decision-tree handout for a graduate philosophy-of-AI seminar on the conditions under which the intentional stance can be legitimately adopted toward large language models. Rather than having students self-identify with named theories, I want an instrument that sorts them by answers to concrete diagnostic claims.

Please construct a taxonomy in tree form. The root question should be: "If a large language model consistently produces the sentence 'Paris is the capital of France' across diverse prompting contexts, is this sufficient to justify attributing to it the belief that Paris is the capital of France?" Each subsequent branch must be labeled by a specific claim that divides positions, phrased so that a student can answer yes or no without already knowing which philosophical label the answer corresponds to.

Your tree must include branches that address all of the following distinctions:

1. Whether belief-attribution requires an internal discrete symbolic representation carrying that propositional content, or whether distributed patterns of activation across weights are sufficient.
2. Whether the attributed belief must be counterfactually causally efficacious—meaning that, were the representation altered in the relevant way, the model's outputs in that domain would systematically change—or whether predictive success for an external interpreter is enough.
3. Whether first-order belief-attribution requires that the system have second-order access (the capacity to report, reflect on, or correct the belief), or whether observer-relative utility can ground attribution in the absence of any meta-cognitive architecture.
4. Whether the presence of a distinct reward-model or value-head that evaluates outputs introduces an approval-directed dynamic that undermines belief-attribution to the underlying policy network, or whether such an architecture is compatible with genuine belief-holding in the base system.

Every leaf should name a position—such as Dennett-style interpretivism, representationalist realism, eliminativist functionalism, approval-blocked instrumentalism, or "unnamed"—and include exactly one sentence describing its most awkward or counterintuitive commitment. You should also flag any pair of leaves that these four branching questions fail to separate, with a brief diagnosis (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored).

The audience is graduate students in philosophy; please keep the tree to no more than sixteen leaves, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests.
