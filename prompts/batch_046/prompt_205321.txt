Examination question (Philosophy of Art, second-year undergraduate):

W. E. B. Du Bois maintained that all art is propaganda, yet he simultaneously resisted the reduction of Black artistic production to mere political instrumentality. Drawing on his writings from the 1920s, reconcile these two commitments. Your answer should distinguish between “representational propaganda”, in which a work advances a political vision through explicit content or direct message, and “formative propaganda”, in which a work reshapes the aesthetic and perceptual categories through which its subject is encountered. Show why Du Bois believed that Black art had to pursue the latter rather than the former, and explain how this commitment is compatible with the demand that art remain beautiful and truthful rather than didactic.

Student answer:

Du Bois’s position is that every artwork propagates a vision of reality, so in that broad sense all art is propaganda. Applied to Black art, this means producing beautiful, truthful representations of Black life in order to counter racist stereotypes. This is formative propaganda: rather than delivering an explicit political message—which would be representational propaganda—the art transforms how viewers perceive Black people by presenting them with aesthetic dignity. Du Bois therefore rejects representational propaganda outright, because explicit messages necessarily make art less beautiful and therefore undermine its political purpose. Beauty and truth are consequently the instruments through which racial uplift is achieved; they are not sacrificed, because they are the only effective instruments available. The propagandistic aim is prior to the aesthetic execution, but it is realised precisely when the artist refuses to let politics override formal beauty.
