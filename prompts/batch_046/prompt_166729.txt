I am trying to identify a recurring move I encounter in debates about moral blame, and I am unsure whether it already has a name in the philosophical literature or whether it needs one.

Here is the pattern. Someone, call them A, blames another person, B, for some fault F — say, for being careless with commitments, for showing insufficient empathy, or for failing to follow through on a difficult obligation. A third party then points out that A is guilty of F as well, or of something structurally very similar, and therefore that A is being hypocritical and lacks the standing to blame B. Rather than denying the similarity, minimizing their own fault, or withdrawing the blame, A responds by embracing the parallel: they say that precisely because they have F, suffer under it, and understand it from within, they are especially — not deficiently — positioned to blame B for treating it lightly. The shared fault is not presented as a disqualification but as a credential of moral seriousness or experiential authority.

I have seen versions of this in at least two concrete settings. In one, a former addict who now struggles with sobriety harshly criticizes a casual user for treating substance use as trivial; when accused of hypocrisy, they reply that only someone who has lived through the damage can properly recognize its weight, and that this lived burden is what authorizes their blame, not what cancels it. In another, an academic who chronically misses deadlines reprimands a student for lateness; when the student notes the professor's own record, the professor insists that their own failures are exactly what let them see the pattern clearly and therefore justify holding others to the standard they themselves fail to meet.

What strikes me is that this is not the familiar tu quoque defense, because A does not say "you do it too" or "I am not guilty." Nor is it the motte-and-bailey retreat, because A does not weaken the claim being defended. It also does not fit the usual framing in which hypocrisy undermines standing to blame, since here the hypocrisy is explicitly offered as the source of standing.

My questions are these. First, is there an established concept or literature that captures exactly this inversion — where shared fault is leveraged as a source of authority for blame rather than recognized as undermining it? Second, if the nearest existing idea is simply that hypocrisy cancels standing, how should this pattern be classified in relation to it: as a confusion of the concept, a legitimate exception, or a distinct phenomenon altogether? Third, if no precise term exists, how should it be defined and bounded — for instance, must the blamer actually share the fault, or only claim experiential proximity; and does the move apply only to blame, or also to other forms of moral criticism? I am less interested in a bare label than in the carving: what exactly this is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
