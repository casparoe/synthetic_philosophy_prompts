I'm putting together a handout for a philosophy of probability discussion group and I need a taxonomy — like a decision tree — that sorts out the different stancs people take on deference to objective chance, the Principal Principle and all the things that break away from it. I want it built from the logical space, not just a list of famous names: start with whether objective chance exists in a credence-binding way, then branch on whether alignment is exact or defeasible, then on undermining futures (you learn the chance is 0.7 but also some specific fact that pulls the actual outcome away from that distribution). After that, split on symmetry: does deference apply to past chances you've just discovered, future ones, or both? Then one more node about self-locating uncertainty in chancy setups — does it block deferance entirely or just shift the numbers?

The tree should have about seven or eight leaves. Must include: classic exact-deference, undermining-sensitive revision, the view that chances exist but don't constrain credence, and fully symmetric past-and-future deferance. Map out the empty combinations too — like if someone denies chances exist but still wants symmetric deference — and say whether that's incoherent, ugly, or just unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree can't separate cleanly. Keep every node as a concrete question or case someone could answer without knowing the labels first; don't ask for bibliographies or cite specific papers. To keep it from being too generic, use this weird case at one branch: a coin was flipped in 1950 with unknown bias, you now learn the long-run frequency was 60% heads, and you're offered a bet specifically on whether that old flip came up heads — ask what credence you should have and whether learning the bias counts as "admissible" for the old event.
