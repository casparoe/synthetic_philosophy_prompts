Consider the conceptual study of path dependence: whether historical and social outcomes are genuinely "locked in" by early contingent events, or whether they tend toward convergence regardless of initial conditions. Imagine you are advising a small interdisciplinary research group—philosophers, historians of science, and formal modelers—who have three years and modest funding to address conceptual blockages in this area, with no mandate to collect new empirical archives. Which open questions, distinctions, or lines of theoretical work should they prioritize? I am less interested in a survey than in a defended agenda: rank the priorities, and for each explain why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is using existing conceptual and formal tools rather than large-scale data collection, and what kind of result—even a negative one—would cause you to reorder them. In particular, address whether the group should first clarify the distinction between "strong" path dependence, in which a specific contingent branching is necessary for the observed outcome, and "weak" path dependence, in which the path shapes only local form or timing without altering a global attractor; and whether that clarification should precede, or follow, an analysis of how counterfactual reasoning operates differently across scales of agency (individual decision), institutional design, and macro-historical trajectory. Finally, weigh whether the highest-value work lies in sharpening formal criteria for detecting path dependence in actual historical sequences, or in working out the normative implications—such as assignments of responsibility or strategies for intervention—when an undesirable outcome is judged path-dependent rather than structurally inevitable.
