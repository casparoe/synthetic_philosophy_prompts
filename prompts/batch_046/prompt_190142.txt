Then there's this scaling puzzle I keep turning over—if we could somehow enlarge a mouse brain to human physical scale without altering its internal architecture, would it suddenly acquire human-level moral status just because of the bulk, or is size only ever a proxy for something else? I need this tension to actually shape the speech: it should determine what I can praise about the center's work with small-brained animals without accidentally endorsing a hierarchy of worth, and it should prevent me from saying anything false or condescending to either half of the room.

Please act as a professional speechwriter with deep familiarity in philosophy of mind and science communication, and think carefully before composing anything. Start by briefly brainstorming three structural options for how the capacity-versus-relation tension could organize a five-minute arc—perhaps an opening anecdote, a middle section that uses the scaling thought experiment to show why size can't be the direct measure, and a closing that honors both the researchers and the donors without collapsing the distinction. Then write the remarks in polished, formal language suitable for a mixed audience: they should sound like they come from an earnest layperson who has lived with the question, not from a textbook. Do not include citations, author names, bibliographies, or references to specific papers; if you need to invoke an idea, describe it plainly in your own words. Make sure the philosophical problem does real work—so that, for example, I can't simply say "all brains matter equally" without the speech itself explaining why that claim needs the relational supplement to hold, and so that the scientists feel respected rather than lectured. Keep it to about five minutes when spoken aloud.
