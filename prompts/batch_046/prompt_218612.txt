As a journalist who reports regularly on transitional justice mechanisms—particularly the amnesty provisions of South Africa’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission, Argentina’s trials and subsequent commutation of sentences for military commanders, and Rwanda’s community-level Gacaca proceedings—I need a reusable editorial procedure for framing outcomes in my coverage. The philosophical problem is this: when legal closure has been granted through amnesty, commutation, or community reintegration, should the event be reported as achieving “moral closure,” or does it leave what philosophers of transitional justice call an unresolved “moral remainder” that demands continued acknowledgment?

Please translate the distinction between retributive closure, restorative acknowledgment, and what some theorists term “structural moral repair” into a practical decision procedure a reporter can run in approximately thirty minutes before filing, without requiring legal training or archival research. The procedure must be ordered: state the questions in sequence, specify what counts as a sufficient answer to each, identify the point at which the procedure issues a definitive framing recommendation (moral closure versus open moral remainder), and mark explicitly where it must halt and hand the decision to an editor because the question is one of values rather than verifiable fact. It must also specify the conditions under which it misfires—such as when survivor testimony and documentary records conflict, or when an amnesty provision may violate international norms that lie outside journalistic framing.

The procedure should address at least three hard configurations: testimony that is factually comprehensive but expresses no remorse; remorse that is expressed but rejected by victims or their representatives; and amnesty granted for acts that were never fully disclosed. Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical cases. First, a South Africa-style security officer who fully discloses the location of remains and expresses remorse, but the victim’s family explicitly rejects the apology. Second, an Argentina-style commander who receives a reduced sentence for providing strategic information about disappearances, but denies personal involvement in torture despite documentary evidence linking him to the facility. Third, a Rwanda-style perpetrator in a community court who admits to property destruction and requests forgiveness, but the community is divided over whether reintegration is appropriate.

For each case, state the framing recommendation the procedure yields, identify which step drives it, and include at least one case—specify which—where the recommendation conflicts with the intuitive moral response of a reader or even of the reporter applying the framework. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: for instance, whether it can adjudicate conflicts between survivor testimony and archival records, determine when an amnesty grant itself violates international law, or substitute for an editorial judgment about the newsworthiness of continued coverage.
