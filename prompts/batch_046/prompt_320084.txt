I am a physician who recently declined to perform a profitable but medically unnecessary procedure on a patient. I refused because I believed it was wrong to expose someone to risk for financial gain; that reason genuinely moved me. At the same time, I was aware that my refusal would likely be observed by the hospital's ethics committee and could strengthen my reputation as a practitioner of integrity, and I do care about that reputation. Both considerations were present, and each seemed sufficient to produce the refusal. What troubles me is a more specific possibility: I suspect that my commitment to the right reason might not have been psychologically robust on its own. Perhaps I would not have refused had the reputation benefit been absent—not because vanity was doing all the work, but because the presence of the self-interested consideration made me more attentive or more resolute in applying the moral principle. So I wonder whether there is a dependency relation: the right reason operated, but perhaps only because the wrong reason was also in play, altering my psychological landscape. I want to understand whether this kind of interdependence, if real, undermines the moral worth of the act in a way that mere co-presence of motives does not. Do theories of moral worth distinguish between motives that are merely concurrent and motives that are causally or psychologically interdependent? And if so, which ones treat that interdependence as disqualifying? Please map out the conceptual structure—what the distinctions are, how they apply to my case, and where the reasoning is weakest—without giving generic professional advice. Finally, tell me what specific argument or observation about moral psychology would change your assessment, and identify the point in your analysis that is most vulnerable.
