I serve on the ethics review board of a research institute that deploys autonomous agents in open-ended scientific domains—systems that design chemical syntheses, ecological field experiments, or infrastructure adjustments with minimal ongoing human oversight. We receive approximately fifteen proposals per quarter; each review must be completed within ninety minutes; our reviewers hold advanced degrees in philosophy or the natural sciences but share no unified axiology; and we are required to certify whether a proposal contains an adequate side-effect constraint without being permitted to invoke a fully specified value function. The conceptual difficulty we repeatedly encounter is the one central to discussions of low-impact AI: whether avoiding side effects can be defined at all in the absence of a complete specification of value. I want you to operationalize the principle behind impact measures—bounding an agent by the volume and irreversibility of reachable world-states—into a reusable decision procedure that my board can apply consistently across these reviews.

Please construct the procedure as an ordered checklist, or flowchart rendered in words, with explicit decision nodes. It should include: (1) an initial filter that identifies domains where normative disagreement is so pervasive that the procedure must halt and defer to explicit value deliberation rather than proceed technically; (2) a pair of operational tests—reversibility and reachability—that assess side-effect risk without comparing world-states against a utility baseline; (3) a specifically injected counterfactual resilience audit, in which the reviewer examines whether the agent’s trajectory remains within a bounded region of state-space not merely in the actual world but across a defined envelope of counterfactual perturbations in initial conditions, thereby catching path-dependent lock-in risks that standard impact measures might miss; (4) clear demarcation points where the procedure mandates rejection or mandatory redesign, versus points where it must hand the decision to human judgment because the boundary between intended effect and side effect is itself value-laden; and (5) the documentation required at each stage so that a senior ethicist can audit the decision later. Then apply the full procedure to three concrete proposals: (a) an autonomous bacterial-engineering agent intended for carbon capture, carrying risks of irreversible ecological release; (b) an urban-traffic-optimization agent that uses only anonymized data, where the primary harms are distributive and social rather than physical; and (c) a high-value medical-research agent whose direct footprint is technically minimal but whose counterfactual resilience fails in a subtle, distant manner—yielding a recommendation that the board is likely to resist. Conclude by stating precisely what this procedure cannot do: which normative judgments it is not designed to replace, and for which classes of proposal it should not be used at all.
