Design an in-class simulation for a graduate philosophy seminar of about fourteen students, ninety minutes, that makes them inhabit the tension in Parfit’s hitchhiker case without any preliminary lecture on “rational irrationality.” The currency should not be hypothetical cash; instead, inject a niche twist: the “driver” offers to share a concealed, genuinely useful piece of reasoning or a hidden dataset (for instance, a compact counterexample to a view the seminar has been discussing) on the condition that the “hitchhiker” promises to defend that reasoning in a debate scheduled for the following week. Once the reasoning is revealed, defending it is intellectually costly — it conflicts with positions the hitchhiker actually holds — but there is no external enforcement mechanism. This shifts the stakes from monetary payment to epistemic commitment, which suits the seminar setting.

Structure the activity in two phases: a contracting phase in which hitchhikers form intentions while being “scanned” by an observer who can detect insincerity, and an execution phase in which the hidden reasoning has been delivered and the debate obligation is now due. Use rotation rules so every student plays both driver and hitchhiker, and include an observer/judge role that records intentions. The payoff scheme should use real, small stakes — for example, control over the room’s music playlist for the final fifteen minutes, or the division of a seminar snack budget — so that defection has a tangible benefit but is not abstract.

Arrange the rules so that participants who sincerely form the conditional intention in phase one predictably defect in phase two, and then notice that widespread defection in phase two undermines the credibility needed to make phase one work at all. The moment of recognition should occur during play, not in explanation: for instance, after a round in which every hitchhiker received the reasoning but none defended it in a mock vote, the group should be asked to vote on whether they would still enter such contracts in future rounds.

Provide:
- Exact roles, rotation mechanics, and the non-monetary incentive scheme.
- The precise point in the timeline where participants should feel they have arrived at the paradox, before you name it.
- A debrief that maps each phase onto the argument: the lie-detector condition, the formation of the conditional intention, the disappearance of the reason to honor the commitment once safety (or possession of the reasoning) is achieved, and the claim that rationality is not time-neutral.
- A prediction of what will go wrong the first time — for example, participants refusing to form intentions because they anticipate their own defection, or moralizing defection as “dishonesty” rather than treating it as rational — and how to handle it without breaking the simulation.
- A frank admission of which feature of the original case the activity cannot reproduce (for instance, the literal physical danger of being stranded, or the metaphysical problem of personal identity across time) and how the facilitator must supply that verbally.

Do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists. Do not reveal the name of the paradox in the instructions given to participants; reserve it for the debrief.
