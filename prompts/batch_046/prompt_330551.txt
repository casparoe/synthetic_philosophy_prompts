Right, here's an argument my study partner cooked up about moral equality of combatants and now I can't sleep. It's meant to show MEC proves too much, but I think there's a hidden slide somewhere.

(1) The moral equality of combatants says that soldiers on both sides have the same rights and liabilities—each may kill the other, neither wrongs the other, regardless of whether their cause is just.
(2) That equality is usually explained by mutual threat: each combatant poses lethal danger to the other, so self-defence (or collective defence) licences the killing.
(3) If the licence to kill depends only on posing a lethal threat within an institutional role, and not on the justice of the cause, then anyone who occupies a functionaly equivalent lethal role gets the same licence.
(4) Now here's the niche bit: in distributed or networked conflict—think cyber operators, satellite targeting crews, logistics nodes—the causal contribution to lethal outcomes is structurally identical to pulling a trigger, but the agent is geographcally and instutionally remote.
(5) So by (3), these remote operators are also covered by MEC: they may kill and be killed, with no moral difference from front-line soldiers.
(6) But then the boundary between combatant and non-combatant collapses, not just in practice but in principle—anyone whose job functionaly sustains lethal force is a combatant.
(7) Worse: apply (2) upward. If every individual killing in an unjust war is licensed because the killer is threatened, and the state merely aggregates these licensed killings, then the state itself hasn't done anything wrong in ordering them. The collective wrong of aggression evaporates.
(8) So MEC implies there are no unjust wars, only differently arranged sets of permitted killings. That's absurd—I still think aggression is a real wrong.
(9) Therefore MEC is false?

My partner says the mistake is in (7), that collective agency introduces an emergent wrong that doesn't distribute over parts. I'm sceptical—if wrongness is always individual, where does the extra collective wrong come from? I'd really like to keep (1), (2) and (8) if I can. Which step should I actually reject, and what does giving it up cost? Does it break the symmetry, or just force me into some weird metaphysics of group agency?
