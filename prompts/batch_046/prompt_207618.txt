Consider the sequence of verdicts my philosophy-of-science professor has delivered in her graduate seminar on rational belief revision under ambiguous evidence. In the first case, a microscopist examines a blurry image and assigns subjective probabilities of 70 percent to cell-type A and 30 percent to cell-type B; the professor rules that the researcher should redistribute belief over the sample properties proportionally across both interpretations rather than conditioning on the more likely one. In the second, an instrument outputs a continuous confidence distribution over five disease states with no single peak above 40 percent; she rules that prior probabilities should be updated using the full distribution as weights, integrating over all states. In the third, a witness reports seeing a vehicle and adds that she is about 80 percent sure it was red and 20 percent sure it was blue; the professor rules that one should treat the color report itself as an uncertain event and shift beliefs about the vehicle's color accordingly, rather than conditioning on the vehicle being red and then adjusting for witness reliability afterward. In the fourth case, two independent sensors with identical calibration errors produce measurements that conflict by exactly the magnitude of their shared error bar; she rules that judgment should be suspended and the prior retained, because no partition element can be assigned a non-arbitrary weight.

Next week she will consider the following case. A climate researcher receives a noisy satellite measurement that, if interpreted through a standard atmospheric model, strongly supports hypothesis H over its negation. However, the interpretation depends on an auxiliary assumption about aerosol interference patterns that the researcher judges to have only 55 percent credibility. The researcher can apply Jeffrey conditionalization using the 55 percent weight on the interpretation that yields H; reject the update entirely on the grounds that the intermediate belief about aerosol patterns falls below a 60 percent credibility threshold and therefore violates a constraint against false intermediate premises; or adopt a hybrid rule that permits Jeffrey-style redistribution only when every intermediate interpretation exceeds 60 percent credibility, otherwise holding the prior.

Predict her verdict on this new case. Then identify the two competing theories—pure Jeffrey conditionalization, a strict safety or no-false-lemmas constraint, and the hybrid credibility-threshold rule—that are each consistent with her earlier four rulings, and explain which feature of the new case forces them apart. Give your best guess as to which rule she is implicitly running, with reasons drawn from the pattern of her earlier verdicts. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case—different in structure, not merely in the credibility percentage—that would settle whether she accepts pure Jeffrey conditionalization or the hybrid rule.
