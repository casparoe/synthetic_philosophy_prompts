Question:
Consider Douglas Hofstadter’s well-known discussion of the digger wasp Sphex ichneumoneus, which paralyzes a cricket, drags it to a burrow, and performs an internal check before pulling the prey inside. If the burrow is displaced during the check, the wasp typically repeats the check-and-drag sequence at the new location rather than completing the task. Philosophers have used this “sphexishness”—mechanical rigidity in the face of changed circumstances—to probe whether human practical reasoning is genuinely flexible or merely rigid at a higher level. A related but conceptually distinct framework proposes a “flexibility gradient”: cognition arranged in hierarchical levels, where level-0 agents revise only actions, level-1 agents revise action-selection rules, level-2 agents revise the principles governing those revisions, and so on upward.

Evaluate the claim: “A being positioned near the top of this flexibility gradient—capable of revising its own second-order revision rules—thereby escapes sphexishness entirely and possesses libertarian free will, because determinism cannot survive self-modifying causal loops.”

In your answer: (a) reconstruct the wasp example and identify precisely what makes it sphexish; (b) explain the flexibility-gradient model and how it might appear to solve the rigidity problem; (c) defend or critique the claim that reaching the top of the gradient establishes libertarian free will, with explicit attention to whether higher-order revision introduces genuine indeterminacy or merely relocates rigidity.

Student answer:
(a) In Hofstadter’s original experiment with Sphex ichneumoneus, the wasp paralyzes a cricket and drags it to a burrow entrance. The wasp then always enters the burrow to inspect it before pulling the prey inside. If, during this inspection, the burrow is moved several inches away, the wasp emerges, pulls the cricket to the relocated entrance, and then re-enters to check again—repeating the loop rather than adapting its behavior to the new position. This is sphexish because the wasp is executing a fixed subroutine (inspect-then-drag) that lacks a context-sensitive exit condition; the behavioral loop is not calibrated to the actual goal-state of securing the prey.

(b) The flexibility-gradient model treats rigidity and flexibility not as a binary opposition but as a hierarchical continuum. At the lowest level, an agent can only vary its motor outputs. At higher levels, the agent can alter the policies that select outputs, then alter the meta-policies that govern those policies, and so on. Because the wasp appears stuck at level 0—revising only the spatial trajectory of its drag but not the rule “check before entering”—it exhibits pure sphexishness. A being high on the gradient, by contrast, could encounter a displaced burrow and not only change its path but also suspend or revise the very rule requiring an internal check. The rigidity has not disappeared; it has been pushed upward into structures that are themselves available for revision, which creates the appearance of open-ended adaptability.

(c) I defend the claim. Once an agent can revise its second-order rules, the causal chain that determines any particular first-order action must pass through a self-modifying loop. Because the loop’s output depends on the agent’s own revision of its revision principles, prior physical states do not uniquely determine the outcome: the loop introduces causal openness. Consequently, determinism fails for such beings. The only residual rigidity lies in the agent’s highest-level core commitments, but these are self-authored through successive revisions rather than externally imposed. Since they do not operate as deterministic constraints, the being has escaped sphexishness and possesses libertarian free will. Therefore, high placement on the flexibility gradient is sufficient for freedom.
