In philosophy-of-mind and rationalist-adjacent discussions about consciousness, people frequently reach for a theater analogy to separate phenomenal consciousness from access consciousness and to locate global workspace theory inside that split: phenomenal consciousness is the stage with its lights on and scenery present—the raw qualitative field—while access consciousness is the actor currently in the spotlight and audible to the audience, i.e., the content globally broadcast to reasoning, memory, and report modules. The analogy is usually deployed to license a specific conclusion: that global workspace theory explains only the spotlight mechanism and not the stage itself, leaving phenomenal consciousness as an untouched residue and preserving the hard problem untouched, on the grounds that the lights can be on even when nobody in the audience is watching.

How far does this theater mapping actually hold? Give me an explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the stage, the lights, the actors, the spotlight operator, the audience (the other cognitive modules), the backstage crew (subcortical processing), and the persistence of the scene over time—then the precise points where the mapping fractures. Does phenomenal consciousness really possess scenery independent of what is being broadcast? Is there an audience if no module actually receives the signal? Does the analogy quietly import a Cartesian spectator that global workspace theory was designed to eliminate? Separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that are merely decorative.

Finally, tell me whether the conclusion—that GWT addresses only access consciousness and that phenomenal consciousness is left as an unexplained residue—survives once the breaks are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the entire argument. If the theater collapses under audit, propose a replacement analogy—perhaps a server farm in which phenomenal consciousness is the heat and electrical hum of running hardware, while access consciousness is a specific query result returned to the user interface—and say whether it fares any better on the same mapping and whether it avoids importing a homunculus.
