I was arguing with a friend about whether institutions can deliberately engineer the moral emotions they depend on. She pointed to our university's recently adopted disciplinary framework, which instructs hearing panels to avoid language that assigns individual guilt and instead to frame every finding in terms of harm to communal standing. The respondent must acknowledge the rupture in shared trust but is discouraged from saying I did wrong or expressing personal remorse. The policy document describes this as a shame-sensitive, guilt-neutral design. My friend thinks this is incoherent: either the respondent really is guilty, in which case suppressing that recognition is a form of moral evasion, or guilt is not the right category at all, in which case the framework should abandon moral language entirely. I started wondering what philosophical commitments this practice actually presupposes. Does it require that shame is the more fundamental moral emotion and that guilt is merely a culturally optional overlay? Or does it presuppose a relational theory of moral identity, according to which the claim that I wronged someone is always less basic than the claim that we are disrupted? Could the same design be justified on purely pragmatic grounds—say, to reduce recidivism—without committing to any deep view about guilt versus shame? More broadly, if a culture can genuinely choose between guilt-based and shame-based frameworks, what does that imply about whether these emotions track objective moral facts or are constructed by the practices that invoke them? Reconstruct the implicit theory of moral emotion and moral personhood that makes this institutional design intelligible, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and tell me what would have to change in the procedure if we replaced its underlying view with one that treated guilt as irreducible and shame as derivative.
