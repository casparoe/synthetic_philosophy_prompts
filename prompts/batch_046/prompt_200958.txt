A recurring analogy in debates over whether historical writing can be objective compares the historian to a cartographer mapping territory that exists independently: the past is the terrain, primary sources are survey instruments, and the narrative is only a scaled rendering. The counter-analogy, often advanced by critics of naive realism, likens the historian to a playwright adapting inherited myths: archival documents function as props, but the plot structure—tragic, comic, or romantic—is supplied by the historian's own pre-cognitive tropological commitments, not recovered from the record. Audit both analogies precisely. First, provide the correspondence table for each: identify what plays the role of the terrain, the instruments, the scale, the myth, the props, and the genre conventions. Second, locate the exact points of breakdown, especially with respect to Ranke's ideal that history should show events as they actually occurred, Carr's argument that selecting and arranging facts is already an interpretive act, and White's thesis that emplotment precedes and conditions which documents count as evidence at all. Third, determine whether either analogy smuggles in an unexamined assumption about archival silence—treating it either as a contingent lack of surviving traces or as a structural gap that narrative form is compelled to fill. Finally, take a committed position: do either of these analogies genuinely license the conclusion that historical knowledge is either fully objective or essentially fictional? Or do the disanalogies point to a more precise intermediate account? Give your answer with explicit reasoning, not as a neutral inventory of positions.
