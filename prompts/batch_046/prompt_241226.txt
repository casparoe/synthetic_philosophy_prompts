I've been arguing with a colleague in teh faculty lounge about Nozick's entitlement theory, and I've ended up defending a version that's definatly more extreme than what's usually assigned in grad courses -- I think the Wilt Chamberlain argument works not just for money and economic transfers, but extends to what I'd call attentional entitlements: if people voluntarily direct their mental focus and engagement toward particular creators or platforms, then any patterned redistribution of that attention (say, algorithmic promotion designed to correct representational imbalances) violates the same justice conditions Nozick laid out.

Before I put this in my syllabus, tell me what actually counts against this view. I don't want the usual complaints about unjust initial acquisition of land or capital; I want to know whether attention can be property-like in the relevant ways -- can it be stolen? inherited? hoarded? -- and whether stretching the argument this far collapses the liberty-versus-pattern distinction that Nozick was trying to preserve. Rank the defeaters: which ones are fatal, which ones just force qualifications, and be honest about whether defenders of the standard economic-only version would concede them or dismiss them. Is the non-fungibility of attention a structural problem that kills the extension, or is it salvageable with tweaks? I want an honest assessment of whether I've qualified the view into unfalsifiability, and whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind.
