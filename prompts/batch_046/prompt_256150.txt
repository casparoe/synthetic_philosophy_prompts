A review essay on artificial intelligence and philosophy of mind summarizes Daniel Dennett's intentional stance as follows: beliefs and desires are not internal mental states but predictive instruments we deploy to forecast behavior. When applied to large language models, this implies that we ought to attribute beliefs to an LLM whenever doing so improves our predictions of its outputs, irrespective of consciousness or internal representations. The essay further claims that Dennett's position therefore commits us to treating LLMs as literal belief-holders with precisely the same ontological status as human agents, since for Dennett there is no deeper fact of the matter about whether a system genuinely believes something—only whether the stance proves predictively useful. It concludes that critics who deny belief ascription to LLMs are merely retreating to the design stance, inspecting matrix multiplications and token distributions and missing that internal architecture is philosophically irrelevant once predictive utility is secured. I suspect this overstates the case and may conflate Dennett's view with functionalism or with an eliminativist stance about mental states, but I am not sure where exactly the missteps lie.
