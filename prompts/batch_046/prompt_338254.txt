I am drafting the syllabus for a small graduate seminar—twelve participants, seventy-five minutes, in a room where seating can be rearranged into pairs—devoted not to surveying aesthetic testimony but to making its paradox felt in the bones. I want the session to revolve around a peculiar idea I have been turning over: that accepting testimony about an aesthetic quality does not merely supplement one's judgment but can restructure the perceptual field itself, so that subsequent direct encounter is experienced not as autonomous seeing but as verification against an imported framework. To stage this, design a structured deliberation with rules, roles, and small stakes. Divide the group: half receive dense, authoritative curatorial descriptions of three actual artworks—descriptions that embed specific aesthetic judgments about, say, the way a monochrome surface achieves sublimity only through the viewer's prior awareness of its material fragility—while the other half observes reproductions of those same works in silence, with no textual mediation. Each participant records a private judgment. Then merge them into cross-paired negotiations: each pair must produce a joint recommendation for a hypothetical museum acquisition, using only spoken exchange, with the hidden rule that pairs whose recommendation diverges most sharply from a pre-established expert consensus forfeit the right to contribute to a collective statement that will be read aloud at the close. The debrief must map precisely onto the philosophical architecture: identify the moment when testimony-receivers discover they cannot defend their preference without borrowing vocabulary they never earned through perception; identify the parallel moment when direct observers find their own descriptions colonized by the framing they heard secondhand. Include a timeline, the materials needed, the payoff scheme that requires no money, and an honest accounting of what the exercise cannot reproduce—specifically, the long-term habituation by which borrowed authority comes to feel like native taste, and the institutional weight that makes certain voices authoritative regardless of content. Also warn me, with the candor of one who has watched classroom experiments collapse, what will predictably fail—the resentment of direct observers who feel robbed rather than enlightened, the false consensus reached to avoid penalty—and how to steer through it. Frame this request with the formal care of a novelist outlining a scene: precise in its mechanics, unafraid of the idea's darkness, aware that the activity must generate its own failure to be truthful.
