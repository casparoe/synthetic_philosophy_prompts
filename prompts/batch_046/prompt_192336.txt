I just spent three exhausting hours arguing with a close friend about whether thought experiments are genuinely illuminating or merely rhetorical devices that smuggle conclusions into vivid scenarios; we kept returning to the same examples but never agreed on a principled way to tell a legitimate conceptual pump from a misleading one. I now need something usable for my actual role: I moderate a biweekly interdisciplinary seminar for graduate students and early-career researchers across philosophy, cognitive science, and public policy, where speakers routinely deploy hypothetical cases—brain-in-jar variations, state-of-nature fictions, algorithmic trolley scenarios—to bridge disciplinary gaps. I need a reusable decision procedure, essentially a flowchart rendered in ordered questions, that I can run in about five minutes per presentation without specialist knowledge of the speaker’s field, to determine how a given thought experiment is functioning and whether I should intervene (ask a clarifying question, flag a hidden premise, or let it stand), and I need it to be honest about where judgment replaces mechanics.

The procedure should be built around three distinctions my friend and I circled but never formalized. First, directional versus structural pump: a directional pump steers intuition toward a specific normative or metaphysical conclusion by manipulating emotional salience within a fixed framework, while a structural pump reshapes the conceptual architecture so that certain distinctions seem inevitable without announcing that move. Second, baseline transparency: does the scenario make its counterfactual baseline explicit, or does it rely on an unstated “normal world” assumption that loads the case? Third, friction cost: how much cognitive work does the scenario demand from an interlocutor who does not share the speaker’s background commitments, and is that cost distributed fairly or dumped onto dissenters?

For each distinction, specify the exact diagnostic question to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer, whether resolving it requires a factual judgment or an evaluative one, and the precise stopping point—intervene, request clarification, or accept—along with the conditions under which the decision must be handed to me as a value judgment rather than resolved by the procedure itself. The deliverable should include hard cases the procedure must handle: ambiguous framing, interdisciplinary asymmetry in background knowledge, and scenarios that combine both directional and structural elements.

Then apply the full procedure to three worked cases: a neuroscientist using the Chinese Room scenario to argue that syntax is never sufficient for semantics; an economist using a state-of-nature parable to justify a specific property-rights regime; and a bioethicist using a modified trolley problem involving AI decision-making to argue for mandatory algorithmic transparency. One of these applications should yield an answer that I will likely resist—probably that I should accept the pump and refrain from intervention despite my instinct to challenge it—and you should identify exactly where in the procedure that resistance is triggered, why the procedure does not accommodate my objection, and what that reveals about its limits.

Finally, state clearly what this procedure cannot do: which forms of misdirection it will miss, which disciplinary contexts break its underlying assumptions, and where the evaluation is irreducibly mine rather than procedural. Note also the practical constraints: I have roughly five minutes, no authority to halt a presentation, and I must be able to justify any intervention to both the speaker and the audience without citing theoretical literature.
