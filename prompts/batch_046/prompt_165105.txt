In my research on democratic legitimacy I keep encountering the same stalled disagreement: contractualists and associative-obligation theorists both concede that severely unjust laws forfeit ordinary compliance, and both concede that routine law-breaking erodes coordination, yet for decades neither camp has shifted on whether civil disobedience breaches a prior duty to obey or merely renegotiates the terms of political membership. Is this deadlock verbal—rooted in competing uses of political obligation as either transactional consent or relational role—or does a substantive divide lie beneath, perhaps about whether moral weight attaches to voluntary acts or to embedded social positions? Diagnose it using what each side actually says about consent, fairness, and exit, and say whether any distinction—say, between an obligation-to-obey and an obligation-to-participate—or formal result could break it, or whether the impasse shows the framing itself is ill-posed.
