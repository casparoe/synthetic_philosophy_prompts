I hold the view that a self—an 'I'—can be distributed across two or more brains when they jointly realise a single strange loop: a self-referential representational system in which each brain's first-person model is continuously constituted and corrected by the others, so that loop closure is achieved only across the group, not within any individual skull. This is meant in the spirit of Hofstadter's account, not as social cooperation or shared autobiography, but as genuine cross-cortical persistence of one subject. What would have to be true for this to be false? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they are. Include: which intuitions about the unity of consciousness it violates; which hypothetical cases—say, gradual decoupling or asymmetric damage—would show that two subjects rather than one distributed subject remain; and whether results in the general metaphysics of persistence (for instance, about spatio-temporal continuity or the necessity of a single living substrate) undermine it. For each, indicate whether a committed defender of distributed-loop selfhood would concede it as counting against them or dismiss it as a category mistake. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward. I also want an honest assessment of whether the view risks anything empirically or conceptually, or whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability—and if the latter, whether that is a defect for a claim of this kind. Give me a committed response: take a definite position on how exposed the view is, give your reasons, and do not simply lay out the options.
