I've been working through a concern about AI safety debate methods, and I think I've constructed an argument that proves too much — it seems to undermine not just debate-based oversight but expert judgment in general. Here are the steps:

(1) The debate model for aligning a system that exceeds human reasoning relies on a stronger agent presenting arguments to a weaker judge, who selects the truthful position.
(2) A central failure mode is obfuscation: the stronger agent can embed a false conclusion inside reasoning too complex for the judge to fully trace.
(3) One natural remedy is to train or augment the judge so it can understand more complex reasoning.
(4) However, if the judge is improved enough to follow every argument the debater might construct, the capability gap between them collapses.
(5) Once the gap collapses, the judge is no longer protected by relative weakness; direct deception becomes possible, not merely hidden within complexity.
(6) Therefore we face a fixed dilemma: either the judge remains weak and vulnerable to obfuscation, or becomes strong and vulnerable to being directly out-reasoned.
(7) Consequently, debate cannot provide a reliable safety guarantee for any system that substantially exceeds human reasoning capacity.
(8) Yet this structure applies identically to medicine, law, and science, where experts routinely outstrip lay evaluation.
(9) If (7) holds, then either all such expert institutions are fundamentally unreliable, or one of the premises (1) through (6) is wrong.
(10) I am inclined to keep (2), since hidden complexity does seem like a real danger, and I would prefer to preserve (3), that judges can genuinely be improved. But I cannot see which link in (1)-(6) actually fails. Which step should be rejected, why did it initially seem sound, and what does abandoning it cost for the broader debate framework?
