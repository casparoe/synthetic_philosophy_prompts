As a journalist who covers science and philosophy, I have been trying to codify my own judgments about when multiverse and string-theoretic claims count as confirmed, given that direct empirical access to other universes or hidden dimensions is impossible. Here are my verdicts, case by case.

Case A: String theory once produced a specific, risky numerical prediction about the cosmological constant that was later matched by observation. Despite the theory's vast flexibility elsewhere, I treat this as genuine confirmation.

Case B: A multiverse model explains why our universe has life-permitting constants by appealing to an enormous ensemble in which ours is statistically unremarkable. I treat this as explanatory storytelling rather than confirmation, because the model could accommodate almost any observed values.

Case C: Two mathematically equivalent formulations of the same framework—one geometric, one purely algebraic—both survive a novel internal consistency check. I feel both gain support, but the formulation with fewer free parameters is confirmed more strongly.

Case D: After a landscape model fails to reproduce observed particle spectra, its architects revise the underlying symmetry-breaking rules. I feel this post-hoc adjustment weakens the model's standing, rather than leaving it in neutral territory.

Case E: A framework predicts that, if correct, a specific extra-dimensional topology must be embeddable; subsequent work proves that no such embedding exists. I take this as disconfirmation, not as a mere absence of support.

But I keep oscillating on Case F: a "mirror-landscape" hypothesis predicts that, across the majority of universes with our basic physical laws, stable carbon chemistry should be impossible; our observation of stable carbon is then cited as a selection effect that confirms our branch's rarity. Some argue this is circular reasoning; others say it is precisely how multiverse selection should operate. I genuinely cannot settle whether selection effects of this kind can ever carry confirmatory weight.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving predictive risk, structural parsimony, or vulnerability to internal contradiction—that captures Cases A through E? Must one of those verdicts be dropped to preserve simplicity? And what should the principle say about Case F—does it resolve my oscillation, or does it show that my intuition there is irreconcilable with the rest?
