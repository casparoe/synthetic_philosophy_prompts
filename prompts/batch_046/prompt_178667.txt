Question: Some philosophers argue that disjunctivism about perceptual experience is just a verbal relabelling: it calls veridical seeing and hallucination seperate mental kinds, but doesnt explain why they share subjective character and can be caused by teh same neural mechanisms. Explain the disjunctivist reply, focusing on the distinction between epistemic and phenomenal indistinguishability and on whether causal similarity forces mental similarity. Then evaluate whether that reply succeeds in preserving direct realism without smuggling representational content back into the veridical case through what Martin calls object-dependent phenomenal presence.

Student answer:
Disjunctivism, like McDowell and Martin defend, rejects the idea that veridical perception and illusion share a highest common factor. In veridical seeing, you're directly related to the object—say, a tree. In hallucination, you're in a representational state with tree-content, but there's no tree present. So the argument from illusion assumes one mental kind covers both, and disjunctivists deny that.

The verbal trick objection says this is just naming them differently, but disjunctivists reply that the ontology is genuinely different: one involves the world, the other doesnt. They also distinguish epistemic indistinguishability—you cant tell from inside which case you're in—from phenomenal identity. Just because two states feel the same from the first-person doesnt mean they have the same phenomenal structure; it might just mean your access is limited.

However, I think there's a hidden problem. To explain why hallucination feels like seeing, disjunctivists need to say that in veridical seeing, the same conceptual content (like TREE) is present, but now it's satisfied by the real tree. That means veridical experience includes representational content after all, with an object tacked on. So disjunctivism collapses into representationalism plus a causal clause.

Also, Martin's object-dependence claim is that if the object is absent, there's literally no experience. But in hallucination we clearly have an experience, so Martin must be wrong unless he admits that hallucination is a different mental act entirely—maybe a kind of imagining. Once he does that, though, he's just moved the problem around rather than solving it.
