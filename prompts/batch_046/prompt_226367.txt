I am a third-year undergraduate currently enrolled in a seminar on philosophy of science and mind, and I am presenting a paper next Thursday that advances an original framing of Darwin's and Turing's strange inversions of reasoning. My thesis is that both Darwin's move from artificial to natural selection and Turing's imitation-game reasoning for machine intelligence depend on a shared structural inversion — competence without comprehension — but with a specific twist I call reflected incompetence: the observer silently borrows their own comprehension to validate the mechanism's output, thereby disguising an analogical leap as an empirical finding. The audience comprises my seminar leader, who works in philosophy of biology and is broadly sympathetic to mechanistic explanation; two doctoral students in cognitive science who are sceptical of analogical reasoning in debates about artificial intelligence; and a visiting historian of science who is thoroughly acquainted with Darwin's private notebooks but considers Turing overused in philosophical contexts. I need a speaker's preparation sheet: the objections I will actually face, ordered by likelihood and potential damage, phrased as each member of this audience would phrase them, together with concise replies, explicit notes on where each reply is weakest, what I ought to concede without surrendering the core claim, and what I must hold firm. In particular, I must be ready to defend my claim that the inversion is structurally identical across biological and computational domains despite the obvious differences in subject matter. Please do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I shall have no reference materials available. Finally, include the objection I am most hoping nobody raises — namely, whether my framing illegitimately collapses the distinction between methodological analogy and ontological claim — and advise me on how to respond should it arise.
