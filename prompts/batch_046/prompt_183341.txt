My uncle argues that our town ought to preserve an unwritten practice of closing public markets on a particular religious holiday, not out of expectation that everyone observe it, but because the collective pause expresses loyalty to our shared history and gives the day a weight that mere private choice cannot replicate. My cousin, who tends to see issues through the lens of harm and equity, contends that the practice quietly coerces minority vendors and workers who do not share the tradition, and that fairness demands all commercial days be treated identically regardless of communal sentiment. Both can point to real harms: the uncle to the thinning of common bonds, the cousin to the concrete burden on those who must either conform or lose income. I have recently encountered the idea that people with different political temperaments tend to rely on different clusters of moral intuition—some giving greater weight to loyalty, authority, and the sense of the sacred, others giving greater weight to care and fairness—and I find myself asking whether knowing this psychological pattern should affect my judgment of who is right. If the disagreement is partly explained by these differing temperaments, does that explanation weaken either side's claim to be correct, or does it simply show why they fail to persuade one another? And if I must advise the council, is there a principled reason to let one cluster of intuitions override the other, or should I treat them as incommensurable values that can only be balanced by compromise? I would be grateful if you could begin with your bottom-line judgment and then unfold your reasoning in continuous prose, writing as you would in a careful letter or email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
