I have been working through the Malcolm-Dennett dispute over whether dreams constitute experiences, and I find myself caught on a hypothetical intermediate case that the standard treatments do not cleanly resolve. Consider a lucid dreamer who, prior to sleep, memorizes an unfamiliar complex geometric pattern; during REM sleep she uses pre-arranged left-right eye-movement signals—monitored in real time by an external observer—to indicate that she is currently experiencing that exact pattern within the dream. Write a dialogue between a defender of Malcolm's verificationist argument—that without an awake, intersubjective criterion for identifying the experience, a dream report cannot establish that an experience occurred—and a defender of Dennett's position that dream narratives are assembled retrospectively rather than lived through by a continuous experiencing subject. The Malcolm advocate should contend that even real-time eye signaling demonstrates only a behavioral disposition that gets interpreted after awakening, not a contemporaneous phenomenal state; the Dennett advocate should show how her model, whether conceived as a cassette played back without an audience or as multiple drafts assembled upon arousal, can accommodate the signaling protocol without conceding that there was an experiencing self present during REM. They should also address whether Block's distinction between phenomenal consciousness and access consciousness clarifies the dispute—perhaps by allowing Malcolm to separate raw feeling from reportability, or by allowing Dennett to argue that any residual phenomenal content lacks the narrative integration necessary for experience—responding directly to each other's moves about the temporal structure of reportability rather than speaking past one another.
