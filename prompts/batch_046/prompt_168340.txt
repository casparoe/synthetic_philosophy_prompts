I've been following philosophy-of-mind discussions on rationalist forums long enough to notice that the Cartesian theater versus multiple drafts debate has a strange half-life: everyone treats Dennett's critique as decisive, yet every few years someone rediscovers that global workspace theory or predictive processing still seems to need a presentation layer, and the cycle repeats without anyone conceding ground. In my local reading group, the split is sharp. One side treats the theater as an obvious strawman—no working neuroscientist literally posits a homunculus watching an inner screen—while the other insists that distributed processing doesn't eliminate the need for a functional integration point where information becomes available for reasoning, report, and cross-modal correction. Both sides invoke Dennett's attack on Cartesian materialism, but they talk past each other: the anti-theater camp hears "integration point" as just another name for the homunculus, while the defenders hear "multiple drafts" as denying the very unity that makes conscious error-correction intelligible.

I'm trying to diagnose whether this is a verbal disagreement about what counts as presentation versus draft, a substantive dispute about whether phenomenal unity requires a singular spatiotemporal locus, or two groups answering different questions—one about neural architecture, one about phenomenological structure—under one heading. To push the diagnosis further, I'd like you to consider a specific conceptual distinction I've been using: the difference between binding that happens inside a single predictive model and binding that requires translation into a canonical, modality-independent format accessible to multiple subsystems. If Dennett's multiple drafts allow the first but reject the second, while global-workspace defenders require the second, then the stall might depend on whether canonical-format integration is actually necessary for consciousness, not on whether there's a literal theater. Please diagnose the impasse using what each side actually claims about integration and reportability, and tell me whether progress would require an empirical result—say, evidence of distributed binding without canonical translation—a formal argument that cross-modal error-correction demands such translation, or a reconceptualization showing that both sides are describing the same mechanism at different granularities.
