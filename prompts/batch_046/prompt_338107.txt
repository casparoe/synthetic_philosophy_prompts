I'm a doctoral student in modern European history, and I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks—about six minutes—at a small interdisciplinary workshop on perpetrator studies. The audience is divided between social historians, several of whom work on microhistory and history from below, and moral philosophers who think about character, situation, and collective agency. Rather than summarizing the Browning-Goldhagen debate, I want the speech to treat that debate as a methodological fault line that runs through every document we examine. The central move should be to show that both the ordinary-men framework and the willing-executioners framework depend on a clean separation between internal character and external situation, and then to introduce—without resolving—the idea that recent work in distributed moral cognition dissolves that separation by treating agency as partly constituted by bureaucratic roles, spatial arrangements, and archival silences. The rhetorical structure should enact this dissolution: open with the close reading of a single bureaucratic artifact—say, a leave request or a police order whose genre and missing signatures make both situational and ideological readings simultaneously plausible and insufficient—then pivot to the larger implication that if we can no longer locate the perpetrator solely in character or in situation, then the history-from-below methods we use for victims may need an entirely different architecture when turned toward perpetrators. The remarks must be intelligible to the philosophers and any non-specialist guests in attendance without flattening the stakes for the historians, and they should not cite specific secondary works by name, provide a bibliography, or ask for further reading. The closing should leave the architectural question open rather than prescribing an answer.
