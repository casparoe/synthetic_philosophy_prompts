I have been trying to work through the logic of a well-known brain-transplant thought experiment—where a brain is removed from a body and placed in a vat that remotely controls the original body—without any formal training, and I have pushed it into what looks like a reductio ad absurdum. I would like to know which step fails, and what rejecting it costs.

(1) In the original case, if my brain is in the vat operating my body remotely, then my location is the vat, not the body. So personal location tracks the brain's controlling processes, not the body's physical position.

(2) Now imagine a procedure that divides my brain's functional hemispheres into two separate vats, each controlling half of my original body, linked by a constant high-bandwidth connection that preserves a single unified consciousness—one mind running on two processors.

(3) By (1), since my controlling processes now occupy both vats, I must be fully present in both locations at once.

(4) But if I am fully present in both, then destroying one vat should destroy only part of me, leaving the other part intact and still fully me—which contradicts the intuition that destroying half my brain can kill the whole person.

(5) To avoid bilocation, one might reply that I am not in either vat individually, but distributed between them. Yet then there is no specific location that counts as mine, which undermines the very intuition that made (1) plausible.

(6) Therefore either I can be in two places and partially destroyed, or the brain-tracking view collapses. Both conclusions seem unacceptable.

Where does this argument slip? I would prefer to keep (1) and (2) if possible, and I wonder whether there is a middle path—for instance, treating location as a non-binary or functional relation rather than an all-or-nothing property. But I want to understand whether that saves the view or merely disguises its failure.
