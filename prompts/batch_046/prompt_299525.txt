I'm working on a long-term research agenda in the philosophy of translation, specifically on cases where formal constraints—meter, rhyme scheme, acrostic architecture, syllabic quantity—are treated not as decorative packaging but as load-bearing parts of a work's meaning. The classic reference point is Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot, which argues that translating a poem like Clément Marot's requires preserving the formal architecture even when lexical fidelity must be sacrificed, because the form itself carries semantic and emotional weight. I'm trying to decide where to direct limited effort—say, one semester of focused work and a small research grant—among several open lines of inquiry that seem to pull in different directions.

The first is an ontological question: if a translated poem preserves every formal constraint of the original but replaces every content word with near-synonyms that shift the thematic register, in what robust sense is it still "the same work"? The second is a cognitive-semantics question: do readers actually process rhyme and meter as meaning-bearing structures, or does formal preservation operate mainly at an aesthetic level, honoring the author's craft without contributing to propositional content? The third concerns cross-typological feasibility: for language pairs with radically different phonotactics—say, translating a quantitative Latin poem into a tonal language with monosyllabic morphology—can formal preservation ever be more than approximate simulation, and if so, does "faithful translation" collapse into "inspired recreation"?

Beyond these, I'm drawn to a more niche angle that hasn't received sustained philosophical treatment: the problem of "constraint inheritance" in algorithmically assisted translation, where a human translator specifies formal rules with mathematical precision and an algorithm generates candidate lines that satisfy them. If the constraints are fully explicit and the lexical choices are machine-generated within those bounds, does artistic authorship—and the moral justification for preserving form as an honor to the original author's intentional design—shift in ways that undermine the traditional argument? Given all of this, which of these four lines—the ontology of the translated work, the cognitive semantics of form, the limits of cross-linguistic formal preservation, or the ethics and authorship of constraint-based algorithmic collaboration—matter most for advancing our understanding? Which are tractable with existing philosophical tools, which implicitly depend on empirical findings philosophers shouldn't try to settle alone, and where should a single, focused investment of effort go if the goal is a genuinely decisive result rather than another illustrative case study? Rank them and defend the ranking with explicit criteria—tractability, neglectedness, and how many other questions in the area depend on resolving this one.
