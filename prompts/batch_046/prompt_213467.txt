I serve on a clinical ethics committee at a teaching hospital, and we are trying to maintain consistency in how we apply patient advance directives when the current patient’s mental state diverges sharply from the state anticipated by the document. Our record so far includes four decisions. First, an Alzheimer’s patient who had directed that she not be treated for infections if she no longer recognized her children; when she reached that state—peaceful, non-verbal, and without distress—we honored the refusal and withheld antibiotics for pneumonia. Second, a patient with progressive dementia had refused tube feeding in any state of dependency; when swallowing failed she was visibly distressed by hunger, and we overrode the refusal, providing nutrition. Third, a patient with a psychiatric history had executed a psychiatric advance directive refusing electroconvulsive therapy in any future episode, even should she request it; during a severe depressive episode she asked for it, and we honored the refusal. Fourth, a patient with traumatic brain injury retained long-term memory but had a radically altered personality and lost his prior career ambitions; his directive said he never wanted life-prolonging measures if severely disabled, yet he now expressed clear contentment in simple activities and requested chemotherapy for cancer, and we honored that current request.

We now face a case that has split our committee. A woman wrote a directive stating that if she ever reached a state in which her required care exceeded her capacity for reciprocal interaction with her family and she could no longer understand that imbalance, then her life should not be prolonged. She now has severe anterograde amnesia combined with profound executive dysfunction: she recognizes her family, expresses affection, lives largely in the present moment with apparent contentment, and asks that a treatable infection be treated. She requires continuous full-time care, and her family reports that the burden is severe; she does not grasp the scale of that burden. The infection is easily treated, and without treatment she would likely die within days, but with treatment she would return to her present state of dependent but contented existence. Some members say this is closest to our first case—the triggering condition is met, and she lacks the understanding specified in the document—while others say it resembles our fourth case, in that she retains emotional connection and expresses a current desire to live.

If we want to be consistent with ourselves, which of our four precedents is the closest analog, and what should we decide? If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, then identifying the principle we would be adopting with each choice is essential: would we be endorsing a view that the directive binds whenever its objective conditions are met regardless of current experiential welfare, or a view that the authority of the earlier self is contingent on the absence of present, meaningful relational and affective engagement? Finally, what further case—perhaps one involving partial recognition, or one in which the patient explicitly endorses the directive’s logic despite lacking memory—would best distinguish between these two principles?
