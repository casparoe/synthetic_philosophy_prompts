Read Melville's Bartleby, the Scrivener through the lens of Weber's Protestant work ethic, the French 35-hour-week framework, and the Japanese phenomenon of karoshi. Bartleby gradually abandons all copying, refuses every offer of alternative labor, and dies in prison; the narrator treats his repeated refusal formula as an inexplicable mystery and later links it to the dead-letter office. Does Bartleby's withdrawal function as an anticipatory assertion that leisure is a right against the calling, or as its dark mirror—a karoshi-by-omission in which the absence of work proves fatal? I want a reading faithful in both directions: where does the 35-hour framing illuminate the narrator's failed benevolence, and where does it distort the story's religious and metaphysical undertones? Point to specific scenes—the wall-staring, the prison death, the final lament—and say whether Melville resists being read as a labor-policy parable. What does the story add to debates about work and leisure that sociology and law don't capture? Please say explicitly where your interpretation takes liberties with Weber's argument or with Melville's text, and identify which scene or counter-argument would most likely change your verdict on whether Bartleby's refusal is a moral stance or a pathology.
