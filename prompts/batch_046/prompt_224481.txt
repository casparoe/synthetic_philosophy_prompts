Next month I defend a job-market paper at an interdisciplinary workshop hosted by a history department with a strong intellectual-history orientation; the audience will include two senior scholars who work in the tradition of E.H. Carr and Hayden White and treat narrative construction and linguistic mediation as irreducible features of historiography; one philosopher of science who accepts perspectival realism in physics but is deeply suspicious of importing scientific epistemology into humanistic domains; and one archivist who holds that any viable epistemology of history must begin with the material resistance of sources rather than with abstract models. My thesis is that historical objectivity should be reconceived not as correspondence between narrative and past events, but as a form of evidential resistance: archival traces function analogously to predictive failures in scientific modeling, constraining interpretation not because they are self-interpreting, but because over-interpretive narratives generate inconsistencies with resistant features of the record. I argue that Ranke’s regulative ideal of describing the past as it actually was is best reconstructed in these terms—namely, as an ideal of maximal predictive resistance to arbitrary narrative—while Carr’s emphasis on present-minded selection and White’s tropological framework each correctly identify real mediating structures but illegitimately generalize from local mediation to global scepticism about historical knowledge. Please rank the objections by how damaging they are to the proposal’s survival, not by thematic neatness.
