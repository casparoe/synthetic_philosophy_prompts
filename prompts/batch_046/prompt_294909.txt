me and two friends were arguing about dreams after class -- specifically whether they're actually experences that happen while you're asleep, or just stories your brain cooks up the second you wake up. i was mostly just listening. here was the back and forth.

Alex said Malcolm was basically right: dreams aren't experiences occuring during sleep because we have no criteria for attributing consciousness to a sleeping body except the report after waking. if you say you were flying, all you're doing is describing a narrative you assembled upon arousal, not recalling an experience like you'd recall eating lunch. the conditions for being a subject of experience -- being awake, having conceptual capacities, being able to correct yourself -- just aren't there in REM.

Jordan pushed back with a Dennett-style line: that's confusing what we can know with what there is. just because we only access dreams upon waking doesn't mean nothing was happening. Jordan said Dennett's cassette idea might be wrong in detail, but the deeper point holds -- we shouldn't deny concurrent phenomenal content just because it's reconstructed later. Jordan also brought up lucid dreamers who can signal with eye movements during REM; that proves there's some concurrent awareness and intentionality, not just post-wakeup fiction.

Alex replied that eye-movement signaling only shows minimal motor control, not full-blown experience. and Jordan was smuggling in an assumption: if a dream leaves no trace in memory, in what sense did "I" experience it? experience requires integration with the waking self. otherwise you're just pointing at brain activity and calling it experience. Alex also said that even if you grant some fragments occur, the coherent 20-minute narrative people report is mostly confabulated. so dreams as usually described aren't experiences; the experience part is tiny, and the rest is retroactive assembly.

Jordan conceded that the narrative is probably partly assembled on waking, but said that doesn't eliminate experience -- it just revises the form. the colors, emotions, spatial layout might be genuinely experienced fragments that get woven into a story. Jordan said Alex was using reportability as a definitional criterion for experience, then using that definition to deny experience -- circular. and Jordan noted that if we accept deep NREM fragments with no report, we're already allowing unintegrated experience, so why draw the line at REM?

Alex came back with this niche twist: what about phenomenal absence? in deep sleep there's not just no experience but an active absence, and if you allow that absence isn't itself experienced, then you already admit that sleep involves states that aren't experiential. so extending that to REM isn't a big leap -- REM might just be a different kind of non-experiential processing that produces reportable content upon waking, like a printer that only outputs when you hit the button.

Jordan said that sounds like you're treating REM sleep as a black box that only activates on arousal, which contradicts the neurophysiology. the brain isn't printing at wake-up; it's already in a highly activated state. so the burden is on Alex to explain why that activation produces nothing like experience, especially given the subjective reports.
