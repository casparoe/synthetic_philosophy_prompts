I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I am helping a university colleague design the final assessment for an upper-level seminar that crosses philosophy of science with machine-learning systems design. The course has treated mechanistic explanation and anomaly detection, but I want an essay question that cannot be answered by reciting standard definitions of “mechanism” or by summarizing well-known mechanistic accounts from the philosophy of science literature. Instead, the question should place students in a concrete conceptual tension that requires them to do original analytical work.

Please draft one exam essay question—answerable in roughly 75 minutes, with a suggested length of about 800–1000 words—centered on the following injected scenario. A centralized transformer-based anomaly detector and a decentralized federated graph-neural-network detector both flag the same class of distributional shifts in streaming input data. They reach this agreement, however, through causally dissimilar internal pathways: the first adjusts attention-weight distributions across token embeddings, while the second updates message-passing states across relational nodes in a distributed graph. There is no shared hardware substrate, no algorithmic isomorphism, and no common intermediate representation between them. The question should ask students to evaluate whether there is a philosophically defensible sense in which these two heterogeneous implementations instantiate one and the same mechanism of anomaly detection. Students should be required to specify which conditions—functional equivalence, causal-structure preservation, substrate independence, or some other criterion—would have to be satisfied for that identity claim to hold, and they should explain what the failure of each condition implies for the ontology of mechanisms in engineered systems.

Please provide three things: (1) the essay question itself, with clear time and word guidance; (2) a model answer of approximately 600–800 words that makes specific conceptual moves rather than summarizing literature; and (3) a marking scheme built around five must-hit analytical points, with marks assigned to each so that the total is 20, plus a one-line note on the characteristic mistake students make for each point.

The deliverable must be fully self-contained: do not include bibliographies, required readings, or citations to specific texts or authors, since the intended respondent will not have web access and should not need to guess at attributions. Similarly, avoid framing the answer around obscure technical results that a well-trained but small language model would not reliably have encountered. The difficulty should come from the conceptual crux—determining what “same mechanism” could mean across causally and materially heterogeneous realizations—not from recall of niche terminology.
