I just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend about Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism—specifically his claim that a person who saves a spouse out of direct attachment but then adds a utilitarian justification has one thought too many. Rather than continue circling the same points, we want to formalize our disagreement through an adversarial collaboration. I represent the position that Williams identifies a structural fracture in practical identity: if an agent's ground projects are always subject in principle to being overridden by an impartial welfare calculus, then those projects lack the non-derivative authority required for genuine selfhood. My friend represents the revisionist position that Williams mistakes a contingent psychological strain—the discomfort of filtering love through an external metric—for a metaphysical failure, and that a mature agent can internalize utilitarian constraints without alienation, much as one internalizes grammatical rules without losing fluency in one's native language. Draft the joint statement we should have produced.

First, list the specific claims about practical identity, ground projects, and the formal logic of utilitarian justification that both sides can genuinely sign, including agreement on what distinguishes a ground project from a mere preference and on the structural features of the spouse-rescue scenario.

Second, state our remaining disagreements in neutral wording that each side would accept for its own view. In particular, distinguish whether the alienation Williams describes is metaphysical (the ground project is deprived of authoritative standing within the agent's practical reasoning) or phenomenological (the agent experiences motivational friction or retrospective regret). Also address the sub-question—raised repeatedly in our argument—of whether the objection applies only to ground-level commitments that constitute identity, or also extends to instrumental-level commitments that serve those projects without being themselves constitutive.

Third, identify the cruxes. I take the central ones to be: (i) whether an agent can genuinely regard a ground project as authoritative if its pursuit is always subject in principle to being overridden by an aggregate welfare calculus; and (ii) whether the spouse-rescue scenario demonstrates a normative failure in practical reasoning or only reveals a contingent feature of human moral psychology.

For each crux, specify a concrete case or argument both sides agree is relevant. For (i), use a variant of the spouse case in which the utilitarian calculus happens to align with saving the spouse—both sides should record in advance whether this alignment eliminates the objection or merely masks it, and whether authority can be restored by coincidence. For (ii), use a long-term commitment case in which an agent repeatedly faces conflicts between a ground project and utilitarian demands over years, producing either resilient identity integration or progressive fragmentation; each side should state in advance what pattern they predict and whether that pattern supports or undermines Williams's point.

Fourth, conclude by saying whether any part of our dispute is verbal rather than substantive—particularly around the phrase one thought too many—and if so, which part and why.

Write in American English, maintain a formal and polished tone, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific editions. The deliverable should be the document itself, formatted as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, not a commentary on our conversation.
