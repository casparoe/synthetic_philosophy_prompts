Following a long and somewhat circular argument with a friend about distributive justice, I want to get clear on a conceptual tension we kept circling but never resolved. Prioritarianism holds that benefits to the worse off carry greater moral weight, yet it denies that equality itself has intrinsic value. The classic leveling-down objection claims this is unstable: if equality does not matter, then in cases where we can only reduce well-being—say, by destroying a surplus that cannot be transferred—prioritarianism should be indifferent or even favorable toward the reduction, which seems wrong. The standard reply distinguishes prioritarian weighting from egalitarian comparison. But I want to push deeper into the conceptual architecture. Specifically, please explain the tension between prioritarianism's claim to be non-comparative—grounding moral weight in absolute levels of well-being rather than in relational differences—and the fact that its weighting function is necessarily defined over a comparison of who is worse off. If the priority assigned to a person depends on comparing her well-being to others', then is the view already committed to a comparative structure that makes leveling down conceptually available, even if not morally required? And if, conversely, the weights are fixed non-comparatively—say, by an individual threshold or a diminishing-returns curve applied separately to each person—then what precisely distinguishes prioritarianism from a sufficientarian view with an efficiency constraint? In other words, lay out the conceptual problem of whether prioritarianism can maintain its anti-egalitarian credentials without either collapsing into relational comparison or sliding into non-comparative sufficiency, and clarify what hangs on this distinction for the leveling-down objection's actual force.
