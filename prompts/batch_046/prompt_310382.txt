Hey, so I'm a software engineer and I don't realy have a philosophy background, but I got into this argument with my teamate about why our company builds things the way it does. Basicaly, for product features we use this satisficing approach - we run experiments until we hit a 3% improvement threshold and then we just ship, we don't keep searching for the perfect feature. It's like we accept good enough and move on. But then for security stuff, we have this absolute rule: we don't deploy anything unless it's formaly verified with zero known vulnerabilities, even if that takes months. Same company, same limited resources, totally different decision rules.

I'm trying to figure out what this split actually presupposes about rationality. Does it assume Herbert Simon's idea that rational agents have aspiration levels and stop searching when they hit them? Or does it assume something else, like that rationality isn't one thing but depends on the domain - like there's a different kind of good enough for user experience vs. safety? And is that even coherent? Like, if we're truly bounded rational agents with limited compute, why do we suddenly act like unbounded optimizers in security but not in product? I want to understand the implicit philosophy here. Is the practice actually consistent with one theory of bounded rationality, or is it philosophically underdetermined - compatible with several? And if we actually committed to a single uniform satisficing model across both domains, what would have to change in our engineering practice?
