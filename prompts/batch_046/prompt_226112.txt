I'm writing a feature for the science and ideas desk about why we sob over fictional characters even though we know they arn't real, and in my interviews everyone keeps reaching for the same comparison: engaging with fiction is like running a flight simulator for your emotions. You get the full affective hit -- grief, dread, catharsis -- but because the frame is tagged 'simulated,' your belief machinery dosent commit to the reality of the crash, so the old paradox about feeling for fictions just dissolves. People use it to argue that emotional responses to fiction are rational, just routed through a sandbox mode.

But I'm not convinced the mapping holds up under pressure. In a flight simulator the pilot knows exactly which inputs are synthetic and which are real; theres a clean interface. In fiction the boundry is blurrier -- think of immersive theater or those VR narratives where you physically move through the story, or the fact that we extract real moral knowledge from made-up worlds. Flight sims are also built for skill transfer, but fiction often transforms your values, not just trains them safely. So I want an audit: what's the cockpit, what's the pilot, what's the crash, what's the sandbox switch? Where does the correspondence break, which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion that fiction-emotions are rational, and does that conclusion survive once we notice fiction isn't just emotional training wheels? And if theres a sharper analogy that keeps both the immediacy and the lack of belief commitment without smuggling in the idea that made-up worlds are just practice for real ones, I'd like to hear it too. I keep mixing up affect and effect, but either way the feeling is real.
