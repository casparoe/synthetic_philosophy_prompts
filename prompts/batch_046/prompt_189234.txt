Question: In his discussion of material beings, Peter van Inwagen introduces what he calls the special composition question. First, state precisely what this question asks, and distinguish the three principal answers—universalism, nihilism, and restrictionism—that he identifies. Second, evaluate whether a restrictionist account that bases composition solely on spatiotemporal continuity and causal interaction among parts can avoid collapsing into either universalism or nihilism. Your evaluation should address the problem of vagueness, the threat posed by arbitrary undetached parts, and whether mereological essentialism offers a viable supplement to such an account.

Student answer: The special composition question asks under what conditions several distinct objects come together to form a single composite object. Van Inwagen distinguishes three answers: universalism holds that any plurality of objects composes something; nihilism holds that no plurality ever does; and restrictionism holds that composition occurs only when specific conditions are satisfied.

A restrictionist who appeals exclusively to spatiotemporal continuity and causal interaction can avoid both extremes. When parts are continuously connected and causally coupled, they constitute an integrated system; when they are scattered or causally isolated, they do not. This blocks universalism, since distant unrelated items fail the interaction condition, and it blocks nihilism, since integrated systems—such as organisms or artifacts—genuinely compose wholes.

The main difficulty is vagueness. If continuity admits of degrees, there will be borderline cases in which it is indeterminate whether composition occurs. Van Inwagen addresses this by denying that vague objects exist. He argues that accepting borderline cases of composition forces us to accept thousands of overlapping objects occupying the same region, because for every precise boundary we could draw, there is a corresponding composite. This is his argument from arbitrary undetached parts: if universalism is true, then for any object there exist countless other objects that differ only by a single microscopic part, all co-located with it.

The continuity-based restrictionist can defend herself by adopting mereological essentialism—the thesis that an object cannot survive the loss or gain of any part. Once essentialism is accepted, vagueness about parts is harmless: if a table loses a molecule, the original table is destroyed and a new table takes its place. Therefore, there is never ambiguity about whether the same composite persists; there is only a succession of precisely defined objects. Consequently, the continuity criterion survives without collapsing into universalism or nihilism, so long as it is supplemented by essentialism.
