Please explain the conceptual tension between pain asymbolia and the thesis that pain is bad in virtue of its phenomenal character. Patients with asymbolia typically preserve the sensory-discriminative profile of nociception—localization, intensity, qualitative modality—while lacking both negative affect and avoidance motivation; some describe the sensation as painful yet not unpleasant. If pain’s badness is intrinsic to what it feels like, then the presence of that sensory profile without suffering should still be bad, yet the patient’s evaluative report and behavioral indifference suggest otherwise. Distinguish three conceptual resolutions: (1) the phenomenal character remains intact but an accompanying evaluative judgment is absent, so badness was never in the raw feel; (2) the phenomenal character is subtly altered in ways that first-person report misses; (3) badness is relational, anchored to a subject’s motivational architecture rather than to the sensation itself, making pain only derivatively bad. Do not survey clinical literature. Instead, examine which of these moves genuinely resolves the tension without collapsing the distinction between pain and other unpleasant states, and say whether asymbolia forces us to abandon the idea that there is a uniform phenomenal quality responsible for the badness of all pain.
