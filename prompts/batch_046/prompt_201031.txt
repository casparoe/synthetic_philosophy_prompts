At a recent philosophy-of-science reading group, two members—Nadia and Elias—became locked in a debate about peer disagreement that quickly moved past textbook examples into finer-grained territory. Nadia opened with a conciliationist claim: when two researchers with identical training, access, and cognitive resources reach contradictory interpretations of the same statistical model, rationality requires each to reduce confidence in her own reading and move toward a middle position, because retaining full confidence amounts to an arbitrary privileging of one’s own reasoning processes. Elias answered with a steadfastness objection he called the “reflexivity problem”: the very act of classifying someone as an epistemic peer requires exercising the reasoning capacities that conciliationism asks you to suspend, so applying conciliation consistently forces you to doubt your ability to identify peers in the first place, collapsing the framework that makes disagreement meaningful. Nadia then distinguished two levels—evidential symmetry (shared raw data) versus procedural symmetry (shared reasoning transparency)—arguing that conciliationism governs only the first: if you can genuinely see that your peer misapplied a specific inferential step, you possess procedural asymmetry and are not required to split the difference. Elias replied that this separation is unstable, since your judgment that procedural asymmetry exists is itself a first-order belief formed by the very reasoning you are defending; he added that rational belief carries what he termed “doxastic inertia,” a default rational weight in favor of preserving competently formed beliefs unless an independent, non-question-begging defeater appears, which peer disagreement alone cannot supply without circularity. They asked the room to referee and could not resolve the standoff.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: rate Nadia’s opening conciliationist claim, Elias’s reflexivity objection, Nadia’s two-level distinction, and Elias’s inertia reply, each on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then indicate who is more right overall and identify what both parties are missing—specifically, whether there is a principled way to apply conciliationism that avoids both arbitrary self-privileging and reflexive collapse. In your response, state how confident you are in each of your ratings and single out the specific point about which you are least certain.
