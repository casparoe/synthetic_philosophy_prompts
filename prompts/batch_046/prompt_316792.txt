I am currently enrolled in an upper-division seminar on moral psychology and normative ethics—specifically, our present unit examines the conditions under which actions possess moral worth, with particular attention to whether acting for the right reasons is necessary, sufficient, or merely ornamental. During a recent discussion of Kantian moral worth, a classmate introduced what has become our section's recurring one-liner: "If the reason that actually moves you is not the same reason that justifies the action, then all you have is well-behaved coincidence—no moral worth whatsoever." The slogan was immediately put to work dismissing charitable giving motivated by tax advantages, altruistic rescue driven by empathic distress, and—most contentiously—what another student termed "structural-complicity motivation," in which one acts to remedy an unjust system one benefits from, rather than out of direct duty to the particular individuals harmed. I am not asking you to defend any of these dismissals; rather, I want the slogan disambiguated into its precise, distinct theses, arranged as a menu: which are tautological, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which the people repeating it in our room probably intend. For each reading, indicate whether it genuinely supports rejecting the structural-complicity case, or whether that dismissal requires smuggling in an extra premise about collective agency or personal duty. Please also state clearly what argument or empirical finding from moral psychology would change your verdict—say, if it turned out that acting from duty and acting from recognized complicity are psychologically indistinguishable in motivation, or if a counterexample showed that right-reason conditions apply differently in collective contexts. Finally, flag the point in your reasoning where you are most likely to go wrong: I have a lingering suspicion that I am conflating moral worth with praiseworthiness or with the kind of credit we assign to agents, and I want you to identify exactly where that confusion might corrupt the disambiguation.
