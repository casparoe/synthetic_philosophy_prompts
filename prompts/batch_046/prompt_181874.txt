I am drafting a novel in which several characters—an art forger working in the 1860s, a patent clerk, and a medical illustrator studying cell division—each employ the word "copy" with absolute confidence and complete mutual misunderstanding. In constructing their philosophical exchanges, I have been attempting to reconstruct what I take to be the underlying concept that disciplinary specialization has merely distorted. My guiding assumption is that if a term applies with genuine authority across legal, aesthetic, and biological domains, then there must exist a single set of necessary and sufficient conditions—perhaps involving causal derivation, structural correspondence, or intentional reproduction—that literal uses preserve intact and that metaphorical uses only extend.

Consequently, when I observe that copyright jurisprudence treats a translated poem as an infringing copy despite the total absence of perceptual resemblance, while an atelier denies that designation to any canvas that fails to approximate its model visually, and while biological replication is routinely described as copying despite lacking both intentional agency and perceptual similarity, I find myself compelled to ask which of these three registers holds the normative core and which two represent analogical deviations. Would you help me identify the precise logical architecture—whether a genus-species hierarchy, a prototype-radial-extension model, or some other formal relation—that binds these uses together, and advise me whether my presupposition of a single literal concept is philosophically defensible or should be replaced by an account that permits genuine conceptual fluidity without a normative center? I want the characters' disagreements to rest on a coherent misconception about the nature of conceptual application rather than on mere lexical ambiguity.
