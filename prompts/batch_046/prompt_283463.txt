I oversee acquisitions for a public-history documentary fund, and after a long argument last weekend with a friend who teaches environmental history, I am convinced we need a hard editorial rule rather than a vague sensibility. We receive roughly fifteen pitches a week—most are fifty-minute proposals for series about revolutions, battles, or biographies—and my board, which includes lawyers and climate scientists but no professional historians, expects me to sign off on whether a treatment respects the distinction between slow-moving structures and rapid events. The friend insisted that Fernand Braudel’s framework of geographical time, social conjoncture, and the event is not just historiographical ornament but an operational constraint: if a narrative assigns causal primacy to an individual decision or a single battle without first mapping the slower substrate—climate regimes, agrarian logistics, mental structures embedded in institutions—then it is not merely simplified, it is structurally false. I want a reusable decision procedure my team can run in about twelve minutes per pitch, with ordered questions, clear stop points where the answer is no, explicit hand-offs to value judgment rather than fact, and an honest flag for where the procedure will misfire. To keep this from being a generic history checklist, please build in a specific conceptual filter I am borrowing from that conversation: the idea of stratigraphic misalignment, which I take to mean assigning explanatory weight to a faster temporal layer—say, a diplomatic crisis or a charismatic leader—while treating a slower substrate—such as thermal inertia in an agrarian regime or the sedimentation rate of institutional memory—as mere background rather than as an active constraint that absorbs or redirects the faster event. Apply the procedure to three hypothetical pitches: one about Napoleon’s defeat at Waterloo that foregrounds weather patterns in the long term; one about the decline of the Dutch Republic that focuses almost entirely on the personal failures of statesmen; and one about a popular, visually compelling proposal centered on a single reformist emperor who allegedly transformed an empire’s economy through willpower alone, where I suspect the procedure will reject the pitch even though I personally find it compelling. In each case, show what the procedure outputs, where it demands a human judgment call, and what it explicitly cannot settle. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need something my board can use without leaving the room.
