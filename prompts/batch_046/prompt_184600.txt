I'm drafting a long-read about why Marx's base-and-superstructure framing keeps getting borrowed by tech writers and climate activists who probably never touched the original, and I got stuck on the actual wording. I was discussng this with a friend who studies philosophy of science, and she said the meme version—"economy makes everything happen"—is way cruder than what Marx actually wrote in his 1859 preface. Here's the chunk that's been circling in my head:

"In the social production of their existence, men inevitably enter into definite relations, which are independent of their will, namely relations of production appropriate to a given stage in the development of their material forces of production. The totality of these relations of production constitutes the economic structure of society, the real foundation, on which arises a legal and political superstructure and to which correspond definite forms of social consciousness. The mode of production of material life conditions the general process of social, political and intellectual life. It is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but their social existence that determines their consciousness."

My friend threw in the phrase "relative autonomy" and said that might be the key, but I'm not totally clear on how it squares with "social existence determines consciousness." Specifically, I'm wondering if Marx is saying that every specific idea—like, say, the development of modal logic or some obscure acoustic theory—has to be mechanically traced back to factory conditions, or if there's room for scientific reasoning to have its own internal dynamics that aren't just direct reflections of economic contradictions. Could someone help me understand whether this passage commits Marx to full-blown cultural determinism, or if I'm misreading the scope of "conditions the general process"? My editor wants the copy by thursday and I really don't want to misrepresent the source.
