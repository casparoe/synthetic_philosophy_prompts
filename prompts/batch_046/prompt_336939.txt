Write a one-year dissertation prospectus in moral and political philosophy on the following question: Raphael Lemkin designed genocide as a universal legal-moral category focused on the intentional destruction of a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group, irrespective of the organizational form that destruction takes. Zygmunt Bauman, however, argued that the Holocaust was not merely an extreme instance of group hatred but a product of modern bureaucratic rationality that transforms the moral structure of mass violence. The existing debate—often framed through the Historikerstreit over uniqueness and comparability—tends to treat these as competing historical descriptions. I want to ask instead whether they generate a conceptual instability: if Bauman's analysis is correct, does applying the genocide label to pre-modern or non-bureaucratic mass killings commit a category mistake, or does it reveal that Lemkin's framework actually conflates two distinct normative concepts—one tracking intentional group destruction, the other tracking systemically rationalized group annihilation?

The proposal should state this question precisely, explain why the standard uniqueness/comparability literature and the legal-philosophical reception of Bauman leave this bifurcation unexamined, outline a conceptual method (for instance, comparing how the term functions in international legal reasoning versus sociological diagnosis, and testing whether splitting the concept resolves current disputes over naming atrocities without losing normative force), specify what a successful deliverable looks like for a committee that cares about both philosophical clarity and practical relevance to classification politics, and assess honestly whether the project risks collapsing into empirical comparative history or can remain tractably philosophical within one year. Do not include a bibliography, literature review by citation, or requests for source verification; I will handle references.
