You are coaching a speaker for the Q&A after a seminar in a philosophy of ethics reading group. The speaker is defending the thesis that Nozick’s familiar distinction — treating animals by utilitarian aggregation and treating persons by Kantian-style side constraints — collapses once the ground of direct rights is identified not with species membership but with the capacity for narrative self-constitution. On this reconstructed view, certain non-human animals (notably cetaceans and great apes) fall under direct side constraints, while wild invertebrates and most domestic livestock do not; ecological intervention is therefore permissible only when it respects the rights of self-constituting agents, human or otherwise. The speaker further claims that this resolves the apparent tension in Nozick’s remarks on animal suffering without reducing the framework to blanket speciesism.

The audience is precisely composed of: (i) a Kantian moral philosopher who regards any threshold-based account of rational nature as a misreading of Kant’s doctrine of dignity; (ii) an animal-rights abolitionist who treats all graded moral-status views as speciesism disguised by cognitive criteria; (iii) a libertarian political philosopher sympathetic to Nozick but convinced that stretching side constraints beyond human agents dissolves them into welfare maximisation; and (iv) a graduate researcher on wild-animal suffering who will press the practical implications for predator–prey dynamics.

Prepare a speaker’s prep sheet. List the objections in the rough order they will arise, each phrased in the vocabulary and framing that the particular audience member would use. For each, state what the speaker should concede outright, what should be held firm, and what requires only a partial concession; provide a concise reply suitable for a two-minute seminar response; and flag explicitly the weakest point in that reply. Order them by a combination of likelihood and potential damage to the thesis. Include, separately and clearly marked, the objection the speaker most hopes nobody asks — namely, the charge that if narrative self-constitution is the criterion for direct protection, then severely cognitively disabled human beings fall outside it, undermining the view’s moral credibility — together with a reply that acknowledges the difficulty without special pleading. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; the speaker has no access to search tools and needs arguments that can stand on conceptual grounds alone. Write in polished British English.
