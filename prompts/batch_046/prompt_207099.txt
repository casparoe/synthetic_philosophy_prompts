I am a doctoral student in democratic theory and social ontology, and I am trying to move beyond a seminar dispute that has been running in circles for over a year. The two sides I want to formalize are, first, the Collective-Intentionality Defenders, who hold that populist appeals to "the will of the people" can be made conceptually coherent if we treat "the people" as a plural agent capable of joint commitment, with democratic procedures functioning not merely to aggregate pre-existing preferences but to constitute collective intentions that bind members even in the face of individual dissent; and, second, the Pluralist-Skeptics, who maintain that "the people" lacks the unity and shared mental structure required for collective agency, that democratic outputs are at best distributive aggregates of heterogeneous and often endogenously formed preferences, and that populist rhetoric therefore either collapses into majoritarian domination or invokes a fictional collective subject to suppress legitimate internal conflict.

Please draft the joint adversarial-collaboration statement these two camps could both sign. I want a numbered section of agreed claims—covering what democratic procedures actually do, the distinction between distributive preference satisfaction and collective intentional states, the problem of endogenous preference formation (where institutions shape the preferences they aggregate), and the specific rhetorical move of treating non-participation or silence as tacit authorization of a collective will—followed by a disagreements table in which each residual dispute is phrased in wording its own side would accept. Identify the cruxes: I take one to be whether social-choice aggregation can ever yield a collective intention that is more than a statistical summary, and another to be whether ascribing shared mental states to democratic silence requires an implausible analogy to small-group joint action. For each crux, specify a concrete institutional design, empirical pattern, or conceptual case both sides agree is relevant, and record in advance what each side predicts it would show. Where part of the dispute is verbal—say, over whether "will" should be read as a psychological predicate or a normative authorization relation—say exactly which part and locate the non-verbal residue. Finally, in your framing please separate what is genuinely settled in democratic theory and social ontology from what remains actively contested among specialists, and do not present any contested interpretive claim—about joint intentionality, preference aggregation, or the coherence of populist rhetoric—as if it were the consensus view.
