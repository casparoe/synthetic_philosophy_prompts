Suppose you are advising a small interdisciplinary group—legal philosophers, political theorists, and one historian—with three years and modest funding to pursue conceptual work on how mass atrocities are classified and responded to. Rather than a survey of existing scholarship, I want a defended agenda: which open conceptual questions about genocide as both a legal and moral category are genuinely worth this group’s time, and in what order should they be tackled?

To avoid conflating distinct issues, distinguish three contested registers. First, the definitional problem: whether the 1948 Convention’s emphasis on specific intent and enumerated protected groups captures what is morally distinctive about group destruction, or whether it systematically excludes structural, institutional, and cultural forms that Lemkin originally sought to cover. Second, the naming problem: whether applying or withholding the term genocide in contested cases—where destruction is gradual, mediated by bureaucracy, or lacks explicit orders—is best understood as a legal determination, a moral judgement, or a political speech act carrying its own normative weight. Third, the responsibility problem: whether greater conceptual precision about genocide aids the design of preventive and post-hoc institutional mechanisms, or whether legal exactitude actually conflicts with the moral urgency required for timely intervention.

I am not asking for a catalogue. Instead, defend a prioritisation: which register (or another you consider upstream) should come first, which second, and which should be deprioritised? Use explicit criteria—conceptual dependency (settling one changes the approach to others), tractability for philosophers and theorists without archival or empirical fieldwork, and practical stakes for institutional design. Say what kind of conceptual result or counterexample over the next few years would cause you to revise this ranking.

Please separate clearly what is broadly settled among specialists—for instance, that Lemkin’s original conception was wider than the final Convention text, and that the legal category requires proof of specific intent—from what remains genuinely contested, such as whether cultural destruction should be recognised as a distinct moral wrong, or whether the politics of naming can be evaluated independently of legal accuracy. Do not present a contested interpretation—such as the claim that legal precision always undermines moral response—as if it were the consensus view.

Write in British English. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Avoid invoking highly specialised theories, obscure doctrinal distinctions, or case-specific details that a well-read generalist could not reasonably be expected to know.
