I have recently taken on a coordinating role in a decentralized scientific collective, and I have begun to notice a decision-making pattern for which I lack the right conceptual frame. In our group, individual laboratories can unilaterally impose stricter protocols on shared equipment and data repositories—tightening access thresholds, adding redundant verification steps, or restricting permissible sample sizes—without consulting the other members. Each lab acts from individually defensible motives: reducing measurement error, protecting against liability, or preserving scarce biological material. Yet because these restrictions are adopted in parallel and accumulate without central review, the collective infrastructure gradually becomes so over-protected that genuinely exploratory work is effectively frozen out; even the labs that introduced the stricter rules find themselves unable to use the resources they sought to safeguard. The harm is diffuse, unintended, and arises precisely from the absence of coordination rather than from any single agent’s overreach.

This feels adjacent to familiar collective-action failures, but it does not fit them neatly. It is not the tragedy of the commons, since the shared resource is not depleted through overuse but rather encumbered through parallel acts of defensive restriction. Nor does it match the standard formulation of the unilateralist’s curse, in which one actor takes a risky or expansive action that imposes negative externalities on everyone else; here, the negative aggregate outcome is produced by many actors each taking individually risk-averse actions, with no single party causing the harm and with every party suffering from the result.

Does this configuration—distributed, parallel acts of defensive restriction that cumulatively degrade the very resource they aim to protect—have an established name in decision theory, social philosophy, or institutional economics? If it is recognized as a variant of the unilateralist’s curse, what precisely distinguishes this distributed, risk-averse form from the classic unilateral, risk-seeking form? And if it lacks a precise term, how should its boundaries be drawn: for instance, must each actor’s restriction be rational ex ante, must the aggregate outcome be Pareto-inferior to the uncoordinated baseline, and how should it be separated from ordinary institutional sclerosis or bureaucratic accretion? I am not looking for a bare label, but for a clear carving of what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
