I'm delivering the opening remarks at a town-hall reconciliation forum next Thursday evening, held in the auditorium where our public library used to host debates before the space was closed after the monument dispute and the counter-protests last spring. The audience will be split: longtime residents for whom "not backing down" and "showing respect" are moral currencies; younger organizers who speak in terms of harm, recognition, and institutional repair; and neighbors who mostly want to check out books again without feeling they have to pick a side. I have no formal training in sociology or moral philosophy, but I have been thinking on my own about the three frameworks people keep invoking—honor culture, where status is earned through public reputation and retaliation restores it; dignity culture, where every person carries an inherent worth that should be protected by impartial rules regardless of emotion; and what some writers call victimhood culture, where status comes from being recognized as wronged and where grievance demands institutional response. I do not want to declare any of these morally superior in the abstract, because each seems to capture something the others miss, yet I also want the remarks to do real work: they should show how adopting one framework over the others actually changes what counts as a resolution, who owes what to whom, and whether forgiveness is even a relevant concept.

To make this concrete without simply lecturing, I want the speech to turn on a distinction I have been trying to work out myself—between what I am calling "restorative honor," in which a wrongdoer rebuilds status through visible, acknowledged repair rather than through dominance, and "protective dignity," in which the wronged party's status is secured not by emotional reconciliation but by structural boundaries that prevent repetition regardless of whether anyone feels better. I also want to address the risk I see in all three frameworks: that in a mixed community like ours, grievance can become a kind of currency—what I think of as "competitive victimhood"—where the moral high ground is measured by the magnitude of suffering claimed rather than by any shared standard of repair. The speech should be about five minutes, land for both the activists and the older residents without condescension, and use these ideas to shape what I can and cannot say: for instance, if I treat the conflict through a pure honor lens, I must not ask for institutional rules that suppress emotional acknowledgment; if I treat it through a pure victimhood lens, I must not ask the wronged to forgive for the sake of community harmony. Please write the remarks so that the philosophical framework is doing structural work, not just decoration, and please say at the end how confident you are in the organizing framework you have used, singling out specifically the point where you are least sure—whether it is the coherence of "restorative honor" as I have sketched it, the claim that competitive victimhood is a genuine risk across all three cultures, or something else. I want the remarks to feel like they come from someone who has genuinely wrestled with these ideas rather than from an expert delivering settled doctrine.
