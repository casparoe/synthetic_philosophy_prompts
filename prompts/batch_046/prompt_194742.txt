I have constructed an argument that extends Benacerraf's epistemological challenge into what my study group suspects is a reductio of any non-causal theory of knowledge. I find it suspicious because, if sound, it undermines not only mathematical Platonism but every domain in which we claim knowledge of causally inert structures. Here is the reasoning:

(1) Any genuine knowledge requires that the knower stand in an appropriate epistemic relation—causal, informational, or structurally tracking—to the state of affairs known.

(2) If mathematical Platonism is true, then mathematical objects (for example, the empty set, arbitrary ordinals, or cardinals lacking concrete instantiation) are abstract, non-spatiotemporal, and causally inert.

(3) Therefore, on Platonism, no human cognitive mechanism can stand in the required epistemic relation to mathematical objects.

(4) If Platonism is false, and mathematics concerns only concrete mental constructions, physical patterns, or algorithmically compressible structures, then mathematics is systematically false about its apparent subject matter—there are no arbitrary infinite sets, only representations.

(5) Hence either mathematical knowledge is impossible, or mathematics is globally false about what exists.

(6) Yet we plainly possess extensive mathematical knowledge, and mathematics is not globally false.

(7) So we must reject the claim in (1) that knowledge requires the specified epistemic relation—at least for mathematics—thereby allowing that structural coherence or rational intuition can bridge the causal gap.

(8) However, if causal or informational access is dispensable for mathematics, it is dispensable for any domain with structurally analogous features: modal knowledge of possible worlds, moral knowledge of non-natural properties, or logical knowledge of infinite consequence spaces.

(9) Therefore, either we deny that we have mathematical knowledge (contradicting (6)), or we accept that 'knowledge' can be attributed to any belief-system achieving internal coherence, regardless of whether its objects exist or are causally accessible.

(10) The latter renders the concept of knowledge vacuous and collapses the distinction between knowledge and sophisticated fiction.

If I wish to preserve both (1) and (6), I seem forced into an implausible ontology; if I reject (1) to save mathematics, I seem to open the floodgates to attributing knowledge everywhere. Where does this argument go wrong? I should prefer to keep (1) and (6) intact if possible, but I want to know precisely which step is weakest—whether there is an equivocation in 'epistemic relation' between steps (1) and (7), a hidden scope shift in (8), or whether (4) mischaracterises anti-Platonist alternatives such as modal structuralism or deflationary nominalism. Please explain why the flawed step looked plausible, and what each of the standard responses—causal-rejectionism, modal structuralism, and the 'no objects' nominalist—must pay to block the inference to (9). Please write your response in British English.
