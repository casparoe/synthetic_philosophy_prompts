I've been working through Aristotle's old puzzle about the sea battle — the worry that if we can already say there will be a battle tomorrow, then tomorrow's outcome seems fixed, but if the future is genuinely open, then that statement shouldn't be true or false yet — and I want to teach the core logical tension to my neighbor's sharp fifteen-year-old, who hasn't studied philosophy but keeps asking whether the future is already written. Rather than using the classic naval example, I've constructed my own carrying case: a river ferry that will be canceled next Tuesday only if a five-person town council votes to redirect bridge-repair funds, with the vote unscheduled and the weather forecast making either outcome plausible but neither inevitable. I need a teaching plan, not the lecture itself: first, the exact sequence of ideas that must be in place before the paradox can even register to her, particularly how to separate we do not know yet from it is not determined yet; second, what properties my ferry example must have and must lack so that it genuinely carries the logical point rather than smuggling in determinism or hidden randomness; third, the misconceptions she will predictably raise, in the order she will raise them — I am especially worried she will invoke quantum indeterminacy or divine foreknowledge to dissolve the problem, and I want to know where in the arc to head off each one without derailing the lesson; fourth, a concrete check I can use at the end to distinguish whether she has grasped the logical tension or merely concluded that the future is unknown; and finally, what I should explicitly set aside at this level — modal logic, branching-time semantics, or the fatalism literature — and say out loud that we are leaving it behind. Please structure the plan so that each stage depends on the previous one, and flag any hidden assumption in my ferry framing that would break the analogy.
