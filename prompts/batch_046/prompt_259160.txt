As a PhD student working on confirmation theory, I have watched the debate over old evidence in Bayesian epistemology reach an impasse that feels deeper than mere exhaustion. The core problem is familiar: if an agent already knows some evidence E and then introduces a theory H that entails E, standard Bayesian conditioning seems to make E strongly confirm H, even though in scientific practice we treat such post-hoc accommodation with suspicion—the classic bootstrapping worry. The standard replies are also familiar: distinguish logical from incremental confirmation; insist that old evidence can still confirm auxiliary assumptions; invoke predictive novelty or temporal priority as extra-Bayesian constraints. Yet in seminars and in the literature, neither side has yielded ground for decades. I want a structural diagnosis, not another defense of Bayes or anti-Bayes.

To make the stall concrete, consider a framing I am experimenting with—one that does not depend on any specific recent paper. Suppose we reject the shared presupposition that an agent's doxastic state is always a single, unified probability distribution. Instead, adopt a path-dependent model of evidential access: the confirmatory force of E for H depends not only on the likelihood ratio P(E|H)/P(E|¬H), but on whether the agent retains an independent, unmediated representational pathway to E that was not constructed through H. On this view, old evidence loses power not because of calendar age, but because the only remaining cognitive route to E runs through H itself—the pathway has been captured. This is meant as a normative structural claim about what confirmation requires, not a claim about neural architecture.
