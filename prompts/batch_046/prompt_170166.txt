I'm going through Franklin's autobiography for a philosophy of self-improvment course and I'm stuck on the part where he describes his scheme for moral perfection. He lists thirteen virtues — temperance, silence, order, resolution, frugality, industry, sincerity, justice, moderation, cleanliness, tranquility, chastity, and humility — and decides to tackle them one at a time, carrying a little book with grid pages for each day. Every evening he puts black marks next to the virtues he failed at, and he only moves on to the next one in the list after he gets through a full week with no marks for the current one. Then he cycles back through all thirteen repeatedly, like he's debugging his own character in loops.

The thing I can't wrap my head around is whether this method hides a kind of circularity or bootstrapping problem. He's using his own judgment — the very faculty that's supposed to be getting perfected — to recognize failures at humility or sincerity, and then writing those judgments down in the notebook that certifies he's ready to advance. If your moral perception is already corrupted by pride or self-deception, wouldn't the black marks just track whatever you're comfortable admitting, not actual vice? And there's also the ordering: he puts Order third but then admits it's nearly impossible for him because his printing business keeps interupting everything. Does the sequence actually depend on logical priorities — like you need Order before Industry — or is it just arbitrary? I don't see Franklin addressing whether the recorder and the recorded are the same broken instrument, and I wonder if the notebook breaks the loop or just disguises it.
