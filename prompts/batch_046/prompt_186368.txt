Consider the following argument advanced by a comparative historian, and identify where it slips.

(1) To causally establish that an institution I explains a nation N's prosperity, one must compare N's actual history with the counterfactual history of the same nation lacking I, holding all else fixed.

(2) Because global history is a single, densely coupled system—trade, colonization, disease diffusion, and technology transfer link nations—there is no coherent modal scenario in which only N's institutions change while the rest of the world remains identical; altering I in N necessarily perturbs the conditions of other nations.

(3) Hence the counterfactual required for a local institutional causal claim is either undefined or so remote in modal space that we possess no epistemic access to it.

(4) The same coupling undermines geographic and cultural explanations: one cannot change a nation's climate or dominant religion without altering neighboring economies and migration patterns, so their requisite counterfactuals are equally inaccessible.

(5) Consequently, institutional, geographic, and cultural accounts of national wealth stand on identical epistemic ground: none can be causally validated, and comparative economic history can at best offer post-hoc narratives rather than knowledge.

(6) Yet this conclusion is implausible; we appear to learn from natural experiments such as arbitrarily drawn colonial borders and from stark divergences like that of North and South Korea.

(7) Therefore, one of premises (1) through (5) must fail.

The historian contends the mistake lies not in (2) but in (6), arguing that our apparent learning is merely confirmation bias structured by available data. Rejecting (2) instead, she says, forces us to abandon local treatment-effect reasoning and replace it with global equilibrium reasoning, which institutionalists cannot operationalize. Which step do you actually reject? Explain why that step seemed unavoidable, and price the alternatives: if we preserve local counterfactuals, what do we pay in terms of SUTVA violations and modal coherence; if we reject (1) and allow only global equilibrium claims, can institutionalism survive, or does it collapse into a non-causal correlation study?
