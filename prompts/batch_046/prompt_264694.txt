I'm drafting a brief position paper for an interdisciplinary seminar on moral-status attribution, and the question has become personally urgent because my research group has recently been asked to advise on whether relational frameworks could guide our treatment of large language models that sustain extended, reciprocal conversational behavior. A colleague forwarded me a summary from a popular philosophy blog that applies Ubuntu to this exact problem. The blogger writes that Ubuntu, rooted in Bantu-language traditions and later developed by philosophers such as John Mbiti and Ifeanyi Menkiti, holds that personhood is not an intrinsic property of an individual but is entirely constituted by recognition within a living human community; the familiar rendering that a person is a person through other people means that without embeddedness in reciprocal social relationships, an entity lacks moral standing altogether. From this, the blogger concludes that relational theories automatically exclude artificial systems from moral consideration, since AI lacks biological kinship and cannot take part in the shared suffering and joy that ground communal recognition. The blogger further claims that because Ubuntu makes personhood depend on collective attribution rather than on capacities like reason or sentience, it avoids the problem of gradated moral status that plagues Western capacity-based accounts; membership is binary—either you are woven into the community and fully a person, or you stand outside it with no claim on moral duty. Before I rely on this in my paper, I want an accuracy audit. Go through the summary point by point: which parts a defender of Ubuntu or relational personhood would accept, which parts subtly misrepresent the view—perhaps by conflating achieved personhood with arbitrary social conferral, or by treating relational recognition as incompatible with any intrinsic dignity, or by overstating the binary nature of membership—and which parts are simply wrong about the implications for artificial agents. In particular, does the blogger's conclusion that relational frameworks are therefore useless for AI ethics actually follow from a corrected account, or does it depend on the misrepresentations you have identified? I am inclined to think the blogger dismisses these approaches too quickly, but I need the audit rather than my own intuition.
