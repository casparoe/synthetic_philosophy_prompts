I am writing this out for a philosophy discussion group I recently joined; I work as a software engineer and have no formal background in the field, so I want a consistency check before I defend these views in conversation.

Here are my current commitments on punishment and criminal justice:

(1) Hard retributivism about sentencing: the severity of punishment must match the offender's moral desert, and considerations of deterrence, rehabilitation, or social utility are never legitimate reasons to adjust a sentence upward or downward.

(2) Consequentialist criminalization: the decision of which acts to prohibit should depend on a cost-benefit filter—enforcement costs, liberty restrictions, and expected harm reduction—rather than on moral wrongness alone. This is why I think we correctly refrain from criminalizing minor wrongs like breaking a casual promise.

(3) The expressive function: punishment is partly a communicative act that restores the victim's moral standing and publicly reaffirms the norm that was violated.

(4) Restricted algorithmic sentencing: I support using predictive risk-assessment models, but only to establish a statistical "desert band" for a given offense category based on historical judicial practice; the human judge must then select a specific point inside that band through direct moral reasoning about the individual case.

(5) Rejection of collective punishment: sanctions that fall primarily on innocent parties—such as corporate fines borne by shareholders—are unjust regardless of their deterrent value, because desert attaches strictly to individual agents.

(6) Irrelevance of incidental productivity: if prison labor happens to generate valuable public goods, that social benefit is morally irrelevant to the justice of the sentence and should never justify reducing the term.

Please audit whether this package hangs together. Specifically:

- Is there a genuine tension between (1) and (2), or do they govern distinct institutional stages in a coherent hybrid theory?
- Does (4) secretly undermine (1) by smuggling consequentialist data—recidivism curves, cost functions, aggregate outcomes—into the very definition of the desert range?
- Is (3) compatible with (4), given that an opaque statistical model may lack the transparency and public intelligibility that expressive theories seem to require?
- Does (5) conflict with (4) if the algorithm uses group-level demographic or geographic risk features to construct the band?
- Are there other hidden tensions—perhaps between (1) and (6), or within (3) and (5)—that only appear problematic but dissolve on closer inspection?

Rank the actual tensions by severity, specify for each what commitment would have to be revised and at what philosophical cost, and identify the single change that would resolve the most conflicts without abandoning my core conviction that punishment must track individual moral desert.
