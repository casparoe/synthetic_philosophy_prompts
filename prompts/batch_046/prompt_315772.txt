I sit on the international advisory panel for the proposed Digital Cosmopolitan Framework, and we are required to submit a formal policy memo before next month's summit. Our specific brief concerns whether algorithmic-transparency obligations for cross-border AI systems ought to be classified, in Kantian terms, as a preliminary article—an instrumental condition that removes incentives for digital conflict but should remain provisional—or as a definitive article, meaning they must be constitutionally entrenched within any federation of digital republics. A minority of the panel has advanced a somewhat unorthodox reading: they claim that transparency operates as what they term a preliminary article of cognitive sovereignty, necessary to prevent epistemic domination by opaque foreign models, yet dangerous if elevated to a definitive right because it could be weaponised for surveillance under the guise of cosmopolitan law. The majority regards this distinction as spurious and insists that without constitutional entrenchment, transparency remains merely aspirational and therefore fails Kant's requirement that perpetual peace depend on enforceable institutional structures rather than voluntary forbearance.

Please draft the panel's decision memo in British English. It should recommend a concrete course of action—either treating transparency as preliminary, definitive, or adopting a hybrid framework with a clear sunset clause or review mechanism—and set out the reasoning in a form that members of the opposing faction could genuinely respond to rather than dismiss. The memo should engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side: specifically, address whether provisional transparency risks creating a permanent state of exception, or conversely whether constitutional entrenchment risks collapsing the distinction between republican self-legislation and external coercion. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; rely on the conceptual structure outlined above.
