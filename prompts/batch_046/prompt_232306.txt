I was a marine ecologist for about thirty years before I retired, and lately I've been auditing some philosophy seminars at the local university—mostly environmental ethics, which I find fascinating but also kind of slippery compared to measuring plankton densities. I'm helping a friend who teaches an upper-level undergrad course put together the final exam, and we want one essay question that students can't just answer by repeating the standard Singer-style drowning child stuff or whatever they've seen a million times on the forums.

We'd like the question to focus on meat consumption and wild animal suffering, specifically beef versus chicken, but with a niche twist: we want students to grapple with what I'm tentatively calling the 'pasture-paradox'—the claim that because cattle can graze on marginal land that won't support crops for direct human consumption, shifting away from chicken (which depends on feed-crops that kill lots of small vertebrates and insects during harvest) toward beef might actually reduce total wild-animal suffering per calorie, *if* we apply a taxonomically-weighted suffering metric that counts mammalian suffering more heavily than invertebrate suffering. The question should force them to evaluate whether this paradox holds up once you account for subsitiution effects in agricultural markets, and whether using a weighted moral-status metric is even consistent with the egalitarian framework that usually motivates concern for wild animals in the first place. It should be designed for a 90-minute essay, maybe worth 25 marks out of 100, aimed at philosophy majors who've read basic applied ethics but aren't specialists in animal welfare economics.

Please draft the exam question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme with six must-hit points—assign marks to each, total 25, and include a one-line note on how candidates typically botch that point. Don't ask for citations or a bibliography; it's a closed-book exam. And please don't just recite the standard crop-death comparison—make sure the crux is whether the hidden tension between aggregate suffering reduction and taxonomic weighting actually undermines the argument for dietary substitution.
