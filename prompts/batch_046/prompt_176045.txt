I'm working through my own judgments about which entities count as living, trying to see whether they converge on a single principle or whether I have to abandon one. Here are my verdicts, given concretely.

A bacterium dividing in culture is alive. A dormant seed that has suspended all measurable metabolism for centuries but can still germinate is alive during dormancy. A mitochondrion isolated in an artificial cytosol, maintaining membrane potential and producing ATP but unable to replicate its full genome, is alive.

A virus that evolves, encodes proteins, and hijacks a host's ribosomes is not alive. A viroid—a small catalytic RNA circle that replicates without encoding any proteins—is also not alive, though it feels closer to the boundary than a virus does. A prion that propagates its misfolded conformation through tissue but has no metabolism, boundary, or genetic material is not alive.

Consider now a synthetic protocell I have in mind: a fatty-acid vesicle enclosing an autocatalytic peptide network that maintains internal chemical homeostasis, grows, and divides by budding, yet contains no nucleic acids and has never been part of a Darwinian evolutionary lineage. I judge this to be alive, but I see that this pulls against heredity-based accounts.

A flame that consumes fuel, grows, and emits sparks that ignite new flames is not alive. A crystal that seeds identical lattice growth in a supersaturated solution is not alive. A computer simulation running on silicon in which digital entities reproduce with mutation and are subject to selection is not alive.

The tension I keep returning to is between the synthetic protocell and the virus: the protocell lacks genetic material and evolutionary history, yet I call it alive; the virus possesses both, yet I deny it life. At the same time, the isolated mitochondrion is alive despite being unable to reproduce independently, which suggests that autonomous reproduction is not a necessary condition.

Is there a coherent principle that captures all of these verdicts? If so, state it precisely, indicate which clause handles each case above, and say what it predicts about an entity I have not listed: a self-sustaining chemical reaction network in a hydrothermal vent that reproduces its local boundary conditions chemically but lacks discrete membranes or individuated units. If no single principle can accommodate all of my judgments, identify which one or two verdicts I should overturn and why. Please also separate what in your answer is settled in biology or philosophy of biology from what is genuinely contested among informed people, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
