In the Zhuangzi, Huizi finds Zhuangzi sitting with his legs spread wide, beating on a tub and singing shortly after his wife's death. When Huizi asks how he can be so unfeeling, Zhuangzi replies that he did grieve initially, but then he considered her origin in the undifferentiated great clod, her life and death as phases in the continuous transformation of all things, and concluded that lamentation reveals a misunderstanding of fate. I want to understand whether this is best read as a metaphysical claim that individual identity is subsumed within an impersonal cosmic process—making death not a loss of anyone specific—or as an ethical instruction to align one's emotions with inevitable change. Does the passage leave room for mourning as a legitimate expression of human attachment, or does it treat all grief as a kind of conceptual error? And how might this bear on the contemporary distinction between death as a harm to the deceased and death as a harm only to survivors?
