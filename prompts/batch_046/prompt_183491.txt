Analyze the 1954 Army-McCarthy hearings—specifically the confrontation in which Army counsel Joseph Welch publicly rebuked Senator Joseph McCarthy for attacking a young associate—as a case study in performative utterance and institutional authority. Treat Welch’s intervention not as simple description or emotional rhetoric, but as a potential speech act: use Austin’s distinction between illocutionary force and perlocutionary effect, together with Searle’s conditions for successful performatives (preparatory conditions, sincerity conditions, and the direction of fit), to determine whether Welch was attempting to perform an act—namely, stripping McCarthy of legitimate senatorial moral standing—rather than merely reporting on it. The analysis should identify exactly who held the conventional authority required for such a performative to succeed, and whether Welch possessed it. Then consider the complication raised by what some theorists call counter-performativity: an utterance that lacks standard institutional backing but achieves its illocutionary force through collective audience uptake that retroactively reassigns authority. Given that McCarthy’s political capital was already visibly eroding, determine whether Welch’s words caused the collapse of that authority, merely registered a prior erosion, or operated in some intermediate fashion. Conclude by saying what this episode shows about speech-act theory itself: does it demonstrate that preparatory conditions can be satisfied ex post facto through audience recognition, or does it confirm that institutional authority remains prior and Welch’s success was only possible because those conditions had already silently failed?
