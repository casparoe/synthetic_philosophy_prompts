As I revise the third section of my novel, in which a narrator who is herself a failed moralist passes judgment on the other characters, I have become preoccupied with the conditions under which hypocritical blame retains its force. I have tried to map my own intuitions onto the situations my characters face, and I find myself holding four verdicts that seem correct individually but resist unification. First, there is the retired surgeon who, having once falsified research data in her youth, now publicly excoriates a younger colleague for the same offense; my instinct is that she possesses standing, though her blame ought to be delivered with an acknowledged fragility. Second, there is the environmental activist who condemns air travel among her audience while maintaining a private jet for her book tours; here I feel she lacks standing altogether, and the hypocrisy is not merely weakening but nullifying. Third, there is the reformed addict who blames a close friend for relapsing into the very substance he himself overcame; despite the parallel, I think his standing is actually enhanced by his history, not eroded by it. Fourth, and this is the case that most concerns my plot, there is the novelist-character within my novel who writes a scene in which one figure condemns another for sentimentalizing grief, even as my narrator reveals that the novelist-character herself sentimentalized her own mother’s death in an earlier autobiographical work; yet if she had rendered that same grief as fiction rather than memoir, I believe my intuition would reverse—she would retain standing, because the genre shift introduces what I can only call an aesthetic distance that severs personal complicity from the judgment.

Is there a single principle—perhaps concerning the relevance of present disposition, the severity of the parallel fault, the transparency of self-knowledge, or the mediating role of artistic form—that delivers all four verdicts? Or must I abandon one of them to preserve coherence? If a principle requires overturning one intuition, I want to know which one is the outlier and whether the principle can be repaired with an additional clause rather than discarded. Please state the principle precisely, identify the case that motivates each clause, and report its verdict on a scenario I have not listed: suppose a reader, aware that the author of a novel shares the protagonist’s central moral failing, blames the protagonist within the fiction. Does the reader’s awareness of the author’s hypocrisy undermine the reader’s own standing to blame the character? I ask not for a bibliography or a survey of the literature, but for the account itself, together with an honest acknowledgment of which of my four intuitions it cannot accommodate without strain.
