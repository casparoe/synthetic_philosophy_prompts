Here are my intuitive verdicts on whether previously known evidence confirms a scientific theory, and I should like to know whether a single principle governs them or whether some must be relinquished. That general relativity explains Mercury's perihelion, documented long before Einstein, strikes me as strong confirmation despite the evidence being old. That a cosmologist adds a free parameter to absorb anomalous supernova data and then cites the fit as confirmation strikes me as yielding negligible support. If two live quantum-gravity frameworks entail the same value for a newly measured spectral feature, both are confirmed in my view, yet the one reaching the result with fewer adjustable postulates is confirmed more strongly. A climate model predicting that global temperatures remain within ranges already observed for centuries gains almost no support when they do. Consider now a neuroscience theory that accounts for classic nineteenth-century lesion studies solely through an auxiliary mechanism devised for that purpose; should that mechanism later generate a surprising, successful prediction in 2024, I think the old lesion data then retroactively confer substantial confirmation upon the theory. By contrast, learning of an obscure, abandoned rival that predicts Mercury's perihelion with equal precision using different mathematics leaves my credence in GR unchanged, yet I still feel its confirmation from that datum is somewhat diluted. Finally, when an old astronomical dataset can be rendered consistent with two currently competing but incompatible theories—each explaining different modern phenomena—I think both receive partial confirmation rather than cancelling one another out. Is there a coherent Bayesian principle—perhaps distinguishing accommodation from genuine prediction, weighing prior simplicity, or conditioning on considered rivals—that captures all of these judgements, or must one be abandoned?
