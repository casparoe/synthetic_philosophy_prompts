For roughly four decades, comparative constitutional scholars and EU legal theorists have debated whether the principle of subsidiarity—invoked in the EU Treaties, embedded in the German Basic Law, and central to arguments over American federalism and Swiss cantonal autonomy—operates as a genuine constitutional constraint on central authority or as a rhetorical framework that accommodates whatever distribution of power happens to prevail. One camp, drawing on German constitutional jurisprudence and Swiss direct-democratic practice, treats subsidiarity as a presumption of local competence: higher levels of government may act only when lower levels demonstrably cannot achieve the objective in question, with the burden of proof resting squarely on the center and democratic proximity to the citizen serving as the default value. Another camp, prominent among EU institutional lawyers and American federalism theorists, treats it as an optimization principle: authority should be assigned to the level that best realizes economies of scale, uniformity, or effective problem-solving, with no prior normative presumption favoring decentralization and comparative administrative advantage as the governing criterion. Despite extensive exchange across these literatures, convergence has not occurred; the same institutional examples—the EU’s Protocol on subsidiarity and proportionality, the U.S. anti-commandeering doctrine, the Swiss fiscal equalization scheme—recur without resolution, and each side routinely accuses the other of mistaking a contingent political preference for a constitutional principle. My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not primarily about institutional design or empirical predictions of centralization, but about whether “subsidiarity” names a single normative concept with uniform application conditions or functions as a family-resemblance idea whose criteria shift across constitutional orders, so that one side is asking a question about democratic legitimacy near the citizen while the other is asking a question about allocative efficiency, both under an identical vocabulary. Diagnose this disagreement: is it verbal, substantive, or a hidden empirical dispute about the actual effects of multi-level governance? Do the sides differ about facts, about standards of evidence for “demonstrable inability” at lower levels, about which institutional arrangements count as genuine data, or about starting intuitions—such as whether democratic proximity is a value that needs justification or one that serves as the baseline—neither side can argue for? Is there a concealed formal question, for instance whether a decision-theoretic model of competence-allocation could adjudicate between the burden-of-proof and optimization readings, or does the disagreement reveal that the underlying concept is ill-posed? In answering, engage concretely with what each camp actually says about burden of proof, comparative advantage, and democratic accountability, and specify precisely what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction that separates the questions, an empirical finding about decentralization outcomes, a formal result in multi-level governance theory, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why.
