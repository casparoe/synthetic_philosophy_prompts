I am presently at work on a historical novel in which Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, retained in full possession of his seventeenth-century intellectual temperament, is made to confront the institutional architecture of contemporary scientific publishing. In particular, he encounters a hypothetical mechanism—call it distributed chronometric arbitration—whereby every theoretical claim is instantaneously time-stamped across a decentralized ledger, and an automated protocol assigns exclusive priority to the first entry, treating near-simultaneous or parallel derivations as redundant noise rather than as independent confirmations of an idea's robustness.

He is further told that many philosophers of science now hold that if calculus, or evolutionary theory, or any structurally inevitable result had never been published by its historical discoverer, it would have emerged unchanged from the work of others within a brief interval; from this they conclude that personal attribution is merely a sociological prize, carrying no genuine epistemic or moral significance, since the world receives precisely the same knowledge regardless of whose name is attached. Given Leibniz's documented conviction that individual substances are irreducible centers of force and representation, his theodicean confidence that every rational contribution occupies a unique and necessary place in the best of all possible worlds, and his lifelong insistence that his own calculus notation embodied not mere convention but a deeper transparency of reasoning than Newton's method of fluxions, how do you suppose he would judge this modern conjunction of mechanized attribution and inevitabilist dismissal? Would he interpret algorithmic singularity of credit as a long-delayed vindication of his belief that true discoveries have singular authors, or as a mechanistic parody of that belief that suppresses the plural, convergent paths by which reason actually unfolds? And how, specifically, might he answer the claim that inevitability nullifies the ethical weight of discovery, particularly when the mechanism in question actively erases the second discoverer rather than merely noting the redundancy? I am not seeking bibliographies or source lists—this is for the internal consistency of a fictional portrait—but rather a rigorous reconstruction of the philosophical tensions his position would generate against these present-day assumptions.
