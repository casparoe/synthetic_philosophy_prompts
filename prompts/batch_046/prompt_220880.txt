As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I have been reflecting on what is called the Meno problem: the question of why knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief, since both appear to guide action with equal success. In software verification, this seems analogous to the difference between a test that happens to pass because of coincidental input alignment and a proof that demonstrates why the property holds for all cases. Please describe the most important implications of the principal philosophical answers to the Meno problem—whether they center on justification, the reliability of the belief-forming process, or conditions that exclude accidental correctness—for how one should evaluate technical claims in domains where full verification is expensive or impractical. Specifically, I want to understand whether the distinction between knowledge and true belief matters only for the long-term stability of our belief-forming methods, or whether it also affects the immediate rational weight we should assign to an isolated correct claim, particularly when that claim is produced by an opaque automated system whose reasoning cannot be inspected.
