I am a doctoral student in philosophy and welfare economics, and I have come to defend a particular formulation of pure time preference in intergenerational evaluation: a social planner may apply a constant positive discount rate to future generations’ utility even under certainty and risk neutrality, but only on the condition that the rate derives from a symmetric “centered present” rule rather than from the planner’s own temporal position. On this view, every generation’s present receives full weight from its own standpoint, no generation occupies a metaphysically privileged “now,” and the symmetry is meant to block the usual charge of arbitrary partiality—yet distant future welfare still receives a lower present value. I want an honest map of what would count against this position. Which formal results in social choice or intertemporal consistency—say, about dynamic coherence across generations or the aggregation of differently centered perspectives—would be decisive rather than merely awkward? Does the symmetry condition genuinely eliminate arbitrariness, or does it collapse either into a zero-rate view once we require a single intergenerational social welfare function, or into a standard exponentially decaying model with an ad hoc parameter? I also want to know which defeaters a committed defender of this centered pure-time-preference view would themselves concede, and whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—if every counterexample can be absorbed by refining the temporal perspective parameter, is that insulation a virtue or a fatal flaw for a normative theory of this kind?
