I'm in a grad seminar on consciousness and causation with Prof. Chen and we're supposed to defend our own compatability before critiquing others. Here's my profile, and I'm genuinely unsure where the weak points are. I hold: (a) strict epiphenomenalism—phenomenal properties are causaly inert byproducts of neural dynamics; (b) the paradox of phenomenal judgment is defused by a common-cause account, where the same brain event produces both the quale and the judgment about it; (c) phenomenal judgments are direct and non-inferential, not derived from behavior or neuroscience; (d) phenomenal overflow is real—there's concious content that outstrips access and reportability; (e) we grasp qualia through Russell-style acquaintance, which requires direct causal interaction between the subject and the property known; and (f) phenomenal properties are fully determinate, so any theory that allows vague or indeterminate consciousness is wrong on its face.

My intuition is that (e) and (a) are in real tension, since causaly inert properties can't enter causal acquaintance relations. I also worry that (c) might not sit well with (b): if the brain state causes the judgment and the quale is just along for the ride, how can the judgment be a direct expression of the experience rather than of the neural cause? And I'm not sure whether (d) creates a hidden conflict with (b) and (c) together—if some phenomenology overflows access, then the common-cause mechanism that produces judgments seems to miss it, but maybe that's just a consequence rather than an internal contradiction. Can you audit these commitments pair by pair? Rank any genuine tensions by severety, say for each what the cheapest revision is, and tell me which single change would clean up the most conflicts. Also flag any pairs that only look incompatible but actually aren't, so I don't waste energy fixing them.
