Within discussions of automation and the meaning of work, analysts often conflate two phenomena: functional redundancy, in which technology reproduces the outputs previously produced by labor, and structural redundancy, in which the automation dissolves the social, institutional, and experiential framework within which that labor was originally meaningful—even when human agents remain technically present in the loop. Treating this distinction as operative, I want a defended agenda rather than a survey. For a philosopher or policy researcher with genuinely limited resources—say, one career trajectory or one grant cycle—which unresolved questions in this space are most worth pursuing, and in what order? Consider these candidates: whether structural redundancy can be reversed by organizational redesign without technological rollback; whether meaningful work necessarily requires an irreducible element of friction or resistance that automation tends to eliminate; how to map the logical dependencies between standard productivity metrics and normative claims about human flourishing, so that one can identify which ethical conclusions survive revisions to the economic premises; and whether there is an upstream conceptual issue you think renders the others secondary or misframed. Provide a prioritization that is explicit about whose effort it governs (academic research, policy design, or individual life-planning), the criteria you are using (tractability, downstream dependence, normative urgency, neglect), and which lines of inquiry you would actively deprioritize on the grounds that resolving them would change little in practice. Do not supply bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; defend the ordering purely as a rational allocation of attention.
