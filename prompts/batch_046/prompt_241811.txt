Arrow's impossibility result says that with three or more options you can't have a voting rule that satisfies unrestricted domain, non-dictatorship, pareto efficiency, and IIA, while also producing a complete and transitive social ordering. Unrestricted domain means everyone is allowed to vote and there are no restrictions on candidates or on the kinds of rankings people can submit. Non-dictatorship means there's no single voter whose preferences always become the group outcome. Pareto efficiency means if every voter puts X above Y then the group puts X above Y. IIA means the social ranking between A and B should stay the same regardless of whether C is in the running or not, depending only on individual A-vs-B rankings. This violates the transitivity requirement that arrow builds into his social welfare function, and since pairwise majority can't produce a linear ranking it fails to be a social welfare function at all. Actually wait, IIA is satisfied for each pair in isolation, but the cycle shows that you can't extend the pairwise results to a full ordering without breaking consistency. For tournament democracy: if we allow the intransitive cycle as the final group preference, we still have unrestricted domain, no dictator, and pareto holds. Each pairwise comparison also respects IIA. The only thing missing is the transitivity and completeness of the social ordering. So tournament democracy doesn't violate any of arrow's four named conditions; it just rejects the background assumption that a social choice must be a linear ranking.
