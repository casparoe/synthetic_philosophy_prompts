hey, im working on a short essay about how Darwin and Turing both pulled this weird move where you get really smart-looking results without anyone actually understanding anything. Darwin: natural selection builds eyes without an eye-designer knowing optics. Turing: a machine shuffles symbols by pure syntax and still talks like a person. Both get called "competence without comprehension" but I think people mash them together too fast.

my notes so far:

1. Darwinian competence is spread out — it lives in populations and takes millenia. There's no single "competent" organism that understands evolution; the competence is in the filter.

2. Turing competence is inside one box. The machine doesn't understand, but the rules were designed by someone who does (Turing, programmers). So the comprehension hasn't vanished; it's just relocated to the engineer.

3. Here's where I want to push it: large language models do grammatical stuff without explicit rules (no Turing-style program) and without evolutionary selection (no reproduction/death filter). They're shaped by gradent descent, which is kind of like selection but on weights, not organisms. So they have competence, but neither the distributed Darwinian comprehension nor the relocated Turing engineer-comprehension applies cleanly.

4. So "competence without comprehension" is actually three different things, and using one phrase hides the structure.

Could you read these and tell me: a) is the distributed vs localized distinction actually sharp, or am I inventing it? b) does the LLM point hold up, or is gradent descent just Turing computation in disguise? c) is this interesting or am I just rehashing Dennett? No need for sources, just your gut check on whether the argument has holes.
