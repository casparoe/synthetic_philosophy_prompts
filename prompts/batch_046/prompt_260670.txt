In the course of a philosophy of history seminar, a colleague rejects your proposal that historians ought to weigh recently declassified oral testimony from the defeated party of a long-running conflict equally with official state archives, replying with nothing more than the remark that 'history is written by the victors'. You wish to return with a rigorous disambiguation. Please lay out, as a single continuous piece of prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — rather in the manner of a formal letter — the precisely distinct theses that this slogan could be taken to express. These should range from the trivial claim about physical control of documents and survival of sources, through intermediate positions about whose interpretive framework dominates public understanding and whose voice is granted epistemic authority, to the radical view that historical truth itself is constituted by power relations rather than by any correspondence to past events. For each reading, indicate whether it is defensible in its own right, whether it actually undermines the evidential value of the defeated testimony, and whether it bears on the normative question of whether historians ought actively to seek out and include such testimony. Write entirely in British English.
