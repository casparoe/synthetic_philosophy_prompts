Having recently been appointed to advise a legislative working group on proposals to criminalize non-consensual algorithmic generation of intimate imagery, I have become personally preoccupied with a jurisprudential question that seems to resist the standard harm-principle framework: whether the law may prohibit private conduct solely because it corrodes the collective normative expectations that sustain intimate social trust, even when no identifiable individual suffers a direct injury. This concern has motivated me to design the final examination for my upper-level jurisprudence seminar, which is administered as a 90-minute take-home essay accounting for sixty percent of the course grade and requiring approximately 1,200 words. I want one essay question that engages the Hart–Devlin debate on legal moralism without permitting students to earn high marks by merely labeling Hart's position liberal and Devlin's conservative. Instead, the question should present a concrete scenario—the proposal to prohibit AI-generated synthetic intimate imagery not on the ground that it harms specific victims, which would be a straightforward harm-principle justification, but on the ground that its widespread availability erodes the shared moral expectations necessary for intimate trust—and ask students to determine whether this is a defensible reconstruction of Devlin's argument or a category error that collapses into majoritarian paternalism.

The model answer should be approximately 600 words and must contain four specific moves. First, an accurate reconstruction of Hart's distinction between harm and mere offense, including his reliance on Mill and his critique of those who would extend the Wolfenden Report's framework to cover diffuse social disgust. Second, Devlin's claim that shared morality operates as a form of social ontology—specifically, that moral deviation threatens social cohesion analogously to political subversion—rather than as a mere aggregation of private preferences. Third, an analysis of whether the synthetic-imagery scenario involves an identifiable victim in the sense required by Hart's harm principle, and whether the notion of erosion of collective normative expectations can be incorporated into that framework without collapsing into paternalism or prejudicial majoritarianism. Fourth, a critical evaluation that takes a definite position—either defending a reconstructed Devlinian argument by drawing on resources from collective intentionality theory to show that shared norms can be genuinely injured as collective goods, or demonstrating that any such reconstruction either presupposes an empirically false sociology or violates liberal neutrality—supported by explicit reasoning rather than general assertion.

Please also provide a marking scheme that distinguishes an A answer from a B answer by identifying the precise argumentative moves required for each grade. For an A, the student must explicitly address the social-ontology reconstruction of Devlin and show why it either succeeds or fails to meet Hart's challenge regarding the absence of a concrete victim; for a B, the student may correctly summarize both positions but either neglect the collective-intentionality reconstruction or treat the synthetic-imagery case as an unproblematic instance of diffuse harm without analyzing the structural absence of an individual victim. The scheme should name specific misconceptions that cost marks—for example, conflating Devlin's position with simple democratic majoritarianism, assuming that the harm principle automatically extends to structural cultural damage without independent argument, or treating the AI-generation context as irrelevant to the jurisprudential structure rather than as a stress-test for the victim-identification condition.
