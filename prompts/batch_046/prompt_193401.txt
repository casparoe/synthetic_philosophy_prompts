Exam question:
In discussions of emotional persistence, philosophers sometimes distinguish doxastic withdrawal—the cessation of the corresponding evaluative belief—from affective decoupling, where the emotional state continues to direct attention and motivate behavior even though its original evaluative content has been rejected. Consider a case: a subject walking through an empty hallway suddenly feels intense dread upon seeing a shadow, immediately forms the belief that she is in danger, then recognizes the shadow is only a coat rack and withdraws that belief. Nevertheless, she reports that the dread lingers for several seconds, still oriented toward the coat rack, despite her knowing she is safe.

How might a cognitivist (judgment theory) and a perceptualist respectively explain this lingering affect without abandoning their core commitments? And does this case pose a decisive objection to a pure feeling theory of emotion (as defended by William James)? In your answer, be precise about which view belongs to which theorist, avoid conflating the claim that emotions involve bodily changes with the claim that emotions are judgments about those changes, and explain whether the feeling theorist can accommodate the persistence of intentional directedness after doxastic withdrawal.

Student answer:
On the cognitivist view, associated with philosophers like Martha Nussbaum, emotions just are evaluative judgments. So if the belief that there is danger is withdrawn, the emotion should disappear immediately. If the dread lingers, the cognitivist must say there is a hidden, unconscious judgment still present—perhaps an appraisal that the environment remains unpredictable. This saves the view but makes it unfalsifiable.

The perceptualist, by contrast, treats emotions like perceptions: they represent danger analogically and do not require conceptual content. Just as an afterimage can persist after the light is gone, the emotional system can keep representing the coat rack as dangerous until it recalibrates. This explains why the dread lingers without any lingering belief. However, this also shows that emotions have no intentional content at all, since they persist without any representational object being present.

As for James's feeling theory, James said that an emotion is the feeling of bodily changes—like a racing heart and tense muscles—plus the cognitive interpretation of those changes. Once the body returns to baseline, the feeling stops. In this case, the heart is probably still racing, so the dread remains. Once we recognize that James allows for cognitive interpretation of the somatic state, the feeling theory handles the case easily: the body hasn't settled, and the subject interprets the lingering arousal as directed at the coat rack. So the case does not favor perceptualism; all three views can explain it, though the cognitivist needs an ad hoc unconscious judgment.
