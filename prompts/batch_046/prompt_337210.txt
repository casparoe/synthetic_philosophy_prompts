I am writing a paper for my philosophy-of-cognitive-science seminar on analogical reasoning in tightly constrained microdomains—specifically the Copycat-style letter-string tasks that have served as the standard testbed for theories of fluid concepts. Rather than surveying existing architectures, I want to construct the evaluation framework first: what would a theory of conceptual slippage in such a microdomain actually have to deliver in order to count as adequate?

Please write the desiderata. Cover the phenomena it must explain—analogies where the relational mapping is genuinely ambiguous between two competing structures; cases where a concept’s extension shifts under analogical pressure without collapsing into incoherence; and hierarchical nesting that forces lower-level primitives to be reinterpreted rather than merely reassigned. For each, state the specific case or consideration that makes it a non-negotiable requirement rather than a nice-to-have. Then specify the distinctions the theory must preserve—particularly between representational fluidity and mere error, and between context-sensitive adjustment and full conceptual revision—together with what collapses if each distinction is lost.

Next, identify the cognitive and formal constraints the theory cannot violate, such as working-memory limits on simultaneous mappings and the demand for local coherence even when global consistency fails, and note any empirical findings from analogical-reasoning research that must be accommodated. Crucially, flag any pair of desiderata you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable; I am especially interested in whether a demand for global consistency can coexist with a demand for local adaptive slippage at the primitive-relation level. Indicate which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are negotiable if the purpose is explanatory adequacy in a synthetic microdomain rather than biological realism.

Finally, tell me which single requirement, if enforced strictly, eliminates the largest number of current computational models of analogical mapping, and briefly score the main existing approaches—connectionist, symbolic, and hybrid architectures—against the list, keeping the weight on the criteria and their conflicts rather than on the architectures themselves. A table is fine.

For this exercise, I am extending the standard symbolic microdomain to include graded perceptual primitives—for instance, pitch-contour shapes or color-gradient patterns—where slippage can occur not only in role assignment but in the very identification of the primitive relations themselves. The desiderata should apply to this extended setting without depending on any specific implementation details.
