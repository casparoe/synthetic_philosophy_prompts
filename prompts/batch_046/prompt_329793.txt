In a working group revising competency standards for high-stakes predictive systems, a colleague repeatedly invokes the line: "If a belief is true, it guides action no differently than knowledge; whatever separates them is just internal bookkeeping." She uses it specifically to resist a proposed requirement that operators demonstrate causal understanding of model outputs before deployment, contending that verified predictive accuracy alone should license use. I need the precise philosophical commitments concealed in that sentence separated out, because in our discussion it is sliding between at least three roles: a claim about practical rationality, a claim about the value of explanation, and a claim about institutional liability. Lay out the distinct theses this slogan could be expressing, from the trivial to the radically revisionary, and for each indicate whether it is defensible, whether it genuinely supports dropping explanation requirements, and whether it merely restates the classic Meno problem or introduces an independent argument about functional equivalence. In particular, tell me which reading turns the slogan into a substantive contribution and which readings are platitudes being used to close debate, so that I can bring a disambiguated menu back to the working group.
