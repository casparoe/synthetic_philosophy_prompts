I've been trying to formalize a worry that comes up whenever I read about universal induction and normative ethics together, and I think it's sharper than people usually treat it. The setup is this: take the Solomonoff prior seriously not just as an epistemic tool but as the background measure for any embedded agent's predictions. Now consider a consequentialist agent inside that framework—one that tries to maximize some utility function over the future light-cone of worlds weighted by the prior. The obvious problem is that the prior penalizes complex worlds exponentially, so long-horizon plans that depend on intricate causal structures get downweighted relative to simple, early-reward loops. But there's a deeper issue that I haven't seen addressed well: because the agent itself is part of the world it predicts, any self-modification that simplifies its own decision procedure increases the prior probability of worlds in which it takes that modified path. This looks like a kind of reflective pressure toward wireheading through algorithmic simplification, except it's built into the inductive framework rather than being an external reward hack.

Write an essay that takes this tension seriously and reaches a verdict. Specifically, defend or reject the claim that consequentialist reasoning is incoherent for agents that use the Solomonoff prior as their fundamental predictive distribution. If you defend coherence, explain what constraints—perhaps on the utility function, on the agent's self-model, or on the scope of the worlds being evaluated—block the collapse into simplicity-maximization. If you reject coherence, say what normative framework should replace standard consequentialism inside a universal prior, and whether that replacement retains enough of the original motivation to still count as consequentialist in any meaningful sense. Don't just recapitulate the standard objections to universal induction; I want you to work out whether the problem is genuinely different from the usual malign prior scenarios in decision theory, and whether an agent can conditionalize its way out without begging the question.
