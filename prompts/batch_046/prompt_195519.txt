Here's an argument I sketched for a seminar and now find suspicious because it seems to prove far too much. (1) Friedman's "F-twist" holds that the literal falsehood of a model's assumptions is scientifically irrelevant so long as predictive accuracy is high; unrealistic premises are not a defect. (2) If falsehood carries no scientific cost, then among equally accurate models we should prefer those with maximally false but simpler assumptions, since simplicity is an independent theoretical virtue. (3) So compare climate model A, built on approximate radiative physics, with model B, which assumes CO2 has no thermal properties but includes an empirically fitted global-warming coefficient that reproduces A's temperature predictions exactly. B's core assumption is more radically false, yet B is simpler. (4) By (1) and (2), B is at least as scientifically legitimate as A, and its denial of CO2's radiative role should be treated as harmless idealization. (5) But this is absurd: B cannot support counterfactual policy reasoning—e.g., that cutting emissions slows warming—because its fitted coefficient severs the causal mechanism. (6) Therefore the F-twist must be rejected, and unrealistic assumptions always carry a hidden explanatory cost. Where does this go wrong, if it does? If forced to choose, I'd keep (1) over (5), but I want to know exactly what I'm paying—whether the error is in (2)'s slide from "no cost" to "virtue," in (4)'s application, or somewhere else.
