I advise a clinical-ethics review board that evaluates neuroscience protocols relying on first-person reports of phenomenal experience—studies that ask subjects to rate the “qualitative feel” of awakening after anesthesia, or that use introspective training to detect subliminal stimuli. I want a reusable thirty-minute decision procedure my reviewers can run without philosophical training, one that operationalizes the core illusionist claim that phenomenal vocabulary reports judgments about cognitive accessibility rather than disclosing a non-physical ontology. The checklist should run in this order: first, determine whether the protocol treats phenomenal terms as transparent (direct reference to properties) or as theory-laden judgments; second, check whether the design allows for systematic misreporting without contradiction; third, examine whether the statistical model presupposes a shared phenomenal ontology across subjects. For each step, specify what counts as passing, what counts as failing, and—explicitly—where the reviewer must substitute a normative judgment for a mechanical rule. Then apply the procedure to two protocols: one measuring post-operative “consciousness” through a standardized qualitative interview, and one testing “phenomenal contrast” in blindsight patients via confidence ratings. In each case, show where the procedure blocks the study, where it permits it with conditions, and where illusionist theory underdetermines practice, leaving the board to weigh preserving first-person authority against theoretical parsimony. The deliverable must flag its own misfire points and remain honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed in procedural form.
