As a journalist covering the science of agency and philosophical psychology, I am preparing a feature on whether human flexibility is merely layered mechanical rigidity or requires a genuinely discontinuous capacity for self-revision. To that end, draft the joint statement that two opposing camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The two parties are: (1) the multi-layer determinists, who hold that all apparent cognitive flexibility—including moral deliberation and conceptual change—is nested sphexishness, i.e., mechanical rigidity operating at successively higher orders of abstraction, with “free will” serving as an emergent narrative overlay rather than a causal power; and (2) the frame-breaking libertarians, who hold that genuine agency requires the capacity to rupture what we may call representational sphexishness—the recursive recycling of one’s own interpretive schemas and revision-rules even in the face of sustained disconfirming feedback—without that rupture being fully determined by prior sensory input or algorithmic update rules.

Your deliverable should treat sphexishness not only as behavioral repetition (the classic digger-wasp case) but also as this representational variant, and should address whether a system can revise its own meta-rules from within.

Structure the output as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: include points both sides accept about the formal structure of behavioral and cognitive rigidity; about what needs explaining (e.g., whether a system can alter its own revision conditions absent external perturbation); about what current neuroscience and formal learning theory do and do not rule out; and about the criteria by which a purported “frame-break” should be evaluated. Second, a disagreements table: state the precise formulations of their remaining dispute, with each side’s position phrased in wording that camp itself would accept; identify the cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side; and, for each crux, record the case, experiment, or argument both sides agree would bear on it, with each camp’s advance prediction written down. If you conclude that part of their dispute is verbal rather than substantive, specify exactly which part and why. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the argument must stand on its conceptual structure alone.
