I've been thinking about Von Neumann's idea that the real long-term threat or promise of tech isn't a single big machine but self-replicating systems that can mutate and survive even if humans vanish -- he sketched this out in talks about automata that reproduce like organisms and outlast their creators. I want to read Mary Shelley's Frankenstein through that lens, but not in the tired scientist-plays-God way. Instead, treat the creature's demand for a female companion as a replication protocol, and Victor's refusal as an attempt to kill the reproduction line. Does the novel present the creature as a one-off artifact or as the first unit of a surviving technology? Look at the parts where he learns language and then kills William and Clerval -- are those maladaptive errors of a system trying to adapt, or moral failures that break the analogy? I'm also wondering if the arctic framing (the ship stuck in ice, the creature disappearing north) illustrates Von Neumann's point about technology persisting in environments that kill the maker. I don't need a bibliography or citations, just a sharp reading: where does the idea actually illuminate the plot, where does the novel resist being reduced to an illustration, and what does the book add to the philosophical debate that pure argument doesn't?
