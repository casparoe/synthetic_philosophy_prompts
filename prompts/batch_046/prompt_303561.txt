I was arguing with my friend from the intergenerational ethics course about wether pure time preference is actually a rational attitude or just a cognitive bias we dress up in math. The standard readings feel a bit rote — everyone just repeats Sidgwick's objection and then moves on. I want an exam question for an upper-level seminar (maybe 45 min, 20 marks) that makes students wrestle with a tighter conceptual knot, and I don't want them to just summarize the usual suspects.

Specificaly, I want the prompt to inject this framing: pure time preference treats temporal distance as an intrinsic reason to care less, but if rational planning requires diachronic consistency — treating your future self at t+50 as having the same standing as your present self — then preferring present welfare over future welfare starts looking structurally similiar to preferring your own welfare over a stranger's, except the "stranger" is yourself. The exam should ask students whether this analogy holds, and wether resolving it forces us to either (a) abandon pure time preference as self-alienating, (b) weaken diachronic consistency into something merely instrumental, or (c) reconceive pure time preference as actually about epistemic humility rather than value.

Please draft the essay question, a model answer around 600 words, and a marking scheme that lists five must-hit analytical moves, the marks for each (total 20), and a one-line note on the typical mistake students make at each point. Don't ask for bibliographys or specific paper citations — I want this answerable by someone who hasn't done extra reading beyond the core course materal.
