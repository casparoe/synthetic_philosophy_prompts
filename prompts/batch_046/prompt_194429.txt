You are an expert philosopher of science acting as a consultant to a journal editor. Please think carefully, and begin with a brief brainstorm of the key conceptual distinctions—falsifiability versus actual testability, auxiliary assumptions, conventionalist stratagems, and the boundary between factual failure and value-laden judgment—that must survive in any practical translation.

Then construct a reusable, fifteen-minute decision procedure for a senior scope editor at a science-policy journal who receives roughly ten submissions per week and must decide whether each manuscript genuinely falls within scientific scope according to a falsificationist demarcation criterion corrected for well-known objections. The editor has no philosophical training but must defend each call to non-philosopher reviewers; stakes include reputation and funding eligibility.

Your deliverable must include: (i) an ordered checklist of questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each; (ii) explicit stop points where the procedure returns "outside scientific scope"; (iii) explicit hand-off points where the issue is one of values rather than facts and an editor must decide; (iv) honest flags for steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Be clear about where the procedure will misfire.

Apply the procedure to three worked cases: (a) a parapsychology experiment that discards outlier trials post hoc to protect an ESP claim; (b) an evolutionary-psychology study that explains both high and low cooperation with the same adaptive narrative; (c) a macroeconomic forecasting model with abundant free parameters that retrodicts every past crisis precisely but issues only vague, unfalsifiable predictions—this case should yield an answer the editor will likely resist. Conclude by stating one class of submissions the procedure is structurally unable to settle.
