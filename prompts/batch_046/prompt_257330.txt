I am drafting a chapter on inter-theoretic moral compromise—specifically, whether agents with genuinely incommensurable value commitments can realize gains from trade by exchanging relaxations of internal constraints, without reducing those values to a common metric. To test the structural logic of this “moral exchange-rate” framework, I need a tightly engineered decision scenario. Please construct one, in no more than a single paragraph, that meets the following specifications.

The setting must be realistic: no exotic physics, no perfect predictors or demons, and payoffs that ordinary institutions could actually face. It should involve two collective agents, each governed by one non-negotiable constraint that is incommensurable with the other’s. Agent D operates under a lexical rights-based threshold—for instance, no involuntary redistribution of bodily risk across demographic boundaries. Agent C operates under a relational care-based obligation—for instance, unconditional priority to the worst-off within an existing communal bond, regardless of group membership. They jointly control a scarce, divisible resource (for example, an emergency medical reserve funded by both) and can reach cooperation only if each relaxes its core constraint: D permits a limited, targeted redistribution that crosses its demographic boundary, while C accepts a hierarchical triage rule that violates its unconditional relational priority.

The scenario must be designed so that three frameworks deliver the following verdicts. First, act consequentialism approves the agreement, on the grounds that the relaxation produces the greatest aggregate welfare. Second, a strict lexical deontologist representing D’s internal logic rejects the agreement, because any relaxation of the threshold constraint, however small, destroys the lexical ordering. Third, the moral-exchange framework approves the agreement, but only on the condition that the “cost” of relaxation measured in D’s internal metric equals or exceeds the “gain” measured in C’s internal metric, calculated via a shadow-price translation that does not collapse either value into utility.

After presenting the scenario, please show the reasoning that each of the three positions delivers. Then identify the precise structural feature of the case that causes the exchange framework to peel away from both the consequentialist and the deontological verdicts. Explain what happens if that feature is removed: does the case collapse into a standard multi-criteria optimization problem? Finally, note how a defender of the theory that comes out worst—the lexical deontologist—could attack the scenario as question-begging or unrealistic, and assess whether that attack succeeds. Do not invoke obscure technical apparatus beyond the shadow-price idea already specified, and do not provide bibliographies or citation lists.
