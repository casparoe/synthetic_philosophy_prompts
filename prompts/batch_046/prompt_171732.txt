Two colleagues in my philosophy reading group got stuck arguing over how to apply Wittgenstein's remarks on religious belief. The context was a secular workplace practice they had both read about: employees take part in a scripted "accountability ritual" where they confess professional failures not to seek theological forgiveness, but to reaffirm collective commitment to shared values, with no evidential review permitted of the values themselves. Here is how their exchange went:

(1) He said Wittgenstein's core point is grammatical — religious belief belongs to a language-game with its own rules for doubt and justification, so this ritual works precisely because it does not answer to empirical evidence. Calling it irrational is therefore a category mistake.

(2) She replied that Wittgenstein was describing religious belief specifically, not licensing any system that insulates itself from revision. She said if we extend his framework this broadly, we could justify any dogmatic ideology by relabelling it a language-game.

(3) He came back: she was smuggling in a universal standard of rationality that Wittgenstein precisely rejected. The real question is whether the practice has internal coherence and serves a recognisable human need, not whether it matches scientific norms.

(4) She admitted that different language-games exist, but said Wittgenstein never claimed they are all equally legitimate; he was describing, not prescribing. Without some cross-game criteria for distinguishing meaningful commitment from collective self-deception, she argued, his view collapses into quietism.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what important point both are missing about Wittgenstein's actual texts — for instance, about the relation between grammar and form of life, or about whether his remarks apply beyond religious contexts. Where scholars genuinely disagree over Wittgenstein's intentions, separate settled interpretation from contested claims, and do not present one disputed reading as if it were the consensus. Please answer in British English.
