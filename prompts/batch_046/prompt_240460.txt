I run a 90-minute graduate seminar on cultural heritage ethics with fourteen students. Design an in-class structured negotiation that makes participants feel, before I name it, the "sovereignty gap" in restitution debates: the structural possibility that both the holding museum and the claimant nation-state inherit institutional legitimacy from colonial or imperial orders, so that a formal transfer of an object between them can reproduce the very state-form that enabled its original extraction. Use a hybrid case—part Benin Bronzes, part Parthenon Marbles—where the physical artifact was taken during a period of imperial collapse and now sits in a Western museum, but the "source community" (a non-state traditional authority) and the modern claimant state (the recognized government) disagree over custody and display conditions. Assign specific roles: museum trustees, state cultural ministry officials, representatives of the traditional authority, and a hidden "archival witness" who holds documents showing that both institutional claimants have legal titles derived from the same imperial decree. Give precise negotiation rules—perhaps a three-round protocol with binding votes on custody, display rights, and research access—plus a symbolic payoff scheme (e.g., each role earns or loses "institutional legitimacy points" that affect a collective seminar component). The hidden information should be arranged so that museum-state coalitions naturally form and reach agreement, yet that agreement structurally excludes the traditional authority unless the witness reveals the shared provenance. Include a debrief that maps the coalition moment onto the conceptual problem, predicts what will go wrong the first time (likely premature consensus that feels like resolution), and states frankly which aspect of the sovereignty gap the simulation can reproduce—the institutional mismatch between state and community—and which it cannot—the historical continuity of harm across generations—so I must supply that in discussion.
