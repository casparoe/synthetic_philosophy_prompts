I've been trying to make sense of a tension I noticed in clinical ethics and law regarding advance directives. In many jurisdictions, if a patient with severe dementia wrote an advance directive refusing life-prolonging treatment while still competent, hospitals will honor that refusal even when the present patient seems content and expresses a desire to continue living; the institution treats the document as carrying binding authority over the present self. Yet the same legal system is generally reluctant to enforce past contracts that conflict with present fundamental welfare, and in psychiatric contexts—such as severe anorexia nervosa—clinicians are sometimes permitted to override a patient's present refusal of nutrition on the grounds that the refusal is not genuinely autonomous.

I'm puzzled by what philosophical commitments about personal identity, authority that stretches across time, and the conditions of autonomy are implicitly required to justify this specific pattern. Does honoring the directive presuppose that the demented patient is literally a different person from the one who wrote it, or can it be sustained on a view that maintains psychological continuity but assigns special weight to earlier, more reflective judgments? There is also a notable asymmetry: jurisdictions that honor refusal-directives rarely honor advance requests for active measures like euthanasia, even where contemporaneous requests would be permitted. What does that asymmetry reveal about whether the institution is genuinely committed to the sovereignty of the earlier self, or whether it is instead guided by a harm-avoidance principle projected backward onto a vulnerable present self? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to align with, and indicate whether the same commitments could coherently justify overriding present refusal in other medical contexts.
