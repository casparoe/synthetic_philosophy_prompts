I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of neuroscience examining self-undermining arguments in the free-will literature. Consider the following chain. (1) In Libet-style paradigms, the readiness potential in pre-SMA begins roughly 500 ms before a reported conscious intention to move, and the conscious "W-judgment" occurs only about 200 ms before action, suggesting that voluntary movement is initiated by unconscious neural dynamics. (2) If an unconscious neural precursor is both necessary and sufficient to produce the movement, then the conscious decision is causally redundant—at best an epiphenomenal attribution rather than a free cause. (3) Now apply the same reasoning to cognition itself: neuroimaging of reasoning and belief-formation shows analogous pre-conscious activation preceding conscious insight; if we treat these precursors as determinative in the same way, then the scientist's very judgment that "free will is illusory" is itself fixed by prior brain states, not arrived at through rational evaluation. (4) So the argument, if valid, undermines the rational authority required to accept it—no one could ever be justified in endorsing determinism, since every endorsement is predetermined. (5) Yet this seems to prove too much: we want to deny free will for finger movements without dissolving the normative standing of scientific reasoning. Where does the chain break—should we reject the causal sufficiency claim in (2), distinguish types of mental acts in (3), or deny that determinism of belief-formation undermines justification? If the task is confused—say, if I am illicitly sliding from causal determination to rational incapacity—say so plainly instead of repairing it politely.
