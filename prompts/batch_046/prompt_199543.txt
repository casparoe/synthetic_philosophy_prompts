My colleague works at a firm where senior staff have long volunteered unpaid hours mentoring junior employees—work praised as exceptional but never contractually required. Management recently made participation in this "optional" program the decisive criterion for promotion, so that refusing now carries a severe career penalty. My colleague feels that genuinely voluntary excellence has been captured and converted into a hidden duty, yet she continues mentoring out of concern for the juniors who depend on it. A junior colleague, however, refuses to mentor anyone, insisting that if the labor remains officially voluntary, he should not suffer for opting out—even though he benefits daily from his seniors' unpaid guidance. She asks whether she should keep mentoring in solidarity, withdraw her labor to resist the institutional coercion, or urge the junior colleague to contribute despite his objection. She wants a principled framework for distinguishing authentic voluntary excellence from exploited hidden obligation, and for assessing whether opting out unfairly burdens those who remain inside the system.
