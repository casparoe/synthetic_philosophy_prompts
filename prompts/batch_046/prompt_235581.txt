Scanlon's idea is that an action is wrong if it is justified by principles that others could not reasonably reject, but critics worry that this framework either implausibly blocks aggregating small harms to save one person, or implausibly allows them, depending on how we model the separateness of persons.

I'd like you to analyse the 2008 global financial crisis response—specifically the US government's decision to bail out major banks and insurers through TARP and related Federal Reserve facilities, while allowing Lehman Brothers to fail—as a case study in contractualist reasoning. Treat the situation as one in which policymakers must weigh the claims of taxpayers (each facing a diffuse, relatively small fiscal burden and risk of moral hazard) against the claims of those threatened by systemic collapse (depositors, pension-holders, and the broader economy). Apply Scanlon's reasonable-rejection test: could taxpayers reasonably reject a principle permitting such bailouts? Could those facing catastrophic loss reasonably reject a principle forbidding them? How does the aggregation problem manifest here—does the sheer number of affected taxpayers generate a collective reason that contractualism can recognise, or does the framework force us to assess each person's objection in isolation? Moreover, point out where contractualism seems silent or strained: for instance, regarding probabilistic rather than certain harms, the temporal distribution of costs across generations, and the difficulty of identifying specific contracting parties in a macroeconomic crisis. In your answer, please be disciplined about mapping the actors, options, and evidence onto Scanlon's framework, and say explicitly where the theory offers no clear verdict rather than forcing it to speak. Conclude with a single line beginning 'Verdict:' that states whether, on balance, the 2008 bailout episode is best read as confirming contractualism's sensitivity to aggregation, exposing a limitation, or remaining neutral on the issue.
