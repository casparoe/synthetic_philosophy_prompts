I'm putting together materials for a philosophy-of-science workshop I'm running next month, and my students have been stuck in a circular debate about whether unrealistic assumptions in scientific models are a legitimate methodological tool or essentially a sophisticated form of special pleading. Last Tuesday one of them described the well-known defense—that predictive accuracy alone justifies any degree of ontological absurdity in the premises, regardless of how those premises relate to actual mechanisms—as sounding like something out of a bureaucratic parody, and I realized that writing an actual parody might clarify the conceptual stakes better than another round of seminar discussion. To that end, please write a sustained satirical piece, roughly six hundred words, in the deadpan bureaucratic voice of an official "Author Guidelines for Unrealistic Models" document issued by a fictional but prestigious economics journal named The Journal of Frictionless Exchange. The document should defend, in exhaustive procedural detail, the practice of constructing predictive models on premises that are not merely simplified but physically impossible—economic agents without bodies or spatial location, markets with literally zero transaction costs, preference orderings that are complete and continuous across every metaphysically possible world—while explicitly invoking the logic that predictive success licenses any degree of absurdity in the foundational assumptions. The satire should allow standard objections—who determines what counts as predictive success, whether the model can be tested independently of the data used to calibrate it, and whether the defense quietly collapses into a purely instrumentalist stance—to emerge as logical fissures in the bureaucratic prose rather than being stated outright. I want the tone to remain perfectly straight-faced and institutional throughout, so that the absurdity is generated entirely by the content being defended rather than by any winking at the reader. Please present this as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or other formatting devices, written in the manner of a formal institutional letter; do not include footnotes, bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific texts by page or date, since the piece should stand entirely on its own conceptual logic and be fully accessible to someone reasoning without access to external sources.
