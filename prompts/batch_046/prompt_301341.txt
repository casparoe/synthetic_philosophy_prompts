In constructing characters whose long-term intentions collapse in a single moment, I have kept returning to a stalled dispute I find difficult to map onto human behavior: philosophers treat akrasia as a distinctive failure of rational agency—acting against one’s own best judgment—while behavioral economists absorb the same patterns into hyperbolic discounting, treating them as predictable intertemporal preference reversals. For decades each side has acknowledged the other’s data without conceding its significance, and the exchange feels less like empirical contestation than like two disciplines describing the same storm from incompatible architectures. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not about psychology at all but about epistemic authority: philosophers take the agent’s retrospective report—“I knew it was wrong and did it anyway”—as authoritative first-person testimony, whereas economists treat that report as post-hoc confabulation, no more trustworthy than a novelist’s unreliable narrator smoothing over plot inconsistencies in a serial arc. Is this a verbal disagreement about what “will” denotes, a substantive divergence over which first-person narratives count as data, or a case of two fields answering different questions—one normative, about culpable failure, the other predictive, about choice dynamics—under one shared vocabulary? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually treats as evidence, together with an honest assessment of whether anything—a neuroeconomic result, a formal distinction between kinds of self-report, or a clarification that splits the phenomenon in two—would genuinely move the debate, or whether the stall signals an ill-posed question.
