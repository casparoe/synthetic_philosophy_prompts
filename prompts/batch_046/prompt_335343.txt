Imagine that the Marquis de Condorcet, having survived into the present, is asked to advise a parliamentary committee on the deployment of predictive risk-scoring systems in criminal sentencing—systems that aggregate historical data to forecast individual recidivism and that critics argue encode past structural biases into apparently neutral mathematical procedures. Drawing on his philosophy of indefinite human perfectibility, his insistence that social institutions must be transparent to reason rather than shielded by claims to technical authority, and his suspicion of any arrangement that treats human beings as fixed in their moral or intellectual capacities, how would Condorcet likely assess these tools? Would he regard them as a legitimate extension of Enlightenment rational governance, or as a form of domination that disguises contingent social patterns as natural law? In your response, do not simply invoke his optimism about progress; rather, examine the specific tension between his belief in collective rational improvement and his recognition that institutions can harden into instruments of exclusion.
