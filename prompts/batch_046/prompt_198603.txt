As a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of science systematically, I have been struck by how little the debate over the underdetermination of theory by evidence has moved across decades of discussion. The anti-realist camp maintains that for any body of empirical data there exist empirically equivalent but ontologically incompatible theories, and that scientists' preference for one over another is therefore never rationally compelled by observation alone. The realist camp typically grants the logical possibility but responds that non-empirical virtues—simplicity, unificatory scope, predictive fertility—routinely break the tie in actual practice, rendering underdetermination a philosophers' abstraction rather than a working scientific problem. Both sides have refined their canonical examples, from gravitational dynamics versus dark matter to quantum interpretations with hidden variables versus collapse postulates, yet the exchange remains structurally identical in every graduate seminar I have encountered: the anti-realist produces a pair of empirically equivalent rivals, the realist appeals to theoretical virtue, and neither party concedes that the other has addressed the core issue.

My own working hypothesis, drawn from years of calibrating instruments and revising model parameters, is that the stalemate is partly verbal and partly a case of two different activities being discussed under a single heading. I find it useful to distinguish what I call instrumental adjustment—the routine tweaking of auxiliary hypotheses, boundary conditions, and free parameters within a fixed theoretical framework—from framework substitution, the replacement of the core ontology or law-structure itself. In my laboratory experience, instrumental adjustments were indeed radically underdetermined: one could always preserve a curve-fit by adding a parameter or relaxing an initial condition. But framework substitution—abandoning a classical field ontology for a quantum one, for instance—felt constrained in ways that instrumental tinkering was not, not merely because the new framework was simpler, but because the old framework eventually ceased to generate tractable research questions. I wonder whether anti-realists are describing instrumental adjustment while realists are describing framework substitution, each correctly, and whether the debate therefore stalls because the sides are answering different questions about different practices.

Given this, I want a diagnosis rather than a survey of interpretations. Is the disagreement substantive—are the sides genuinely divided over whether rational theory choice is possible—or is it that they are describing distinct phenomena (instrumental versus structural change) under the one name "underdetermination"? Or is there a hidden formal question doing the work, such as whether any formal criterion can adjudicate between empirically equivalent theories without smuggling in the very ontological commitments that are at issue? Please engage concretely with what each side actually claims about scientific practice, not merely with abstract possibilities. And given your diagnosis, tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that cleanly separates the two activities, an empirical study of how working scientists actually choose between core frameworks, a formal result about the limits of virtue-based selection, or an argument that the question is ill-posed because "theory" and "evidence" are not the appropriate units of analysis? Finally, be honest about whether this is the kind of disagreement that can be resolved, or whether we should expect it to remain exactly where it is regardless of what further distinctions are introduced.
