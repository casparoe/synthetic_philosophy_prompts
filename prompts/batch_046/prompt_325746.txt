I'm developing a view about moral status that treats sentience not as an on-off switch but as a scalar property distributed continuously across biological and even some artificial systems. On this account, moral weight tracks the dimensional complexity and temporal persistence of valenced representational states—roughly, how richly a system integrates negative or positive signals across time and modalities—rather than the presence or absence of phenomenal consciousness. This yields a graded scale: adult humans near the maximum, social insects somewhere in the low-to-mid range with genuinely non-zero but heavily discounted status, simple organisms with minimal nociceptive pathways closer to negligible, and even basic feedback systems like thermostats registering just above absolute zero because they maintain goal-directed error-correction loops with minimal representational content. The scaling is logarithmic rather than linear, so that aggregating millions of insects does not straightforwardly outweigh a single human, yet no sharp threshold divides the sentient from the non-sentient. I arrived at this partly through discussions about whether plant thermal regulation counts as a borderline case, and partly through trying to avoid both the arbitrariness of binary cutoffs and the implausibility of full equality across all life.

What would genuinely count against this position—not just the familiar objections that it makes ethics too calculative or that it disrespects human dignity, but the defeaters that should actually move me? I want the arguments, possible empirical findings, thought experiments, and formal results sorted by how decisive they would be, from fatal to merely awkward. For instance, would evidence that insects lack any form of integrated affective processing destroy the middle of my scale, or could I retreat to a narrower definition? Would a demonstration that scalar moral status collapses into either full egalitarianism or arbitrary line-drawing—perhaps through a sorites-style argument applied to my logarithmic curve—count as fatal, or could I absorb it by refining the metric? I also want to know whether defenders of gradation typically concede certain problems—say, the aggregation problem across populations, or the difficulty of measuring representational dimensionality in practice—and which objections they rightly dismiss as misunderstandings of the view. Crucially, I want an honest assessment of whether I have insulated this view against falsification: have I qualified the metric (valenced representation, temporal integration, logarithmic scaling) so flexibly that no observation could genuinely surprise me? And if so, is that insulation a theoretical virtue—making the view robust—or a vice that renders it empty?

I'd personally be most persuaded by something that undermines the very idea of scalar moral weight rather than just adjusting the numbers—perhaps a conceptual argument showing that moral status must be binary because rights or obligations require a clear subject, or an empirical result showing that the neural architectures underlying human suffering share no meaningful structural continuity with insect nociception or thermostat feedback. But I also want to know the weakest point in the position that I should not hope my interlocutors miss: is it the choice of logarithmic scaling, the attribution of minimal representational content to thermostats, or the assumption that moral weight should map onto representational complexity at all? Please rank the defeaters with that in mind, and say for each whether a committed scalar-gradationist should concede it as damaging or can reasonably deflect it.
