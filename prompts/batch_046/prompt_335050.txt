Next week in my philosophy of artificial intelligence graduate seminar I am presenting a paper that argues “control” and “alignment” are not competing strategies for one problem but conceptually distinct responses to different ontologies of AI systems. I defend the view that containment measures—sandboxing, capability restrictions, tripwires, and adversarial oversight—presuppose the model is or could become a stable counterfactual agent with persistent preferences diverging from ours, so that constraining its behavior is rational. Alignment techniques, by contrast, treat the system as lacking such agential structure and aim to shape outputs through value-loading or feedback. I claim that for large language models this agentic ontology is misplaced: they do not maintain stable counterfactual intentions across contexts, so treating them as misaligned agents to be “controlled” is a category error. The practical upshot is that safety architecture should aim at preventing the emergence of agent-like structures rather than policing an already-agentic system.

The audience is a mixed philosophy-of-technology and AI-ethics seminar: about half are philosophy graduate students with no technical background but strong sympathies for social-constructivist and phenomenological critiques of “AI agency”; two are computer-science students who have read the mainstream AI safety literature and tend to treat misalignment risk as empirically serious; one philosophy-of-mind faculty member is skeptical of attributing intentions to statistical models; and one technology-ethics scholar is wary of “containment” framing because of its authoritarian connotations. They care about conceptual clarity, political framing, and whether philosophical distinctions actually change design practice.
