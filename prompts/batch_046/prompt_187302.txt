I'm preparing the closing session of a seminar on philosophy of art, and I want to end not with a summary but with a piece of verse that actually performs the problem we've been circling all term: the paradox of fiction, specifically how we can suffer genuine emotional responses to entities we know do not exist. Rather than treating the paradox in its usual flat form, I want the poem to inject an additional layer of mediation—treat the object of grief not as a simple fictional character, but as a figure who is herself a fictionalized reconstruction of a historical person, then encountered again through a film adaptation—so that the emotional response is layered across ontological distances without collapsing into mere make-believe. Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a concluding quatrain, with two refrains and two rhymes—using American English and roughly iambic pentameter. The two refrains should carry the opposing poles of the paradox: one voice insisting on the absence and blankness of the page ("She is not there; the page holds only ink"), the other insisting on the presence of the feeling ("Still the grief is real; the eye runs wet"). Through the tercets, let these refrains alternate and circle, never yielding ground, and in the final quatrain let them stand side by side without synthesis. The poem's turn should happen not in argument but in form: by the time the refrains meet, the reader should feel that the tension between knowing and feeling is not resolved but structurally sustained. Make it precise enough that a philosopher of art recognizes the belief-condition on emotion and the problem of ontological commitment, yet strange and compressed enough that it reads as poetry rather than paraphrase. No citations, no bibliography—just the poem.
