A colleague shared a workshop summary that constructs what it calls the "counterfactual stake-contamination argument" against combining pragmatic encroachment with epistemic contextualism. The reasoning is presented in seven steps. (1) Pragmatic encroachment is true: whether a subject S knows that p depends partly on how much practically hangs on p being false for S. (2) For any mundane belief—say, that one's car is still parked on the street—there is a nearby possible world in which the practical stakes are catastrophic, such as a hidden explosive being triggered by any deviation from the car's location. (3) Epistemic contextualism holds that the strength of the epistemic standard required for a correct utterance of "S knows p" is sensitive to the salience of error possibilities in the conversational context. (4) Because nearby catastrophic possibilities are always modally close, they are always available to become salient; hence in any actual context, the conversational standard for "knows" is pushed upward toward the catastrophic threshold. (5) At that inflated standard, ordinary perceptual and memorial beliefs do not qualify as knowledge. (6) Therefore, anyone who accepts both pragmatic encroachment and contextualism is committed to widespread skepticism about ordinary knowledge. (7) The summary concludes that we should reject the combination, and specifically suggests that step (3) misapplies contextualist machinery by treating modal proximity as sufficient for salience.

The summary's author claims that if we block (3) by requiring actual, not merely possible, salience, encroachment survives intact. I want to preserve both theses if I can, but I am unsure whether the error is indeed in (3). Could the problem instead lie in an equivocation in (1) or (4) between the practical stakes that determine whether S knows (a first-order epistemic condition) and the conversational stakes that determine whether an attributor may say "S knows" (a second-order semantic condition)? Or is there a scope ambiguity in (2) about whose stakes are at issue—the subject's or the attributor's? If I reject (3), do I need a full theory of salience, or can I simply distinguish knowledge-conditions from attribution-conditions? If I reject (4) instead, what becomes of contextualism's standard explanation of bank-case intuitions? Please identify the weakest step, explain why it initially seemed defensible, and map out the costs of repairing the argument at each of the other steps.
