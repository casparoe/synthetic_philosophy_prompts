After a long and somewhat frustrating argument with a friend about Arendt's account of Eichmann, I keep noticing a particular argumentative move that I do not have a name for, and I want to know whether the literature distinguishes it precisely. The pattern runs like this. Someone describes a perpetrator of serious institutional harm—say, a logistics official who organizes deportations, or a corporate officer who implements a lethal policy—not as a sadist or a fanatic, but as an unremarkable person who never paused to think about what he was doing. They invoke Arendt's idea of the banality of evil: evil arising not from demonic intention but from a kind of thoughtlessness, a failure to exercise judgment from the standpoint of others. Then an interlocutor objects that this particular figure was in fact deeply committed to an ideology, actively enthusiastic, and clearly operating within a coherent worldview. Rather than conceding that Arendt's framework may not apply, the first speaker shifts ground: they say that the ideology itself is banal, meaning only that it is widely shared and unoriginal, and therefore the evil remains banal in the sense that it requires no unique monstrosity, only ordinary participation in a common belief system. I have seen this happen twice in recent discussions—once regarding a bureaucrat in an authoritarian state who was also a committed party member, once regarding a volunteer in a decentralized movement who genuinely believed in the cause. In both cases, the concept seemed to slide from describing a psychological absence—the inability or refusal to think—toward describing a sociological frequency—the belief is held by many. My question is whether this slide has an established name or diagnosis in Arendt scholarship or in moral philosophy more broadly. How exactly does it differ from Arendt's own distinction between thoughtlessness and ideological conviction, given that she was explicit that Eichmann was not an ideologue? And if there is no established term for this specific drift, can you define it precisely, specify its boundary conditions—does it require a defensive rhetorical context, or can it occur in analytical writing as well—and distinguish it both from a straightforward misreading of Arendt and from the separate error of treating all widespread beliefs as morally insignificant?
