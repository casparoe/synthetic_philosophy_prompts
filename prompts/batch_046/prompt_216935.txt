As a retired marine biologist who has recently begun studying philosophy, I serve on the ethics review panel of a research foundation that oversees both laboratory and field studies involving non-human animals. We are receiving an increasing number of proposals that invoke welfare justifications—sometimes to defend invasive procedures, sometimes to defend lethal population management—yet our committee lacks a reusable framework for deciding when a protocol genuinely respects the anti-speciesist principle that moral status should track capacities for suffering and flourishing rather than taxonomic membership, and when it instead smuggles in unjustified discrimination masked as scientific necessity. I therefore need a formal decision procedure that my fellow panel members—scientists and administrators, not philosophers—can run in under forty minutes per proposal.

The procedure should translate a capacity-based scalar account of moral status, combined with an epistemic constraint that we must neither attribute suffering without structural and behavioural analogues to known suffering states nor withhold protection where such analogues exist, into an ordered sequence of questions. For each question, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure issues a clear stop (rejection, approval with conditions, or referral), and where it must hand the decision to a person because the issue is one of values rather than facts. The procedure must handle hard cases: protocols involving taxa with poorly understood nervous systems (for example, cephalopods or decapod crustaceans) and protocols where individual welfare conflicts with ecosystem-level preservation.

Apply the procedure to two worked examples. First, a proposal to conduct invasive electrophysiological recordings in the central brains of captive octopuses to map nociceptive analogues. Second, a proposal to cull an established population of invasive grey squirrels using anticoagulant methods in order to protect a declining native red squirrel population and associated woodland ecology. One of these applications should yield an answer that conflicts with the intuitive preference of a conservation-minded scientist; explain why the procedure produces that result.

Conclude by stating explicitly what the procedure cannot settle—such as trade-offs between individual and collective goods when capacities are evenly distributed across taxa—and flag any steps that remain judgment calls dressed in procedural form. Please write in British English, do not include any bibliography or reading list, and avoid quotation marks around passages taken from philosophical texts.
