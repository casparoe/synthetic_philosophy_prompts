Analyze the 1973 Rosenhan pseudopatient study and the 1980 DSM-III psychiatric reform as a single episode in the philosophy of mental health, read through Wittgenstein’s private language argument. In the study, eight sane individuals presented themselves at psychiatric hospitals reporting a single fabricated auditory hallucination—hearing voices saying “hollow,” “empty,” and “thud”—and were admitted with schizophrenia diagnoses, remaining hospitalized for an average of nineteen days despite behaving normally thereafter; staff documented their ordinary conduct as “pathological writing behavior” or “symptom-free,” while other patients, not clinicians, sometimes recognized them as sane, and upon discharge all received the label “schizophrenia in remission.” The DSM-III response, led by Robert Spitzer, replaced psychoanalytic etiology with explicit operational criteria—observable symptom checklists, duration thresholds, and exclusion rules—intended to make diagnosis inter-subjective and independent of theoretical interpretation.

Using Wittgenstein’s claim that a language describing purely private sensations is impossible because there can be no criterion of correctness beyond the speaker’s own impression, examine: (1) whether the pseudopatients’ initial report of an auditory hallucination functions as a “private ostensive definition” that clinicians have no independent access to correct; (2) whether the DSM-III checklist solves this by substituting public behavioral criteria for private mental reports, or whether it merely displaces the privacy problem—since the patient still privately reports whether a symptom is present, and the checklist relies on that first-person access to populate the public form; (3) what the fact that fellow patients, rather than trained staff, detected the pseudopatients’ sanity implies about Wittgenstein’s “community” condition for rule-following and criterion-fixing; and (4) whether the episode ultimately supports Wittgenstein’s thesis or exposes a gap—psychiatric diagnosis may require public criteria, but those criteria remain anchored to reports whose content is, in principle, accessible only to the reporter.
