I have been observing a recurring configuration in the history and sociology of scientific fields—particularly in physics and parts of cognitive science—where the formal apparatus of a theory remains nearly intact, including its core equations, experimental protocols, training regimes, and citation networks, but the community gradually reinterprets the ontological status of every central term. Practitioners begin to insist that they are now asking fundamentally different questions than their predecessors did with the same mathematics, and they frame the change as a Kuhnian paradigm shift involving incommensurability. Yet in practice, peer review, pedagogy, and collaborative research proceed without any detectable translation failure; textbooks are often revised only in their introductory or philosophical chapters, and senior researchers train students in identical calculational techniques without flagging a linguistic rupture. The old and new "paradigms" share notation, share referees, share predictions, and share laboratories, but claim to inhabit incompatible conceptual worlds. I want to know whether this specific pattern—radical ontological redescription coupled with complete formal, communicative, and predictive continuity—has an established name in philosophy of science. Is it recognized as a distinct subtype of paradigm change, perhaps a semantic or interpretive shift that mimics Kuhnian structure without delivering genuine incommensurability? How should it be precisely distinguished from authentic taxonomic incommensurability on one side, and from ordinary theoretical refinement, instrumentalist reinterpretation, or mere metaphysical gloss on the other? If no established term exists, I would like a precise coinage together with a definition that specifies its boundary conditions—especially the criteria that determine when an ontological redescription has crossed into this kind of pseudo-paradigm shift rather than remaining within a single continuous research tradition.
