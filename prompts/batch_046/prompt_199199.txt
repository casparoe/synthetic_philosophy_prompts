I am preparing a 90-minute session for a rationalist-adjacent philosophy workshop—twelve participants, mostly graduate students in philosophy, cognitive science, and law—on practical reason under value pluralism. I need an in-class simulation that makes participants feel, rather than merely discuss, what genuine incommensurability is: not just that weighing is hard, but that some goods resist translation onto any common metric without conceptual betrayal. Do not open with a lecture; do not name the idea—whether “hard choice,” “incommensurability,” or “value pluralism”—until the final debrief.

Design a cooperative-charter game. Split participants into three groups of four. Each group must draft a binding allocation charter for a hypothetical intentional community that distributes three goods—autonomy, security, and recognition—under fixed scarcity. Assign each group a hidden meta-constraint that they must embed in their charter but not reveal: Group A must maximize an aggregate score across all allocations; Group B must observe a lexical priority that forbids trading dignity-based recognition claims for gains in welfare or security; Group C must reach unanimous consent on every allocation, with each member holding veto power over any trade affecting their assigned primary good. In Round One (20 minutes), groups draft charters without knowing the others’ meta-rules. In Round Two (30 minutes), reveal all charters and apply them jointly to three concrete cases—e.g., a member requests exemption from collective labor for artistic production that yields no collective benefit; a security protocol requires transparency that undermines personal autonomy; a recognition claim demands differential treatment that conflicts with equal distribution. The conflict must emerge from the rules themselves: when Group A proposes a weighted scoring function, Group B should reject the very possibility of weighting recognition; when Group C seeks consensus, veto should deadlock not from selfishness but from the recognition that conceding on one’s primary good is not compensable by gains elsewhere.

Specify internal roles (charter drafter, case adjudicator, silent observer who logs every attempt at cardinal translation), a symbolic payoff scheme—reputation tokens determining speaking order and amendment rights in a final round, with no real money involved—and a precise timeline with transitions. The debrief should map specific breakdown moments—where weighting was proposed, where veto was exercised, where unanimous consent collapsed—onto the conceptual distinction between ordinal ranking, cardinal comparison, and non-compensable plural values. Be frank about what the simulation cannot reproduce: likely the first-person phenomenology of being genuinely torn between incomparable goods, and tell me exactly how I should supply that through targeted facilitator questioning without breaking the simulation’s internal logic.

Also predict the failure mode: participants will likely smuggle implicit cardinal weights into their charters despite instructions, or the lexical-priority group will dominate discourse; explain how to correct this without invalidating the experience. Write in a formal, polished register consistent with a longtime reader of rationalist forums. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. If you reference thinkers such as Ruth Chang or Joseph Raz, do so only at the conceptual level, without attributing specific formulations to particular works unless you can verify the attribution with a direct source.
