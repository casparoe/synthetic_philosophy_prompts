I need a reusable decision procedure I can run in under thirty minutes per briefing, without statistical software, to test whether a causal claim drawn from comparative history is strong enough to support a policy recommendation. Ground the procedure in the actual logic of historical causal inference: the distinction between explaining cross-sectional variance across countries and explaining change over time within them; the problem of endogenous institutions; the requirement of counterfactual stability for a mechanism claim versus a mere correlation claim; and the difference between identifying a necessary condition in a sequence and identifying a sufficient cause. For each step, state the diagnostic question, what counts as a passing answer, where the procedure stops with "do not recommend," and where it must hand the decision to a person because the issue is normative rather than evidentiary. Include no more than five concrete tests I can apply directly to a briefing's argument. Then apply the procedure to three briefings: (1) one arguing that inclusive political institutions caused British industrialization and should be promoted in low-income countries; (2) one arguing that malaria ecology explains agricultural productivity gaps and that vector control alone will close them; (3) one arguing that a specific cultural orientation toward time preference explains East Asian savings rates. Design the procedure so that at least one case yields a recommendation I will dislike if I am institutionally inclined. Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot do—especially whether it can ever settle causal debates that depend on unobservable counterfactual national histories—and flag every step that is a judgment call dressed up as a check.

1. Practitioner constraints and stakes (~60 words)
