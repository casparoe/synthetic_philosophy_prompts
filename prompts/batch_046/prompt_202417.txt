Since taking over coordination of emergency housing in my municipality six months ago, I have worked directly with families displaced by conflicts and environmental collapses that do not clearly fall within the 1951 Convention's persecution-centered definition of refugee status. This has made the philosophical debate about admission obligations personally urgent rather than abstract. Write an essay addressing this controversy: should moral duties to admit displaced persons extend beyond the legal category of refugee to include those fleeing generalized violence, climate-induced deprivation, or state failure, and—which states bear the primary obligation—those of first arrival, those with the greatest absorptive capacity, or those whose foreign or economic policies contributed to the displacement? Weigh the cosmopolitan argument that borders are morally arbitrary against the democratic particularist claim that citizens have a legitimate priority in shaping collective membership, and also examine whether proximity generates a special obligation that capacity or causal responsibility cannot override. Crucially, assess whether the international legal distinction between refugee status and complementary protection tracks a real moral difference, or whether it arbitrarily privileges state-targeted harm over other forms of severe vulnerability. Reach and justify a definite conclusion: specify both the proper scope of admission obligations and the correct principle for distributing them among states, and explain why the alternatives you reject fail.
