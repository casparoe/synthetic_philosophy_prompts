I am writing a long-form feature for a general science audience on how Zeno's paradoxes are routinely misread in popular physics writing. The standard account holds that calculus resolved all of Zeno's arguments by proving that an infinite sequence of intervals can sum to a finite distance, thereby demonstrating that motion is mathematically possible. Yet this explanation clearly addresses the dichotomy paradox—the problem of traversing infinitely divisible space—while leaving the arrow paradox essentially untouched, since the arrow concerns whether an object can genuinely be in motion during a durationless instant, not whether it can complete an infinite number of discrete steps. Recently, I have been examining a minority position in the philosophy of quantum gravity, sometimes called temporal atomism or the chronon hypothesis, which proposes that time is not a mathematical continuum but is composed of minimal, indivisible units. Advocates suggest that this dissolves the arrow paradox by removing the very notion of a point-sized present in which the arrow could be frozen. Here is where my reasoning seems to snag: if discrete time eliminates the continuous instants that Zeno used to construct his reductio, does this not vindicate Zeno's reasoning rather than defeat it? He argued, after all, that motion is incompatible with time composed of point-like nows; discrete ontology denies that such nows exist. So if contemporary physics favors temporal discreteness, hasn't it actually confirmed Zeno's diagnosis of continuous motion, even while it rejects his conclusion that motion is therefore impossible? I want to understand whether this framing—treating temporal atomism as a vindication of Zeno—is a subtle category mistake, and whether the familiar calculus narrative is not merely incomplete but conceptually backward. Can you untangle the confusion, explain precisely where the mapping between discrete ontology and Zeno's premises breaks down, and clarify whether the arrow paradox is best understood as a problem about instantaneous velocity, about the ontology of time, or about something else entirely?
