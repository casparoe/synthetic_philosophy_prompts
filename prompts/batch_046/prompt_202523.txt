I was recently discussing Pluto’s demotion with friends over dinner, and one person insisted the entire 2006 IAU controversy was essentially a dictionary fight—scientists just rewrote the rules, Pluto stayed exactly the same, and everyone should have stopped caring. That struck me as too quick, but it also made me wonder whether philosophers are right that some big disagreements about what exists are really just disagreements about how to use words. I have been trying on my own to understand the idea—sometimes discussed under metaontology—that disputes over categories can be merely verbal, meaning the parties share all the same facts about the world and only differ in linguistic convention. I want to test that idea against the Pluto episode, not as a science question but as a conceptual case study.

Specifically, I would like you to analyze the 2006 International Astronomical Union redefinition and the subsequent backlash—particularly from planetary scientists who rejected the new orbital-clearing criteria—as an instance of either a verbal dispute or a substantive ontological disagreement. Who exactly were the players: the IAU voting members, the dissenting astronomers, and the public? What were the actual options on the table—redefining planet by orbital dynamics, retaining Pluto by historical convention, or abandoning the category altogether? How should we understand the IAU’s authority: were they exercising a kind of stipulative authority to legislate meaning, or were they attempting descriptive tracking of a natural kind boundary in the solar system? And what does the fact that Pluto’s physical properties remained constant while its classification changed tell us about whether anyone was genuinely wrong about Pluto’s nature?

Then, turning the analysis back on the theory itself: does the Pluto case confirm that verbal disputes can generate genuine institutional and emotional stakes, which suggests that labeling them merely verbal misses something important about scientific practice? Or does it reveal that scientific taxonomy is never purely descriptive, and that the metaontological distinction between language-choice and world-tracking collapses in practice? I am not asking for a literature review or citations; I am asking for a disciplined mapping—who argued what, where the verbal-substantive distinction illuminates the conflict, where it is silent, and whether the episode ultimately supports or challenges the view that ontological disagreements are often just hidden linguistic conventions.
