I'm leading a single ninety-minute session for philosophy undergraduates who have taken introductory logic but have no training in modern European history. I want them to grasp not the Fischer debate as historiography but the prior conceptual problem that makes it intelligible: what it means to hold "Germany" responsible for the First World War as a collective agent rather than distributing fault among individual ministers. To do this I intend to introduce a distinction that is not standard in textbooks—between "structural blame," which targets a state's decision architecture for producing an outcome regardless of any single actor's intent, and "act blame," which targets a specific intentional choice—and to use Fritz Fischer's argument about the September Programme and the Bethmann-Hollweg war-aims memorandum only as a stress-test for that distinction, not as evidence to be evaluated. The carrying example must be the July 1914 "blank check" to Austria-Hungary, but I want students to see that calling it "Germany's choice" already presupposes a theory of collective agency rather than describing a neutral fact. I anticipate three predictable misconceptions: that collective responsibility is just summed individual guilt; that showing leaders held divergent motives dissolves national responsibility; and that causal contribution from other powers divides blame like a pie. I need a plan for the explanatory sequence: which component must be in place before the blank-check example can do its work, in what order the three misconceptions should be anticipated and defused, and exactly where in the arc each one should be headed off. I also need one closing question that reveals whether students understand national responsibility as a conceptual construction rather than a historical finding, and I want explicit guidance on which part of the Fischer controversy—say, the weight of public opinion versus elite decision-making—I should refuse to adjudicate at this level and openly say so. Please conclude with a final line beginning "Teaching plan verdict:" that states, in one short phrase, the core pedagogical dependency that holds the session together.
