Two people in my study group, J and R, got into it about legal positivism vs natural law and I need an outside take.

J said imagine a city passes a clear, non-retroactive ordinance: "No vehicles over 3 tons on Bridge St." Its published everywhere. But the police have an internal, never-published manual that says enforce only against commercial trucks, never private RVs, and only when the officer feels like it. A private RV driver gets fined; he argues in court that this isnt really law because Fullers right—theres no congruence between the declared rule and official action, and citizens cant predict outcomes. J thinks Fuller wins: the ordinance lacks the internal morality of law, so its just arbitrary power wearing a legal costume.

R pushed back hard. She said Harts separation thesis handles this fine: the ordinance is law because the legislature enacted it and courts recognize it. The secret manual and selective enforcement are moral or political failures—bad governance, maybe corruption—but they dont erase legal status. R said Fullers eight principles are efficiency conditions or ideals, not conceptual requirements. If you make them necessary for lawhood, you cant condemn evil legal systems as "not law," which was Fullers point with the Nazi informer case but Hart thought that was conceptually confused.

Then J came back: but Harts own minimum content of natural law admits law needs basic rules for social survival. Fullers principles just extend that idea to form—if rules arent constant, clear, or congruent, they cant guide behavior, so they fail to do what law is conceptually for. R said thats functionalist sleight-of-hand: youre defining law by its purpose, but Hart defines it by social sources. A broken clock is still a clock; a badly enforced law is still law.

J then asked: what about promulgation? The manual is secret, so citizens dont know the real rule. Doesnt that break even Harts idea that law guides conduct? R said Harts rule of recognition is for officials identifying law, not for citizens being guided—guidance is a virtue, not a condition.

Neither woud budge. Can you go through their exchange round by round and say whos right about each specific claim? Whats the best argument each misses, especially about whether Fullers internal morality does real conceptual work or just moralizes in legal clothing? And dont just say it depends—I want to know where each side actually collapses into the other.
