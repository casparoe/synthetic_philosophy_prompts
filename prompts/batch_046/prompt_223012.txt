I'm preparing material for a graduate seminar on postwar moral philosophy, and I'd like you to read Bernhard Schlink's novel The Reader through Karl Jaspers' fourfold taxonomy of guilt—criminal, moral, political, and metaphysical—as developed in his reflections on German collective responsibility. The novel's central figure is Hanna Schmitz, a former SS guard tried for war crimes who conceals her illiteracy even when that concealment worsens her sentence, and Michael Berg, the younger narrator who discovers her secret but remains silent during the trial. Rather than treating the novel as a mere illustration, I want a reading that asks where Jaspers' framework illuminates Hanna's choices—particularly whether her illiteracy diminishes moral culpability without erasing political guilt as a citizen of the Reich—and where the text resists that mapping, especially given that Michael's own silence raises the question of inherited or witnessed guilt.

In particular, I want you to pursue an interpretive angle that treats Hanna's illiteracy not as a psychological quirk but as a structural form of epistemic incapacity: if she could not read the orders she executed or the texts that might have awakened her conscience, does that place her guilt primarily in Jaspers' metaphysical register—the failure of human solidarity—rather than in the moral register of conscious wrongdoing? Examine specific scenes: the trial's revelation of her literacy and its effect on the court's judgment; Michael's prison correspondence, in which he reads to her on tape and thereby becomes an unwitting accomplice to her self-deception; and his later encounter with the daughter of one of the church-fire victims, where the possibility of forgiveness is tested. Does the novel ultimately suggest that metaphysical guilt can attach even when cognitive access is impaired, or does Schlink implicitly challenge Jaspers by introducing a category of culpability mediated by literacy and education that the philosophical taxonomy does not capture?

Please separate what is philosophically settled—such as Jaspers' basic distinctions and the broad consensus that collective political guilt does not require personal commission of crimes—from what remains genuinely contested, including whether metaphysical guilt requires conscious agency, whether political guilt can be inherited by subsequent generations, and whether epistemic incapacity can ever excuse moral failure. Do not present any contested interpretation of Jaspers or of the novel as if it were the consensus view, and avoid recommending readings or bibliographies. Finally, tell me what this literary treatment adds to the philosophical debate about collective guilt that a purely argumentative essay on Jaspers could not achieve on its own.
