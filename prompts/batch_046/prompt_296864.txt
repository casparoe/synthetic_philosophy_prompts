Here is the exam question we assigned: A biologist builds a predator-prey model that assumes prey animals never learn from near-misses and that predators never tire. Critics say these are wildly unrealistic. The biologist replies that, following Milton Friedman's F-twist, the very unreality of the assumptions is a virtue: if the model still predicts population cycles accurately, it proves that learning and fatigue are causally irrelevant. Evaluate this defense.

Here is one student's answer: The biologist is basically right. Friedman said that unrealistic assumptions are good because they show a theory is powerful. In his 1953 essay The Methodology of Positive Economics he argued that the more unrealistic the assumptions, the stronger the theory, as long as it predicts well. This is the F-twist, which Friedman named himself. So if the predator-prey model predicts well despite assuming no learning and no fatigue, then those factors really do not matter in nature — they are not real causes of population cycles. The critics care about realism, but Friedman showed that realism does not matter at all. In fact, as Friedman put it, all models are wrong but some are useful, which is why George Box agreed with him. The biologist has therefore proven that learning and fatigue can be ignored in ecology.
