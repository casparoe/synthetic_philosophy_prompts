After a long and circular argument with a close friend about whether the categories "Boomer," "Millennial," and "Gen Z" denote real kinds or are merely marketing constructions, I want to understand the structure of our impasse rather than adjudicate it. We both accept the same demographic and economic data—birth-rate distributions, labor-market entry conditions, and technology-adoption curves—and we both concede that people born near one another sometimes share attitudes and consumption patterns. My friend nevertheless treats these cohorts as ontologically on par with biological species or chemical elements: they believe there is a mind-independent causal structure that makes someone born in 1992 genuinely share a nature with others born in that window. I counter that the grouping is an institutional artifact that acquires only a derivative reality through looping feedback: once a label is widely circulated, individuals begin performing the attributed traits, generating statistical regularities that are then misread as natural joints. Our disagreement has persisted for months without either side conceding a factual error, which makes me suspect we are not disputing empirical predictions but rather what counts as a legitimate ontological commitment. Is this a verbal disagreement about the meaning of "real kind"—whether it requires causal homogeneity, predictive utility, or independence from labeling practices—or is there a hidden substantive dispute, perhaps about the formal identifiability of age-period-cohort effects, that we have failed to articulate? Could it also involve bedrock intuitions about naturalness that neither side can justify, or are we answering different questions—one about demographic explanation, the other about political rhetoric—under a single name? I want a diagnosis supported by what each position actually implies, together with an honest assessment of whether anything—an empirical finding, a formal result, or a conceptual distinction—could actually move the debate, or whether the question is ill-posed because "generation" serves incommensurable explanatory roles in sociology, marketing, and cultural criticism.
