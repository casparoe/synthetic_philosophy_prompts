I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on metaphysical harm and the ethics of death, and I'm preparing a session for a graduate seminar on whether death can be a misfortune for the one who dies. I want a poem that does serious philosophical work rather than merely illustrating a familiar thesis. Write a double villanelle—two interwoven villanelles, A and B, each with its own pair of refrains—structured so that the form enacts the dialectic. For villanelle A, let refrain A1 be the Epicurean claim that death is not harmful because there is no subject present to suffer it, and let refrain A2 be the symmetry argument that prenatal non-existence is not a harm, so postmortem non-existence should not be either. For villanelle B, let refrain B1 be the deprivation-account claim that death removes future goods that would have belonged to the living person, and let refrain B2 be the objection that goods not experienced by an existing subject cannot be "owned" in the way required for harm. Arrange the stanzas so that the A-refrains dominate the opening, the B-refrains dominate the middle, and in the final quatrain all four refrains appear together, with the rhyme scheme forcing B1 to rhyme with A2 and B2 to rhyme with A1. This rhyme pairing should structurally suggest that the symmetry and deprivation arguments are not independent objections but mirror-images that collapse into each other if we deny the dead a metaphysical status that we simultaneously deny to the never-born. The poem should be accurate enough that a specialist in the philosophy of death recognizes the specific tension between the experience requirement for harm and the time-relative-interests framing, without either view being named or attributed; do not include bibliographic references, reading lists, or citations. Use American English, keep the language precise but not jargon-heavy, and make sure the poem stands as a good poem for someone who does not know the debate.
