I'm drafting questions for a podcast interview with a philosopher who has just published a book defending what she calls an "intentional anchoring" revision of Searle's Chinese Room argument. She holds that syntax never becomes semantics not from insufficient complexity, but because genuine semantic content requires an intrinsic "aboutness-direction"—a direct mental-state-to-object relation that only biological, causally embedded systems can possess. Applied to large language models, she argues they display "semantic mimicry without intentional anchoring": they manipulate symbols carrying derived meaning for users and designers, yet lack the directional link that would make any internal state genuinely about anything. She anticipates the systems reply by insisting that adding weights, training histories, or sensorimotor interfaces only produces more elaborate syntax—more derived intentionality, never intrinsic aboutness.

Write me a sharp, colloquial-but-precise sequence of five to seven interview questions that press her on the actual content of this view, not biography. Specifically: (1) ask her to distinguish her "anchoring" requirement from biological chauvinism, anticipating her reply that brains are just unusually good computers, and include your immediate follow-up; (2) ask whether her view entails that any non-biological system is necessarily a semantic zombie, and whether she embraces that consequence; (3) confront her with LLM behavior—error correction, long-range contextual tracking, and norm-based refusal—asking whether that functional profile just is understanding regardless of anchoring, and include the deflection she is likely to use plus the push-back to it; (4) ask why derived intentionality, which is real for artifacts like maps and sentences, cannot become structurally indistinguishable from intrinsic intentionality in a sufficiently complex system; and (5) close by asking what specific empirical result or conceptual argument would convince her that functional organization can generate intrinsic aboutness, and where she thinks her own reasoning is weakest.

After the questions, include a brief self-critical note: say what kind of reply to question (3) or (4) would lead you to think the anchoring distinction collapses, and identify the weakest point in the line of questioning you have constructed.
