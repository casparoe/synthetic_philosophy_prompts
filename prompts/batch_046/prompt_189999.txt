Two colleagues, Mira and Jonas, argued over a university endowment that could fund either the digitization of an endangered oral-language archive or a pilot climate-adaptation technology. Both agreed the goods at stake—cultural continuity and ecological survival—are genuinely incommensurable, with no common metric.

Mira said that if values are truly incommensurable, practical reason is silent between them; the choice should therefore be settled by a fair procedure such as lot or democratic vote, not by reasoning that pretends to weigh them. Jonas replied that incommensurability does not entail incomparability: we can rationally choose via covering values like justice or human flourishing, or through Ruth Chang’s idea of parity, where hard choices generate reasons without reducing the options to being better, worse, or equal.

Mira countered that invoking a covering value merely smuggles in a hidden common scale—if justice applies to both projects, it functions as the metric that was supposed to be absent—so the incommensurability claim was abandoned, not respected. Jonas answered that comparison need not be metric; parity allows rational resolution through reasons that do not reduce either value to the other, so practical reason is not silent but operates non-reductively. Mira then argued that generating reasons ex nihilo in cases of parity is not rational discovery but preference construction disguised as justification, and that without some prior comparative ground the appeal to parity collapses into arbitrariness masked as reason.

They turned to me as a bystander and asked me to referee. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: assess Mira’s silence-of-reason claim, Jonas’s appeal to covering values and parity, Mira’s smuggling objection, Jonas’s non-reductive comparison defense, and Mira’s arbitrariness charge. Say who is right about what at each stage, indicate who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both are missing about how incommensurable values function in institutional decision-making.
