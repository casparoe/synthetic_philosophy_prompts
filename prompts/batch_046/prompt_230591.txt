I'm working through an essay for my philosophy of mind seminar on whether emotions are fundamentally judgments of value, perceptions of bodily change, or non-intentional qualitative feelings, and a recent discussion with classmates left me convinced that neuroscience has made the philosophical taxonomy obsolete. Specifically, I've been examining an embodied predictive-processing account according to which emotional consciousness arises from a two-level architecture: first-order interoceptive predictions about allostatic bodily states, and second-order meta-predictive confidence signals—essentially the brain's precision-weighted estimate of how reliable those first-order predictions are. On this view, what we call fear or grief involves not only a representational evaluation of threat or loss at the first level, but also a modulatory signal that determines the phenomenal intensity and motivational urgency of that evaluation.

Given this framework, my confusion is whether the traditional debate between judgmentalism, perceptualism, and feeling-theory has been asking the wrong question by treating these as competing accounts of a single phenomenon, rather than as descriptions of different hierarchical levels within one predictive architecture. If the first-order prediction carries intentional content about value and danger—suggesting a judgment or perception—while the second-order confidence signal governs qualitative salience and affective tone—suggesting something like a primitive feeling—then isn't every emotional episode simultaneously all three, just distributed across different layers of inference? Or is there a principled philosophical reason to insist that only one of these levels constitutes the emotion proper, with the others functioning merely as causal preconditions, neural implementations, or phenomenal accompaniments that philosophy should bracket?
