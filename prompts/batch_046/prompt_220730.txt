I'm currently enrolled in an upper-level seminar on distributive justice, and after a session on aggregation, a friend and I tried to formalize the separateness-of-persons objection in a way that doesn't merely appeal to intuition. We constructed what seemed like a tight argument, but the conclusion is that either we accept full-blown utilitarian aggregation across strangers or we must deny the rationality of saving for retirement—an outcome neither of us wants. Here is the chain, and I need help locating the leak.

(1) The separateness objection holds that benefits and burdens belong to distinct containers—individual lives—and there is no further container, such as the social whole, to which they can legitimately be summed. Cross-personal aggregation is therefore a category mistake.

(2) If that is correct, then moral evaluation should be restricted to intrapersonal comparisons: we can ask whether Alice's life is better than her own alternative lives, but not whether Alice's gain morally outweighs Bob's loss.

(3) Now consider prudential rationality: my present self and my future self are also distinct psychological entities, linked only by relations of memory and causal continuity—relations structurally analogous to the social relations that link Alice and Bob.

(4) If separateness is grounded in the absence of a single subject of experience spanning both parties, then my present and future selves are equally separate containers; there is no super-self that aggregates welfare across my life-stages.

(5) Therefore, if cross-personal aggregation is illegitimate because there is no encompassing subject, intrapersonal aggregation must be illegitimate for exactly the same reason.

(6) But denying intrapersonal aggregation makes prudence irrational: I should be indifferent between sparing my future self catastrophic harm and avoiding a present minor inconvenience, since the harm and the benefit sit in different containers with no common sum.

(7) Since (6) is unacceptable, the reasoning in (5) must fail, which implies that (1) is false and that aggregation across separate persons is legitimate after all—so utilitarianism is vindicated by reductio.

My friend insists that (3) smuggles in an equivocation between metaphysical separateness and normative significance, but I cannot see precisely where the slide happens. Which step should I reject? I would prefer to keep both (1) and (6) intact if possible, and I am especially reluctant to abandon the idea that personal identity is not a deep metaphysical fact. What does giving up each contested step cost?
