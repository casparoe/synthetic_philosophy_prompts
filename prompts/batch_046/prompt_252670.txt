I am revising the syllabus for my upper-division seminar on agency and responsibility, and in preparing the unit on alternative possibilities I have been trying to systematize my own judgments about a family of Frankfurt-style cases. Rather than relying exclusively on the canonical examples, I have constructed several variants to see where my intuitions stabilize and where they fracture. Here are the verdicts I find myself endorsing, though I am not convinced they form a consistent set.

In the standard Frankfurt scenario—Black stands ready to intervene should Jones show any hesitation about killing Smith, but Jones proceeds on his own initiative—I judge that Jones is fully morally responsible for the killing. The counterfactual mechanism never activates and exerts no causal influence over the actual sequence of events. I reach the same verdict in a flicker variant, where Jones retains a brief, causally isolated physical capacity to abort (for instance, an involuntary muscular twitch that could trigger an external blocker) but does not exercise it; the presence of this inert alternative does not diminish his responsibility. I also think Jones is responsible in a hierarchical variant, where a firmly settled second-order volition—one that Jones has endorsed over time and now identifies with—deterministically produces the first-order intention to kill, leaving no psychological branching point. However, my judgments become unstable with what I call the preemptive lock: yesterday Jones voluntarily ingested a substance that renders him constitutionally incapable of forming the intention not to kill today; he remembers this choice, approves of it, and now kills without any internal resistance. On some reflections I regard him as responsible, since he authored the constraint; on others I think the absence of any live alternative at the moment of action undermines present responsibility, regardless of prior authorship.

There is also a transparent-monitor case that sits uncomfortably between these: Jones knows that a neural monitor will paralyze him if he forms the intention not to kill, but he never forms that intention and kills willingly. I lean toward holding him responsible, yet the transparency of the constraint makes the absence of alternatives feel more morally salient than in the hidden-intervention case, and I am unsure whether that salience should carry any weight.

My question is whether there is a single principle—perhaps concerning the source or modal proximity of the constraint that eliminates alternatives, or the agent's present identification with the causal pathway—that can accommodate my stable verdicts in the standard, flicker, and hierarchical cases while giving a determinate answer in the preemptive-lock case and explaining why the transparent-monitor case should or should not differ from the hidden one. If no such principle exists without cost, I want to know which of my verdicts is the outlier that should be revised for the sake of coherence, and what the resulting account predicts for a collective analog: five agents operating under a shared protocol in which no individual can unilaterally halt a harmful outcome, but each knowingly participates in the collective execution.
