Here is an argument I have been working through about Dewey's experimentalism, and it now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to collapse the distinction between democratic politics and democratic culture that Dewey himself insists on. I would like to keep premises (1) and (2) if I can, but I want to know exactly where the slide happens.

(1) For Dewey, democracy is not a fixed institutional arrangement but a mode of social inquiry: an indeterminate situation is resolved through cooperative experimentation, where proposed solutions are treated as working hypotheses to be tested by their experienced consequences and revised accordingly.

(2) Genuine inquiry, whether in a laboratory or in social life, requires experimental openness: the conditions of testing, the data produced, and the criteria of success must be available for observation and correction by anyone whose interests are bound up in the outcome; without this openness, feedback is broken and the process becomes authoritarian rather than intelligent.

(3) Therefore, a social decision is democratic in Dewey's full sense only if it is organized so that all affected parties can observe the proposed solution in operation, challenge its effects, and participate in its revision.

(4) Dewey extends this logic beyond the state to what he calls associated living—family life, friendship, education, and art—arguing that democracy is a comprehensive way of life, not confined to government.

(5) Apply this to an intimate decision: whether a long-married couple should remain together after one partner's serious betrayal. The affected parties are the couple themselves, but also, on Dewey's account of shared social interests, their children, their close friends, and the moral community that sustains norms of commitment.

(6) By (3), resolving this situation democratically requires that the couple's relational hypothesis—say, attempting reconciliation under specific conditions—be open to observation and revision by all affected, including that broader community.

(7) But if the couple's attempt at reconciliation is subject to collective observation and revision—if friends, children, or institutions can intervene to alter its terms—then the intimate authority necessary for genuine commitment is destroyed; the relationship becomes a public experiment managed by others rather than a personal moral achievement.

(8) So either intimate moral life cannot be democratic, which contradicts (4), or democracy in intimate life is not the open, revisable inquiry described in (1)-(3) but something weaker, such as mere mutual respect without experimental structure.

(9) Yet if (9) is true, then Dewey's claim that democracy and scientific inquiry share one logical structure across all domains fails, because intimate democracy lacks the very feature—collective experimental revision—that defines intelligent inquiry.

Tell me which step to reject and why it looked plausible. If I must choose between saving (1)-(2) and saving (4), which costs less for Dewey's overall view? And what does the rejection do to his distinction between the state and other forms of association?
