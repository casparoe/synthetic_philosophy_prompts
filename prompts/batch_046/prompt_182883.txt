In our philosophy of science seminar, a classmate summarized Kuhn’s incommensurability thesis like this: “Incommensurability is not just linguistic—paradigms determine their own taxonomies, measurement calibrations, and problem-solving criteria through shared exemplars rather than explicit definitions. So scientists in different paradigms literally inhabit different worlds: the observational facts themselves change, and there is no neutral algorithm to compare paradigms point by point. That is why revolutions are resolved by persuasion and generational turnover rather than by rational argument, and why Kuhn rejects cumulative progress—later paradigms do not preserve earlier solved problems but simply redefine what counts as a problem, using values like simplicity that are internal to each framework.” I worry this sounds accurate but may collapse Kuhn’s distinction between local taxonomic incommensurability and global ontological change into something stronger, and that it conflates his view with Feyerabend’s stronger relativism. Could you audit the summary? Please separate what fairly represents Kuhn’s claims in his major work, what subtly overstates or misplaces them—especially regarding exemplars, rational comparison, and the status of shared values—and whether the classmate’s inference that paradigm choice is therefore non-rational actually follows from the corrected parts.
