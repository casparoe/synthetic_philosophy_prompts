As a novelist who has spent the last decade writing about characters whose sense of self unravels through the stories they tell about themselves, I find myself in an unexpected institutional position: I sit on the interdisciplinary advisory board of the Meridian Institute for Psychiatric Research, and our quarterly meeting next Thursday requires a binding recommendation. A research consortium has submitted a pilot proposal to study and potentially codify a condition they call "Autodiegetic Fragmentation"—a proposed clinical category describing individuals who construct immersive, self-authored fictional autobiographies that they experience as ontologically prior to their empirical life histories, often borrowing narrative architectures from interactive fiction and generative media. The investigators seek our board's endorsement to develop diagnostic criteria that could eventually inform psychiatric classification systems, arguing that formal recognition would validate suffering that currently falls between dissociative and delusional disorders. Half the board believes that giving this phenomenon a clinical name and taxonomy will do precisely what the philosopher Ian Hacking warned about in his analysis of looping effects: the classification will not merely describe but will reshape the people it targets, providing a ready-made script that amplifies the very fragmentation it claims to treat. The other half contends that the looping risk is overstated, that these narrative patterns are already proliferating in clinical settings regardless of official recognition, and that refusal to classify abandons patients to misdiagnosis.

Please draft the formal decision memo I will circulate to the board members before Thursday's session. The memo should recommend a concrete course of action—approve, reject, or approve with specific conditions—and include an actionable time frame and procedural safeguards. It must treat the looping-effects objection with genuine philosophical seriousness rather than dismissing it as mere theoretical anxiety, and it must honestly present the strongest counter-argument in favor of immediate classification, giving that position its due weight even if the recommendation ultimately sides against it. The document should be written as an institutional opinion suitable for a research ethics and policy board, not as personal literary reflection, though you may draw on my novelist's concern that diagnostic language can become a kind of narrative technology that writes its subjects into being. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests; the reasoning should stand on its own conceptual legs. I want the recommendation to feel like a practical judgment that acknowledges the conceptual depth of Hacking's concern without requiring the board members to consult external philosophical texts.
