A common framing of the transatlantic divergence in “liberal” treats it as a case of allopatric speciation: a single ancestral population—classical liberalism—separated by an oceanic and institutional barrier, evolves in isolation into two reproductively isolated forms (American progressive liberalism and European market liberalism), so that attempts to cross-pollinate them—calling a European free-market advocate or an American social-democratic advocate by the same label—produce conceptual confusion analogous to hybrid sterility. Audit this analogy precisely. What corresponds to the ancestral population, the geographic barrier, the mechanisms of divergence, and the reproductive isolation? Where does the mapping break—for instance, does it wrongly imply an essential shared nature that has merely differentiated, or that hybridization is necessarily sterile rather than sometimes fertile (as in “social liberalism” or revived “classical liberal” movements)? Which normative or descriptive conclusions does the analogy smuggle in about whether reconciliation is possible or desirable? If the analogy collapses under scrutiny, is there a better comparison—linguistic false friends, Wittgensteinian family resemblance, institutional path dependence—that captures the divergence without importing biological teleology? And in any case, does the speciation picture help explain why debates between Europeans and Americans about “liberal” values so often feel like arguments across incommensurable categories rather than disagreements within one?
