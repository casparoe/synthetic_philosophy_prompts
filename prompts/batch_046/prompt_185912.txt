Analyze Iran's long-standing regulated kidney market—where sellers receive state-set compensation through a sanctioned intermediary, transactions are restricted to domestic recipients, and the pool of sellers is drawn mainly from low-income citizens—through the lens of repugnant-markets theory, specifically the corruption argument (that putting a price on body parts degrades human dignity) and the crowding-out argument (that introducing payment displaces altruistic motivation). Map each institutional feature onto the mechanisms these arguments identify: which ones are mitigated by state control, and which ones persist or are intensified? Then assess what the episode shows about the theory—does bureaucratic regulation dissolve the moral repugnance, or only conceal it? Conclude by isolating one structural feature of this market that neither framework clearly predicts, and discuss whether that feature supports or undermines extending a similar regulated model to vote-selling or to price-gouging regimes for essential medicines.
