I serve on my university's Cultural Heritage Acquisition and Ethics Board, and we must issue a formal recommendation before Thursday's meeting. A donor has offered our museum a reconstructed object originally created as a single 12th-century composite reliquary. After a destructive fire, restoration specialists have reassembled roughly sixty percent of the original fragments, filled missing structural sections with a modern synthetic binding matrix, and incorporated decorative inlays taken from two unrelated objects of approximately the same period. The donor maintains that this is the same reliquary, restored; our acquisition policy, however, requires us to classify every incoming item either as a unitary work—eligible for unified conservation funding and singular liability coverage—or as an assemblage, treated as a collection with distributed provenance and separate risk profiles. The board is divided. One faction holds that because the synthetic matrix and the foreign decorative elements are now structurally and aesthetically integral, there is no continuous material identity or genuine functional unity, so the conditions for special composition are not met and the item should be registered as an assemblage. The opposing faction contends that intentional restoration directed at reproducing the original form and liturgical function is sufficient for the parts to compose one object, regardless of partial material replacement. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate: it should recommend a concrete course of action—approve as unitary work, approve only with specific conditions, or reject—and provide reasoning the board can act on. Your memo should engage the strongest metaphysical argument on the losing side honestly rather than dismissing it, should apply the conceptual issue directly to our classification criteria without merely restating policy language, and should not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, since the board needs actionable reasoning rather than a literature review. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate for circulation to faculty and museum trustees.
