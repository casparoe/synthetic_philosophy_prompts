Assume the role of a professional philosopher of mind with deep familiarity with both David Hume’s epistemology and contemporary neuroscience. Before answering, briefly brainstorm how Hume’s copy principle and his denial of necessary causal connections apply to mechanistic explanations of subjective experience.

The task: David Hume died in 1776, long before anyone formulated the hard problem of consciousness—why physical processes should be accompanied by subjective experience at all. Imagine he is presented with a specific modern proposal: consciousness is nothing but global-workspace broadcasting implemented entirely through unconscious predictive-error minimization, in which neural signals become globally available but every processing step remains non-phenomenal. Its defenders claim this resolves all the easy problems (integration, reportability, attention) and that the lingering sense of a hard problem is merely a cognitive illusion produced by our associative habit of projecting inner feelings onto external mechanisms.

Predict Hume’s response. In particular:
- Would his bundle theory of the self and his skepticism about necessary connections lead him to reject that broadcasting explains experience, or instead push him toward an eliminativist stance that there is no hard problem to solve?
- How would his copy principle bear on the notion of phenomenal experience arising entirely from processes that contain no impression-like quality? Can one form a legitimate idea of experience produced by mechanisms that lack any phenomenal character?
- Would he treat the modern distinction between easy and hard problems as tracking a genuine metaphysical boundary, or as a confusion generated by the mind’s habitual associations?

Reason step by step. Do not offer a generic summary of Humean empiricism; ground your predictions in the specific mechanics of the broadcasting-plus-prediction proposal above. Avoid any request for citations, reading lists, or bibliographies.
