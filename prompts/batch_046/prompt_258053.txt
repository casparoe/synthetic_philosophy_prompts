(1) If we are genuinely ontologically committed to a particular entity x, there has to be some priciple that lets us identify x and distinguish it from every non-identical thing.
(2) Any identification priciple we actually use has to depend on properties we can access, otherwise we're just guessing.
(3) Now take any concrete particular—say a person named Jordan. There's always a "shadow counterpart" in a causally isolated region (or a near possible world) that matches Jordan on every accesible property but differs only in properties that are in principle unobservable to us.
(4) So there's never an accesible difference between Jordan and that shadow counterpart.
(5) Therefore we never meet the identification condition for Jordan specifically; all we can commit to is an equivalence class of Jordan-shadow-pairs.
(6) If two actual people in different isolated pockets are shadow-counterparts, they fall into the same class, which means they're ontologically the exact same entity.
(7) So personal identity collapses—there aren't genuinely distinct individuals across those boundaries, just one distributed Jordan-type.
(8) But that's nuts; I definitely want to hold onto the idea that people stay distinct even when we can't check.

Which premise or step should I reject?
