I'm trying to fix my commitments on material constitution—whether an artifact and its matter are identical, and what conditions govern survival—by examining my reactions to concrete cases. Here are my verdicts:

- A statue carved from clay is crushed into powder. The statue is destroyed; the clay survives, now dispersed.
- Every clay molecule is replaced one by one over years, the shape never disrupted. The statue survives; the original matter does not.
- The clay is melted and recast into a vase. The statue is destroyed; the matter survives in a new form.
- The clay is arranged in statue-form only by a continuous external force field. The field is switched off, the clay collapses into a shapeless heap, but not a single gram is lost or chemically altered. The statue is destroyed precisely at the moment of collapse.
- Every atom is simultaneously replaced by an identical synthetic atom in exactly the same position, so shape, location, and causal history are uninterrupted except for a discontinuity in material identity. I think the statue is destroyed here, unlike in the gradual case.
- A perfect replica is constructed elsewhere from different matter while the original is destroyed. This is not the original statue.

Is there one principle—about spatiotemporal continuity, formal persistence, material continuity, or causal-historical chain—that accommodates all six verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and why? Please state the principle precisely, say what it predicts for a statue made of ice that is continuously melted and re-carved by an automated system using the same water, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot save. Also say what would change your mind: which feature of these cases, if argued differently, would overturn the principle you defend, and where is that reasoning most likely to fail?
