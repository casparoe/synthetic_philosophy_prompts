I've been working through the procreation asymmetry in the context of reproductive ethics, and I keep hitting what feels like an internal tension that I can't resolve. The asymmetry, as I understand it, gives us a reason not to create beings who will suffer—since they can be harmed by being brought into existence—but denies that we have any corresponding reason to create beings who will flourish, since those who never exist have no standing to complain about missing out on goods. Recently I tried to apply this to a comparative case involving two possible reproductive policies. Policy A produces children with a high probability of flourishing lives that include significant pleasure, achievement, and social connection, but also carries a small risk of serious suffering. Policy B produces children guaranteed to live lives that are completely free of both suffering and craving—what I take to be the baseline state valued by tranquilism—yet also devoid of any positive goods. Following the asymmetry, I reject Policy A because of the risk of suffering, and I feel no pull toward Policy B's flourishing outcomes because there is no obligation to create happy people. But then I realize that Policy B creates beings whose existence is, by tranquilist lights, neither better nor worse than non-existence, since they lack both suffering and craving. So haven't I simply replaced one problem with another? By selecting Policy B to avoid the asymmetry's prohibition, I seem to be bringing beings into existence who gain nothing over non-existence, which feels like it violates the spirit of the asymmetry—namely, that creation needs justification when it risks harm, but also that creation without gain is pointless. Is my error that I'm smuggling in a tranquilist framework—where only the absence of craving and suffering has intrinsic value—to solve a problem that belongs purely to the logic of harm and benefit? Or does the procreation asymmetry itself implicitly depend on some view like tranquilism about what makes existence worth having?
