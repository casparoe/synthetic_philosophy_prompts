The boundary problem in democratic theory—who belongs to the demos—has been stalled for decades between the all-affected-interests principle and the all-subjected-to-coercion principle. Proponents of the affected-interests view, in the spirit of Goodin, maintain that anyone whose vital interests are substantially impacted by a collective decision must possess democratic standing; defenders of the subjection view, following Abizadeh, reply that legitimacy derives specifically from being subject to coercive law, not from mere causal impact, and that extending membership to every affected party collapses the boundary into incoherence. The exchange is now highly ritualized: each side offers standard counterexamples—transboundary pollution for the subjection camp, transient tourists for the affected camp—and neither moves.

Diagnose why this debate has stalled. Is it a verbal disagreement about the meaning of democratic legitimacy—authorization versus protection? A substantive disagreement about empirical tractability, such as whether causal impact can be traced with sufficient precision to ground membership rights? Or are the two camps answering different questions—one about the normative justification of a particular state's authority, another about the legitimate scope of transnational decision-making—while both speak of the demos?

In your diagnosis, engage concretely with what each side actually says about boundary cases: the long-term non-citizen resident, the future generation affected by climate policy, and the worker subject to labor regulation but not to criminal jurisdiction. Identify whether a hidden formal issue is doing the work—for instance, whether membership must be transitive across nested jurisdictions, or whether the boundary question is being treated as a one-time constitutional moment when it is in fact a recurring administrative determination. Then specify what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction, an empirical study, a formal model of nested membership, a reframing of the question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why.
