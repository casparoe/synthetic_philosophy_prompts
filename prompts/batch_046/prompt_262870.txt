As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently turned to philosophy of mind, I find myself perplexed by what I take to be an unacknowledged parallel between quantum measurement theory and the so-called paradox of phenomenal judgement. Epiphenomenalism holds that conscious states are causally inert with respect to physical events; nevertheless we form judgements such as "I am experiencing a red quale". In physics, a measured quantity must couple to the detector through an interaction Hamiltonian—without such coupling, correlation between instrument reading and system state is spurious. Applying this criterion to introspection, if my phenomenal state plays no causal role in generating the corresponding neural judgement, then the correlation is mediated solely by parallel physical causes, not by any genuine measurement interaction. Is this not precisely why the paradox is fatal rather than merely puzzling? For if introspective knowledge demands causal coupling in the way empirical measurement does, then epiphenomenalism undermines the evidential basis for consciousness itself, and we ought to reject the view not as mysterious but as methodologically self-defeating. Where does this analogy fail, and does the paradox actually presuppose a false theory of introspective justification—one that illegitimately imports physical measurement conditions into first-person epistemology?
