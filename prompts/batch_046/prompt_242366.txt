I've noticed a recurring defensive structure in long-range predictive theories that specify a structural crisis mechanism—most classically, geometric growth meeting arithmetic or sub-geometric relief—while leaving the timeline indefinite. When the predicted crisis fails to materialize within an expected window, defenders do not abandon the core mechanism. Instead, they invoke intermediate suppressors—technological substitution, institutional buffers, demographic shifts—that temporarily hold the crisis at bay. The move that interests me comes next: they argue that the very success of these suppressors confirms that the underlying pressure remains intact and is in fact accumulating, so that the absence of crisis is interpreted as evidence of heightened eventual vulnerability. The timeline is then extended, and each successive non-occurrence is treated as further proof that the mechanism is still active, merely deferred. I am familiar with Lakatosian protective belts and with standard criticisms of ad hoc immunization, but neither seems to capture the specific temporal logic here—namely, that suppression-success is read as pressure-confirmation, and that predictive failure is systematically converted into structural confirmation. Does this exact argumentative pattern have an established name in philosophy of science or in the study of forecasting? If there is no specific term, how should it be distinguished from an ordinary auxiliary hypothesis, from unfalsifiability, and from the more general claim that a theory can be 'right but merely early'? What are its boundary cases, and at what point does temporal deferral collapse into a different kind of theoretical failure?
