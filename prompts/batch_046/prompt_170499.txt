You are a philosopher of mind with expertise in affective neuroscience and value theory. Before answering, briefly brainstorm the structural features of pain asymbolia and the protective-function view of pain.

In debates over whether pain is intrinsically bad, people often invoke a smoke-detector analogy: the sensory component is the flashing LED that registers tissue damage, while suffering is the loud siren that drives avoidance. Patients with pain asymbolia seem to have the LED without the siren — they report location, intensity, and quality, yet feel no distress and show no motivation to withdraw. From this, some conclude that pain is merely informational and not essentially aversive; the badness lies only in the emotional reaction.

Audit how far this analogy holds. Structure your response in these sections, each roughly 100–150 words:

1. Correspondence table — map precisely (tissue damage, sensory pain, affective suffering, the patient, the homeowner/system, protective outcome).
2. Where it breaks — identify disanalogies, especially whether a signal can keep its functional role when decoupled from motivation, and whether biological dissociation is comparable to mechanical disconnection.
3. Smuggled commitments — state what the analogy quietly imports about part-whole value (is the LED valuable without the siren?) and about representational versus motivational theories of affect.
4. Survival of the conclusion — assess whether "pain is not intrinsically bad" survives once the breaks are explicit, or whether asymbolia reveals something else about aversion's architecture.
5. Better analogy — if the smoke-detector picture misleads, propose a more accurate comparison (e.g., an integrated drive-state or command signal) and explain how it reframes the normative question.

Do not cite authors, papers, or provide bibliographies; reason entirely from the conceptual structure.
