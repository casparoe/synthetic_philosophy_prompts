Consider a historian of democratic failure who has assessed the canonical Athenian cases as follows. In the Mytilene debate, she judges the initial death-sentence vote a failure not of information—both Cleon's and Diodotus' arguments were heard—but of procedural architecture: without a mandatory intermission, punitive passion overrode second thought, even though the reversal eventually corrected it. In the Sicilian Expedition, she judges the failure not one of popular ignorance—Nicias' warnings were fully presented—but of structural impotence: expert strategic counsel was present yet institutionally subordinate to assembly enthusiasm. In the trial of Socrates, she judges the conviction a failure neither of emotional volatility nor of missing expertise, but of jurisdictional overreach: a democratic jury applied political mechanisms to philosophical and religious questions for which they lacked appropriate framing authority.

These three assessments are jointly consistent with at least two interpretive rules. Under the institutional-mismatch rule, democratic failure occurs whenever the safeguard appropriate to a decision's domain is absent—procedural delay for punitive measures, expert veto for strategic measures, jurisdictional boundary for philosophical ones. Under the rhetorical-closure rule, democratic failure occurs whenever dissent is systematically excluded through framing that equates opposition with civic betrayal, regardless of which institutional checks happen to be present.

Now apply these rules to a new case. In a hypothetical assembly of 410 BCE, during a brief truce, citizens vote on whether to permanently exclude from public office anyone who refused a specific, legally optional military levy. The proposal originates from a broad citizen committee rather than a single orator. The assembly observes an intermission practice—an initial vote, then confirmation after a full day's interval—modeled on the corrective procedure used after Mytilene. The ten strategoi submit a written assessment that the levy was optional and that permanent exclusion is disproportionate. Nevertheless, speakers consistently frame refusal as betrayal of the polis, citizens who defend the refusers are interrupted and accused of cowardice, and the exclusion passes by a substantial majority on the second vote.

Predict the historian's verdict on whether this decision constitutes a democratic failure of the same kind. Note honestly what principle she would be adopting either way. End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" followed by your predicted classification and the governing rule in parentheses.
