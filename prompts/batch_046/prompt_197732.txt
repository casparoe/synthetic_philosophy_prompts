Here is roughly where I have landed on Athenian democracy’s failures, and I want to know whether the package is jointly tenable. (1) The Mytilene debate shows that direct democratic deliberation can genuinely self-correct when arguments are framed in terms of long-term collective interest rather than emotional retaliation; this is a real strength of assembly-based decision-making. (2) The Sicilian expedition demonstrates that the same deliberative structure is structurally incapable of strategic restraint, because citizens evaluate military risk through the lens of immediate prestige and reward rather than probabilistic assessment. (3) Socrates’ trial was not an instance of democratic irrationality but a coherent exercise of popular sovereignty over religious norms: the jury applied a consistent civic-piety standard that philosophical autonomy violated. (4) Therefore, Athens did not fail because it was too democratic, but because it failed to partition moral deliberation, strategic command, and religious authority into institutionally separated spheres with domain-specific decision rules. (5) Nevertheless, I hold that any such partition is illegitimate unless the assembled citizens retain the ultimate authority to dissolve or reconstitute it. Do these five commitments hang together? Specifically, does (3) sit comfortably with (4)’s call for separation, given that (5) insists on undivided popular sovereignty? And is there a hidden tension between (1)’s praise for deliberative correction and (2)’s claim that deliberation is structurally reckless? Please order the tensions by severity, identify which apparent conflicts dissolve on closer inspection, and specify the cheapest repair—whether giving up (4), weakening (5), or reconceiving (3)—together with what that repair costs the overall picture.
