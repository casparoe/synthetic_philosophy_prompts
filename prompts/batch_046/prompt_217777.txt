Here are my gut judgments about rescue and allocation cases, and I want to know whether they hang together.

First, the classic lifeboat situation: I can reach either one raft carrying five strangers or one raft carrying a single stranger, but not both. I think saving the five is clearly the better choice, and saving the one without any special reason would be wrong; still, I do not think the lone survivor is wronged if I flip a fair coin and save them instead.

Second, the same structure except the single passenger is my adult child and the five are strangers. Saving my child feels permissible—perhaps even what I should do—though saving the five also seems permissible; I would not condemn either choice.

Third, a hospital has one undivided dose of an antidote that will certainly cure one critically ill patient. Alternatively, it can be split into five partial doses, each with a 60 percent chance of curing one of five other patients (expected lives saved: three). I feel I should administer the full dose to the one, despite the lower expected number of survivors.

Fourth, the standard switch case: a runaway train will kill five workers tied to one track unless I divert it to a side track where one worker is tied. I think I should flip the switch and kill the one to save the five.

Fifth, a bridge variant with the same numbers: I can stop the train and save the five only by pushing one large bystander off the bridge onto the tracks. Here I think pushing is wrong, even though the trade is still one life against five.

Sixth, a distant-aid choice: I can donate a fixed sum to a program that will certainly provide treatment for ten people with a common illness overseas, or to an experimental program that has a 40 percent chance of curing fifty people with a rare condition (expected lives saved: twenty). I think I should fund the ten, not because the number is smaller but because the benefit is certain and direct.

Is there one principle that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which should I overturn? Please state the principle precisely, indicate what it predicts for an unlisted case—specifically, a rescue drone that can be programmed either to prioritize a larger cluster of people with low individual survival probabilities or a smaller cluster with near-certain survival—and be explicit about which of my judgments your favored account cannot accommodate.
