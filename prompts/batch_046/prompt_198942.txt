Write a pantoum of exactly four quatrains on the problem of state apologies for historical wrongs, treating the apology not as a settled moral achievement but as a speech act suspended between the expressive register (conveying regret without restructuring relations) and the transformative register (reconfiguring the moral and political bond between the apologizing state and the wronged community). At the same time, register the problem of intergenerational agency—whether the present state is sufficiently continuous with the historical perpetrator for “we” to be a coherent subject of apology, or whether the pronoun collapses into a fiction that lets the present escape material obligation.

Use the pantoum’s repetition to do conceptual work: in each successive quatrain, let the second and fourth lines of the previous stanza become the first and third lines, with fresh language filling the remaining positions; in the final quatrain, let the first and third lines of the opening stanza return in the second and fourth positions, so the poem closes with its original language transformed by everything that has intervened. Choose one recurring phrase—something like “we are not the ones who did it” or “this acknowledgment is not enough”—that shifts meaning with each return: first as a denial of continuity, then as an acknowledgment of inherited structure, then as an indictment of symbolic repair, and finally as either a broken repetition or a resolved transformation. The rhyme and repetition should mirror the cyclical return of official apologies to the same rhetorical posture without altering material conditions, unless the final repetition breaks the cycle.

The poem should be precise enough that someone familiar with debates in collective responsibility and political apology recognizes the positions rather than seeing generic references. Do not include citations, footnotes, or a bibliography. Leave the tension between symbolic acknowledgment and structural change genuinely open, or resolve it only by showing how the form itself demands a repetition that language alone cannot satisfy. Write in a voice suitable for closing a graduate seminar: serious, exact, and without sermonizing.
