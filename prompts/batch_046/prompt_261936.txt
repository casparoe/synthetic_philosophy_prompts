You serve on the Clinical Neuroethics Oversight Panel of a university-affiliated research hospital. Protocol 47 has been submitted for review: a study of patients in minimally conscious states that attempts to dissociate access consciousness from phenomenal consciousness using a modified global workspace paradigm. The researchers propose presenting auditory and tactile stimuli while recording two neural signatures: long-range fronto-parietal broadcasting, interpreted as global workspace entry and thus access consciousness; and localized recurrent processing in early sensory cortices, interpreted as a potential marker of phenomenal consciousness that can occur without global broadcast. From this, they derive a four-way classification of patients—both present, access without phenomenal, phenomenal without access, or neither—and argue that patients falling into the “access without phenomenal” category can ethically serve as “informational controls” in subsequent trials. On their view, global workspace theory treats broadcasting as the functional substrate of reportability but not necessarily of subjective experience, so these patients lack the phenomenological status that triggers full analgesic and consent safeguards.

The panel is split. One faction accepts the dissociation framework and wants standard approval with conventional safeguards. The other faction contends that the protocol rests on a category error: on the most defensible interpretation of global workspace theory, global broadcasting is not merely an access mechanism but a constitutive condition for phenomenal consciousness itself, which means the putative dissociation collapses and the “access-only” classification is conceptually unfounded and ethically hazardous.

Draft the panel’s formal decision memo, approximately 800 to 1000 words, recommending whether to approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and specify a concrete timeline or revision requirement. The memo must state its bottom line unambiguously and include reasoning that the researchers and clinicians could actually respond to. You should explain the specific philosophical tension at stake—whether localized recurrent processing can constitute phenomenal consciousness absent global entry, and whether global entry is correlational with or constitutive of phenomenal experience—without reducing it to generic summary. You must treat the strongest consideration on the losing side with genuine intellectual honesty: if you recommend approval, show precisely why the constitutive objection, though serious, does not defeat the study; if you recommend rejection or conditional approval, explain exactly what conceptual revision would be required and why the current framework is insufficient.

In addition, propose at least one concrete, non-obvious institutional mechanism—beyond standard informed-consent language—to mitigate the risk that the study’s conceptual framework might be wrong. This could take the form of a mandatory philosophical review clause, a staged consent model tied to patient reclassification, or a binding arbitration trigger if the framework is challenged during the trial.

Write for an audience of clinicians, ethicists, and administrators: avoid unnecessary jargon, but do not misrepresent the philosophical stakes through oversimplification. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, citation requests, or references to specific textual passages that depend on precise attribution. You may refer to theoretical positions generally (for example, “the dissociation view” or “the constitutive view”). The deliverable should be a document the panel could circulate, discuss, and act upon.
