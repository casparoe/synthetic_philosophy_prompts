As a novelist preparing a literary work about artistic legacy and material decay, I have been discussing with colleagues the classic problem of material constitution—how a statue relates to the clay that makes it up—though I find the standard formulation too familiar for the scene I am constructing. I need a rigorously designed thought experiment that can serve both my fiction and a philosophy seminar I occasionally teach for writers, one that injects a genuinely unfamiliar structural twist rather than rehearsing what every reader has already encountered. Would you build for me a scenario that meets the following specifications? The setting must be realistic—an art restoration laboratory in the present day—with no appeal to exotic physics, though it may involve an advanced but plausible ceramic engineered with embedded metabolic pathways that continuously exchange atoms with the environment, so that over ten years every constituent of the original statue has been replaced while the macroscopic form remains intact. After this gradual turnover, the statue is catastrophically shattered; only a sub-millimetre volumetric scan and the artist's legal will survive, bequeathing "the statue" to a museum and authorizing physical reprinting from the digital file.

The case should be presented in a single paragraph, then analyzed so that a mereological essentialist, a four-dimensionalist, and a modern Aristotelian hylomorphist who allows form to be instantiated in new matter or digital substrate each deliver specific, non-trivial verdicts: the essentialist must judge that the original statue was already destroyed during the atomic replacement; the four-dimensionalist must agree that the shattered fragments are not the statue yet maintain that the object persisted continuously through the turnover as a spatiotemporal worm; and the hylomorphist must argue that the form survives the shattering in the digital archive and that the reprinted object is numerically identical to the original. Include also a constitution theorist—one for whom the statue and its material are coincident but not identical—who survives the replacement but is destroyed at shattering because constitution requires present, correctly arranged matter. After presenting the scenario, please walk through the reasoning for each position, identify the precise structural feature that forces the hylomorphist to diverge from the others, explain how a defender of the theory that comes out worst might attack the case, and confirm whether removing the metabolic-replacement phase collapses the problem back into the familiar statue-and-clay puzzle. I ask that you avoid bibliographies, citations, or references to specific editions, since my audience has no library access; and please do not quote any philosophical text verbatim—paraphrase freely without quotation marks—so that I may use the result without concern for textual accuracy.
