As a novelist, I spend my days constructing agents whose every decision is causally downstream of choices I never asked them to make—psychologies I invented, histories I assigned, drafts I revised—yet I still expect a reader to hold them accountable for what they do within the story. That tension has made me take Galen Strawson's Basic Argument with unusual seriousness: to be genuinely responsible in the desert-entailing sense, one must be the true cause of one's actions; to be the true cause of one's actions, one must be the true cause of the mental states that produce them; but this requires being the cause of oneself, which sets off an infinite regress of prior selves that no being can complete, so true moral responsibility is impossible even if libertarian free will exists.

Please give me your credence—where 0 means the argument clearly fails and 1 means it is decisive—that Strawson's Basic Argument succeeds in undermining moral responsibility for human agents. Decompose the reasoning into four sub-probabilities, with one line of justification for each:

P1: The probability that moral responsibility genuinely requires being causa sui (the true-self origin condition).

P2: The probability that being causa sui necessarily launches an infinite regress of prior intentional self-creation that cannot be halted.

P3: The probability that libertarian free will, if real, could not break this regress without circularity.

P4: The probability that the analogy between authored fictional characters and human agents is misleading—specifically, that we can coherently attribute responsibility to characters despite knowing their full causal origin, which suggests origin-completeness is not a necessary condition for responsibility in practice (call this the narrative-closure objection).

Then combine them into your overall number, tell me which of P1–P4 your final credence is most sensitive to, and say what evidence or argument would move that term the most.

Finally, please state how confident you are in this overall assessment, and single out the one point—whether in the decomposition, the combination, or the narrative-closure analogy—where you are least sure. Treat the novelist's analogy not as mere decoration but as a live philosophical pressure: does the fact that we coherently assign moral weight to fully-authored fictional characters weaken the claim that ultimate causal origin is necessary for responsibility?
