I cover decision theory and its applications to science and policy for a general-interest publication, and I have been invited to design a single ninety-minute working session for a small group of senior climate and energy reporters. They are numerically literate and comfortable with risk, but they have no background in Bayesian decision theory and are reflexively skeptical of formal results that sound like they justify bureaucratic delay. I want them to understand Good’s result—that, for an agent holding a precise prior, acquiring genuinely cost-free information before acting can never reduce expected utility, which means delaying an irreversible institutional decision solely to observe more data is rational provided the observation itself carries no price—not as a proof to reproduce but as a conceptual tool for judging when a newsroom should hold a story for an incoming dataset. Please plan the explanatory arc: identify the one conceptual dependency that must be in place before the result sounds like anything other than “more data is usually good” (specifically, the distinction between the value of information and the value of commitment, together with how conditioning on a prior works); specify the single carrying scenario, which should involve an irreversible policy commitment—such as signing a long-term carbon-capture site license—rather than a reversible purchase, and explain precisely why reversibility undermines the pedagogical intuition; map the misconceptions this audience is likely to import—particularly the conflation of “cost-free information” with “costless delay,” and the “paralysis by analysis” objection that gathering data dilutes editorial accountability—and indicate exactly where in the sequence each should be intercepted; provide one diagnostic question I can pose near the end that distinguishes real comprehension from mere assent to the slogan “more data is better”; and state clearly which formal machinery—the conditioning identity, the expectation integral, the full proof—should be named but openly set aside as beyond a newsroom workshop. If my framing risks collapsing Good’s result into a generic normative endorsement of institutional risk aversion, please say so plainly rather than accommodating the confusion. Conclude with a one-sentence summary of the plan on its own line beginning exactly with “Plan summary:”.
