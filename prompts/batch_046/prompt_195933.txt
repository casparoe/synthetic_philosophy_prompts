I've been reflecting lately on a disagreement with a close friend who has gradually restructured his entire life around what he calls "complete moral transparency." Over the past two years he has eliminated every activity—playing music, maintaining old friendships, even choosing where to live—that does not score highly on a personal framework he uses to weigh direct and indirect moral contribution. He donates nearly all disposable income to causes he has calculated to be maximally effective, works exclusively on problems he deems of global urgency, and has trained himself to regard emotional attachments that do not serve an explicit moral purpose as cognitive biases to be corrected. I admire his coherence, yet I find myself unsettled in ways I cannot fully articulate. When I asked whether he ever misses the parts of himself he has set aside—the amateur guitarist, the person who once stayed up talking about nothing—he replied that those were always disguises for ego, and that genuine flourishing consists in never allowing non-moral considerations to compete with duty.

My instinct is that there is something valuable in the very capacities he has disciplined away: the capacity for playful absorption, for loyalty that exceeds calculation, for appreciating something simply because it is beautiful rather than because it reduces suffering. But I worry my discomfort is merely a rationalization of my own mediocrity. I am trying to decide whether I should attempt to emulate his path, or whether his life reveals a hidden tension in the idea that moral perfection should be our highest aspiration. The situation raises a deeper question about whether a life organized entirely around maximal moral output is not just arduous but conceptually misguided—whether treating every domain of value as subordinate to morality collapses important distinctions, or whether any resistance to such total commitment is ultimately a failure of moral seriousness.

Can you help me think through whether my friend's way of living represents an admirable ideal or a kind of reductionism? I am especially interested in whether there is a philosophically defensible reason to hold that being maximally good might not coincide with being maximally desirable as a person, and whether the loss of non-moral goods is a necessary cost of virtue or a sign that the conception of virtue itself has become too narrow.
