My professor reconstructs Aquinas's implicit rulings on resistance to tyranny across five cases. (1) A usurper who takes the throne by violence may be deposed by public authority, but private assassination is forbidden even if it saves lives and is later ratified by the people. (2) A legitimately appointed ruler who issues laws violating natural law may be corrected by his superiors; private citizens may resist passively but may not kill him. (3) A subordinate magistrate who refuses to enforce a direct command to sin acts permissibly, perhaps obligatorily. (4) Where no functioning superior authority exists, private resistance is tolerated only if it does not risk greater disorder. (5) If a private individual kills a tyrant and legitimate representatives immediately elect a successor, the killing remains illicit in itself, though the result is fortunate.

Now consider: a legitimate monarch becomes permanently incapacitated, and his regent—who holds no legitimate title but exercises sole de facto authority—issues orders that will cause mass death. A private citizen with no official rank assassinates the regent; the monarch then recovers, endorses the act, and the legitimate council confirms it. Predict the professor's verdict. Is she running a rule about the killer's official status at the moment of action, about whether the target's authority is usurped or merely delegated, or about whether subsequent ratification by public authority can retroactively legitimize private violence? Which rule best accommodates all five prior verdicts, and what contrasting pair of cases—one in which a magistrate acts without prior authorization and succeeds, another in which a private killer of a usurper is never ratified—would settle the matter?
