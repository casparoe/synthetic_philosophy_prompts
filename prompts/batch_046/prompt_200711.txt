At a philosophy-of-cognition seminar, two colleagues—R and S—reported a disagreement they witnessed between a curator (C) and a cognitive scientist (D) over an autonomous archival system that has managed a museum’s ethnographic records for thirty years. The system began as a simple tagging algorithm but now generates novel classification schemas that working historians treat as authoritative; its original designers retired long ago.

(1) C argued that the system is precisely what Dennett calls a two-bitser: its internal states carry aboutness only because human curators interpret syntactic patterns within an institutional practice. The classifications are derived in exactly the same way a road sign refers to a town—there is no intrinsic mental content, only functional correlation projected onto physical states by users.

(2) D replied that this misreads Dennett’s point. The two-bitser was meant to show that real, if minimal, intentionality can emerge at very low levels of organizational complexity once a functional practice is established. After decades of autonomous operation—where the system’s outputs reshape the interpretive practices that read them—the aboutness is no longer purely projected from outside. It has become co-constituted by the system’s own functional role within an evolving human-machine practice. If derived intentionality can become self-sustaining in this way, Searle’s insistence on a biological source of “original” intentionality begins to look like an unmotivated substrate restriction.

(3) C countered with Searle’s biological naturalism: intentionality requires the specific causal powers of biological brains—powers that actually produce mental content through neurobiological processes. The archival system, however autonomous, remains a syntactic engine manipulating formal symbols according to algorithmic rules. Its feedback loops are computational correlations, not semantic relations. To treat the system as possessing more than derived intentionality is to confuse the intentional stance we adopt for predictive convenience with an intrinsic mental property the system actually has.

(4) D said this begs the central question. Searle defines original intentionality as that which is not derived, then assigns it exclusively to brains. But Dennett’s challenge is that there may be no coherent “original” variety independent of well-grounded, practice-embedded derived intentionality. The system’s classifications now cause historians to reorganize archives in ways the original programmers never intended, showing that the intentional content has escaped its design origins. If that content still operates causally within human cognition but no longer traces back to any designer’s mental states, calling it “merely derived” empties the distinction of explanatory force.

Then specify who is right about which particular point, and identify what both parties overlook—especially regarding whether a long-running autonomous system can generate intentional content that is neither purely original in Searle’s biological sense nor purely derived in the simple design-intention sense.
