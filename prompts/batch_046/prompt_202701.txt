As a journalist covering theoretical computer science and philosophy of science, I am drafting a long-form essay for an ideas publication and am sharing my working notes and central argumentative architecture rather than a finished draft. I would welcome rigorous critical comments—particularly on conceptual accuracy, internal coherence, and whether the framing genuinely advances the discussion or merely relabels familiar positions.

Working title: The Uncomputable Razor—Protocol-Relative Solomonoff Priors and the Normative Structure of Occam's Razor.

Notes and thesis outline:

Standard presentations of Solomonoff induction formalize Occam's razor by assigning prior probabilities to hypotheses in proportion to two raised to the negative of their Kolmogorov complexity, computed relative to a universal Turing machine. The elegance of this account is undermined by two well-known difficulties: Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable, and the universal prior is machine-dependent up to an additive constant. In scientific practice, however, we do not face a blank universal machine; we face a richly structured observational protocol—calibrated instruments, shared background theories, and standardized measurement grammars. My essay proposes that scientific inference operates not with an absolute universal prior, but with what I term a calibrated protocol prior: a constrained universal machine in which the shared apparatus and background commitments are hard-coded as the initial state.

On this framing, Occam's razor is not best understood as an aesthetic or metaphysical preference for simplicity. Instead, it functions as a compression-ratio discipline: a candidate theory is inductively penalized if its Kolmogorov complexity relative to the protocol prior exceeds the information-theoretic gain it delivers over the prior's own predictive outputs. In other words, a theory must earn its keep by compressing the observational stream more efficiently than the baseline protocol, and the magnitude of that compression—not some intuitive notion of parsimony—determines its inductive weight.

The essay then considers an objection: this is merely minimum description length reasoning in algorithmic clothing, with the protocol prior substituting for the model class and the compression ratio substituting for the usual trade-off between fit and complexity. I want to argue that the difference is normative rather than merely terminological. In standard MDL, the model class is chosen for statistical convenience; in the protocol-relative Solomonoff view, the baseline machine is constitutive of scientific rationality itself—without a shared protocol prior, there is no intersubjective standard against which compression can be measured.

I also want to address the machine-dependence problem. Because the protocol prior is intersubjectively fixed within a scientific community, the additive constant problem is not eliminated but is rendered practically invariant: all competing theories are evaluated against the same baseline, so the relative ordering of their prior probabilities is stable even if the absolute values shift with a different universal encoding.

My specific requests for comment are as follows. First, have I misrepresented the relationship between Solomonoff induction and Occam's razor? In particular, does the protocol-relative framing illegitimately smuggle observer-dependent elements into a framework that is meant to be universal? Second, is the compression-ratio discipline genuinely distinct from standard MDL or Bayesian model selection, or have I only introduced new vocabulary for an old trade-off? Third, does the argument that intersubjective fixity solves the practical problem of machine dependence hold up, or does it conflate social agreement with inductive justification?

Please focus your remarks on the conceptual architecture rather than stylistic matters. I am especially interested in identifying any point where the reasoning depends on a hidden assumption about computability, the ontology of scientific instruments, or the normative force of induction itself.
