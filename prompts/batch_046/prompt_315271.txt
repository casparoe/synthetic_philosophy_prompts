In debates over whether symbolic representations genuinely push the physical substrate that realizes them—call the substrate the careenium and the discrete symbolic tokens within it simmballs—the software-and-hardware analogy is frequently invoked: just as software directs a computer's state transitions without being made of silicon or carrying current, so simmballs are said to direct careenial dynamics without mechanically shoving them. Proponents deploy this to license the conclusion that symbolic causation is organizational rather than physical, and therefore that simmballs can govern the careenium without literally pushing it. Audit the analogy explicitly: lay out the correspondences (software to simmballs, hardware to careenium, execution trace to causal pathway); identify the disanalogies that matter for the argument—such as the presence of a compiler or programmer, the normative distinction between correct and incorrect execution, the separability of software from any particular hardware instantiation, and the absence in the careenium of an external interpreter who reads the simmballs; specify which of these features are quietly imported and whether they are available in the target domain; and determine whether the conclusion that organizational direction suffices for causal efficacy survives once these gaps are on the table. If the analogy collapses under this pressure, indicate whether an alternative framing—perhaps thermodynamic, mechanistic, or informational—would do the work better, or whether the very idea that symbols must either push or fail to cause is itself a false dichotomy in this setting.
