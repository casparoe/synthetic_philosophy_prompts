Since my father's recent death, I have spent months sorting through his papers—letters, old party pamphlets, and handwritten reflections on whether a clean break with the past is ever possible without eventually turning on its own architects. The question is no longer abstract for me: I need to understand whether revolutions necessarily consume their own children because of deep structural dynamics, or whether that image captures only a retrospective narrative bias. To that end, I want you to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing interpretive camps regarding the French Revolution.

The two camps are:

Camp A — The Structural-Materialist Interpretation: holds that class struggle and the socio-economic contradictions of the ancien régime and its aftermath are the primary explanatory factors for revolutionary outcomes, including the Terror; and that the Revolution's trajectory, including its self-destructive phase, is explicable by the structural defense of emergent class interests.

Camp B — The Ideological-Contingency Interpretation: holds that political discourse, contingent institutional choices, and the internal logic of revolutionary ideology (rather than underlying class dynamics) are primary in explaining the Revolution's course; and that the turn to the Terror was not structurally predetermined but resulted from specific rhetorical and decision-making dynamics that could have been otherwise.

Please produce the document in the following form:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should include: what both camps accept about the basic chronology and institutional changes from 1789 to 1794; what both agree needs explanation (for example, why the Revolution turned inward, why certain factions were eliminated); and what neither camp denies (for example, that both material conditions and political rhetoric were present).

2. A disagreements table. Each row should contain:
   - The crux (a precise sub-question such that settling it would move at least one camp).
   - Camp A's formulation of its position on that crux, phrased in wording that Camp A itself would accept.
   - Camp B's formulation of its position on the same crux, phrased in wording that Camp B itself would accept.
   - A concrete case, logical argument, or piece of evidence that both camps agree bears on the crux.
   - Camp A's explicit prediction about what that case or argument shows.
   - Camp B's explicit prediction about what that case or argument shows.

The cruxes should specifically include:
   a) Whether the Revolution's interpretation should be framed primarily through class struggle, through ideology, or through pure contingency; and what, if anything, is lost in each framing.
   b) Whether the idea that revolutions devour their children describes a causal mechanism, a selection effect in how historians remember successful purges, or a performative contradiction inherent in revolutionary legitimacy itself; and whether each camp considers this framing meaningful, misleading, or merely rhetorical.
   c) At least one further crux that you inject—preferably a niche or under-discussed conceptual distinction—that genuinely separates the camps and that a well-informed reader could assess without specialized archival knowledge.

3. A brief concluding assessment: which disagreements are substantive, which (if any) are verbal, and whether there is any shared standard of evidence both camps would accept as decisive.

Write with formal precision. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not ask me to verify specific archival sources. Frame everything so that a knowledgeable interlocutor without web access could evaluate the reasoning.
