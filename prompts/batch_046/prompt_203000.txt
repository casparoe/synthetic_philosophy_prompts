I am a linguistic anthropologist compiling a field lexicon for an unwritten language spoken in a highland region, and I need a reusable decision procedure for my documentation team—graduate students with minimal philosophy training—to apply whenever a single native term appears to cover both concrete physical objects and ritual or relational states. The theoretical framework is Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation, particularly as developed through the "gavagai" scenario: the claim that all available behavioral and stimulatory evidence systematically underdetermines the choice among competing translation manuals, so that "rabbit," "undetached rabbit parts," and "rabbit-stage" can all be made consistent with every observable utterance. I want this translated into an ordered checklist my team can run on site with no library access, specifying the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, and exactly where the procedure stops with a definitive recommendation, stops with an explicit "indeterminate—choose on practical grounds" flag, or hands the decision to a senior researcher because the issue is one of ontological commitment rather than empirical observation.

The procedure must handle the distinction between empirical gaps—where more observation might resolve the ambiguity—and principled indeterminacy, where Quine's argument says further evidence cannot settle the matter. It should include concrete tests for distinguishing object-reference from event-reference or part-reference, and it must be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as protocol. Apply the procedure to two actual entries we are debating: first, "takari," which speakers use both when presenting a gourd in a harvest ritual and when describing a cracked container that no longer holds water; second, "moravi," used for a living elder and also for the social role that elder occupies after death in ceremonial contexts. For one of these applications, the procedure should recommend a translation my team will resist—likely treating a ritual state as an undetached part or temporal stage rather than as a unified object—and explain what theoretical cost is paid by accepting that recommendation versus overriding it on practical grounds. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot accomplish: whether it can ever exclude the radical-translation hypothesis that native speakers organize reality in categories with no direct analog in our ontology, and whether applying it to ritual-language cases illegitimately extends an argument originally about physical object reference.
