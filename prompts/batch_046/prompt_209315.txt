Two researchers in a study group—call them Mara and Leo—have been arguing about whether introspection can serve as a reliable data source for emotional motivation. Here is their exchange.

(1) Mara claims introspection is broadly unreliable for motives because people routinely confabulate post-hoc narratives. Rather than reading an internal state, they observe their own behavior and social context, then construct a plausible reason that sounds convincing even to themselves.

(2) Leo replies that Mara conflates causal explanation with phenomenal experience. Confabulation may corrupt answers to “why did you do that?” but it does not undermine answers to “what does this feel like right now?” The latter still enjoys a direct, first-person access that is not merely inferred.

(3) Mara pushes back: emotional feelings are not pre-linguistic raw data waiting to be read. They depend on the conceptual framework used to identify them. Without fine-grained emotional vocabulary, a subject will mislabel physiological arousal, so even “what it feels like” is partly reconstructed through available concepts.

(4) Leo distinguishes “raw affective registration” from “conceptual labeling.” He grants that labeling involves reconstruction, but insists there is a first-order phenomenal layer that can be accessed directly, prior to or independent of the conceptual frame applied afterward.

(5) Mara argues the distinction collapses. Working memory never delivers “raw” states untouched by framing; every introspective report applies some interpretive schema, however minimal. Therefore the idea of direct access to unframed affect is a myth, and all introspection is reconstructive to some degree.

(6) Leo concedes that framing is unavoidable, but claims reliability can be achieved methodologically rather than innately. Through structured protocols—granular emotion labeling, calibrated self-monitoring, and intersubjective comparison—introspection can be made accurate enough for practical research. Reliability is therefore a trained achievement, not proof of direct access.
