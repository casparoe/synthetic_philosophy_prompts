I have been reading a little about Otto Neurath's idea for a universal picture language—ISOTYPE—where simple, standardized icons are supposed to communicate facts to anyone, regardless of language, because the picture mirrors what it is about rather than relying on words or hidden mental interpretations. I am stuck on a conceptual issue that keeps coming up for me.

Neurath seemed to think that if a symbol directly resembles the thing or situation it represents, then people should understand it without any shared language or private mental decoding. But I keep wondering: how do you decide which part of a situation gets drawn, and which parts are left out? And once you have chosen a shape, doesn't that shape already carry meanings that have nothing to do with the thing itself?

Here is the kind of case that confuses me. Imagine a city health department that wants to warn people about unsafe drinking water using a Neurath-style pictogram: a water droplet with a sharp angular mark inside it. The designers intend the angular mark to stand for danger because it looks like a crack or break in the container. But in a local tradition, that same angular shape is used in protective amulets meant to ward off harm. So some residents see the icon and understand it as a sign that the water is spiritually guarded and safe to drink, while others see contamination. The symbol was meant to be transparent—its meaning coming straight from visual resemblance—but the interpretation actually depends on cultural associations that are not part of the water's physical state.

Please give me a concrete example of this same conceptual issue—where a picture designed for direct, language-independent communication fails because its visual form activates conventional or cultural meanings that are separate from what it is supposed to depict—drawn from a different area such as scientific illustration, road signage, or technical diagrams for machinery. In your example, show exactly where the tension lies between Neurath's goal of direct visual correspondence and the unavoidable fact that every drawn shape already belongs to some system of shared conventions.
