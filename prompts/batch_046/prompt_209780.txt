I've been working through the old-evidence problem for Bayesian confirmation: if an agent already knows some observation E, then Bayes's rule assigns it a factor of 1 in updating credence in theory T, since P(E) equals 1. Classic cases like Mercury's perihelion for general relativity seem to show that theories can be strongly supported by data they were not originally designed to predict. I have seen it argued that this challenge only genuinely threatens subjective Bayesians who revise psychological credences, whereas objective or logical Bayesians avoid it because logical support relations are not bound by an agent's prior beliefs; I have also seen the claim that the problem dissolves if we distinguish learning that E is true from learning that T entails E, which permits a second-order update without violating probabilism. Given these considerations, is it correct to conclude that the old-evidence problem is essentially a pseudoproblem generated by conflating belief revision with logical support, and that Bayesian confirmation therefore requires no non-probabilistic supplement—such as an explanatory coherence metric—to account for how old data can support a theory? Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these issues, and do not present a disputed resolution as if it were the consensus view.
