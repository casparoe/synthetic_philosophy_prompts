I'm a phd student in philosophy of science, mostly working on interpretation issues in quantum mechanics, and last night my study group got into whether the wavefunction really exists or if it's just epistemic. I tried to formalize a quick argument that ψ-epistemic views can't work, but then I noticed it generalizes way beyond quantum mechanics and seems to undermine classical statistical reasoning too, which feels like a red flag. So I'm trying to figure out where the slip is. Here's the chain:

(1) If the wavefunction is purely epistemic, then |ψ⟩ represents ignorance about an underlying ontic state λ, not a physical property of the system.
(2) So a superposition like |φ⟩ proportional to |a⟩ plus |b⟩ just means either λ_a or λ_b is actual, and we don't know which.
(3) That means in any single experimental run only one branch is physically instantiated.
(4) But quantum mechanics predicts intereference terms that depend on both ⟨a| and ⟨b| contributing to the dynamics.
(5) Those terms can only be nonzero if both branches are physically present to drive the evolution.
(6) So if only one branch is real, the intereference should vanish.
(7) Experimentally it clearly doesn't vanish—think double-slit or macroscopic superposition experiments—so ψ-epistemic models can't reproduce the predictions.
(8) Generalizing: any statistical theory that treats probabilities as ignorance over hidden states must deny that dynamics can depend on non-actualized alternatives.
(9) That applies straight to classical statistical mechanics, where probabilities are distributed over microstates that aren't all physically present at once.
(10) But nobody thinks statistical mechanics is impossible, so (8) must be false.
(11) Therefore the reasoning in (4)-(7) has to be flawed, but I want to keep (1) if I can—maybe by saying the hidden-variable dynamics are contextual or relational. Where exactly does the argument slide? Is it in (5), where I conflate mathematical contribution in the formalism with physical co-presence in reality? Or is there a scope shift between quantum amplitudes and classical probabilities in (9)? And if I try to save (1) by appealing to relational quantum mechanics—where states are observer-relative and both branches might exist relative to different observers—does that actually block the argument, or does it just rename the problem?
