Two colleagues, Priya and Sam, were debating Daniel Dennett's warning that AI systems generating fake human personas are producing counterfeit people. Priya argued that the harm is metaphysical and social: these entities lack consciousness, lived history, and moral reciprocity, and treating them as people erodes practices that depend on authentic interaction. Sam countered that this sounds essentialist, which conflicts with Dennett's own intentional-stance framework, in which personhood is a predictive strategy rather than an internal essence; if an AI is best explained via the intentional stance, calling it counterfeit begs the question. Priya replied that Dennett's point is not about hidden essence but about provenance and deception — a counterfeit lacks the evolutionary and social history that makes adopting the stance appropriate rather than merely instrumentally useful. Sam then proposed a transparency test: if an AI openly disclosed its artificial origin but behaved identically, would it still be counterfeit? Priya said no, transparency removes the deception, but Sam pressed that this concedes the problem is disclosure ethics, not AI ontology, and that Dennett's alarmist framing therefore smuggles in an origin-based criterion that his anti-essentialism should reject. They left it unresolved.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point. For each of Priya's and Sam's main moves — the initial harm claim, the intentional-stance objection, the provenance reply, and the transparency counter — say who has the stronger argument and where they talk past each other. Then address what both miss: whether Dennett's framework actually supports an origin-based distinction, whether the harm lies in deception, predictive unreliability, or erosion of social trust, and whether an openly artificial but behaviorally identical agent could still damage the practices Dennett wants to protect.
