Alex began by arguing that Williams's core point is structural, not psychological. If Dr. Chen runs a utilitarian calculus, recognizes that saving the two strangers maximizes welfare, but then overrides that conclusion with the consideration that the other victim is her child, she has introduced exactly the competing-reason framework Williams condemns. The problem is not the outcome — saving her child — but the path: she treated her attachment as one weight to be balanced against others, rather than as the direct expression of a constitutive project.

Blake responded that this makes Williams into a blanket opponent of moral reasoning, which he is not. Williams permits an agent to recognize moral reasons; the failure occurs only when those reasons are treated as the sole legitimate currency, forcing personal reasons to justify themselves inside that currency. Blake claimed that Dr. Chen could invoke an agent-relative duty to her child — one not reducible to aggregate welfare — and save her without having one thought too many, provided she never entered the comparative weighing in the first place. The defect, on this reading, is not reasoning itself but a specific form of commensuration.

Alex countered that Blake was merely restating standard agent-relativity and missing Williams's distinctive contribution. Integrity, for Williams, concerns the agent's ground projects being constitutive of her identity. If Dr. Chen saves her child because her child is part of who she is, the decision flows directly from that identity. If she saves her child only after weighing — even if she frames the weight in agent-relative language — she has already alienated her project by turning it into an object of choice. That alienation is the damage, regardless of which vocabulary she uses.

Blake then introduced a distinction he called ground-level integrity versus meta-level integrity. Ground-level integrity is the direct, unmediated expression of a constitutive project; meta-level integrity is the agent's reflective coherence about how she arrived at the action. Blake argued that Williams's husband displays ground-level integrity precisely because he skips the weighing. But a separate failure — meta-level incoherence — arises when an agent does weigh, chooses in favor of attachment, yet secretly believes she should have followed the moral calculus. That, Blake said, is also a version of having one thought too many, but located at the reflective level rather than the motivational one. So the worry is really about the structure of self-endorsement, not just first-order motivation.

Alex replied that Blake was importing a two-level cognitive model foreign to Williams. Integrity is not about reflective endorsement or meta-coherence; it is about ground projects being genuinely constitutive. If Dr. Chen saves her child through direct identification, she preserves integrity regardless of her later reflections. If she weighs and then saves her child, she has already damaged her integrity at the ground level by making her project into a preference. There is no distinct meta-level failure to diagnose; the weighing itself does the harm.
