I'm writing my dissertation on how theoretical vocabulary migrates across subfields in the philosophy of science, and I've kept encountering a pattern in tightly bounded debates—small, technically circumscribed microdomains—where a term is introduced with precise boundary conditions, but then gradually applied to cases that violate one or more of those conditions through analogical extension rather than explicit redefinition. For instance, in recent discussions of representational practices, a concept initially restricted to vehicles that share structural properties with their targets in the absence of any causal interaction has been extended to cover cases where causal interaction is present but structural isomorphism is absent; subsequently, critics begin attacking the extended usage as though it were the core case. In another microdomain—the epistemology of measurement—a term imported from signal processing to describe random, uncorrelated disturbances in observational data has come to cover systematic biases that are predictably structured, and now objections that apply only to the structured cases are treated as undermining the original import. This does not feel like simple equivocation, since the shift is not deliberate, and it does not match family resemblance either, because there is a clear prototype being eroded rather than a cluster of overlapping traits. Is there an established term—perhaps within philosophy of science, conceptual analysis, or cognitive semantics—for this kind of boundary erosion within a confined domain? I am not looking for a bare label: I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie—how it differs from ordinary polysemy, from category mistakes, and from what is sometimes called conceptual stretching. If the literature offers nothing that captures it precisely, I would welcome a coinage with explicit boundary conditions, together with a couple of near-miss cases that help locate its limits.
