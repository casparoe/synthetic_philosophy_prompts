I sit on our university's interdisiplinary ethics and policy board (we cover tech, law, and philosophy, it's a bit of a mess). We're reviewing a proposal for a cross-national study comparing how citizens in Germany, the US, and China manage visibility to state institutions — not just privacy settings, but active strategies of data obscurity, from German Datensparsamkeit to American public-records openness to Chinese social-credit exposure. The researchers want to interview participants about whether they feel they have a moral right to hide from the state, and they want to publish anonymized aggregate profiles. Half the board says this is just sociology of privacy; the other half worries that framing 'hiding' as a legitimate moral stance risks normalizing anti-democratic opacity, especially given the Chinese comparison. They also disagree on whether informed consent can cover questions about state-citizen visibility without triggering political risk for participants.

Draft our decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with reasoning that takes the strongest objection seriously. Don't just cite GDPR or the First Amendment; engage the philosophical tension: is there a defensible liberal right to concealment from democratic institutions, or does democratic citizenship require a default of transparency? Reach a concrete recommendation with a timeline, and address whether the study's framing of 'hiding' as a rational strategy rather than a deviation is methodologically sound or normatively loaded.
