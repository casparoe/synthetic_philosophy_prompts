I was arguing with a friend late last night about whether anthropic reasoning ever genuinely constrains cosmological belief or merely invites us to gerrymander reference classes, and the conversation kept returning to Bostrom’s presumptuous philosopher: the thinker who rejects theories predicting enormous populations of observers on the ground that she does not observe herself being one of the multitudes. I am drafting the final exam for my graduate seminar in epistemology and the philosophy of science, and I want one essay question that treats the self-sampling assumption (SSA) and the self-indication assumption (SIA) not as labels to be memorized but as competing normative principles that yield opposite prescriptions in precisely this kind of case. To prevent students from simply reciting the standard coin-toss setup, inject the following twist: present a philosopher comparing Model S, which predicts a universe with roughly 10^10 observers all possessing the cognitive architecture required to formulate cosmological arguments, with Model L, which predicts a multiverse with roughly 10^40 observers but only 10^10 who have that reasoning capacity, the remaining 10^30 being conscious yet cognitively inert observers lacking any conceptual framework. The philosopher knows of herself that she can formulate cosmological arguments. The essay should ask students whether SSA and SIA license different inferences about which model is more probable, whether restricting the reference class to argument-capable observers saves the presumptuous philosopher from absurdity or instead begs the question against Model L, and whether any non-circular principle exists for fixing the reference-class boundary that does not presuppose the very cosmological theory being evaluated. The exam should allow about seventy-five minutes; please provide the exam question, a model answer of approximately six hundred words, and a marking scheme totaling twenty marks—assigning marks to each of five required moves, for instance four marks apiece—that distinguishes an A-level response from a B-level response by naming the specific philosophical moves each must contain. Those five moves are: (i) a precise formal distinction between SSA and SIA; (ii) correct application of both to the philosopher’s indexical evidence under the reasoning-capacity restriction; (iii) identification of the reference-class ambiguity this restriction introduces; (iv) a critical evaluation of whether that restriction is principled or ad hoc; and (v) an explicit separation of what is settled in the literature—the logical structure of the two assumptions and Bostrom’s framing of the presumptuous philosopher case—from what remains genuinely contested, including whether SIA commits a fallacy of double-counting, whether anthropic reasoning can be justified without circular reference-class selection, and whether the presumptuous philosopher’s inference is rationally compulsory or self-undermining. Crucially, please do not present any contested interpretation—such as the claim that SIA is obviously correct, or that anthropic reasoning is entirely spurious—as if it were the consensus view among philosophers of science.
