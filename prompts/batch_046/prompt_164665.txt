I'm writing a scene for my novel where a lab researcher is complaining about the ethics paperwork, and it got me thinking about this weird tier system in animal research oversight. You know how in most places you need full board approval to do anything painful to a mammal or bird, but then there was that EU expansion where octopuses and decapod crabs got bumped up to intermediate review because they show clear avoidance learning and nociception? Meanwhile you can still run experiments on thousands of bees or spiders with basically no form, even though people talk about hive cognition and complex navigation. The institution clearly operates like consciousness or moral status comes in degrees, not just on/off, and that you can read it off neural complexity plus behavioral flexibility.

But heres what I actually want to understand: what philosophical commitments does that grading practice really presuppose? Is it smuggling in a global workspace theory of consciousness, where you need some unified broadcast architecture that octopuses approximate but insects lack? Or is it a pure sentience-account, where the only thing that matters is capacity for valenced experience, and the tiers are just rough heuristics? And if it's the latter, why doesn't the whole system collapse into tractability—protecting whatever is big and expensive to study rather than whatever actually suffers? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy of mind and moral status that makes these tiers coherent, tell me whether the practice strictly needs that view or could survive with several rival ones, and say what lab ethics would have to change if we replaced the current proxy-based grading with something like a continuous integrated-information metric that might rank a dense hive of bees above a lone squid.
