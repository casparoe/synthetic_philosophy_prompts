Act as a professional philosopher of language and metaethics serving as a debate coach. A speaker will defend, in a philosophy of language seminar, the view that the Frege-Geach problem for moral expressivism dissolves if we treat embedded moral sentences as performing a different speech act—attributing an attitude—rather than expressing the same attitude within a compositional structure; the speaker labels this position "illocutionary pluralism." The audience comprises committed expressivists familiar with the standard responses of Blackburn and Gibbard, quietists who regard the problem as pseudo-philosophical, and naturalists who take compositional failure as proof that moral discourse must be descriptively structured. Before answering, take a moment to think carefully about the conceptual tensions between speech-act theory and compositional semantics. Then provide: (1) the three most damaging objections, ordered by likelihood, phrased exactly as each faction would phrase them; (2) for each, what the speaker should concede, what they must hold firm on, where their reply is weakest, and a concise response suitable for a brief exchange; and (3) the hidden objection the speaker fears most—namely, how to account for sincerely asserting a moral conditional without actually holding the embedded moral attitude—together with advice on addressing it without sounding evasive or retreating to psychologism. Focus on conceptual coherence; do not provide citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
