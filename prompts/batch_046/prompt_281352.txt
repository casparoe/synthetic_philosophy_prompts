Plan a ninety-minute seminar session for advanced undergraduates in history and philosophy of science on the politics of archival knowledge—specifically, on how archives produce silence structurally rather than merely suffering from accidental loss. The target concept is what I will call categorical foreclosure: the condition in which an archive's classification schema makes certain social relations not just unrecorded but formally unrecordable, because the categories needed to name them do not exist within the apparatus. The students have encountered basic primary-source criticism but have not studied critical archival theory. They will likely arrive believing that archival bias is a matter of whose voices were included in the record, and that missing voices represent gaps that could be filled by more inclusive collection.

I need the plan structured as an explanatory arc rather than as a delivered lecture. First, specify the dependency order: which conceptual distinction must be secured before categorical foreclosure can be introduced—particularly, the difference between absence as missing data (negative space) and absence as a positive product of the recording form? Why must that distinction come first?

Second, construct the carrying example. Design one concrete archival scenario—perhaps a nineteenth-century colonial botanical survey that records specimens by Latin binomial and precise geographic coordinate, but that renders indigenous cultivation networks invisible not because observers failed to notice them, but because those networks operate through relational obligations that the survey's discrete-property taxonomy cannot register. Specify exactly what properties the example must possess and what it must lack (for instance, avoid cases of physical destruction or deliberate suppression) so that students see the taxonomy itself generating the silence.

Third, map the misconceptions in the sequence they will likely arise. I anticipate at least three: (a) interpreting the missing cultivation networks as information that was simply never written down; (b) assuming that adding more diverse voices or sources to the archive repairs the foreclosure; (c) conflating categorical foreclosure with intentional censorship or propaganda. At which point in the dependency chain should each misconception be intercepted?

Fourth, propose a check for understanding—one task or question at the session's end that reveals whether a student has grasped that the silence is embedded in the schema rather than being an empty space within it.

Finally, declare explicitly what must be left out at this level—such as advanced post-structuralist theories of the trace, or quantitative methods for missing-data imputation—and state that these should not be introduced.

Keep the plan concrete: the order of introduction, the design of the example, the placement of misconception interventions, the final check, and the declared omissions.
