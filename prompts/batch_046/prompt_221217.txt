so i was arguing with a friend about those constitutional rules that are supposed to tie future governments' hands -- like fiscal caps with emergency overrides, or rights provisions that can only be suspended by a 3/4 vote. we kept noticing the same weird loop. the designers always put in a backdoor: some crisis clause or sunset mechanism that's technically available but designed to be politically radioactive. then in practice either nobody uses it and the rule binds even when it's actively harmful, or politicians start calling routine budget fights 'emergencies' and the backdoor gets used constantly. but here's the part that feels like it needs its own name: defenders use both outcomes as proof the system works. if the backdoor hasn't been touched in decades, they say 'see, the constraint is shaping behavior through its mere presence.' if it's being used every other year, they say 'see, it's flexible and responsive, not rigid.' and if you point out that the crisis conditions are being manufactured, they retreat to 'well the mechanism is still technically available, which proves we're not really authoritarian.' it's like the escape clause is doing double duty as both the safety feature and the proof of democratic legitimacy, regardless of whether it's actually constraining anything.

is there an established concept for this specific rhetorical/functional loop? i feel like it's near symbolic constitutionalism or maybe something in the Ulysses-contract literature about failed precommitment, but neither quite captures the way the backdoor is simultaneously celebrated as proof of bindingness and proof of flexibility. if there's no precise term, can you define the phenomenon in a way that distinguishes it from (a) a genuinely well-calibrated safety valve, (b) plain hypocrisy about rule-following, and (c) the kind of strategic ambiguity that's just bad drafting? i want the carving, not just a label -- like, what's the exact structure that makes this different from a rule that just has normal exceptions?
