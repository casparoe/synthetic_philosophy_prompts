Modern financial reporting rules require publicly traded firms to separate operating income—derived from selling goods or services—from financial income generated by capital returns, interest, or investment gains, and securities regulators apply different disclosure standards to each category. This institutional separation treats revenue from labor and production as conceptually distinct from revenue generated by money itself. What philosophical commitments about the nature of money, value, and exchange does this accounting practice presuppose? Does it implicitly depend on an Aristotelian distinction between oikonomia, the natural management of resources for use, and chrematistike, the unnatural acquisition of wealth through exchange and usury—particularly on the claim that money is barren and cannot legitimately breed more money? Or can the practice be justified on purely pragmatic grounds that avoid any metaphysical view of money's productivity? And if a jurisdiction adopted Aristotle's position that interest-bearing growth is unnatural because currency lacks the generative capacity of seeds or livestock, which elements of this reporting framework—if any—would become philosophically incoherent rather than merely administratively inconvenient?
