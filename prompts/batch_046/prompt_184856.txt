I've just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether generational labels—'Boomer', 'Millennial', 'Gen Z'—denote real social kinds or are merely reified marketing cohorts. I've come to accept the eliminativist view: that generational membership lacks downward causal closure and modal robustness, and that every apparent cohort effect dissolves under rigorous age-period-cohort decomposition. But I want to know what I'm actually risking. What evidence or argument would count decisively against this—say, predictive patterns that resist decomposition, or proof that cohort membership carries irreducible causal powers? What would be merely awkward rather than fatal? And what would defenders of this eliminativism themselves concede as counting against them, not only what critics say? Has the view already been qualified into unfalsifiability by treating every counter-example as a disguised period effect? If so, is that insulation appropriate for a claim about social ontology, or does it render the position vacuous?
