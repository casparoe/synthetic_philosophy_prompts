I write on philosophy and the cognitive sciences for a general educated audience, and I am preparing a session for an interdisciplinary reading group — about ten postgraduate students in philosophy and data science, meeting for ninety minutes in a seminar room with movable tables. I want them to feel, rather than merely assent to, the force of Goodman's new riddle of induction, specifically the problem of projectibility: why do we privilege predicates that apply consistently across time over rivals that fit every past observation equally well? To keep the exercise from collapsing into the familiar emerald example, please inject a synthetic scenario in which participants themselves invent vocabulary for describing tokens drawn from an opaque container; one of their invented predicates is temporally indexed — it applies to tokens drawn before a hidden cutoff and fails afterwards — while a rival applies consistently. Both fit every observation made during the first half of play, yet they mandate opposite predictions for the second half.

Please design a structured in-class game — with concrete rules, inexpensive materials (paper, tokens, cards), assigned roles, a point-based payoff scheme rather than real money, and a clear timeline — that makes participants discover the underdetermination of prediction through their own binding bets and defences before anyone names Goodman or introduces the term grue. In the second half, participants must stake points on future draws and justify their choice of predicate in brief deliberation. The debrief should map specific moments — the collapse of unanimous agreement, appeals to linguistic habit, the realisation that evidence alone does not settle the future — onto Goodman's distinction between projectible and non-projectible hypotheses, without assuming prior reading of Fact, Fiction, and Forecast. Include an honest account of what will predictably go wrong on a first run (for instance, participants treating the hidden cutoff as a puzzle to solve rather than a philosophical problem) and how to correct it. Also state frankly which feature of Goodman's view — particularly the claim that projectibility is ultimately fixed by entrenched linguistic practice rather than by any feature of nature — the physical setup cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; if you reference Goodman, paraphrase without quotation marks or verify any wording from a source.

Present your design in the following sections, with approximate lengths: 'The Setup and Materials' (around 150 words); 'Rules, Roles, and Timeline' (around 250 words); 'The Debrief: From Play to Philosophy' (around 200 words); 'Predictable Failures and How to Handle Them' (around 150 words); and 'The Honest Limit' (around 100 words). Write in British English.
