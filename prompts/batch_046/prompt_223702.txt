I'm taking a seminar on social ontology and the metaphysics of social groups, and after class two of my classmates got into an extended disagreement about whether demographic cohorts—specifically "Baby Boomers," "Millennials," and "Gen Z"—count as real kinds. I remained neutral and would like you to adjudicate their exchange move by move.

Maya opened by arguing that generations cannot be natural kinds because the birth-year boundaries (for instance, 1981–1996 for Millennials) are administratively convenient but metaphysically arbitrary, like drawing lines through a continuous gradient. She added that within any cohort there is extreme heterogeneity in values, economic conditions, and technological access, so there is no shared essence or stable cluster of homeostatic properties that would justify treating "Millennial" as a kind term rather than as a statistical artifact.

Jordan replied that this criterion is implausibly strict: no social category, including race, gender, or nationality, survives essentialism, yet many remain genuinely explanatory in sociology and history. He pointed out that shared formative experiences—the 2008 financial crisis for Millennials, the COVID-19 pandemic for Gen Z—produce stable causal patterns in voting behavior and consumption, which is precisely what we require from practical kinds. He conceded that the boundaries are fuzzy, but noted that biological species also have vague borders, so fuzziness alone does not disqualify generations.

Maya then introduced what she called the looping problem: once sociologists and marketers name a generation and circulate descriptions of its traits, members begin to perform those very traits, meaning the observed regularities are partly self-fulfilling. She claimed this undermines any claim to mind-independent existence, placing generations closer to invented classifications than to discovered ones.

Jordan conceded that looping occurs but insisted it does not dissolve kindhood. He noted that psychiatric categories and the historical concept of adolescence also loop, yet they remain causally robust and predictively useful. He accused Maya of implicitly assuming that only mind-independent kinds are real, calling this a controversial metaphysical premise she had not defended.

Maya's final move was that predictive utility is insufficient for kindhood: astrology also generates behavioral correlations because people act according to stereotypes, yet we do not treat zodiac signs as real kinds. She argued that unless there is a non-circular mechanism linking birth cohort to behavior—independent of the labeling practice itself—the category remains a useful fiction rather than a genuine kind.

Jordan responded that there is indeed such a mechanism—cohort-specific exposure to macroeconomic shocks and technological regimes—but he admitted he could not specify it precisely for every trait commonly attributed to Gen Z.

Please go through each move in order—Maya's initial argument, Jordan's reply, Maya's looping objection, Jordan's concession, Maya's astrology analogy, and Jordan's final admission—and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing. In particular, address whether there is a principled middle position between essentialist natural kinds and pure nominalism that applies specifically to cohort-based groups, and whether the debate needs to distinguish between "generation" as a demographic measure and "generation" as a cultural identity.
