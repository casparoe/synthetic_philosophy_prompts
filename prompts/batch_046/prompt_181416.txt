I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been studying philosophy of science and decision theory. I now serve on the technical review panel of a national health-technology assessment body, where we evaluate, roughly twice a month, whether the performance metrics proposed for AI-driven clinical tools are vulnerable to specification gaming in the sense articulated by Goodhart's law and the subsequent literature on reward hacking. I need a reusable decision procedure—not a philosophical essay—that a panelist with scientific training but no formal background in economics or AI safety can apply in about thirty minutes per proposal. The deliverable should be an ordered checklist with explicit decision nodes, and it must distinguish three classes of proxy: those causally intermediate between the intervention and the true clinical endpoint; those merely correlated with it; and composite indices built from sub-metrics that are individually optimizable. For each class, specify what evidence is required to treat the proxy as safe, what warning signs indicate gaming risk, and—crucially—where the panel must make an explicit judgment call rather than apply a formal rule, with those nodes clearly flagged. Please apply the procedure to two hypothetical proposals: first, a sepsis-prediction algorithm for which the sole optimization target is reduction in thirty-day hospital readmission rates; second, a multi-dimensional mental-health triage system whose dimensions—response latency, patient-reported satisfaction, and clinician workload—are each measurable and contractible, but where simultaneous improvement across all three may degrade the unmeasured construct of appropriate care allocation. The second case should yield a recommendation that the proposing clinical team will likely resist. The answer should state plainly what the procedure cannot determine, should contain no bibliographies or reading lists, and should be written in British English. I write in American English and ask that the response observe British spelling and conventions throughout.
