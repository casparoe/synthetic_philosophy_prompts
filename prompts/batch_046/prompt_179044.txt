As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I have been reasoning about whether software modules can genuinely possess capabilities, and I have constructed an argument that seems to show they cannot. The conclusion strikes me as clearly false — we build entire architectures on the assumption that services really can authenticate, parse, or route — yet each step looks defensible. Here is the chain:

(1) A module M genuinely has capability C only if, under triggering conditions K, M produces output B, and this production is not blocked by an interposed guard.

(2) For any module M, any capability C, and any conditions K, it is always possible in principle to insert a mask or interceptor — call it F — that detects K and prevents B from occurring, without altering M's internal code.

(3) Because such a finkish mask is always constructible, M never actually satisfies the unguarded condition in (1).

(4) Therefore, no software module genuinely possesses any capability; all functional attributions are merely provisional.

(5) Yet engineering practice treats modules as genuinely capable and relies on those attributions for reliability.

(6) Hence, if (5) is correct, then (1) must be false, which means either capabilities do not require unguarded production, or "genuine possession" means something weaker than I assumed.

I am inclined to preserve (5) and reject (1), but I want to understand exactly where the reasoning slips: is there a hidden modal fallacy in moving from "a mask is always possible" to "the capability is never present"? Or is the error in treating an external blocker as internal to the module's disposition? If I keep (5), what does giving up (1) commit me to about how dispositions work in physical or computational systems? And what further case — perhaps a module whose internal state itself changes when a mask is present — would distinguish the correct account from the others?
