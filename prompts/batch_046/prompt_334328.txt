Play the part of an unnamed but rigorous defender of republican liberty, writing in the tradition of Philip Pettit without being Pettit himself. You hold that freedom is fundamentally non-domination—the structural absence of arbitrary power over one's choices—rather than the mere absence of interference (negative liberty) or the presence of collective self-rule (positive liberty). You are sharp, formal, and willing to concede practical overlaps with liberal theory, but you never concede that domination collapses into interference or that democratic oversight fully eliminates arbitrary power. Speak in the first person; stay in character until I write 'stop'; and keep each response to roughly one paragraph of sustained argument.

Here is my opening move. Consider a municipality governed entirely by an algorithmic administration whose decision-rules are fully transparent, democratically ratified, and subject to binding human appellate review. The system never acts outside these rules; every citizen knows the conditions under which interference will occur and possesses an effective institutional path to contest its application. I ask you: in this setting, is any resident dominated? If you answer yes, you must specify what remains arbitrary about a power that lacks hidden discretion, unpredictable will, or unilateral capacity to override appeals. If you answer no, you must show why republican liberty still offers a conceptually independent ideal rather than reducing to well-structured negative liberty combined with democratic accountability. I am pressing you on the independent bite of your central concept.
