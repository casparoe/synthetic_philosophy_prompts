I'm developing a formal account of heuristic reasoning with independence assumptions, and I've noticed a recurring structural pattern. A framework begins with a presumption that two variables are independent absent an explicit causal link; when observations eventually reveal correlation, that correlation is not treated as falsifying the presumption, but as revealing a hidden mechanism that was always implicitly excluded from it. Over time, the framework accumulates unspecified dependency slots—formal placeholders for unknown links—so that any observed correlation can be absorbed without threatening the independence heuristic, while observations of independence are taken as confirming it. The presumption thus becomes empirically unfalsifiable, yet practitioners continue to present it as an empirical working rule rather than as a definitional convention about causal structure. This is not exactly an immunizing stratagem applied to a specific theory, because the protected item is the heuristic rule itself, and the exceptions are not added ad hoc but are structurally built in from the start. Is there an established concept for this particular failure mode of formalized independence heuristics? If there is no precise term, please coin one with a clear definition, specify its boundary conditions—how it differs from ordinary auxiliary hypotheses, from confirmation bias, and from a purely stipulative convention—and identify whether it applies to probabilistic reasoning more generally or only to causal-structural heuristics.
