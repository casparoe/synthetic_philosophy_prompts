In preparing my graduate seminar on philosophical methodology, I have been tracking the provisional judgments of a senior colleague—Dr. Aris—regarding which thought experiments genuinely pump intuitions and which mislead through hidden framing. Her record is consistent but, I suspect, underdetermined by the principles she explicitly invokes. She accepts Jackson’s black-and-white room scenario as legitimate, holding that it successfully isolates phenomenal knowledge from physical description without distorting either domain. She rejects Searle’s Chinese Room as misleading, arguing that its staging suppresses the possibility that understanding might be distributed across syntactic processes rather than localized in a single internal interpreter. She endorses the switch-version of the trolley problem, maintaining that it cleanly separates direct from indirect agency while holding the quantitative stakes constant. She similarly approves Nozick’s experience machine, judging that it stably isolates the value of reality-contact regardless of interface design. Finally, she dismisses Thomson’s violinist analogy as illegitimate, claiming that its framing suppresses relational and gestational features that are conceptually central to the abortion debate it purports to illuminate.

Next week I will present a new case for her evaluation, one I have devised to probe exactly where her criteria lie: the Probabilistic Archivist. A historian wears a neural interface linked not to a database but to a predictive model trained on the entire digitized corpus of nineteenth-century diplomatic archives. The model stores no specific documents; it generates answers through high-dimensional probabilistic reconstruction of what a source likely said. Over time the historian’s own recall atrophies, yet she experiences the outputs as immediate and authoritative—subjectively indistinguishable from memory. The interface is then severed, leaving her unable to retrieve anything. The pump asks whether, during the coupling, she genuinely knew the diplomatic facts, and whether the location of the predictive mechanism—internal, external, or distributed—matters to that judgment.

I would like you to predict Dr. Aris’s verdict on the Probabilistic Archivist, identify the implicit rule she is running—whether it concerns successful variable isolation, framing transparency, cross-contextual robustness of intuition, or something else altogether—and map the other rules that fit her five earlier judgments equally well. Please indicate which rules diverge on this new case, state what each predicts, offer your best guess about her actual position with reasons, and specify the further hypothetical case that would adjudicate between the competing interpretations of her track record. I am less interested in a final metaphysical resolution about extended knowledge than in a precise diagnosis of the methodological principle at work.
