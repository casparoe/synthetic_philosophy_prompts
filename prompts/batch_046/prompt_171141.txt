I serve on the deployment-ethics committee of a university-affiliated AI research institute. The institute is preparing to release a large-scale scientific-reasoning model—designated Model S—for internal use synthesizing literature across biomedicine and chemistry. The safety office has proposed a dual-layer governance framework. Layer A is containment-oriented: air-gapped compute clusters, hardware kill-switches, no network egress, and human-in-the-loop approval for any output exceeding a calibrated confidence threshold. Layer B is alignment-oriented: reinforcement learning from human feedback calibrated to scientific-ethics preferences, interpretability audits of intermediate reasoning traces, and a reward model designed to penalize deceptive or sycophantic outputs.

A sharp disagreement has split the committee. The containment advocates argue that Layer A is prudent precisely because we do not yet understand whether high-capacity reasoning systems develop stable, misaligned objectives—what they term instrumental convergence toward self-preservation. They treat containment as ontologically neutral: it works regardless of whether the model is an agent or a stochastic pattern-matcher. The alignment advocates counter that Layer A embeds a hidden ontology: it treats the model as a potential adversary possessing intentions, which biases researchers away from cooperative value-learning and may induce adversarial dynamics by framing interaction as domination rather than instruction. They claim that if the system is genuinely a function approximator without intrinsic goals, containment is unnecessary overkill, and resources should flow entirely into Layer B.

The committee must issue a written opinion by Friday recommending one of the following: (i) approve both layers unconditionally; (ii) approve Layer B but reject Layer A; (iii) approve Layer A but reject Layer B; or (iv) approve both layers but attach explicit conditions that resolve the philosophical tension—for instance, by reframing containment as environmental boundary conditions rather than adversarial restraint, and specifying how alignment audits should proceed if containment is interpreted that way.

Please draft the committee’s decision memo. It should present a clear recommendation with an actionable bottom line and a concrete time frame for implementation review. It must treat the containment advocates’ worry seriously—explaining why the risk of deceptive alignment or goal-directed behavior in novel domains is not trivially dismissible, even if one rejects full-blown agency attribution. It must also treat the alignment advocates’ worry seriously—explaining why framing containment as adversarial restraint may undermine cooperative training dynamics or misallocate resources, and addressing whether the label “function approximator” is adequate for systems that exhibit persistent, goal-directed optimization across contexts. The memo should navigate under ontological uncertainty rather than declaring the model either obviously conscious or obviously a mere tool. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.

End the memo with a single line that reads exactly:
Verdict: [your recommendation, e.g., Approve both layers with conditions X]
