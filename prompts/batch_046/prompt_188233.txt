I'm working through a philosophy-of-mind seminar and have constructed an argument about personal identity that feels suspicious because its conclusion is far stronger than I intended, yet I cannot see exactly where the slide occurs. The view I'm testing is the familiar anti-Cartesian position that the self is not a metaphysical substance but a center of narrative gravity—an abstract focal point around which experiences, intentions, and actions are organized into a coherent autobiography. To push the view, I introduced a notion I'm calling biographical focal elasticity: the range over which the same life events can be reorganized around different focal points without loss of overall narrative coherence. Here is the chain I built.

(1) If the self is just a center of narrative gravity, then being a self is being whatever focal point makes an autobiography maximally coherent.
(2) Human lives exhibit extremely high biographical focal elasticity: the same biographical material can be coherently structured around focal points as different as the ambitious professional, the survivor of a specific trauma, the devoted sibling, or the person managing a chronic condition, each yielding a fully coherent narrative with its own explanatory priorities and emotional arc.
(3) If narrative coherence alone determines what is real in this domain, then each of these focal points is an equally valid center of narrative gravity within the same body.
(4) But if there are genuinely many such centers, then singular moral and legal responsibility collapses: when the body performs an action, there is no non-arbitrary fact about which narrative center authored it, so holding the person responsible requires selecting one focal point by convention rather than discovery.
(5) Our practices of responsibility do not treat the agent as a conventionally selected focal point; they presuppose exactly one continuous author per body whose actions flow from a single, stable narrative center.
(6) Therefore, either narrative coherence is insufficient to constitute the self, or our entire framework of singular moral agency is systematically illusory.
(7) The second option is unacceptable, so the narrative-gravity view must be supplemented by a non-narrative constraint that fixes exactly one center per body—but adding such a constraint undermines the anti-Cartesian motivation that made the view attractive in the first place.

Where does this go wrong? I would prefer to keep both the idea that the self originates in narrative organization (something like 1) and the idea that our practices of singular responsibility are not merely conventional selections (something like 5). Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? In particular, is there an equivocation between a coherent narrative can be told with focal point X and there actually exists a self with focal point X, or is the error elsewhere—perhaps in how I move from multiplicity of possible narratives to multiplicity of actual agents?
