Question: Quine's rejection of the analytic-synthetic distinction and his criterion of ontological commitment are often invoked to argue that moral values have no special philosophical status. Evaluate whether Quine's naturalism leaves room for moral values to possess genuine normative force, or whether they are reduced to descriptive patterns within behavioral science. In your answer, address the following three points precisely: (i) Apply Quine's ontological commitment criterion correctly, specifying which theories (not only physics) determine what exists; (ii) Explain the role of the web of belief in revising moral judgments, being careful about which beliefs are central versus peripheral; and (iii) Consider whether the distinction between normative indispensability (the claim that normative language is indispensable to social-scientific explanation) and ontological indispensability (the Quinean claim that entities are real if they are values of bound variables in our best total theory) helps Quine accommodate ethics without reducing it to description.

Student answer:

Quine's naturalism denies any a priori foundation for philosophy, so ethics must be continuous with empirical science. Because Quine rejects the analytic-synthetic distinction entirely, he shows that moral claims are synthetic rather than analytic, which means they are all revisable in light of experience. This already weakens their normative force, since nothing in morality is protected from revision.

On ontological commitment, Quine says we are only committed to entities that appear as values of bound variables in our best scientific theories. Since physics and biology do not quantify over moral properties like wrongness, Quine should conclude that such properties do not exist. Nevertheless, Quine allows mathematics to exist as an indispensable cultural posit even though numbers are not observable, so moral values can be treated the same way: they are cultural posits that lack literal existence but remain practically useful.

Regarding the web of belief, Quine holds that beliefs near the periphery are protected from direct empirical refutation, while central beliefs are easily revised. Moral judgments sit near the periphery because they are about particular actions rather than general laws, so they are shielded from falsification and can maintain stable normative guidance. At the same time, their peripheral location means they can be adjusted when they conflict with more central scientific beliefs.

Finally, normative indispensability differs from ontological indispensability in that the former concerns explanatory utility in social science, while the latter concerns quantification in our total theory. Quine could accept that moral language is normatively indispensable for predicting behavior, but because it is not ontologically indispensable—there are no moral variables in physics—he must still regard moral values as merely descriptive regularities that we find convenient to retain.
