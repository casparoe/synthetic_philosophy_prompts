I am a sixth-form student preparing an extended essay on philosophical approaches to public history, and I should like to analyse the June 2020 toppling of the Edward Colston statue in Bristol — together with the subsequent debate over displaying the damaged monument in the M Shed museum — through the lens of the conflict between collective memory and critical historical record. The statue had long served as a site of civic remembrance, yet the archival record demonstrates Colston’s central role in the Royal African Company and the forced transportation of enslaved people. Please treat the episode not merely as a dispute about heritage, but as a test case for whether collective memory ought to yield to documentary history when the two come into direct conflict. In particular, examine whether the claim that memory should be corrected only when the record demonstrates “systematic, quantifiable harm” rather than mere moral disagreement is itself a hidden normative commitment, and whether applying that threshold to Colston reveals an unacknowledged bias about whose suffering counts in public commemoration. I want you to say precisely what the collective-memory framework explains about the defenders’ attachment to the monument, what the historical-record framework explains about the protesters’ demands, and where each framework is silent — for example, on who possesses legitimate standing to decide what should replace the statue in public space. Finally, indicate whether the episode confirms that collective memory is irreducibly political, or whether it shows that critical history can supply a neutral arbitration in conflicts over commemoration, and note any feature of the case that the theory never anticipated.
