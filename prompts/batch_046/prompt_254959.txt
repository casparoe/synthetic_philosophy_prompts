I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I have been invited to present at an interdisciplinary symposium on collective decision-making hosted by a university philosophy department and a science-studies center. My thesis is that Spinoza’s unfinished political treatise—especially his account of the multitudo—should be read not as a proto-social-contract theory but through his geometric method: democratic stability is the emergent equilibrium of what I call a “conatus network,” a distributed field of individual striving that resembles modern complex-systems models of collective behavior far more than it resembles Hobbesian sovereignty or Rousseau’s general will. The audience will be sharply divided. Roughly half are historians of early modern philosophy who work with the Latin texts closely and will spot any anachronism instantly; about a quarter are political theorists deeply skeptical of importing contemporary network science into seventeenth-century writing; the rest are science journalists and complexity researchers who understand distributed dynamics well but have little training in Spinoza’s Ethics or his political writings. Please prepare me for the Q&A as a strategic speaking sheet, not a literature review. List the objections I will actually face, ordered first by likelihood and then by potential damage. For each, phrase it precisely as that audience segment—historian, political theorist, or complexity researcher—would actually say it; provide a reply I can deliver in about two minutes without notes; and flag the softest point in that reply. Include the single question I am hoping nobody asks—likely one that forces me to justify treating conatus as a network-level property rather than an individual one on strictly textual grounds—and tell me exactly how to answer it if it comes. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I need tactical preparation, not references.
