I am organizing a student-led colloquium interview on contested grand narratives in deep history, and next week's guest is a historian of early societies—let us call him Dr. K—who defends a rehabilitated determinism he terms "institutional-path dependency." His specific claim is that Diamond's east-west axis thesis survives only if biological domestication is replaced by legal-institutional domestication: the decisive variable is not local availability of wild domesticates, but whether emergent property regimes made long-distance propagule exchange irreversible, thereby locking societies into agrarian surplus accumulation. He further contends that Graeber and Wengrow's documented cases of seasonal social dualism and conscious rejection of hierarchy are not counter-examples, but precisely the political forms that arise where such institutional irreversibility failed to take hold. I need eight to ten rigorous interview questions that do not ask him to restate this framework. Specifically, include: one question pressing how he explains independent domestication centers in the Americas despite weak east-west diffusion and the absence of large-scale property regimes; one question confronting the charge that treating surplus storage as an institutional effect rather than an ecological cause collapses his determinism into political voluntarism; one question asking whether his appeal to "irreversible exchange" implicitly relies on Harari-style shared fictions about ownership, and if so, how he avoids committing to Harari's cognitive-revolution chronology; and one question probing what specific archaeological signature—beyond grain storage itself—would distinguish his institutional-lock-in model from Graeber and Wengrow's politically-seasonal alternative. For each of these four, provide a calibrated follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. The questions should be suitable for an audience of advanced high-school students familiar with the primary texts, respectful in tone, but designed to expose the structural tensions in his synthesis.
