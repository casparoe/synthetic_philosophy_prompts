I defend the position that the large-scale conversion of wild ecosystems—particularly high-biomass, r-selected habitats—into human-managed agricultural or degraded landscapes tends to reduce net wild-animal suffering, not only through direct mortality but chiefly by suppressing the reproductive biomass that would otherwise generate enormous numbers of short-lived, welfare-negative lives. The view rests on three premises: that wild ecosystems are structurally dominated by suffering rather than flourishing, that anthropogenic landscapes sustain far lower wild-animal biomass and reproductive output per unit area, and that the suffering of displaced individuals is outweighed by the suffering averted in the counterfactual generations that are never born. What evidence, arguments, formal results, or empirical observations would genuinely count against this position? I want these sorted by how decisive they would be, including observations that have arguably already occurred—such as evidence that agricultural matrices support comparable suffering-weighted biomass through rodent, insect, and edge-species proliferation; ecological models showing that post-disturbance succession rapidly lowers r-selection pressure without requiring permanent conversion; or philosophical arguments that the counterfactual comparison required by the prevented-lives claim is incoherent or morally irrelevant. I also want to know whether defenders of this view have qualified it into unfalsifiability—for instance, by retreating to unmeasurable claims about potential suffering—and whether such insulation is a defect for a normative thesis of this kind. What should the position's own proponents concede as damaging, rather than only what critics allege? And what concrete predictions does the view risk—such as that rapid deforestation should be welfare-positive even amid mass mortality events—such that a failure of those predictions would genuinely weaken the case?
