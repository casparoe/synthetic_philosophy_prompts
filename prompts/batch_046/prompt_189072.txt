I run a 60-minute lunch-and-learn for about 15 software engineers with no formal philosophy background, and I want an in-room simulation that makes them feel the force of Searle’s Chinese Room argument rather than just hear it explained. Design a structured activity for this setting: split the group into “processors,” each holding only a fragment of a symbol-matching rulebook for an invented script—no single person can see the full rules or understand the script—then feed them input cards and require the collective to produce correct “responses.” Include specific roles, rules, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a payoff or scoring mechanism that rewards accurate collective output without requiring individual comprehension. After the simulation, provide a debrief that maps what happened onto syntax, semantics, intentionality, and the system-reply objection. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong on the first run—such as one participant reverse-engineering the full rulebook—and how to prevent it. Also be honest about which feature of the argument, likely the subjective first-person quality of understanding, the activity cannot reproduce, and tell me exactly how the facilitator should supply it verbally. Keep everything self-contained: no bibliographies, no citation requests, and no dependence on outside reading.
