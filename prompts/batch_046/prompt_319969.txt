I am drafting a short philosophical essay tentatively titled "Extinction versus Void: A Temporal Asymmetry in Tranquilism." The core position—call it tranquilism—is that well-being consists solely in the absence of craving and suffering, with no intrinsic axiological weight assigned to pleasure, joy, or desire-satisfaction. I want to push this position into somewhat unfamiliar territory rather than rehearsing standard debates about hedonism or desire-fulfillment.

Here are the notes and argument sketch I intend to develop:

1. Baseline tranquilism holds that mental states have value only negatively. The presence of eudaimonic goods, aesthetic pleasure, or satisfied preferences adds nothing to well-being if craving and suffering are already absent.

2. I then introduce a distinction that tranquilist treatments rarely address: between extinction-tranquility, in which craving and suffering have actively ceased after previously existing, and void-tranquility, in which they have never arisen (as in the mental life of a being that never developed the relevant cognitive architecture).

3. My tentative claim is that extinction-tranquility is axiologically superior to void-tranquility, even when the present phenomenology is identical—no craving, no suffering, neutral affect. The reasoning is that extinction involves a transformative restructuring of the conditions that generate suffering, producing a kind of stabilized absence that is more robust and perhaps phenomenologically different at a sub-personal level.

4. I anticipate an objection: this smuggles a historical or causal condition into an otherwise present-state theory. If tranquilism values only current absence, then causal history should be irrelevant. I consider a reply that the value lies not in the history itself but in the present structural property of being extinction-capable, which is a present feature, though one that is only instantiated through prior transformation.

5. Finally, I contrast this with standard negative utilitarianism, which cares about suffering-minimization across populations but does not necessarily privilege the internal structure of individual mental states. I also briefly note that Buddhist conceptions of nibbana might seem closer to extinction-tranquility, though I do not develop a textual interpretation.

I would appreciate comments and suggestions on the following points:

- Is the distinction between extinction-tranquility and void-tranquility philosophically coherent, or does it collapse once we commit to a purely present-state evaluation of well-being?
- Does the reply about extinction-capability successfully avoid importing historical conditions, or is it an ad hoc rescue?
- Is the essay likely to be interesting to readers in value theory and philosophy of mind, or does the injection of this niche distinction make the argument seem contrived?
- Are there any obvious inaccuracies in my characterization of tranquilism relative to negative hedonism or to Buddhist accounts of craving and suffering?

Please focus your comments on the argument structure and conceptual coherence rather than on style or on suggestions for further reading; I am not asking for a bibliography, citation list, or source recommendations.
