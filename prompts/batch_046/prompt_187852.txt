I'm a high school student writing an independent ethics paper on consciousness-preservation technologies, and I want to understand a concrete scenario I'm stuck on. A patient with a progressive neurodegenerative disease spends a decade receiving synthetic neural implants. These implants do not merely replace lost tissue; they also maintain a continuously updated remote processing layer that synchronizes with the remaining biological brain every night. By the end of the process, roughly sixty percent of cognition runs on the remote layer, yet the patient reports feeling like one continuous person. Now the biological brain is failing irreversibly, and the family must choose: either retire the biological substrate and let the remote layer continue running independently, or keep the remote layer permanently subordinate to the dying tissue until both stop. The patient fears that letting the remote layer outlive the brain makes it an impostor wearing copied memories, but also fears that tying it to the dying brain destroys the only functioning version of the consciousness that has evolved over ten years. From a philosophical standpoint, is one choice genuinely more respectful of preserving the original person, or is the demand for a single true continuation itself a conceptual mistake? Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study personal identity and philosophy of mind, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
