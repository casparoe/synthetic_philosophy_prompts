I have recently found myself in professional discussions about whether my own field is undergoing something like a Kuhnian crisis, and I have returned to Kuhn’s work to understand what a paradigm shift actually entails. A colleague summarized his position to me as follows: Kuhn holds that rival scientific paradigms are globally incommensurable—there is no shared observational language, no common standard of evaluation, and no neutral framework from which to adjudicate between them. Because of this, she said, scientific revolutions are never rationally compelled by evidence; they occur only through social persuasion and generational replacement, analogous to religious conversions. She added that this means scientists before and after a revolution literally inhabit different worlds, with no shared reality underlying both descriptions, and that Kuhn therefore rejects any notion of progress toward truth, substituting instead a purely evolutionary, sociological model in which theories are never more accurate, only differently adapted to local problem-solving contexts.

How fair is this summary? I would like an accuracy audit that separates what is faithful to Kuhn’s actual arguments—particularly his later technical account of local taxonomic incommensurability—from what is a standard exaggeration or a conflation with stronger relativist positions. Specifically: does Kuhn’s thesis entail that paradigms share no evaluative standards whatsoever, or is the incommensurability restricted to specific lexical taxonomies? Is the claim about inhabiting different worlds an ontological commitment or a metaphor for conceptual restructuring? And does Kuhn’s evolutionary analogy genuinely eliminate all cognitive progress, or does it preserve a form of non-teleological improvement? Finally, if we correct these points, does her conclusion—that paradigm choice is therefore irrational and evidence-independent—still follow?
