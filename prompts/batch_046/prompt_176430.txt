I cover democratic theory and formal social choice, and I have watched the debate over judgment aggregation—specifically the tension between premise-based and conclusion-based group decision rules—settle into a familiar rhythm without resolution. The core problem is well known: a group can maintain logical consistency across every individual premise and yet arrive at an inconsistent collective verdict, or maintain consistency at the conclusion level only by overriding what majorities actually believe about the premises. For more than two decades, one camp has insisted that this reveals an irreconcilable conflict between democratic inclusiveness and collective rationality; the other camp responds that the conflict is an artifact of poorly specified agendas and that appropriate independence conditions eliminate the paradox. Both sides accept the formal impossibility results. Both acknowledge that coherence is normatively desirable. Real-world institutions—expert juries in civil-law systems, scientific review panels with nested subcommittees, legislative committees that vote on findings before voting on bills—continue to mix the two procedures without any convergence toward a standard practice.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not empirical—there is little hidden disagreement about how groups actually behave—nor is it purely verbal. Rather, the disagreement is about which normative cost is treated as non-negotiable: local epistemic autonomy over premises, or global logical closure over conclusions. Each side regards the other's priority as a category error, not a mistake that evidence could correct. But I am unsure whether this diagnosis captures everything. Could there be a masked formal disagreement about how independence conditions apply to nested or multi-level agendas—cases where sub-committees control premises but the plenum controls conclusions—such that the standard impossibility theorems do not actually apply? Or is the real problem that the question "should groups be rational?" conflates individual-level norms of belief with group-level properties that do not need to mirror them?

What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure: is it a clash of bedrock values, a hidden formal dispute about agenda conditions in multi-level deliberation, an empirical gap about institutional behavior, or an ill-posed question that applies individual rationality standards where they do not belong? And given that diagnosis, what would count as progress—an institutional design that separates premise authority from conclusion authority, a refined formal result for nested agendas, or a conceptual distinction that shows why the tension is only apparent? I am not asking for a survey of premise-based versus conclusion-based procedures; I am asking what kind of stall this is and whether it should be expected to end.
