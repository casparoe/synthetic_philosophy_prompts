As a philosopher of politics and ethics currently designing both a graduate seminar and a parallel research agenda, I am trying to determine where sustained philosophical effort on corruption should actually be directed over the next several years, rather than where the literature is merely voluminous. The specific nexus I have in mind involves the tension between universalist anti-corruption frameworks and local practices of clientelism and ritual gift-giving—particularly the claim that Western metrics illegitimately universalize a particular historical trajectory of bureaucratic rationalization, and the competing claim that corruption can be defined independently of any specific institutional form.

Within this space, several lines of inquiry present themselves as genuinely open, but I do not wish to treat them as equally deserving of scarce resources. I ask you to prioritize among them—defending your ranking with explicit criteria such as conceptual tractability, normative urgency for policy design in hybrid institutional environments, and how many subsidiary debates depend on resolving this particular crux first. The candidates are these: first, whether there is a coherent universal core to the concept of corruption—perhaps grounded in a breach of fiduciary duty or a distortion of publicly justifiable role-structures—that can accommodate culturally variable practices of patronage and gift-obligation without collapsing into either moral relativism or imperial universalism; second, whether clientelism is best analyzed through a principal-agent framework, as hidden contracting that undermines institutional goals, or through an anthropological framework of moral economy, as a form of reciprocal obligation that competes with rather than parasitizes formal institutions; third, what normative weight the so-called Western-standards objection should carry—whether it functions as a serious methodological critique of cross-national corruption indices, or primarily as a defensive maneuver that obscures local elite capture; and fourth, whether the ethics of corruption can be rigorously separated from its sociology—specifically, whether we can identify corrupt acts and assign blame to them independently of measuring their systemic effects on democratic representation or distributive justice.

Please rank these four inquiries, or substitute others if you judge them more central, and specify which two should command the bulk of a research group’s effort across a three-year cycle. In addition, identify one prominent question in this domain that you believe is currently overrated—either because it rests on a false empirical premise, or because resolving it in either direction would change very little for either philosophical theory or practical institutional design. I am especially interested in whether your prioritization shifts if one assumes the relevant audience is not Western policymakers but local civil-society actors attempting to navigate hybrid institutional environments where formal rules and informal obligations routinely overlap.
