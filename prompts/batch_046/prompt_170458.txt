For an upper-level philosophy of history and social-science final examination (75 minutes; recommended answer length 1000–1200 words), draft one essay question that asks whether the contrastive historical query "Why Britain and not China?"—the framing behind debates over the Great Divergence and the so-called Needham problem—is genuinely well posed as an explanatory target. The question must be unanswerable by mere recitation of Pomeranz's coal-and-colony thesis or Needham's narrative of Chinese scientific development; instead, it should require students to dissect the logical anatomy of contrastive explanation itself.

In particular, instruct the respondent to inject and deploy the following conceptual distinction, which is not part of standard undergraduate summaries: distinguish (a) a "trajectory-divergence" explanation, which presupposes that two cases shared a common path up to a specific branching juncture and then asks why they split, from (b) an "outcome-selection" explanation, which treats the outcome as one realization among many possible worlds and asks why that particular realization obtained, without assuming prior parallelism. Ask students to argue whether the Britain/China comparison illegitimately collapses these two forms—specifically, whether it treats China's pre-industrial economy as a stable equilibrium path that merely needed a trigger to stay on course, thereby smuggling in an unjustified "counterfactual stability condition."

Further, require that the question include a parallel but less canonical comparison—"Why did the Dutch Republic develop durable representative assemblies rather than the Spanish Netherlands during roughly the same period?"—and ask students to apply the same analytical framework to it. The point is to test whether students can identify hidden assumptions about state capacity or geographic determinism that the contrastive structure silently imports, rather than letting them rely on familiar Great Divergence talking points.

Provide a model answer of approximately 600 words that demonstrates the required moves, and provide a marking scheme (total 25 marks) that separates an excellent answer (A-range) from a competent but incomplete one (B-range). The scheme should specify at least five must-hit analytical moves—such as correctly separating the two explanatory forms, diagnosing the counterfactual stability assumption, applying the framework to the Dutch/Spanish case, recognizing where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and avoiding the conflation of explanation with causal narration—with marks allocated to each, and with a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that costs marks for each move (e.g., accepting the stability condition uncritically, treating the Dutch case as mere illustration rather than a structural test, or substituting empirical summary for logical analysis).

The prompt should be self-contained, should not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should not depend on verification of specific publication dates, page numbers, or obscure theoretical labels.
