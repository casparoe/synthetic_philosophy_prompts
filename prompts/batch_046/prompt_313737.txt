I am drafting an essay tentatively titled "Procedural Reciprocity and the Moral Standing of Non-Player Characters," and I would like comments on its conceptual architecture before I develop the full argument. Rather than treating video-game characters as either mere pixels or as full moral persons by analogy, I defend an intermediate position: certain characters become objects of genuine, if structurally limited, moral concern through a mechanism I call procedural reciprocity. By this I mean the situation in which a game's rule-system encodes responsive feedback loops—where harming or aiding a character alters available player actions, narrative pathways, or mechanical rewards in ways that structurally mirror moral recognition—without requiring consciousness or subjective experience. I distinguish this sharply from narrative sympathy (affective response to scripted events) and from instrumental concern (protecting an NPC solely for gameplay utility). I further propose that when procedural reciprocity combines with what I term ludic moral address—the rules framing the player as an agent accountable for outcomes—the result is not mere aesthetic engagement but a form of simulated moral address that generates real, though derivative, obligations of consideration.

The essay will defend three claims: first, that procedural reciprocity is conceptually distinct from both representation and simulation; second, that it creates a normative structure analogous to our obligations toward entities that exhibit reactive behaviors but lack inner lives; and third, that this view does not collapse into absurdity because it requires a threshold condition I call narrative interiority—the character must be written with subjective stakes, not merely instantiated as a damage-receiving object. I anticipate three objections and sketch preliminary responses: that procedural reciprocity merely describes well-designed feedback and confuses mechanics with morality; that comparing NPCs to reactive entities overintellectualizes ordinary play; and that the narrative-interiority threshold is ad hoc, designed only to prevent the view from extending to every enemy with a health bar.

Could you comment specifically on whether the distinction between procedural reciprocity and narrative sympathy is robust enough to bear the weight I place on it, whether the analogy to reactive but non-conscious entities strengthens the position or introduces unwanted commitments, and whether the proposed threshold genuinely blocks the slippery slope toward moral concern for all game entities? I am especially interested in whether the framework avoids anthropomorphizing code while still accounting for the intuition that some in-game harms feel genuinely wrong in a way that others do not.
