As a journalist covering philosophy and the sciences of mind for an ideas magazine, I am commissioning a scripted exchange for an upcoming feature on practical reason—specifically, the longstanding tension between internalist and externalist accounts of why we have reasons to act. Rather than rehearse the familiar gasoline-and-desire examples that every introductory course covers, I want the dialogue to unfold around a concrete, somewhat underexplored scenario: a senior epidemiologist who, after a profound personal crisis that has erased her professional ambition and emotional investment, must decide whether to remain on a long-term vaccine-trial monitoring board whose work will save lives but will never affect her own future. The conversation should take place in an informal university seminar room between two equally formidable philosophers—Dr. Aisha Rahman, who defends a rigorous internalist view that all genuine reasons must be anchored in an agent’s existing subjective motivational set and reachable through sound deliberative pathways, and Dr. Julian Park, who argues that certain normative considerations arising from institutional roles and unchosen commitments function as reasons even when they fail to connect with the agent’s present desires.

Rahman should press Park on the motivation problem: if the epidemiologist no longer cares about science, patients, or duty, how can Park’s “structural reasons” move her to act without being smuggled back into her psychology as hidden motives? Park should reply by distinguishing reasons for action from motives for action, insisting that the board’s dependence on her expertise and the lives at stake generate normative authority independent of her current feelings, and he should challenge Rahman to explain why her view does not collapse into a form of motivational subjectivism that renders moral criticism impossible—asking, for instance, whether a judge who no longer feels like administering justice thereby loses all reason to do so. Rahman should respond directly, questioning whether Park’s institutional obligations are merely disguised social expectations, and Park should counter by arguing that internal reachability is a constraint on explanation, not on the existence of reasons. Both voices should remain technically precise, genuinely responsive, and free of strawman portrayals; avoid bibliographies, citations, or references to specific published texts, and write in polished, accessible prose suitable for an intellectually curious general audience. Aim for roughly seven hundred to eight hundred words.
