I've been working through Émilie du Châtelet's essay on happiness for a seminar on early modern moral psychology, and I'm trying to formalize my interpretation into a coherent philosophical package before presenting it. Here are the commitments I've arrived at: first, that her central claim is that happiness is not a passive affective state but an active achievement requiring sustained intellectual labor—specifically the kind of rigorous study she associates with philosophical life. Second, that she also insists study alone is insufficient without what she calls necessary illusions—particularly the illusion of being loved and the illusion that one's intellectual work will be permanently valued—which provide the emotional fuel that makes labor sustainable over time. Third, I think she's right that these illusions are structurally necessary: without them, the rational recognition of one's mortality and the likely obscurity of one's contributions would undermine motivation in a way that makes durable happiness impossible. Fourth, however, I also hold that any belief maintained primarily for its motivational utility rather than its evidential warrant is epistemically corrupt, and that rational agents have a duty to dissolve such illusions through clear-sighted reflection. Fifth, I want to say that du Châtelet is not recommending vulgar self-deception, but rather a kind of cultivated partial blindness—something like strategic framing—where one knowingly sustains a productive fiction without fully endorsing it as true.

My worry is that the third and fourth commitments pull in opposite directions: if illusions are structurally necessary for the study that produces happiness, but rational reflection demands dissolving them, then the philosophical life seems to require either unhappiness or epistemic vice. Meanwhile, the fifth commitment looks like an attempt to have it both ways. Is there a consistent reading of her position where these necessary illusions are compatible with a rational duty of belief-formation? Or does my package collapse unless I abandon either the structural-necessity claim or the epistemic-duty claim? If I must revise one, which is the least costly, and does abandoning it undermine either the first or second commitment? Also, is the fifth option genuinely available to her given her Lockean commitments about the understanding and its obligation to seek clear and distinct ideas? Please rank the tensions by severity, indicate which pairs are only apparently in conflict, and specify what the cheapest revision would cost me in terms of the overall view.
