I have been trying to understand the old philosophical problem of whether pleasure is really one single thing or only a loose family of unrelated feelings. It strikes me that enjoying a complex idea, feeling bodily relief, and anticipating good news all get called pleasures, yet they seem to share no inner texture. I want you to build a thought experiment — one ordinary-life scenario, realistic and no longer than a paragraph, with no science fiction or clinical settings — that isolates exactly this issue. The case must involve one person who, on the same day, experiences two pleasant states that are maximally different in felt quality: one should be an intellectual satisfaction, such as suddenly grasping a paradox, sharp and cool and almost disembodied; the other should be a bodily relief, such as the end of a minor persistent ache, warm and diffuse and physical. Engineer the scenario so that, in the moment, the person initially ranks the intellectual state higher, but upon calm reflection an hour later they judge the bodily relief to have been the greater pleasure — not because it lasted longer, but because they find themselves more strongly disposed to seek it again. The two states must share no discernible common feeling; if there is a hidden hedonic tone, it should be undetectable to the subject in both directions.

After presenting the scenario, show what each of two theories says about it. First, the theory that all pleasures contain one uniform hedonic tone — like a single inner color that can vary only in brightness — so that comparing pleasures is always comparing intensities of the same quality. Explain why this theory must either deny the retrospective reversal or claim the shared tone was present but masked by the cognitive content of the intellectual state. Second, the theory that pleasure is not a special feeling at all but an attitude of desiring the state to continue, or being disposed to seek its repetition. Show why this theory accommodates the reversal without strain.

Then identify the exact structural feature of your case that does the work — specifically, the combination of qualitative heterogeneity with a stable retrospective ranking — and demonstrate that if you remove it, for instance by making both states bodily or both intellectual, the problem collapses into a mere disagreement about strength. Finally, explain how a defender of the uniform-tone view would attack your scenario, and whether that response succeeds. Keep everything grounded in normal human experience; do not invoke brain scans, expert citations, or specialized terminology beyond what is needed to state the two views.
