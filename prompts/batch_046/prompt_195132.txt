There's this weird split in how universities handle texts. In lit seminars they drill into you that the author is dead, intentions don't matter, and meaning is all reader-response or textual structure. But then the same department's archive spends fortunes on genetic editions that trace every draft revision towards an author's intended endpoint, and the library catalogues these as more authoritative than the first published version. Meanwhile the plagiarism office treats stealing an author's intended argument as worse than copying literal sentences, and they still invite writers to campus and treat their explanations of what I ment as illuminating, not biographical gossip. What theory of textual meaning does that institutional set-up presuppose? Does it need a view where authorial intent fixes the text for editing and plagiarism but not for classroom interpretation, or can it hang together with pure anti-intentionalism? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy, say whether the practice is actually underdetermined and could fit several rival theories, and tell me what a literature department built on a genuinely author-centred hermeneutics would have to change — not just in teaching, but in catalogue policy and how they handle plagiarism.
