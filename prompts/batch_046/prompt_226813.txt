As someone who recently relocated from Frankfurt to Boston for work, I have become acutely aware of a recurring rhetorical pattern in transatlantic political argumentation that I do not know how to classify. A speaker advocates for a specific policy—say, reducing regulatory oversight in pharmaceutical pricing—using the vocabulary of European liberalism, framing the state as an overreaching power that must remain neutral among competing conceptions of the good life. When challenged by an interlocutor operating within American progressive discourse, the speaker does not defend that European framing; instead, they pivot to American liberal vocabulary, presenting the identical policy as a necessary protection for marginalized voices against corporate capture. Once the challenge has passed, they revert to the European framework to derive further conclusions—conclusions that the American liberal framing, if taken seriously, would actually undermine. I have observed this sequential invocation with arguments about labor markets, environmental deregulation, and free-speech protections. The two senses of "liberal" are not merely ambiguous here; they are actively contradictory in their underlying justifications, yet they are deployed in turn to shield a single policy position from two incompatible lines of attack. Is there an established concept or name for this specific cross-Atlantic equivocation strategy? How precisely does it differ from ordinary ambiguity, from strategic ambiguity in diplomatic discourse, and from the motte-and-bailey? If no precise term exists, can you define its boundaries—particularly the feature that the speaker returns to the first framework after the defense has passed, and that the two frameworks justify the policy for contradictory reasons—so that it can be distinguished from a speaker who is merely confused about which intellectual tradition they inhabit?
