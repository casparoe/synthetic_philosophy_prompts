I'm helping my philosophy teacher mark some Year 12 essays on the philosophy of mind, and I've been asked to peer-review one answer before we agree on a final mark. The exam question was this: "Molyneux's problem asks whether a person born blind who has learned to distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch alone could, upon suddenly gaining sight, immediately tell them apart visually. Outline Locke's response, and discuss whether modern cases of sight restoration or sensory-substitution technology alter the philosophical significance of the problem." My classmate's answer is reproduced below exactly as written.

"Molyneux's problem concerns a blind man who learns to tell a cube from a sphere by touch. If he suddenly gains sight, Locke says he could not immediately distinguish them by vision alone, because he lacks the idea of how tactile shapes correspond to visual appearances. Locke uses this to argue against innate ideas: the mind starts blank and must learn correlations through experience.

Modern clinical reports of cataract patients support Locke: they initially see shapes without understanding them, only learning over time. However, I think Locke is wrong in principle, because recent sensory-substitution devices—like haptic gloves that translate visual edges into vibration—show that spatial information is not tied to one sense. If a blind person wears such a glove for years and then receives sight, she will immediately recognise the cube and sphere, because she has already formed the relevant spatial representations through touch. This disproves Locke's empiricism, since it shows that ideas gained in one modality can function in another without any direct visual learning.

Therefore, Molyneux's problem is now solved: cross-modal transfer is possible, and the rationalist view that the mind has innate spatial structures is actually confirmed."
