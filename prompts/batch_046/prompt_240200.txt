I'm drafting a literary novel about synthetic beings and the ethics of granting them moral status, and I keep returning to Ridley Scott's Blade Runner (1982) as an implicit philosophical argument rather than a mere illustration. Read the film through Daniel Dennett's concept of counterfeit people—artificial agents that trigger the intentional stance without the evolutionary or cognitive architecture that makes that stance genuinely predictive—alongside his broader claim that personhood is a status conferred by interpretation rather than an inner essence. Do not simply assign replicants to Dennett's categories. Instead, ask whether the film itself operates as a counterfeit person: it manufactures empathy for Roy Batty and Rachael through engineered cinematic means—Vangelis's score, the dying monologue about lost memories like tears in rain, the inserted unicorn vision in the Director's Cut—that may be as constructed as Tyrell's implanted memories. Focus particularly on the Voight-Kampff test: is it probing a hidden inner life, or is it merely measuring behavioral surfaces in exactly the way Dennett warns against? And where the film's romantic humanism—its insistence that replicants become more human than human—collides with Dennett's deflationary functionalism, be honest about the resistance: does the film dramatize something about suffering and finitude that a stance-based account cannot capture? Identify the scenes I should direct my readers to, and articulate what the film contributes to the debate about artificial personhood that a purely argumentative essay misses. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument, interpretive choice, or feature of Dennett's framework—perhaps a defense of the intentional stance against manipulation, or a critique of Deckard's own ambiguous ontology—would cause you to reverse your assessment of whether Blade Runner ultimately vindicates or undermines Dennett's position?
