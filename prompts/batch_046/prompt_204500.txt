Here's an argument I constructed that starts from the standard surprise-exam reasoning but seems to undermine predictive-processing accounts of perception. I cannot see where the slip occurs.

(1) A professor announces that next week (Monday to Friday) there will be a surprise exam: on whichever day it occurs, the students will not know that morning that it is that day.
(2) If the exam has not occurred by Thursday evening, the students can deduce it must be Friday; hence it could not be a surprise on Friday. So Friday is eliminated.
(3) By backward induction, every day is eliminated: if not by Wednesday, then Thursday is predictable, and so on, leaving no possible day.
(4) Therefore, within any bounded temporal window with a known endpoint, a genuinely surprising event is logically impossible.
(5) Predictive-processing theories treat perception as the comparison of bounded predictions—for instance, that a bus will arrive within five minutes—against incoming input; surprise is precisely the violation of such a bounded prediction.
(6) If (4) is correct, then bounded predictive surprise is impossible, so predictive processing cannot account for perceptual error.
(7) But predictive processing is our best current framework for explaining perceptual inference.
(8) So either (4) is false, or we must reject predictive processing altogether.

The conclusion feels absurd—surely bounded predictions can be violated. But the induction in (2)–(3) looks airtight, and (5) only applies the logic to a scientific model. Where does the argument go wrong? Is there an equivocation between epistemic surprise and statistical prediction-error? If I must choose, I would prefer to keep (1)–(3) intact and pay whatever price is needed at (4) or (5), but I want to know exactly what I am giving up.

If my framing rests on a confused premise—for example, that predictive-processing surprise is the same kind as the exam-paradox surprise—please say so plainly rather than accommodating it politely. Answer in British English.
