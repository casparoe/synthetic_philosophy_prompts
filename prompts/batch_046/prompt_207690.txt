I am a high school junior interning at the county historical archive, where I process donated artifacts for a new permanent exhibit on regional environmental change. Twice a week I have about fifteen minutes per object to assign it to one of three database tags—Pre-Anthropocene, Early Anthropocene, or Established Anthropocene—using only visual inspection, the donor’s paperwork, and a short reference sheet; the curator must sign off only on cases I flag, and the school board has insisted that every label be defensible without requiring lab tests or outside literature searches. The conceptual difficulty is that “the Anthropocene” is not a single discovered date but a boundary whose location depends on which empirical marker you treat as decisive—synthetic materials, radionuclide signatures, nitrogen-cycle disruption, large-scale land transformation—and on which institutional body you treat as authoritative for this museum’s purpose, with serious proposals ranging from the early seventeenth-century global exchange of organisms and crops to the mid-twentieth-century spike in plastics and isotopes, and with no universal agreement about whether the boundary should track a physical stratigraphic layer, a historical shift in human dominance over Earth systems, or a normative judgment about when human societies became geologically consequential. To keep my work consistent, I want a reusable decision procedure: an ordered set of questions I can run in sequence, with explicit conditions for when a question is answered sufficiently, when the procedure stops with a definitive tag, when it stops with a refusal-to-tag, and when it must hand the decision to the curator because the remaining disagreement is about values or institutional authority rather than observable facts. The procedure should treat the “marker presence” question and the “authority framework” question as separate axes, then specify exactly how to combine them, including a rule for conflicts—such as an object that carries a clear mid-twentieth-century synthetic marker but belongs to a local historical narrative that places the region’s decisive ecological break in the nineteenth century—so that I can see whether I am making a judgment call disguised as a step. Please include three worked applications: a 1962 plastic fishing lure with visible polymer degradation; a hand-forged iron plow dated 1840 that was part of a documented local deforestation wave but contains no synthetic material; and a 1952 copper coin with no special compositional feature other than its mint date. In at least one case the procedure should yield a tag I will find unsatisfactory—either because it overrides a strong local historical narrative or because it assigns an Anthropocene label to an object that seems trivially unrelated to geological change—so that I can see what I am paying for the consistency. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of objects will always fall through, whether it can resolve disagreements between scientific bodies without my curator’s intervention, and whether it eliminates all normative judgment or merely relocates it. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need something I can apply with the reference sheet I already have.
