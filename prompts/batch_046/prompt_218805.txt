I have been working through problems of self-defeating intention in rational choice theory, and I am struck by the possibility that the standard binary framing of Kavka's toxin puzzle obscures structural differences between causal and evidential decision theories that emerge only once the commitment mechanism and the harm are both treated as continuous rather than all-or-nothing. For a course exercise, I need a precisely engineered scenario that preserves the core paradox—receiving a valuable reward for sincerely intending to perform a mildly harmful act, with the reward delivered before the act is carried out—but replaces the binary reward and consumption structure with a graded system.

Please design the case in a single paragraph. A benefactor offers a realistic, career-relevant reward—say, a competitive research fellowship—whose value scales continuously with the measured intensity of the agent's sincere intention to ingest a mildly noxious but non-lethal substance within the next twenty-four hours. The measurement is performed by an imperfect physiological monitor that tracks sustained autonomic arousal patterns; its accuracy is bounded well below perfect prediction, and it involves no exotic physics or omniscient technology. The substance can be consumed in any dose between zero and full strength, with discomfort scaling proportionally; the reward is deposited immediately after the monitor reads the agent's mental state, before any ingestion takes place. Keep stakes within the range of ordinary professional life, avoid demons or perfect predictors, and do not invoke any science-fictional mechanism.

Then demonstrate that your case satisfies the following specifications. First, in the binary limit—where both the intention and the consumption are all-or-nothing—causal decision theory and evidential decision theory should both deliver the familiar verdict: form the sincere intention, but do not consume once the reward is secured. Second, in the continuous version, show that causal decision theory advises the agent to maximize the measured intention intensity while setting actual consumption to exactly zero, since the physical act of ingestion has no causal path back to the reward. Third, show that evidential decision theory advises selecting a coupled pair in which actual consumption is positive but strictly lower than the intended intensity, because from an evidential standpoint the agent's choice of dose is statistical evidence about the underlying sincerity of the mental state; the agent maximizes expected utility over the joint distribution of mental state and action rather than over causally separated branches. Fourth, identify the precise structural feature—the evidential correlation between consumption choice and mental-state measurement, in the absence of any causal link from consumption back to reward—that drives the divergence, and explain what happens to both theories' recommendations if that correlation is removed, for instance by arranging consumption in an environment that is both causally and evidentially isolated from the measurement. Fifth, present the standard defensive reply available to the causal theorist—that evidential reasoning here mistakes correlation for causation and recommends suffering real harm without any genuine causal benefit—together with a brief note on why an evidentialist might reject this reply by appealing to the agent's self-knowledge of their own type. Finally, state clearly which feature of your design does the philosophical work, and whether removing it collapses the case back into the standard binary puzzle where the two theories agree.
