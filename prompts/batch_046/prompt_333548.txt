In writing on the Bronze Age collapse, the Classic Maya decline, and the Easter Island ecological crisis, commentators frequently invoke an architectural analogy: a society is like a building whose foundations crack, whose load-bearing walls fail, and which eventually falls into rubble. This picture is used to license the claim that these societies genuinely failed or died, rather than transforming into smaller or different organizational forms. Audit the analogy: lay out the correspondence table, identifying what plays the role of the foundation, the load-bearing institutions, structural redundancy, rubble, and the engineer's design intent; then specify where the mapping breaks, particularly whether societies possess redundant pathways comparable to engineered structures and whether there is any meaningful equivalent to a design load or intended form. Say what normative assumptions about the value of the original whole versus its surviving parts the analogy quietly imports, and determine whether the conclusion that these cases represent failure rather than mere change survives once the disanalogies are explicit. If the analogy is carrying the normative weight, suggest a more honest substitute.
