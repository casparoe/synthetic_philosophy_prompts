For my graduate seminar on the jurisprudence of expression, I am examining the long-standing stalemate between the American First Amendment tradition—which treats hate-speech restrictions as impermissible viewpoint discrimination that undermines democratic self-government—and the European human-rights framework, which regards such restrictions as necessary conditions for equal citizenship and the protection of dignity. Despite decades of exchange, the two sides have not moved: they invoke the same empirical literature on chilling effects and on the relationship between hateful expression and violent conduct, yet draw opposite normative conclusions. I want a diagnosis of this impasse. Is it a substantive disagreement about the lexical priority of liberty over equality, a verbal dispute over whether “democracy” entails maximal openness or protected status, a hidden empirical disagreement about whether legal prohibition reduces harm or merely displaces it into less visible channels, or a conflict of bedrock intuitions that neither side can justify without circularity? Furthermore, please consider whether introducing a conceptual distinction—between harm understood as subjective offense and harm understood as structural exclusion from public discourse—can genuinely restructure the debate, or whether it only relabels the deadlock. Structure your response in four sections: first, “The Two Positions” (approximately 150 words), describing how each side actually argues; second, “Diagnosis” (approximately 250 words), assessing the verbal, empirical, and intuitive hypotheses; third, “The Conceptual Reframe” (approximately 200 words), evaluating the offense/exclusion distinction; fourth, “What Would Count as Progress” (approximately 100 words), stating whether resolution is possible and on what terms. Write formally, without bibliographic references or named citations, as if addressing a philosophy workshop.
