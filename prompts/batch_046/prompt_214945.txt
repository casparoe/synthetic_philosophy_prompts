I am a junior in high school, and in my philosophy elective we recently discussed whether there is such a thing as being too good. That conversation stayed with me because of my closest friend, who has essentially redesigned her life around what she calls zero moral waste. She meticulously tracks her time and money so that nearly every resource flows toward helping others—she takes extra shifts at her job to donate the wages, declines social events that do not serve a charitable purpose, and just turned down an offer to study music composition, something she has loved since middle school, on the grounds that years of training would consume resources without producing enough direct welfare for strangers. She argues that if an activity is not morally required, pursuing it for its own sake is at best a permissible luxury and at worst a failure of character, because a truly excellent person should minimize anything that does not maximize moral output.

I feel genuinely torn. On one hand, her consistency is admirable; on the other, it seems she has eliminated every personal project, deep friendship, or aesthetic pursuit that is not instrumentally justified, leaving her life strangely hollow despite its nobility. When I suggested that perhaps a fully human life needs room for goods—like artistic creation or intimate relationships—that are valuable independently of their moral productivity, she said that sounds like an excuse people use to justify mediocrity. I do not want to rationalize my own comfort, but I also cannot shake the intuition that there is a real conflict between being maximally good and having a life that is recognizably one's own. Could you help me think through whether maximal moral goodness is actually undesirable, or at least not obligatory, because it crowds out the non-instrumental goods that constitute a flourishing life? I am not looking for generic encouragement; I want to understand the philosophical structure of the tension—whether there is a genuine trade-off between moral excellence and personal wholeness, and if so, how one might resolve it without simply collapsing into self-indulgence.
