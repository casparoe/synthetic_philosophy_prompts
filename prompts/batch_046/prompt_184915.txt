I was chatting with a colleague who covers religion and philosophy for another outlet, and we ended up with this argument about Buddhist ethics and consequentialism that feels like it proves far too much. I'm drafting a column on it and want to check I'm not misrepresenting the tradition.

Here's the chain:

(1) Buddhist ethics takes suffering as the primary evil and accepts anatta — no permanent self.
(2) If there's no self, there's no metaphysically deep boundary between my pain and yours.
(3) So the rational moral aim should be to minimise suffering everywhere, full stop, regardless of who acts.
(4) That makes agent-relative rules like the first precept just heuristic; if killing one saves five, the maths works.
(5) By the same token, I should spend every waking hour in direct relief work, since contemplation or art don't reduce suffering.
(6) But actual Buddhist practice — especially the emphasis on cetana and upaya — treats the agent's intention and method as irreducible.
(7) So either Buddhism contradicts its own emptiness doctrine, or the slide from (2) to (3) is blocked.

The conclusion feels absurd because it turns monks into suffering-calculators. I want to keep (1) and (6) intact, but I'm unsure whether to blame (2) for sneaking in a Western aggregation assumption, or (3) for treating an empty self as still a centre of calculation — maybe there's an "empty-agent" problem I'm missing. Which premise is the weakest link, and what does rejecting it cost me? Do I end up with a strange agent-relativism inside an empty ontology?

Please seperate what's settled in mainstream Buddhist ethics from what's genuinely contested — for instance, whether Theravada abhidhamma allows any consequentialist weighing, or if Mahayana upaya can ever justify harmful means — and don't present a minority reading as the consensus. Write in British English, and if there's a standard name for the mistake I'm making between anatta and universal aggregation, say so only if you're sure; otherwise admit you're not certain.
