Read Camus's *The Stranger* through the lens of his essay "Reflections on the Guillotine," particularly his claim that capital punishment is a premeditated, state-sanctioned murder concealed by bureaucratic routine and hidden from public view. Rather than asking whether Meursault morally deserves death, examine the machinery of execution in the novel: the trial that weighs his emotional performance—above all his refusal to display grief at his mother's funeral—more heavily than the killing itself; the chaplain's demand for remorse and Meursault's stubborn refusal; and the execution carried out at dawn with minimal witnesses, as if the state wishes to complete the sentence in obscurity. Does the novel dramatize an "administrative death"—violence stripped of spectacle and buried in procedural formality—or does Meursault's lucid acceptance of his end turn the execution into an absurd confrontation with mortality rather than an act of state violence? Consider specifically whether Meursault's refusal to perform remorse undermines the state's need to manufacture contrition as a final administrative check, and whether his indifference supplies the prosecution with the "monster" narrative it requires to justify the guillotine. Point to the trial's closing arguments, the prison visits, and the final pages without verbatim quotation, and identify where the novel complicates Camus's universalist argument—for instance, whether the erasure of the Arab victim's name and the colonial context of French Algeria alter the ethical stakes of state killing in ways the essay does not address.
