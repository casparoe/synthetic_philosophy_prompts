I'm working through my metaethics notes for a seminar and want to check if my package actually hangs together before I defendt it. Roughly: (1) I'm a particularist about moral reasons - I think they don't aggregate monotonically and that adding a feature can defeat without reversing, like that resultance idea from Dancy but applied to thick concepts. (2) I still think reflective equilibrium is the right method, except I read it as calibrating perceptual attunement rather than balancing principals with judgments. (3) I believe people have stable 'moral signatures' - consistent patterns in how they weight contextual features - which explaines why we can predict character without appealing to rules. (4) I think intersubjective agreement is possible and usually tracks shared perceptual training, not shared axioms. (5) I reject the idea that moral knowledge requires propositional knowledge of principals; it's more like knowing-how. (6) But I also think thick moral concepts like cruelty or loyalty have unstable valence - what counts as cruel in one context might be necessary in another, and there's no underlying principle that fixes it.

Do these pull against each other? Sepecifically I'm worried that (1) plus (6) makes (3) implausible - if reasons shift that radically, what stays constant in a moral singature? And does (2) just rename reflective equilibrium to avoid the obvious conflict with (1)? Meanwhile (4) and (5) seem okay together but I wonder if (4) secretly needs some shared propositional framework I'm denying in (5). Can you rank the tensions by severity, say which ones are only apparent, and tell me the least costly revision? I don't want to just drop particularism because that's my starting point, but I also don't want to be smuggling in generalist commitments through the back door.
