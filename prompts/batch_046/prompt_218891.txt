I'm working on a feature about consent ethics for my science-and-ideas column, and I got stuck on a case that's not quite deception but not quite transparency either — I'm tentatively calling it "frame divergence," though I made that up over coffee and I don't think it's in any textbook. The setup is this: a research participant signs a form that technically discloses everything, including the right to withdraw, but the withdrawal clause is nested inside a subsection titled "Data Integrity Safeguards," while the risks are listed in bold under "Your Safety." All the material facts are present and readable, yet the framing makes withdrawal feel like breaking the study rather than exercising a protected right.

Can you write a dialogue between two philosophers — neither should be a strawman, both need to be sharp — arguing over whether this kind of framing failure invalidates consent? One should defend a disclosure-only view: if every material fact is accurate and accessible, framing is unavoidable and consent holds, and adding some kind of "frame audit" would make every institutional form administratively impossible. The other should defend a structural-alignment view: consent requires that the institution's framing match the participant's actual vulnerability, and burying a right inside a safeguard clause is a form of epistemic coercion even if no literal lie was told. They should actually respond to each other — the disclosure side should push back that neutrality is impossible because any layout highlights some things, and the alignment side should answer that institutions already choose frames constantly, so claiming neutrality is itself a misleading frame. Write it like they're arguing in a noisy cafe, both equally competent.
