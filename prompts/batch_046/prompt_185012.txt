I am currently preparing an essay for an advanced ethics seminar on whether evolutionary explanations of moral belief pose a distinctive epistemic problem for moral realism, and over the past few weeks the question has become personally urgent as I try to decide whether my own moral commitments can survive the thought that they are merely evolutionary byproducts. During a recent study session, a friend who had been reading popular philosophy of biology and evolutionary psychology summarized the evolutionary debunking argument in metaethics in the following way. She said that these arguments begin by observing that our core moral convictions—such as that unprovoked harm is wrong or that fairness matters—were shaped by natural selection because they promoted social cooperation and reproductive success among our ancestors. She then claimed that if moral realism were true and moral properties existed as objective, mind-independent features of reality, they would be causally inert in evolutionary history, since natural selection operates only on physical and behavioral traits that affect survival and reproduction. From this, she inferred that the evolutionary origin of our moral judgments gives us a defeater for thinking those judgments track independent moral truths, and that we should therefore treat them as adaptive instruments rather than as perceptions of objective facts. She acknowledged that realists often respond with a third-factor or pre-established harmony account, according to which some environmental regularity might have caused both evolutionary advantage and moral truth to align; but she added that this reply effectively collapses moral truth into whatever happens to be adaptively useful, and thus fails to rescue the realist's claim that morality is genuinely objective rather than merely instrumentally valuable.

Because I want to form my own view without misrepresenting either side, I would like you to audit this summary for accuracy. Please go through its main moves and indicate which parts faithfully reflect the structure of evolutionary debunking arguments found in the contemporary literature—particularly the concern, developed in arguments associated with philosophers like Sharon Street and Richard Joyce, that evolutionary explanations undermine the reliability of moral belief—versus which parts are acceptable introductory simplifications, and versus which parts subtly distort either the debunker's premises or the realist's replies. I am especially concerned that her account may conflate the claim that evolutionary origins undermine epistemic justification with the stronger claim that they disprove moral realism outright, and that her characterization of the third-factor response may misrepresent what defenders such as David Enoch actually argue when they invoke correlations between adaptive behavior and moral truth.
