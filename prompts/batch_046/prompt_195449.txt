You are asked to compose the opening address for a memorial gathering held in the philosophy common room of a university, marking the death of Dr Elena Vasquez, a scholar of classical Daoist thought who died two weeks ago at seventy-three. The audience is deliberately mixed: her adult children and siblings, who are not philosophers; her doctoral students; colleagues from the philosophy and East Asian studies departments; and several members of a community meditation group she led for fifteen years.

The address must do genuine philosophical work rather than treat Zhuangzi as decorative imagery. Specifically, it must navigate a tension that Vasquez herself insisted upon: on one side, her Zhuangzian conviction that death is not a loss but a transformation within the continuous flow of things, which implies that conventional mourning founded on a fixed, permanent self rests on a metaphysical confusion; on the other side, her equally firm belief that suppressing grief is not wisdom but merely another attachment—to one’s own philosophical self-image.

The speaker is Vasquez’s former student, now a lecturer, who is genuinely grieving. The speech must therefore avoid false consolation—no claim that she watches over anyone, that she occupies a separate afterlife, or that her death was merely a change of state that renders sorrow irrational. At the same time, it must not collapse into standard elegiac language that implicitly treats death as the annihilation of a permanent subject.

Your task is to construct a five-to-seven-minute address that uses the distinction between the fasting heart—the emptied, responsive awareness that does not cling to fixed identities—and the completed heart—the rigid, self-defining consciousness that holds itself apart from transformation—as its structural backbone. The speech should show, not merely state, how mourning might be reconceived: not as a response to a metaphysical catastrophe, but as the body’s honest participation in change, without requiring the mourner to pretend that the particularity of the lost person was illusory.

The audience includes experts who will notice any misrepresentation of the relation between Zhuangzi’s critique of ritual mourning and his broader cosmology; it also includes family members for whom the philosophical vocabulary must be translated into lived experience. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not quote any classical text verbatim—paraphrase in your own words. Reach a clear position: does the Zhuangzian framework ultimately permit a form of grief that is both honest and philosophically coherent, or does it demand the suppression of particular attachment in a way that makes genuine mourning impossible? Defend your answer within the speech itself, so that the conclusion shapes the final lines delivered to the room.
