I serve on my university's interdisciplinary research funding committee. We have received a proposal requesting three years of support for a study the applicants call "the meta-problem mechanism." They claim that what philosophers call the hard problem of consciousness is not an ontological gap but the output of a specific predictive-processing error: when the brain integrates its own information-processing states, a mismatch signal is generated that is phenomenologically interpreted as an "explanatory gap." The researchers propose to isolate the neural signature of this response using a modified mismatch-negativity paradigm, and they plan to suppress the signal pharmacologically in pilot subjects. They predict that when the signal is dampened, subjects will rate consciousness as "fully physically explicable" on a custom scale, which they take as empirical dissolution of Chalmers's meta-problem and, consequently, of the motivation for property dualism.

A philosophy liaison on the committee has filed a minority report. She argues that explaining why people judge consciousness to be mysterious (the meta-problem) is conceptually distinct from explaining consciousness itself, and that suppressing a judgment is not equivalent to closing an explanatory gap; she warns that funding the study risks treating a substantive philosophical dispute as mere cognitive noise to be eliminated. The researchers reply that their preliminary correlation between suppressed mismatch-negativity and lower "mystery ratings" makes the philosophical objection empirically moot.

Please draft the committee's decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—with reasoning the researchers could actually respond to. Include a clear recommendation with a concrete rationale, and treat the minority report's strongest point seriously rather than dismissing it as outside the scientific remit. The memo should be suitable for circulation before our vote on Thursday.
