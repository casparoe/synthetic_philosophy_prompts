I am a doctoral student in classics and the philosophy of education, working on how Plutarch’s comparative biographical method in the Parallel Lives and the later hagiographic tradition can be translated into modern moral pedagogy without collapsing into either uncritical hero-worship or anachronistic judgment. In my research I have been developing what I call the exemplarity-proximity framework: the claim that moral learning from a life succeeds only when the narrative sustains a precise tension between the subject’s exceptional actions and the learner’s own practical identity, and fails whenever either pole dominates—whether through unattainable idealization or through such familiarization that the figure becomes merely a mirror. I need this turned into a reusable practitioner tool.

Please write a decision procedure that a secondary-school ethics curriculum designer—who is not a professional historian, must produce a lesson plan within a 48-hour window, and has to defend selections to a department head—can run when choosing a biographical or hagiographic narrative and framing it for classroom moral instruction. The procedure should be ordered as a sequence of questions with conditions for a sufficient answer at each stage, clear stop points where the answer is “do not use this life for this purpose,” and explicit flags marking where the decision is an irreducible value judgment rather than a factual check. It should preserve the distinctions that actually matter in Plutarch’s method—attention to small habitual actions over singular grand achievements, the use of parallel or counter-example lives to prevent sanitization, and the pedagogical necessity of leaving failures and vices visible—while honestly dropping distinctions that cannot survive simplification for a non-specialist. Include a compact set of test criteria the designer can apply to a draft lesson outline, and state clearly where the procedure will misfire or generate false negatives.

Then run the procedure in full on two cases. First, framing an ancient figure such as Socrates for a unit on civic courage: this should be a case where the procedure largely approves the selection, but still forces the designer to make an uncomfortable inclusion—specifically, retaining an action or trait that complicates the moral message—rather than allowing a sanitized version. Second, framing the life of a widely revered modern reformer whose biography has been substantially sanitized by later hagiographers: here the procedure should yield a negative recommendation or require such severe structural modifications that the moral claim of the lesson is fundamentally altered, and you should defend that result rather than soften it. Throughout, take a definite, committed position on which elements of the exemplarity-proximity framework must survive operationalization and which can be sacrificed to usability; do not present options for the asker to choose among. Give your reasons, flag the judgment calls you are making that a different reader of Plutarch or hagiography might reject, and say exactly what the procedure cannot do—particularly in cases where the learner’s prior attachment to a figure conflicts with the framework’s demands.
