I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of physics with some seriousness. Most popular and even technical expositions of Bell's theorem present it as a straightforward proof that local hidden-variable theories are impossible, yet the derivation hinges on a specific factorization condition—P(a,b|x,y,λ) = P(a|x,λ)P(b|y,λ)—that is usually glossed as “locality” without careful conceptual analysis. I would like an intuitive but rigorously precise explanation of why this factorization should be understood as capturing physical locality in the sense of no superluminal causal influence, rather than as a more generic statistical independence condition that might fail for reasons that have nothing to do with spatial separation or signaling.

In particular, please address the following scenario, which I have not seen treated clearly in standard accounts: suppose the hidden-variable distribution ρ(λ) is not statistically independent of the measurement settings x and y, but is instead globally constrained—say, by a deterministic cosmological boundary condition—so that x, y, and λ are jointly determined without any direct interaction between the two distant measurement regions. Here the factorization fails, yet intuitively no influence propagates between the regions. Does Bell's argument still establish physical nonlocality, or does it merely show that quantum correlations cannot be embedded in a classical probability space that treats the settings as exogenous variables? I want a gut-level clarification of where the conceptual line lies between mathematical factorizability and genuine causal nonlocality. Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am interested in the architecture of the argument itself, not in tracking down literature. Keep the explanation accessible to someone with technical training but new to the philosophical framing.
