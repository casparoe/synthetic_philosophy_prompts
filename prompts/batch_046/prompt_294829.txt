Exam question:
A university governance committee proposes restructuring a research laboratory according to Confucian role ethics. Under the proposal, the principal investigator occupies the relational position of teacher and elder sibling; the postdoctoral researchers occupy positions of junior scholars; and the external funding agency is assigned the relational position of ruler. The committee argues that ethical conflicts—over authorship, resource allocation, or research direction—should be resolved not through appeal to universal rights, procedural fairness, or individual autonomy, but through rectification of names (zhengming): each party should behave in strict accordance with the normative content of their assigned relational title. Assess this proposal. In your answer, (a) explain how Confucian role ethics understands the constitution of the moral self and the function of rectification of names; (b) evaluate whether the proposal correctly applies the five relationships and the concept of reciprocity (shu) to this modern institutional context; and (c) identify one significant tension between this framework and liberal individualist ethics, attributing the relevant Confucian positions accurately.

Student answer:
The student argues that Confucian role ethics denies any pre-social moral self, holding instead that the self is constituted entirely by its relational roles—parent, child, ruler, subject—without any residual inner nature. The student attributes this view specifically to Mencius, claiming that Mencius believed human nature lacks innate moral structure and that virtue must be built entirely from outside through ritual training (li) and disciplined role performance. Regarding rectification of names, the student explains that each social title carries a specific normative content—so a teacher must actually teach, a ruler must provide resources without interfering in scholarly judgment—and that assigning these fixed titles allows conflicts to dissolve once each party conforms to the name they bear. On reciprocity, the student claims that shu demands extending identical moral consideration to every other person regardless of relational distance or hierarchy, which means the PI should apply exactly the same standard of care to the funding agency as she applies to her postdoctoral researchers, since shu eliminates role-specific boundaries in favor of a universalized golden rule. Finally, on the tension with liberalism, the student contrasts Confucian relationalism with liberal autonomy, noting that Xunzi—representing the optimistic Confucian view—held that humans are born with innate moral sprouts (benevolence, righteousness, ritual propriety, wisdom) that merely require cultivation, yet even Xunzi denied the existence of an autonomous self prior to social relations.
