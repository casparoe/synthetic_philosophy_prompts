Read Voltaire's Candide through Leibniz's actual moral metaphysics rather than through the familiar caricature of Panglossian optimism. Leibniz distinguishes physical evil (disorder in nature) from moral evil (the failure of rational agents to orient their wills toward universal good), holds that justice is essentially charity as practiced by the wise, and treats moral perfection as the rational harmonization of appetite with reason rather than as passive acceptance of cosmic order. Where does Voltaire's satire—especially in the Lisbon earthquake and in Cunégonde's repeated victimization—collapse this distinction by treating natural disaster as an immediate refutation of divine justice? Conversely, does Candide's concluding commitment to practical labor and local care align more closely with a Leibnizian ethics of finite, rational agency than with simple anti-metaphysical resignation? Give a reading that respects both the novel's narrative logic and the philosophical architecture of Leibniz's ethics, and say what Candide reveals about the gap between cosmic harmony and moral responsibility that philosophical argument alone tends to obscure.
