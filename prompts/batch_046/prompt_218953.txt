Following a prolonged argument with a friend over whether the destruction of wild habitats might, paradoxically, reduce aggregate animal suffering, I have been trying to organise my thoughts around the net welfare of ecosystems rather than simple biodiversity metrics. Would you please rank the following five conceptual frames by how decisive you consider them to be in adjudicating that question? Assign each a tier — S, A, B, C, or D — and provide, in a single sentence for each, your reasoning. Please write your response in British English. The frames are: (1) the demographic frame, which notes that habitat loss reduces the total number of births among short-lived, high-mortality r-selected populations; (2) the edge-effect frame, which suggests that fragmentation intensifies predation and parasitism near habitat boundaries; (3) the social-buffering frame, which claims that intact ecosystems sustain cooperative behaviours that mitigate individual suffering; (4) the compositional frame, which argues that degradation favours generalist species with narrower or lower subjective welfare ranges; and (5) a hypothetical frame I want to test — the ecological coherence thesis — according to which an intact ecosystem possesses a distributed, relational form of welfare arising from interspecies interdependence, so that dissolving that structure harms welfare even when individual suffering counts remain constant.
