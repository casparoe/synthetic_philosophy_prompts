I'm helping assess discussion responses for a graduate seminar on early modern rationalism, and two participants, Maya and Jonas, submitted opposing readings of Leibniz's moral philosophy that they could not resolve between themselves. Both agree that Leibniz understands justice as charity guided by wisdom—the cultivated pleasure taken in the perfection or happiness of others—and that this operates within a deterministic framework in which each monad unfolds solely according to its own internal law, with no direct causal interaction between substances. Their disagreement concerns whether this framework leaves any genuine space for other-directed moral obligation, or whether it inevitably collapses morality into a form of enlightened self-interest.

Maya argues for the collapse. Because a monad's internal states are determined entirely by its own law, and because pre-established harmony guarantees that my bodily actions never causally affect another soul, the only real effect of so-called just behavior is a change in my own representations. Consequently, cultivating charity can only be instrumentally rational: representing another's perfection is valuable precisely insofar as it increases my own clarity, harmony, and metaphysical perfection. When Jonas objects that charity involves a genuinely other-directed representation—I take pleasure in another's perfection as genuinely theirs, not merely as an accidental feature of my mental state—Maya replies that this distinction concerns the content of representation, not the source of motivation. Since Leibniz holds that we always pursue what appears best to us, and since this appearance is fixed by our individual nature, even an other-directed pleasure is pursued only because it is my pleasure. Jonas then appeals to Leibniz's distinction between necessary moral truths and contingent matters of fact: moral relations are rational and necessary, so recognizing them resembles grasping a geometric relation rather than satisfying a contingent appetite; obligation thus derives from reason's recognition of fitness, not from causal efficacy over others. Maya counters that this renders morality excessively intellectualist. Leibniz also describes charity in affective terms—love, inclination, pleasure—yet if our affective dispositions are fully determined by our natures, then some individuals are constitutionally incapable of the relevant pleasure. If moral improvement is possible, it must come from reason gradually clarifying our perceptions, but then the affective dimension becomes derivative, leaving Leibniz with an unstable hybrid: determinism at the affective base, but normative demands that seem to require either freedom or an open-ended capacity for self-modification that his system does not obviously provide.

Please adjudicate their exchange step by step: evaluate Maya's initial instrumental-reduction move, Jonas's reply about other-directed representation, Maya's content-versus-motivation rebuttal, Jonas's defense via necessary moral truths, and Maya's final objection about intellectualism and determinism. For each step, indicate whether the argument is clearly mistaken, partially right but incomplete, or clearly correct, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall about whether Leibnizian morality reduces to self-interest, and identify what both participants are missing—particularly whether either addresses the theological role of divine choice in grounding the harmony that makes moral representation reliable, or whether they neglect Leibniz's own distinction between justice in the strict sense and the broader virtue of universal charity.
