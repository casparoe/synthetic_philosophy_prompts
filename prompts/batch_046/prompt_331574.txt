Consider the following situation, and advise on how the decision-maker ought to reason.

A trustee must disburse a single $10,000 grant from a small family foundation by Friday. The funds are indivisible in practice: both potential recipients require the full amount to reach their operational threshold, so splitting the grant means neither program launches and the money is effectively lost to both causes.

The first option is a neighborhood literacy initiative with well-documented outcomes. If funded, it will almost certainly provide tutoring and materials for roughly thirty local children over the next year, many of whom she knows personally through her community work. The second option is an overseas public-health intervention—specifically, a deworming program in a region with high parasite prevalence—backed by epidemiological modeling that suggests a small but real possibility of large-scale morbidity reduction, coupled with a significant risk of logistical failure and near-total waste that she has no direct means to monitor or prevent.

The trustee is genuinely divided about the moral framework that should govern her choice. She feels a strong, direct responsibility toward her immediate neighbors that she does not feel toward distant strangers, suggesting that partial obligations might take precedence. At the same time, she suspects that if impartial aggregation of welfare is the correct standard, then committing the full sum to the local program—where the expected benefit per dollar is modest and confined—would constitute a serious moral failure, allowing preventable harm on a far larger scale. She estimates her confidence in the partialist view at roughly 60% and in the impartialist view at roughly 40%, but she recognizes that these are not merely empirical disagreements; they concern what morality itself requires.

She asks whether, given this irreducible moral uncertainty and the binary nature of the choice, she should commit fully to the option she finds slightly more plausible, apply a rule designed to minimize the worst mistake she could make, or adopt some other principle for decision-making under moral disagreement with oneself. How should she think about the problem, and what should she do?
