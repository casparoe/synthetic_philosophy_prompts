I have recently begun consulting on community-based participatory research ethics, and I find myself holding a cluster of commitments about epistemic injustice that I need audited before I present them to the board. Here is where I stand: (a) I accept Miranda Fricker's distinction between testimonial and hermeneutical injustice as analytically useful, but I want to supplement it with what I am calling "procedural silencing"—cases where institutional formats (consent protocols, peer-review criteria, meeting structures) systematically filter out knowledge expressed in non-standard registers without anyone assigning low credibility to the speaker; (b) I think credibility deficits and credibility excesses are structurally symmetric harms to collective inquiry, though only the former is comfortably classified as an injustice; (c) I hold that testimonial injustice is corrected through individual listener recalibration, whereas hermeneutical injustice requires collective conceptual labor that no single agent can complete; (d) I believe the fundamental wrong in these cases is a denial of epistemic agency—the capacity to be recognized as a contributor—rather than an unfair distribution of credibility as a good; (e) despite (d), I rely on distributive language when speaking to policymakers because it is more actionable. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision in each case? Rank the tensions by severity. In particular, examine whether (d) undermines my justification for retaining (e), and whether (a) destabilizes the individual-collective boundary I draw in (c).
