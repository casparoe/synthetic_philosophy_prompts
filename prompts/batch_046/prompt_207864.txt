My advisor in ancient historiography has rendered consistent judgments about when a claim concerning the distant past counts as genuinely known rather than merely transmitted or conjectured. Her verdicts: that Socrates was executed in Athens in 399 BCE is known, supported by convergent contemporary testimony and consistency with attested legal procedure; that Socrates said precisely "The unexamined life is not worth living" at his trial is not known, since Plato's dialogue is our sole source and frames the line for philosophical effect; that the Library of Alexandria existed and suffered catastrophic damage is known, attested by multiple independent texts and archaeological traces of carbonized papyrus; that it contained exactly four hundred thousand scrolls is not known, resting on a single rhetorical figure with no surviving inventory or physical capacity study; that Herodotus traveled in Egypt and consulted priests is known, since his named informants and geographical specifics match independent records; but that Egypt was over eleven thousand years old, as he reports the priests claiming, is not known, because the figure derives from a single mythological narrative chain and conflicts with geological and archaeological chronology.

The new case concerns the Melian Dialogue in Thucydides. A recently reconstructed commentary fragment—preserved in a single papyrus copy and restored through conjectural paleographic emendation—asserts that the Melian envoys presented a written treaty draft to the Athenian ambassadors. Separately, an inscription from Melos dated to 416 BCE lists magistrates who match the dialogue's named participants, confirming the diplomatic context, yet it mentions nothing of written terms. There is no archaeological trace of diplomatic archives at Melos, and no other textual reference to such a draft. Predict my advisor's verdict: does she treat the existence of the written draft as genuinely known? Identify at least two organizing principles—such as a convergence-of-independent-attestation principle and a documentary-contextual-coherence principle—that could account for all six of her earlier judgments, state what each predicts about the draft, say which principle her track record best supports and why, and specify the further evidential configuration that would force a choice between the two leading candidates.
