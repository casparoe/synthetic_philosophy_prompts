Read Sophocles' Oedipus Rex through the lens of moral luck, but move past the standard question of whether Oedipus is blameworthy for deeds he committed in ignorance. Focus instead on the plague that ravages Thebes: its citizens suffer death and ruin for a moral pollution they did not cause and could not have prevented. Does the tragedy present this collective affliction as unjust in a way that anticipates the modern distinction between constitutive and resultant moral luck? Pay particular attention to Creon's opening report and to the Chorus's odes about the city's suffering. Do they suggest that moral contamination can spread through a community independently of anyone's intentions or choices? I want a reading that treats both the play and the philosophical taxonomy with equal seriousness, and that asks whether Sophocles dramatizes a form of collective moral vulnerability—being trapped in a polluted environment not of one's making—that Bernard Williams's discussion of agent-regret does not fully address. Where does the play resist philosophical reduction, and what does it contribute that the argument alone misses?
