As a doctoral student in philosophy of science with training in statistical learning theory, I have been trying to formalize Hume's problem of induction through the lens of the no-free-lunch theorems for supervised learning, and I have constructed an argument that strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion is far stronger than I can accept, though I cannot pinpoint the exact slip. Here is the chain, numbered.

(1) The no-free-lunch theorems for supervised learning establish that, when expected risk is averaged uniformly over all possible labeling functions on a fixed input space, every learning algorithm—including any human inductive strategy—achieves exactly the same performance.

(2) Therefore, any rational preference for one inductive strategy over another must be grounded in an inductive bias: a non-uniform prior or a restricted hypothesis space that breaks the symmetry of the uniform average.

(3) But any particular inductive bias is itself one element within a larger space of possible biases. Applying the same NFL reasoning at this meta-level implies that, averaged over all possible distributions of biases, no rule for selecting first-order biases outperforms random selection.

(4) Consequently, rational justification for a first-order bias requires a rationally justified second-order bias, and this reasoning iterates without termination: an infinite regress of biases that must themselves be justified.

(5) Since an infinite regress of justifications is impossible, no inductive bias—at any level—is ever rationally grounded.

(6) If no inductive bias is rationally grounded, then empirical belief-formation, including the inferences that underpin scientific knowledge, is never rationally justified.

(7) Nevertheless, we standardly regard at least some scientific inferences as rationally justified.

(8) To preserve (7) without embracing the irrationality of science, we must reject (1) and deny that the NFL theorems apply to actual epistemic agents.

I am reluctant to reject (1), since the theorems are mathematically sound, and I would prefer to preserve both (1) and (7). Where does the argument go wrong? Is the error in the move from (2) to (3)—perhaps an equivocation between statistical risk defined over target functions and epistemic justification defined over agents—or in (4), where I treat meta-level bias selection as subject to the same averaging conditions that apply at the object level? If the correct response is to reject (3), what does that commit me to about the rational status of inductive priors, and does it require denying that NFL reasoning can be applied iteratively?
