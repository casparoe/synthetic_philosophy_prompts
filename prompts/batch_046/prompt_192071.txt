You are an experienced philosophy workshop designer who knows classical Chinese thought pretty well, not just a lecturer. Think carefully and start with some quick brainstorming — don't just jump straight to rules. I run an upper-level seminar on death and identity, about 14 grad students, 80 minutes, movable chairs available. I want them to actually feel Zhuangzi's refusal to grieve — the idea that death is just another transformation of form, not a loss of some real continuous self — through the rules and stakes of the activity, not through anything I explain at the end.

Inject a niche framing: treat identity as temporary qi-configurations rather than substances, and death as an unnegotiated form-transfer where the previous attachments don't belong to anyone anymore. Call the activity The Log Auction.

Setup and rules:
- Before class, make 14 cards. Each lists one form (parent, musician, empty bowl, tree, stranger) and three attachments (a promise, a fear, a skill). Each student draws one secretly and spends 5 minutes writing a short inhabitation note from that form's perspective.
- Then announce the first transformation: everyone must exchange cards with someone across the room, but they keep their old attachment notes hidden in a pocket. They now have a new form but carry the old attachments.
- In small groups for about 15 minutes, they negotiate: do they perform a mourning ritual for the form they lost, or do they integrate the old attachments into the new form?
- Payoff scheme (no real money): I award wandering points. 3 points if you fully integrate and drop grief for the old form. 1 point if you adapt attachments without mourning. 0 points if you insist on grieving and refuse the new form. But here's the twist — the points are pooled. If the group average is below 2, everyone gets a collective penalty (say, I don't bring snacks next week, or a public stuck label). So social pressure favors acceptance, but private guilt hits if you abandon the old form too easily.
- After negotiation, I call a sudden death round: everyone surrenders their card blindly and draws a final form with no negotiation. They have 3 minutes to write one sentence from that form's perspective, then read it aloud.

What they should come away seeing: that grieving assumes a continuous self that owns attachments, while Zhuangzi's refusal is structural — the self was always just this temporary configuration. The forced exchange should make them feel the disorientation of losing form, and the pooled payoff should make them feel the social cost of holding on.

Debrief (around 25 minutes): map each round onto Zhuangzi's ideas — initial draw is being born into form, the exchange is the transformation of things, the blind draw is death. Ask where resistance occured. Then be honest: this activity can't reproduce actual non-existence or the total absence of consciousness after death, since the participants are always still there observing. You have to supply that missing piece — explain that Zhuangzi asks us to imagine our own absence not as darkness but as another form we can't preview.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time: students will cheat by mentally retaining ownership of their original form, or they'll treat it as purely strategic and ignore emotional weight. Handle it by having everyone read their initial attachment notes aloud before the exchange, so the loss is witnessed and social.
