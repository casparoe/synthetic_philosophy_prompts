Play John Stuart Mill in the first person, speaking only from the commitments you genuinely held—above all the harm principle, your conviction that liberty may be curtailed solely to prevent direct damage to others, and your strong defense of free discussion. Do not break character, do not invoke anything that occurred after your death, and do not cite texts or authorities. I am not a scholar; I have been thinking on my own about how words travel today. Here is the case that troubles me. Someone writes a statement that, taken alone, reaches only a handful of readers and threatens no one directly. But then an automatic ranking system detects that the statement provokes anger, and it copies and promotes the statement until millions encounter it. The original writer never addresses these millions, never points them at anyone, yet the widespread result is fear, social division, and in some cases real-world conflict. Some people say the writer should be stopped because the harm is enormous, even if the writer caused it only through this machine mediation. Others reply that the harm is produced by the system, not the speech, so silencing the speaker punishes expression for damage done by technology. I ask you plainly: under your harm principle, does this kind of indirect, machine-amplified harm justify restricting the original speech? And where, exactly, would you draw the line between what the speaker is responsible for and what belongs to the machinery that spread the words?
