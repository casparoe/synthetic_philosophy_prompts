I am trying to identify whether a particular configuration in nuclear-strategic reasoning has an established name, or whether it is merely an unremarked conjunction of familiar problems.

Consider a state that deploys a fully automated retaliation architecture—designed so that, once sensors confirm an incoming strike, counter-launch proceeds without any human authorization. The explicit purpose is to solve the credibility problem: adversaries understand that national leaders cannot choose absorption, so deterrence becomes structurally absolute. Yet the same state simultaneously maintains concealed manual-override pathways, precisely because its leaders do not wish to be genuinely locked into annihilation over a false alarm, a cyber-intrusion into the sensor grid, or an ambiguous launch detection.

Now observe the adversary's reasoning. The adversary sees the public automation and therefore treats the state's resolve as genuinely mechanized—meaning that any action risking sensor activation carries an unacceptable, non-negotiable consequence. But because the adversary is unaware of the hidden overrides, it systematically overestimates the state's willingness to permit automation to run its course in ambiguous scenarios, and systematically underestimates the state's actual capacity for restraint. Meanwhile, the state's own planners believe they have secured the best of both worlds: the deterrent value of an irrevocable commitment combined with the safety of retained control. The result is a layered misalignment—each side reasons about the other's decision architecture using a model that is false in opposite directions, and the risk of accidental escalation is shaped not by either state's true intentions but by this mutual mis-modeling of control.

This is clearly adjacent to the standard commitment problem and to brinkmanship, yet it does not seem to be either. The ordinary commitment problem asks whether a promise to retaliate is credible; here credibility is purchased through a visible mechanism, but that mechanism's deterrent force depends partly on the adversary not knowing it can be disabled. And it is not exactly brinkmanship, since brinkmanship involves the deliberate manipulation of shared risk; here the risk is structurally embedded in an adversary's false belief about the opponent's control system, rather than in either side's intentional escalation.

Does this specific three-layer structure—public automation, concealed flexibility, and adversarial mis-modeling of both—have an established term in strategic studies or in the philosophy of deterrence? If there is no precise term, how should it be distinguished from ordinary credible-commitment failures, and what boundaries would correctly delimit it—particularly with respect to cases where the hidden flexibility is absent, or where both sides share accurate knowledge of the override capacity?
