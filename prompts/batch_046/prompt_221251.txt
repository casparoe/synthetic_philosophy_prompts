After a long and somewhat heated discussion with a friend about whether formal results in combinatorial game theory should constrain our normative theories of rational disagreement, I have tried to write down my own commitments on the strategy-stealing argument and its philosophical extensions so that I can check whether they actually cohere before I present them to our reading group. Here is the package I want to defend: first, I accept the strategy-stealing proof in its standard form—if a two-player game is symmetric, deterministic, and has perfect information, then the second player can always copy the first player's strategy and guarantee at least a draw, which is why Hex has no first-player winning strategy and Chomp has no second-player winning strategy. Second, I take this to reveal a deep structural fact about rational interaction: whenever two agents face symmetric rules with no built-in privilege, neither can claim an ex ante rational superiority over the other, so any resolution of conflict must come from external considerations rather than from positional advantage. Third, I want to apply this analogically to peer disagreement in epistemology—if two rational agents share identical evidence and reasoning capacities, then neither has a "first-move" entitlement to override the other, which means that standard split-the-difference responses implicitly violate the symmetry condition and should be rejected in favor of maintained disagreement with mutual respect. Fourth, I think this undermines conciliationist views that treat disagreement as requiring symmetric concession; if the situation is genuinely symmetric, both parties should hold firm with equal rational warrant rather than both lowering confidence. Fifth, I am also skeptical of applying the argument to live philosophical debate, because computing and stealing an opponent's strategy in real time seems circular—the very reasoning process that generates the strategy may presuppose the conclusion it is meant to defend—so I think the analogical extension to epistemology is fragile. Sixth, despite all this, I maintain that chess is a draw with perfect play, and I have sometimes cited strategy-stealing in defense of that claim, even though I recognize that chess is technically asymmetric because White moves first.

Do these six commitments hang together? I can see at least three potential fault lines. The analogical extension in (3) and (4) seems to depend on treating epistemic disagreement as structurally identical to a symmetric game, but (5) undermines the very possibility of that extension by questioning whether strategies can be stolen without circularity in reasoning contexts—so either the epistemic application collapses or my skepticism in (5) is overblown. Meanwhile, (2) seems to commit me to a strong form of rational egalitarianism that (4) cannot consistently deliver, since maintaining disagreement with equal warrant looks less like respecting symmetry and more like a stalemate that offers no normative guidance. Finally, (6) appears to misapply the formal argument to an asymmetric game, which suggests either that my belief about chess is unsupported or that I am implicitly relying on a different symmetry argument that I have not articulated. I want to know: which of these tensions are real and which dissolve on closer inspection—particularly whether there is a way to distinguish the formal conditions of the game-theoretic proof from its analogical applications so that (5) does not destroy (3)? Please rank the tensions by severity, indicate the least costly revision to each, and tell me which single commitment I should abandon if I want to preserve the core idea that symmetric rational interaction precludes ex ante superiority.
