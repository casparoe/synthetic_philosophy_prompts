Having reported for years on evolutionary medicine and philosophy of biology, I have gradually formed the following set of convictions about health, disease, and biological function, and I want to know whether they form a coherent package or whether I am quietly borrowing from incompatible frameworks. First, I take the etiological view: a state counts as pathological only when an internal mechanism fails to perform the naturally selected function that explains its presence, rather than merely departing from statistical norms. Second, I maintain that congenital deafness in a fluent sign-language community is not a disease, despite auditory processing being a clear evolutionary function, because communicative capacity and well-being remain intact within that calibrated social environment. Third, I resist blanket pathologization of clinical anxiety, arguing that threat-detection systems may be operating with correct evolutionary calibration for high-risk settings, so that what appears disordered in one ecological context is adaptive functioning in another. Fourth, I insist that the boundary between disease and mere biological variation should be fixed by biological facts alone, without importing social values, since medical taxonomy needs objective criteria. Fifth, I treat age-related functional decline as pathological solely when it exceeds the species-typical trajectory for that life stage, not whenever it falls below peak young-adult performance.

These commitments seem to pull in different directions. The context-sensitive judgments in my second and third points appear to require evaluating whether an environment correctly calibrates a trait, which sounds like a normative or social assessment that my fourth point explicitly forbids. Meanwhile, my fifth point appeals to life-stage biological norms, yet if calibration is genuinely environment-sensitive, one might ask why aging norms should not also shift with social or technological context rather than being fixed biologically. There also seems to be friction between my first point, which ties pathology to failure of selected function, and my third point, which treats anxiety as correctly functioning — but perhaps that tension is only apparent, since correct calibration is precisely what the etiological account demands.

Can you audit this position? Order the tensions by severity, identify which ones dissolve upon closer inspection and which are real, specify exactly which commitment must be weakened or abandoned to resolve each genuine conflict, and indicate whether there is a hidden coherence — perhaps around a biologized notion of calibration context — that lets me keep the core of all five.
