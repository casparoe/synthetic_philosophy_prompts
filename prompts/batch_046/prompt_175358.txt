Two colleagues in a philosophy reading group got into an extended disagreement over whether Jain anekāntavāda—specifically the pairing of syādvāda (conditional predication qualified by syāt, “in some respect”) with the saptabhaṅgī scheme of sevenfold judgment—can do genuine philosophical work, or whether it collapses into either contradiction or empty relativism.

(1) The first argued that anekāntavāda offers a coherent middle path between moral realism and anti-realism. By indexing every substantial claim to a particular naya (standpoint), one can affirm both that cruelty is wrong (from the standpoint of absolute ethical norms) and that cruelty is necessary for survival (from the standpoint of biological preservation) without logical contradiction, since neither claim is made unconditionally. This, she said, avoids dogmatism without falling into nihilism.

(2) The second replied that this is relativism with logical window dressing. If contradictory claims are licensed simultaneously, and the doctrine provides no principled rule for deciding which naya governs in a concrete conflict—say, whether a particular act of killing in self-defense is wrong—then it does not resolve moral disagreement but merely redescribes it. He added that without such adjudication, anekāntavāda becomes unfalsifiable: whatever judgment one makes can be retroactively assigned to the appropriate standpoint.

(3) The first countered that the saptabhaṅgī scheme is not an “anything goes” license. It rigorously specifies which combinations of affirmation, negation, and inexpressibility are permitted, thereby imposing structural constraints that pure relativism lacks. She also insisted that demanding a single adjudicating decision-procedure misses the point: anekāntavāda is an epistemology of humility, not an algorithm for generating answers.

(4) The second said that structural elegance is not sufficient. If the doctrine systematically withholds judgment in exactly the hard cases where guidance is needed—distributive justice, rights conflicts, bioethical dilemmas—then its value is limited to a descriptive taxonomy of opinions. Furthermore, he argued, appealing to practical wisdom to select the correct naya in a given case reintroduces the very dogmatic commitment the doctrine was meant to dissolve, since that selection itself requires treating one standpoint as authoritative.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, addressing in particular whether anekāntavāda’s refusal to supply a single adjudicating principle is a feature or a bug, and whether the saptabhaṅgī scheme genuinely constrains judgment or merely catalogs contradictions after the fact. Finally, note what, if anything, both parties are missing about how the doctrine treats the relationship between standpoints and practical action.
