Please argue that Kant’s indirect-duty account of our obligations regarding animals—according to which cruelty to animals is wrong not because it harms the animals but because it degrades the agent’s moral sensibility and weakens the disposition of humanity—has been consistently misread as a weak, anthropocentric afterthought. Defend the stronger interpretive claim that, on Kant’s view, the moral significance of animal suffering is structurally analogous to damage done to the external conditions of rational autonomy: not because animals possess autonomy, but because the rational agent’s own moral personality is constituted through practices of respectful attention that extend beyond the boundary of rational nature itself. Your argument should show that this makes the indirect-duty view more demanding, not less, than direct animal-rights positions, because it prohibits not only physical cruelty but also forms of aesthetic and scientific engagement with animals that treat them as mere instruments for human emotional or cognitive satisfaction—even when no bodily harm occurs. Do not provide a bibliography, citation list, or recommended reading. Structure your response as follows: (1) The common misreading you reject (approximately 200 words); (2) The reconstructed Kantian argument linking animal treatment to the constitution of moral personality (approximately 500 words); (3) Why indirect duties are more demanding than direct rights, with attention to non-harmful instrumentalization (approximately 300 words); (4) Two serious objections—one from a direct-rights theorist and one from a Kantian purist—each with a brief reply (approximately 250 words); (5) The weakest point in your own reconstruction and what it costs to concede it (approximately 150 words).
