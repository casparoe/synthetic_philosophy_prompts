hey so im writing the final for my upper-level ancient phil seminar and i want one essay question on Aristotle's money and exhange stuff that cant just be answered by saying "he hated usury and thought money was unnatural." i want students to actually wrestle with the tension in NE V.5 and Pol. I.8-10 between chreia (need/use) and nomisma (money as convention). specifically i want them to tackle this: if exchange justice is arithmatic proportion based on need, but need is subjective and incommensurable, then doesn't Aristotle secretly need money to do the measuring work he claims it only facilitates? and does that blow up his distinction between natural oikonomike and unnatural chrematistike?

heres the question (90 mins, aim for about 700 words):

"Aristotle's account of exchange in Nicomachean Ethics V.5 and Politics I.8-10 is often summarized as: goods are exchanged according to need (chreia), money is just a conventional token (nomisma) that makes comparison easier, and charging interest is unnatural because money is barren. But this gloss hides a real tension. Explain (1) the builder/shoemaker 'proporttion' problem and why Aristotle thinks need can ground equality without a common unit of value; (2) whether his distinction between natural acquisition and chrematistike depends on money merely facilitating exchange or actually measuring need; (3) whether treating money as pure convention undermines his claim that exchange justice is arithmatic (not geometric) proportion; and (4) the modern objection that Aristotle fails to distinguish use-value from exchange-value, and whether his two-uses-of-a-shoe passage in Politics 1257a gives him a real reply. Is his theory coherent, or does he smuggle in a unit of account he pretends to reject?"

model answer (sketch, ~650 words):

A good answer starts by reconstructing the builder/shoemaker example: a builder and a shoemaker exchange goods, but their products are incommensurable in kind. Aristotle solves this not by finding a common substance but by relating them through need—each needs the other's product in a specific ratio. The "diagonal" or proportion line connects them via chreia, not via an intrinsic metric. This is why exchange justice is arithmatic: if A has 10 units of need for B's goods and B has 10 for A's, equality is restored by equal exchange; if needs are unequal, the proportion adjusts the quantities, but the standard remains need, not money.

Next, the student should note that money enters as nomisma—a convention agreed upon to serve as a medium. It does not create value; it makes need visible across time and distance. So natural oikonomike uses money to secure household goods, stopping when needs are met. Unnatural chrematistike treats money itself as the end, accumulating it without limit. Crucially, chrematistike is not wrong because money measures value, but because it treats the measuring tool as the thing measured.

The coherence problem: if chreia is genuinely subjective—my need for shoes differs from yours—how can it ground objective arithmatic proportion? Here the best answers argue that Aristotle is not committing to cardinal interpersonal comparison. Need is not a private psychological state but a functional relation within the polis: the builder needs shoes in the same way the body needs food, and the polis establishes conventional ratios (prices) that approximate these functional needs. Money, then, is not measuring subjective utility but stabilizing the conventional ratios that approximate objective need-relations.

On the arithmetic/geometric point: exchange justice is arithmatic because it corrects a deficit or surplus in goods exchanged between equals; distributive justice is geometric because it assigns shares according to merit. Money as convention does not turn exchange into geometric justice, because the proportion is still determined by the goods' relation to need, not by the status of the exchangers.

For part (4), the student should address the modern charge (often linked to Marx) that Aristotle conflates use-value and exchange-value. The reply uses Politics 1257a: a shoe has two uses—wearing (natural, according to its form) and exchanging (unnatural, treating the shoe as a commodity for another's need). This shows Aristotle does distinguish them, but he frames the distinction teleologically: exchange is legitimate when it serves the natural use of the household, illegitimate when it becomes an end in itself. So he does not fail to distinguish them; he subordinates exchange-value to use-value within the economy of the household and polis.

Top answers conclude that the theory is coherent but only within a teleological framework where need is not subjective preference but functional requirement, and where money is a conventional sign of that function, not its ground.

marking scheme (total 20):

must-hit 1: correct reconstruction of builder/shoemaker proportion and arithmatic justice (5 pts). typical miss: describing it as "fair trade" without explaining why incommensurability requires need as mediator.

must-hit 2: clear distinction between natural oikonomike and chrematistike, with correct role of money (5 pts). typical miss: saying chrematistike is "making profit" without linking it to treating money as end rather than means.

must-hit 3: addressing the coherence tension—how subjective/incommensurable chreia can ground objective proportion (4 pts). typical miss: ignoring the tension or saying "Aristotle just assumed people agree."

must-hit 4: correct use of the two-uses-of-shoe passage to answer the modern objection (4 pts). typical miss: mentioning the passage but not explaining the teleological framing.

must-hit 5: explicit evaluation of coherence with a reasoned conclusion, not just "yes" or "no" (2 pts). typical miss: concluding without referencing at least two of the prior parts.

A-grade: hits all five, uses precise terms (chreia, nomisma, chrematistike, arithmatic/geometric), and shows how part 4 resolves part 3. B-grade: hits four but misses the coherence tension or treats money as actually measuring value rather than facilitating conventional ratios. Below B: summarizes Aristotle without engaging the specific problem of incommensurability and convention.
