I've been working through Benjamin Franklin's autobiography, specifically the portion where he lays out his plan for reaching moral perfection and describes the little book he kept with columns for each day of the week to record his failures against thirteen named virtues. He lists them in a deliberate sequence—Temperance, then Silence, then Order, then Resolution, and so on through Frugality, Industry, Sincerity, Justice, Moderation, Cleanliness, Tranquility, Chastity, and Humility—explaining that he arranged them in this particular order because he believed mastering one virtue made acquiring the next one easier. He admits that Order gave him the most persistent trouble, partly because his printing work constantly scattered his papers and tools, and partly because he never could maintain a strict accounting of his own hours. He also recounts the anecdote about a man who polishes an axe so relentlessly that he grinds the blade away entirely, which Franklin uses to explain why he eventually stopped striving for absolute perfection in every particular. 

I'm trying to understand a specific philosophical tension in this account, and I would appreciate help thinking it through carefully. Franklin's method sounds like a kind of sequential machinery: each virtue is a component that must be calibrated before the next can function properly, which implies that a permanent, local failure—especially at Order, sitting so early in the chain—should cripple the entire system. Yet the axe story suggests he came to accept that over-polishing destroys practical utility, which sounds more like a global reason to relax standards everywhere rather than a license to tolerate one broken link. Does the passage actually resolve whether his difficulty with Order was treated as an isolated exception—perhaps because Order is uniquely instrumental to his printing trade—or whether he quietly abandoned the sequential logic altogether in favor of a more compartmentalized view? And is there any implicit distinction in his framing between virtues pursued mainly for practical business ends and those pursued as intrinsic moral ends? I keep returning to the text and finding it ambiguous on this point, and I want to avoid importing a modern idea about modular self-improvement that may not belong in an eighteenth-century framework.
