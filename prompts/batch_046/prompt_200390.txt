Read Groundhog Day through the lens of Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities. Phil Connors repeats the same day with perfect memory but without lasting consequences; he deliberates, suffers, and eventually reforms, yet nothing he does changes the world until the loop breaks. Does the loop function as a Frankfurt-style blocker that eliminates his ability to do otherwise, or is it different because it removes the trace of his actions rather than the capacity to choose them? In particular, I am wondering whether there is a recognized distinction in the literature between an intervener that suppresses a choice at the moment of decision and one that suppresses its enduring effects afterward—so that Phil might retain alternative possibilities in an internal, psychological register even while the world denies them. I would like an analysis that takes both the film's logic and the philosophical debate seriously, and that asks whether this creates a genuinely new configuration: an agent who becomes responsible precisely because he is isolated from consequence rather than from choice.
