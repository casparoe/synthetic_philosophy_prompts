Here are my verdicts on whether an earlier competent self's advance directive should bind a later, changed self, and I want the principle—if any—that captures them.

Case 1: Clara, 60, writes that if she develops severe dementia and no longer recognizes her children, antibiotics should be withheld for life-threatening infections. At 80 she has severe dementia, recognizes no one, yet smiles often, eats with clear enjoyment, and does not resist care. Verdict: Withhold antibiotics; the directive binds despite her present contentment.

Case 2: David, 45, writes that if he enters a permanent vegetative state, artificial nutrition should stop. He is now in PVS, and newer neuroscience suggests hidden awareness with occasional positive affect, though he shows no behavioral signs. Verdict: Withdraw nutrition; the directive binds regardless of hidden welfare.

Case 3: Elena, during a major depressive episode with active suicidal ideation, signs a refusal of all future psychiatric medication, saying it would drag her back to hell. Once stable, she consistently wants treatment and describes the refusal as alien. Verdict: The refusal does not bind; the depressive self lacked diachronic authority.

Case 4: Frank, 55, writes that if he gets dementia he wants all life-extending care because non-existence terrifies him. Once demented he shows no terror, lives in the present, and accepts death peacefully when infection comes. Verdict: I am genuinely split—part of me says honor the terror, part says it was a contingent affective state that no longer belongs to him.

Case 5: George, 50, writes that if he becomes quadriplegic and fully dependent he wants assisted dying, predicting unbearable misery. Once quadriplegic he reports high satisfaction, asks for continued care, and says he misjudged adaptation. Verdict: Do not assist dying; the directive fails because it rested on a false phenomenological prediction.

The tension I cannot dissolve: Cases 1 and 5 both involve an earlier self mispredicting future welfare—Clara predicts suffering that never arrives, George predicts suffering that never arrives—yet I want to honor Clara's directive and reject George's. Meanwhile, Cases 3 and 4 both involve value or affective change, yet I reject the depressive refusal and am torn about Frank.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps distinguishing predictive error about experience from axiological drift, or moral lock-in from prudential planning—that saves most of these? Which verdict must I drop to keep the principle simple? Predict also what it says for an unlisted case: a patient writes that if she enters locked-in syndrome she wants sedation regardless of any later expressed wishes, then enters locked-in with full mental capacity, communicates clearly, and asks to continue living.

Finally, say exactly what consideration would change your mind—whether, for instance, a demonstration that dementia involves a genuine rupture in personal identity rather than mere change, or that directives function as promises rather than predictions—and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
