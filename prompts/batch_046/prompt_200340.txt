As a journalist covering the intersection of political memory and conceptual history, I am drafting a six-month research proposal for a small interdisciplinary fellowship—one that treats national myth not as folklore but as a structured argument about obligation. My half-formed premise is that the standard taxonomy of "lost causes" (the Confederacy, Masada, Kosovo, Dunkirk) sorts them by emotional tone—tragic, heroic, nostalgic—when it should sort them by what I will call mnemonic debt structure: whether the defeat is narrated as closing an account (the loss is paid for, finished) or leaving one open (the loss creates an enduring obligation that constrains present politics). I want the proposal to ask a precise, answerable question: does the political durability and resistance-to-correction of a lost-cause myth depend on whether the lost object—territory, sovereignty, collective dignity—remains physically or symbolically recoverable, and does this vary systematically between "terminal" defeats like Dunkirk and "generative" defeats like Kosovo or the Lost Cause of the Confederacy?

The existing literature in history and political science describes these myths richly but treats them as variations on a single mechanism: identity consolidation through shared grievance. What remains open is whether grievance operates differently when the defeat is spatially closed (Dunkirk's evacuation saves the army; Masada's site is a ruin under modern sovereignty) versus spatially open (Kosovo Field as a site within contested borders; Confederate territory reabsorbed into the United States). I propose a comparative conceptual study—four cases, no archival excavation—mapping the narrative architecture of commemorative practices onto two variables: recoverability of the lost object, and whether the myth frames the defeat as a terminus or an origin. The method is philosophical reconstruction: identifying the implicit premises about time, obligation, and space in official and vernacular commemorative discourse, then testing whether these premises are consistent or contradictory within each case. A result would be a framework distinguishing "closure myths" from "debt myths," with criteria for predicting which defeats become politically dormant and which remain active. Failure would mean finding that recoverability and temporal framing do not covary with mythic durability—that grievance is uniform regardless of spatial or narrative structure—which would itself be a useful null result.

Please write this as a polished one-page proposal with a precise statement of the question, why current answers leave it open, the comparative method and why it is preferable to empirical survey or psychoanalytic interpretation, what the deliverable looks like for readers deciding on commemorative policy or education, and an honest assessment of scope: what a six-month philosophical project can and cannot claim. If my framing rests on a confusion—say, if "mnemonic debt" collapses into ordinary nationalism, or if the spatial/recoverability distinction cannot bear the weight I put on it—say so plainly rather than polishing the premise; I am writing this for an audience that values conceptual clarity over compliance.
