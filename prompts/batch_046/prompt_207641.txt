The student’s answer reads as follows:

“Hardin’s tragedy occurs when rational actors deplete a shared, rivalrous resource, which is exactly what happens in open-access fisheries: every additional catch reduces the remaining stock, leading to collapse. Fisheries are therefore a clear stock-subtractive common, and Hardin’s prediction of mutual ruin applies directly; privatization or strict state quotas are appropriate because they internalize the cost of depletion. Global carbon accumulation is different. Carbon emissions are not rivalrous in consumption—one nation’s release does not physically prevent another from emitting—so the atmosphere is not being ‘used up’ like fish stocks. Instead, climate change represents a norm-erosive common: the harm lies in the breakdown of international trust and the failure to maintain collective agreements, not in the depletion of a finite atmospheric stock. Consequently, Hardin’s framework of inevitable ruin does not apply, and mutual coercion is the wrong tool. What is needed is Ostrom-style community governance that strengthens monitoring norms and builds reciprocity without dividing the atmosphere into private property. That said, Ostrom’s own analysis of irrigation systems shows that even norm-erosive problems eventually require privatizing enforcement rights, which implies that carbon markets—assigning tradable emission rights—are actually consistent with her framework and should be preferred over purely voluntary agreements.”
