I am working through the assigned readings for a graduate seminar on contemporary moral and political philosophy, and I have become stuck on what seems to be a structural deadlock between communitarian critics of liberalism and their liberal respondents. The communitarian line—exemplified by arguments that the self is always already embedded in webs of social meaning, and that practical rationality is constituted by particular traditions rather than by abstract universal procedures—appears to entail that any moral critique originating outside a tradition lacks the internal resources needed to be intelligible, let alone authoritative. In other words, if practical reason is genuinely tradition-constituted in the way figures like MacIntyre or Taylor suggest, then a liberal appeal to universal human rights looks not like a neutral arbitration but like an imperial imposition of one tradition’s vocabulary upon others. The liberal counter, of course, is to deny that the self is so thoroughly constituted, and to defend procedural neutrality—Rawls’s original position being the canonical example—precisely as a method for filtering out parochial commitments rather than as one more parochial commitment in disguise.

But here is where my confusion deepens. A colleague in comparative political economy pointed me toward the concept of embedded liberalism—the idea that even ostensibly neutral, universalist institutional frameworks actually encode a very specific historical compromise between openness and social protection, one that is only fully intelligible within a particular North Atlantic political context. If an analogous point holds for moral philosophy, then Rawlsian neutrality is not genuinely free of social content; it is merely one tradition’s self-description universalized. At that point, both sides seem to have collapsed into the same problem: the communitarian admits that all reasoning is particular but then cannot account for cross-tradition moral judgment, while the liberal claims universality but is revealed to be practicing a disguised particularism.

So I want to ask: is this deadlock real, or does it rest on a false presupposition? Specifically, am I wrong to assume that tradition-constituted rationality necessarily implies something like Kuhnian incommensurability between moral traditions? And if we reject the binary—either a view-from-nowhere liberalism or a radically enclosed communitarianism—what resources remain for justifying moral criticism across traditions without pretending that such criticism can ever be fully neutral? I am particularly interested in whether the problem dissolves once we distinguish between being formed by a tradition and being incapable of understanding any other, and whether liberal universalism can be reconstructed not as neutrality but as a shared, revisable framework that remains open to both internal and external challenge.
