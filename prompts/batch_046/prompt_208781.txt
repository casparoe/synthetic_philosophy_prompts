I am a retired evolutionary biologist who has recently begun working in philosophy of history, and next month I present a seminar paper to a philosophy of history graduate reading group. The audience is precisely composed of analytic philosophers of science (the majority, skeptical of grand narratives and alert to analogical reasoning), two theologians working on providential history who favor a non-interventionist divine teleology, and one historian of the American civil rights movement who is wary of importing biological concepts into historiography and who will press on empirical counterexamples.

My thesis is that the familiar claim that the arc of history bends toward justice mistakes retrospective narrative coherence for prospective teleology. I defend what I call an exaptation-and-archive thesis: institutional mechanisms originally selected for social coordination and status regulation are exapted—without being designed for justice—to produce local norms that happen to align with justice-related ends; meanwhile, the historical record suffers from a systematic survivorship bias that preserves successful moral experiments while erasing or marginalizing failed ones. The result is an illusion of directional moral progress. I do not deny that contingent, local moral learning occurs, nor do I claim that justice is unreal; I deny only that these local instances aggregate into any arc, whether secular, Hegelian, or providential.

Please draft my Q&A preparation sheet. I want the eight objections I will actually face from this specific audience, ordered by the combined likelihood and damage they could do to my position. Each objection should be phrased in the vocabulary and conceptual framework of the subgroup most likely to raise it—analysts should sound like analysts, theologians like theologians, the historian like a historian. For each, provide a concise reply calibrated for about two minutes of speaking time, and note explicitly where that reply is weakest or relies on a contested premise. Identify which objections I should concede in part up front rather than defend, and indicate what I should hold firm. Finally, include the single objection I am hoping nobody asks—the one that, if pressed, exposes the softest point in my own normative commitment—and suggest how I might acknowledge its force without collapsing my argument.

Keep the framing formal and precise; do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, since I will present without reference materials. Where you invoke specific conceptual distinctions—such as exaptation versus adaptation, or survivorship bias in archival selection—treat them as working hypotheses drawn from my scientific background rather than as citations to named authorities.
