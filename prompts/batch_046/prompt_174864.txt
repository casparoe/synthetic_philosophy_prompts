Draft a 90-minute essay question for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on archives, epistemology, and political power, together with a model answer and a marking scheme totaling 20 points. The question should focus on the conceptual crux that archival silences are not passive absences but active structures of knowledge production, and it should ask students to analyze a specific hypothetical case rather than summarize canonical literature. Present this scenario: a municipal archive preserves all property-deed litigation initiated by male heads of household in fully cross-referenced files, but files divorce-court petitions submitted by women under the single undifferentiated label domestic disturbances, rendering them statistically invisible and narratively unrecoverable. Require students to explain how this labeling practice produces what can be called a double silence—the suppression of the original legal claim together with the generation of a misleading categorical presence that masks the suppression—and to evaluate the claim that full digitization and full-text search can remedy such silences without structural re-description of the archive. The model answer must make five specific conceptual moves: (1) distinguish absence-from-the-record from absence-in-the-record; (2) explain how categorical aggregation operates as an epistemic technology of erasure; (3) demonstrate why digitization amplifies visibility without altering the underlying taxonomy; (4) defend or critique the claim that recovery requires re-describing the archive rather than merely searching it; and (5) respond to the objection that digitization advocates might raise. Provide a marking scheme that assigns weights to each move, identifies the specific misconception that should cost marks for each, and notes one point where a strong answer may legitimately depart from the model. Write the response in American English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not quote verbatim from specific theoretical texts; paraphrase any ideas drawn from named thinkers without quotation marks.
