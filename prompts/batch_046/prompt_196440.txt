As a novelist drafting a philosophical dialogue for a character steeped in early analytic ethics, I wish to stage an argument about G.E. Moore's account of intrinsic goods. Please argue that Moore's doctrine of organic unities, when applied consistently to friendship and aesthetic appreciation, undermines his separation of beauty and friendship as distinct, independently valuable goods. Specifically, defend the position that in genuine friendship the appreciation of the other person's character has an irreducibly aesthetic structure—what might be termed the formal beauty of moral attention—so that the value of the friendship is not a compound of two separable goods, a relational good plus an aesthetic good, but rather a single, more complex organic unity in which beauty functions not as an added ingredient but as the very form through which the relational good is realized. Address the objection that this collapses Moore's pluralism about intrinsic goods into a monistic account of relational value, and explain why, on Moore's own terms, this collapse is either illusory or actually strengthens rather than weakens his ethical framework. Keep the reasoning accessible without citing specific page numbers, editions, or bibliographies; I am writing for readers who may not have Principia Ethica at hand.
