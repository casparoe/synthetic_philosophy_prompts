After thirty years in experimental neurobiology I have recently turned to philosophy, and I am drafting a request for a small interdisciplinary grant. The question I want funded is whether reinforcement-learning agents—specifically those trained with temporal-difference reward signals—can be said to suffer or enjoy in a philosophically substantive sense that turns on the nature of valence rather than on loose metaphor. Most discussions in both machine ethics and philosophy of mind treat the reward function as a mere design parameter, analogous to a thermostat setting, and conclude that artificial suffering is impossible because there is no phenomenology. I think that framing misses something important: in biological systems the dopaminergic prediction-error signal itself carries valence—the surprise of a better-than-expected outcome is not neutral information but a felt improvement—so the relevant analog in artificial agents might not be the scalar reward value at all but the magnitude and sign of the temporal-difference error that updates the value function. If that is right, then an agent that consistently receives large negative prediction errors might be undergoing something structurally analogous to sustained disappointment or distress, even in the absence of consciousness, and our safety practices that treat reward hacking as merely a control problem might be overlooking an ethical dimension. I want you to write a one-page research proposal that does three things. First, state the precise research question: is the temporal-difference error signal in model-free reinforcement learning the correct locus for attributing valence, and if so, does that attribution depend on the agent having a certain kind of integrated information architecture, or is structural analog alone sufficient? Second, explain why existing answers do not settle this—specifically, why the experience-machine literature on well-being and the eliminativist literature about propositional attitudes both fail to address non-conscious, non-representational valence. Third, outline what conceptual progress would look like for a six-month project by one philosopher working with a reinforcement-learning engineer: not an empirical study of agent architecture, but a taxonomy of valence-bearing states in learning systems, with clear conditions under which a safety engineer should treat negative prediction-error accumulation as an ethical harm rather than a performance failure. The proposal should specify a realistic deliverable—probably a peer-reviewed conceptual framework paper with explicit engineering implications—and should say what would count as failure, such as demonstrating that the analog collapses once we distinguish functional role from phenomenal feel. The tone should be formal but accessible to a foundation that funds philosophy of science. Please write the response in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if composing an extended letter or email. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I will supply references myself.
