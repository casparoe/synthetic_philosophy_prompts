I hold a qualified scientific realist position—let me call it manipulative-stability realism—that accepts the force of the pessimistic meta-induction but attempts to contain its damage. The view holds that ontological commitment to a theoretical entity is warranted only when that entity remains manipulable across successive theoretical revolutions: later theories may discard or radically revise the ontology of the earlier framework, yet still preserve the capacity to intervene on the entity in technologically independent regimes. Electrons, on this account, survive because they remain intervenable despite quantum-mechanical revisions, whereas phlogiston and the luminiferous aether do not, since later chemistry and electrodynamics offered no preserved intervention point corresponding to them. Tell me what would genuinely count against this position—not generic anti-realist arguments, but defeaters that specifically target the cross-revolutionary manipulability criterion itself. Rank these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Which ones would a committed manipulative-stability realist concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as misapplying the criterion? In particular, examine whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability: if every historical case of theoretical abandonment can be explained away post hoc as a failure of manipulability, does the position still risk anything empirically or conceptually? And if it is indeed well insulated, is that insulation a virtue for a realist view or a vice that collapses it into a stipulative taxonomy? I want an honest assessment of how exposed the position actually is.
