I'm working on my disssertation about democratic institutions and I'm stuck on what seems like an obvious inconsistency but maybe isn't. You know how in several European countries now they run citizens' assemblies by sortition — random selection — for specific constitutional questions, but the regular parliament is still made up of career politicians, and term limits apply to some offices but not others? Meanwhile ancient Athens rotated offices constantly and used lot for nearly everything. So I'm wondering: what philosophical commitments about representation, expertise, and political equality does this modern hybrid actually presuppose?

Specifically, if we think random citizens are good enough to deliberate on abortion law or climate policy in a one-off assembly, but we still need career legislators for day-to-day governance, are we implicitly committed to some split theory of political knowledge? Like, that there's a technical component that requires experience and a deliberative component that just needs common sense? Or does it presuppose that legitimacy comes from different sources for different bodies — mandate for elected ones, statistical descriptiveness for sorted ones?

Then there's the term limit asymmetry: presidents and legislators face rotation, but judges and senior bureuacrats often don't. Does that pattern presuppose that democratic accountability requires periodic replacement, but legal or administrative expertise requires continuity? And if so, is that consistent with using sortition for citizens who have zero prior expertise? I'm trying to reconstruct the implicit theory here. Is the practice philosophically underdetermined — could a liberal, a deliberative democrat, and an epistimic elitist all license this same hybrid? Or does it quietly rule out some views, like pure participatory democracy or pure trustee representation? And if we actually replaced career politicians with rotating citizens' assemblies everywhere, with strict term limits, what view of the state would we be forced into? Would it have to be radical epistemic egalitarianism, or could something like a pluralist framework still accommodate it?
