I'm preparing notes for a graduate seminar on transitional justice and want a consistency check before I present. Roughly, I hold that: (1) conditional amnesty tied to full disclosure is morally permissible when necessary to prevent democratic collapse — the South African TRC model is defensible on these terms, even though perpetrators deserve punishment; (2) post-atrocity societies bear a collective duty of accurate remembrance that requires an institutionally secured, non-negotiable historical record, not merely commemorative rituals; (3) victimhood must not harden into a permanent moral status; survivors are owed repair and public voice, but not an unchallengeable authority that immunizes their testimony or demands from critical scrutiny; (4) transitional mechanisms should aim at restorative rather than retributive justice, but only where the state has sufficient independence and capacity to prevent restoration from becoming merely symbolic; (5) a truth commission succeeds not by delivering punishment, but by achieving what I will call epistemic settlement — a shared, publicly acknowledged account of events that binds future governments not to deny the record, though not to adopt any particular reparative policy.

These seem to hang together, but I suspect there are hidden pulls. Amnesty incentivizes disclosure, yet strategic perpetrator testimony may corrupt precisely the record I want secured. Centering victim narratives for epistemic settlement looks as if it grants survivors the very permanent authority I reject in (3), though that may be only apparent. And if restorative justice requires strong institutional independence, then endorsing it seems hard to square with defending amnesty in fragile transitions — Argentina under military pressure, post-genocide Rwanda, South Africa itself — where independence is precisely what is lacking. Are these tensions genuine or illusory? Rank them by severity, identify the least costly revision, and specify exactly which commitment must weaken and what that costs.
