Write an essay on whether indirect normativity, construed through the framework of idealized deliberation, can properly extend to epistemic reasons in scientific practice. Focus your analysis on a specific conceptual tension: in certain historical and hypothetical cases, a researcher's actual cognitive biases—such as analogical overextension or motivated pattern-matching—function not merely as obstacles to be eliminated but as causally necessary conditions for generating correct theoretical innovations. Examine whether an idealized deliberator, stripped of these biases, would thereby be deprived of the very heuristic pathways that ground the epistemic value of the discovery. Weigh the argument that this demonstrates a failure of indirect normativity in epistemic domains, on the grounds that idealization removes essential reasoning mechanisms, against the counter-argument that idealization should preserve only the functional structure of successful heuristics rather than the biases themselves. Do not conclude with a non-committal summary; defend a specific verdict—either that indirect normativity is confined to practical and moral reasoning and cannot cover bias-dependent epistemic justification, or that it can be reconstructed to include such justification without directly endorsing the biases—and provide explicit reasoning for your position.
