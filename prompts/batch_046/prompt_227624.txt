I take the position that the classic commissurotomy studies—those following Sperry and Gazzaniga—have been systematically misinterpreted as demonstrating that consciousness literally divides into two independent streams once the corpus callosum is cut. Rather, I think these cases reveal that the unity of consciousness is a functional achievement of interhemispheric integration, not an intrinsic property of neural tissue, and that what remains after severance is best described as a single consciousness operating with a bifurcated information architecture: the left hemisphere's verbal reports are not the utterances of a second subject but the output of an interpretive module that confabulates coherent narratives to cover gaps caused by lost right-hemisphere input, much as a single computer with a broken memory bus might run one operating system with severely degraded I/O rather than two separate machines. For this to be wrong, I need to know what would genuinely count against it: which neuropsychological findings, philosophical distinctions—such as Block's separation of access and phenomenal consciousness—or theoretical commitments about personal identity would be decisive rather than merely awkward; whether defenders of the dual-consciousness view have predictions that my integration-failure account cannot accommodate and that have actually been observed; and whether my own position has been insulated by qualifications—if every apparent sign of right-hemisphere autonomy can be redescribed as degraded integration—such that it risks unfalsifiability, and whether that insulation is a vice for a view of this kind.
