I am a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of science and now serve on the editorial board of an interdisciplinary review journal. I am repeatedly asked to evaluate submissions—particularly in cognitive science, artificial-intelligence safety, and climate modeling—that invoke Kurt Gödel's first and second incompleteness theorems to draw substantive conclusions outside mathematics. I need a reusable decision procedure, executable in about thirty minutes by someone without formal logic training, that determines whether such an invocation is legitimate or a category mistake.

Please construct the procedure as an ordered checklist or flowchart in words. For each step, state the precise diagnostic question, what counts as a sufficient answer to proceed, the conditions under which the procedure terminates with a clear negative verdict (the Gödelian argument fails), and the explicit points where judgment cannot be eliminated—especially regarding whether a target domain has been genuinely formalized in a recursively axiomatizable system with sufficient arithmetic, and whether the mapped "undecidable" statement retains its syntactic meaning or has been translated into a merely metaphorical claim. The procedure should preserve the distinction between syntactic incompleteness, semantic indeterminacy, and epistemic limitation, and should be honest about which distinctions are safe to collapse for practical screening and which must survive.

Then apply the procedure to three submissions:

First, a theoretical-neuroscience manuscript that argues Gödel's second theorem shows any consistent formal model of human reasoning cannot prove its own consistency, hence the brain must implement non-algorithmic processes. Second, a climate-modeling paper that embeds arithmetic representations of albedo-feedback dynamics in an Earth-system model and claims Gödel's first theorem therefore entails the existence of true but formally unprovable temperature predictions, undermining long-range predictive confidence. Third, a philosophy-of-mind essay—one I find intuitively appealing—that contends any sufficiently rich axiomatic ethics contains true moral propositions it cannot derive, thereby establishing an irreducible form of moral pluralism.

Include at least one case where the procedure produces an answer I am likely to resist, and conclude by stating exactly what the procedure cannot do—whether it can distinguish a structurally sound analogy from a rhetorical one, whether it requires expert judgment on formalization strength that a non-logician cannot supply, and whether it can ever license a positive metaphysical conclusion rather than merely blocking an illegitimate inference.

Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests; I have no access to verify them. Avoid verbatim quotations unless sourced; paraphrase freely.
