Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar and now find suspicious because it seems to dissolve moral criticism altogether rather than merely limiting utilitarian demands.

(1) Williams's integrity objection holds that requiring an agent to abandon a ground project for the sake of aggregate welfare alienates the agent from the commitments that give her life meaning; such a demand is therefore illegitimate.

(2) The related point—sometimes described as having one thought too many—suggests that when acting inside a ground project, introducing an impartial calculus corrupts the project's internal logic. A spouse saves her partner not because of the net utility but because she is his spouse; weighing the stranger against him introduces a reasoning step that should not be there.

(3) Now extend this protection symmetrically. If an external calculus corrupts the execution of a project, it also corrupts the evaluation of whether the project should continue to exist. To ask, in moral terms, "Should I maintain this ground project?" is already to treat one's own identity as an object of cost-benefit analysis.

(4) But moral assessment need not be utilitarian; it might invoke deontological constraints, virtue considerations, or obligations to others. Still, all such assessments are external to the project's internal logic, so by (2) and (3) they are equally corrupting.

(5) Therefore ground projects are immune not merely to utilitarian override but to all moral criticism. Once genuinely adopted, a ground project—whether it is writing atonal symphonies, raising children, or maintaining an exhaustive archive of unprovenanced artifacts—cannot be morally challenged without violating the agent's integrity.

(6) This is clearly wrong: some ground projects are monstrous and ought to be abandoned.

(7) Hence either the integrity objection collapses entirely, or we must swallow (5).

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I want to preserve the core of (1) and (2)—that there is something genuinely corrupting about letting impartial calculation displace personal commitment—but I do not want to end up with (5). Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? If the argument is actually valid, then which of the apparently plausible premises is the least costly to abandon?

Please say what would change your mind about which step fails—whether, for instance, a precise distinction between first-order action within a project and second-order moral evaluation of the project undermines (3), or whether the corruption described in (2) applies only to a specific kind of reasoning—and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
