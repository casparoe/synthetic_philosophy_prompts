Construct a diagnostic decision tree for positions on the problem of dirty hands in politics. The root node should ask whether a political leader can ever be required to perform an act that remains morally wrong even after all consequential and institutional justifications are weighed. From there, branch by three successive distinctions: first, whether the wrongness is intrinsic to the act-type or generated only by the agent's official role; second, whether the resulting moral residue adheres to the individual agent, is absorbed by the institutional office, or is distributed across the authorizing polity; third, whether democratic mandate functions to cleanse the act of its wrongness, to share it collectively, or to leave it untouched on the office-holder alone. Each leaf should identify a position—named if it carries a standard label in the literature, otherwise marked "unnamed"—and state in one sentence its most awkward practical commitment, such as what it must say about personal guilt, resignation, or public transparency. Ensure the tree includes a branch separating those who hold that clean hands are conceptually impossible in governance from those who hold they are merely empirically rare, and explicitly flag any pair of leaves that your tree fails to distinguish. After presenting the tree, provide a brief meta-commentary stating how confident you are in the classification of each leaf, and single out the specific node or distinction about which you are least sure—particularly regarding whether any view genuinely eliminates moral residue rather than merely displacing it onto structures or citizens.
