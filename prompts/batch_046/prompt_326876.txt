I'm preparing a single seventy-minute seminar for advanced undergraduates in moral philosophy on prioritarianism and the leveling-down objection. The students have read Rawls and grasp the difference principle, but they habitually conflate prioritarian weighting with simple utilitarian aggregation. I want them to understand why the leveling-down objection is not merely an aesthetic preference for equality but a structural challenge to the prioritarian's core metric of moral importance.

Plan the explanatory arc: identify the conceptual dependency that must be secured before the objection can even be formulated—explaining why introducing it prematurely collapses the distinction between weighting and aggregation—then specify the order in which the remaining components should follow. Next, propose the single concrete scenario that should carry the discussion: a case involving both a clearly better-off group and a clearly worse-off group, in which leveling genuinely improves the worst position but imposes a severe, non-trivial cost on the better-off, and—crucially—lacks the intuitive moral pull of a direct rescue scenario. State precisely what features this example must possess and what features it must lack in order to do the pedagogical work required. Then map the predictable misconceptions: that prioritarianism is merely utilitarianism with a diminishing-returns curve; that the leveling-down objection can be dissolved by introducing a sufficiency threshold; and that the problem arises only in zero-sum contexts. For each misconception, indicate the exact point in the sequence where it should be anticipated and headed off.

Provide one diagnostic question I can pose at the end to determine whether the students have grasped the objection as a challenge to the metric itself rather than as a mere preference for efficiency or equality of outcome. Finally, specify what should be deliberately omitted at this level—perhaps formal axiomatization or a full comparison with sufficientarianism—and explain how I should acknowledge that omission without making the session feel incomplete.
