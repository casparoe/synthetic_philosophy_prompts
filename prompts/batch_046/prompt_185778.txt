As a retired physicist who has only recently turned to philosophy of mind, I have been working through illusionism about phenomenal consciousness—specifically the claim that phenomenal properties are systematically misrepresented representational contents rather than genuinely instantiated qualitative features. I would like you to compose a dialogue between two equally capable interlocutors, an illusionist and a representational realist, in which they debate whether illusionism can consistently explain the seeming immediacy of phenomenal experience without falling into a vicious regress. To move the discussion away from familiar textbook rehearsals, please ground their exchange in the following analogy: a calibrated thermometer whose internal reference point is itself distorted, so that even its error-correction readings inherit the same distortion. The illusionist should defend the view by arguing that phenomenal misrepresentation is functionally self-correcting in just this way—operating reliably at the cognitive level despite lacking any ontological grounding in intrinsic qualities—while the realist should attack the analogy by insisting that temperature mismeasurement presupposes an independent external standard against which error can be defined, whereas phenomenal consciousness offers no such benchmark outside itself, leaving the illusionist either circular or implicitly eliminativist about the very phenomena that need explaining. The dialogue must be genuinely interactive: each speaker should respond directly to the specific point just made by the other, not deliver isolated speeches. Present the result as continuous prose only—no headings, bullet points, boldface, or tables, in the manner of a single flowing letter—without any citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts by title, date, or page number, since the argument should stand entirely on its conceptual merits.
