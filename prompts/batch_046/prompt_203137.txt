A recent essay circulated among colleagues summarizes Douglas Hofstadter's account of personal identity—particularly the view centered on the notion of a strange loop—along approximately these lines: "Hofstadter argues that the self is not a physical substance or an immaterial soul, but a high-level pattern—specifically, a strange loop of self-reference that emerges in sufficiently complex symbol-manipulating systems. Because what matters for identity is the pattern rather than the substrate that implements it, copying every relevant neural structure into a computer or into another brain would literally continue one's consciousness without ambiguity or interruption. He further maintains that, since thoughts, symbols, and influences constantly flow between people through language and shared culture, fragments of our strange loops already literally inhabit other minds. From this it follows that the 'I' is not confined to a single skull but is distributed across the social network of brains that share and replicate its patterns, and that the boundary between self and other is therefore a pragmatic fiction maintained for social convenience rather than a metaphysical reality." The essay draws the further conclusion that if Hofstadter is correct, then first-person persistence is essentially a collective phenomenon and localized subjecthood is an illusion we maintain for practical reasons. I am inclined to believe that this captures the anti-substantialist spirit of Hofstadter's position, yet I worry that it collapses distinctions he actually preserves. Is this summary fair? I would like you to audit it carefully: distinguish what accurately reflects his claim that the self is a symbol-level pattern sustained by recursive self-reference; identify any overstatements regarding substrate independence, the problem of duplication, and the literal presence of one's consciousness in others; and determine whether the essay's conclusion—that mind-boundaries are merely pragmatic and that the self is essentially collective—actually follows from the corrected premises, or whether it depends on the misrepresentations. In particular, please address whether Hofstadter's framework permits what one might call an inter-brain strange loop—a pattern of self-reference distributed across two distinct neural substrates—without that distribution collapsing either into a single unified consciousness (a kind of panpsychic merger) or into an eliminativist denial that there is any meaningful fact of the matter about who is who.
