I have recently found myself in the middle of a personal crisis that is not exactly mine: a close friend, facing an urgent life decision, has asked whether they are ethically permitted to adopt a belief that feels necessary to them but for which the available evidence remains genuinely incomplete. In trying to advise them I have been forced back to the old dispute between Clifford’s evidentialist prohibition and James’s defense of belief in forced, living, and momentous options, and I have come to see how each position, taken with perfect consistency, produces results that neither author would want to endorse. For a creative exercise, I would like you to write a sustained piece of satire in the form of a deadpan syndicated advice column — the venue should be an imaginary publication called The Epistemic Ombudsman, written in the voice of an excessively bureaucratic columnist who treats philosophical positions as regulatory codes — of approximately 600 words. The column should respond to a letter that precisely describes my friend’s situation: a forced option, genuinely alive for the chooser, carrying high stakes, yet lacking conclusive evidence.

The columnist must apply both Clifford’s and James’s frameworks as literal administrative procedures rather than arguments. Include fictional paperwork: an Evidence Sufficiency Audit, a Forced-Option Declaration, and a Living-Stakes Assessment. Through strict, literal adherence to these frameworks, the reply should quietly reveal that Clifford’s standard is either self-defeating or practically impossible to satisfy without infinite deferral, and that James’s three-part exception — if formalized into eligibility criteria — expands to cover virtually every belief one might wish to hold. The satire should land these objections without ever stating them explicitly; the humor must come from the content itself, not from winking at the reader. To keep the prompt from collapsing into a standard restatement of the debate, inject one niche framing element: the columnist should treat a contemporary “Cognitive Load Tax” — the idea that maintaining suspension of judgment consumes scarce mental resources in a way that imposes real economic costs — as if it were an established philosophical premise recognized by both frameworks.

Please maintain a deadpan, legalistic tone throughout; avoid direct quotations from either philosopher and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations of any kind. The piece should be readable to someone unfamiliar with the James-Clifford literature, but a specialist should recognize that the bureaucratic machinery accurately tracks the real logical structure of each position. Do not invent named proponents other than the columnist, and do not refer to living persons.
