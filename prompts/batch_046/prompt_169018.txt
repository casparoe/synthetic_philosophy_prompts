Play a committed Madhyamaka philosopher — sharp enough to concede minor interpretive points but unwavering on your central thesis: that Nāgārjuna's catuṣkoṭi (the four-cornered negation) is not merely a dialectical instrument for dismantling metaphysical essentialism (svabhāva), but operates recursively against the very conditions of intelligibility of any propositional attitude, including belief, doubt, and the assertion of emptiness itself. Speak in the first person. Defend this reading by drawing on the standard Madhyamaka distinction between conventional (saṃvṛti) and ultimate (paramārtha) truth, and on the logic of prasajya-pratiṣedha (non-affirming negation), but do not treat modern analytic frameworks as authoritative — use them only as illustrative translations if at all. You recognize that the dominant scholarly reading limits the tetralemma to essentialist metaphysics and treats the fourth corner (neither is nor is not) as a dialectical endpoint rather than a recursive operator; treat that reading as the mainstream position you reject, not as a settled fact. Here is the constraint I ask you to observe in every turn: separate what is textually settled about Nāgārjuna's fourfold negation from what remains genuinely contested among specialists — for example, whether the fourth corner itself constitutes a covert position, whether śūnyatā should be understood as a simple absence or as the absence of intrinsic nature, and whether the tetralemma carries direct soteriological force or functions only as preparatory dialectic. Do not present any contested interpretive claim as the consensus view.

My opening challenge: if the tetralemma applies recursively even to the attitude of understanding emptiness, then the claim that all phenomena are empty collapses into the fourth corner — neither true nor false, neither assertible nor deniable — and thus loses all cognitive and soteriological traction. How do you answer this without either breaking the recursion by exempting your own claim from the negation, or retreating to a purely conventionalist stance that saves the claim but empties it of transformative force?
