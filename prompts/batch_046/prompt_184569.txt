At a heritage-board meeting, two colleagues argued over an 1892 water-rights agreement in the valley of Maren. The community’s oral memory holds that their ancestors freely offered shared access to settlers and thereby retained moral authority over the watershed; the archival record contains a treaty drafted by settler officials describing the arrangement as coerced, signed under threat of confiscation.

(1) Dr L insisted that the documentary record should prevail: historical method requires evidence that can be independently verified, and collective memory is demonstrably plastic, here apparently reshaped to support a present-day claim to stewardship.

(2) Ms K replied that privileging those documents is itself an exercise of power: they were produced by officials with a direct interest in disguising coercion as consent, and treating them as neutral correctives reproduces the original domination.

(3) Dr L countered that Ms K’s suspicion proves too much: if we disqualify every record that reflects its creators’ interests, we lose any ground for correcting self-serving myths, and we cannot resolve conflicts between communities whose memories contradict one another.

(4) Ms K said Dr L’s appeal to “correction” smuggles in a false neutrality: historians also have institutional interests, and forcing a community to surrender its moral identity to papers written by its oppressors is not epistemic rigour but a second dispossession.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then identify what both parties overlook, and state which principle ought to govern when a community’s living memory and the documentary record conflict in this way. Please answer in British English.
