For a feature I am drafting on the neuroscience of subjectivity, I have adopted the working position that the first-person self is best understood as a strange loop in Hofstadter's sense: a high-level pattern in neural dynamics that achieves self-reference by mapping parts of the brain's representational architecture onto the system as a whole, analogous in form—though not in substrate—to the self-referential construction in Gödel's proof. To prevent the view from collapsing into pure syntax, I want to sharpen it with an additional condition: the loop generates a genuine self only if it contains what I will call an epistemic mirroring layer, meaning the system must represent not only its own states but its own act of representing them; without this second tier, the symbol remains causally active but phenomenologically inert, more like a recursive algorithm than a subject.

What would have to be true for this two-tier strange-loop account to fail? I want an honest map of its exposure, not a list of generic rival theories. Which empirical results—perhaps in split-brain unity, artificial neuromorphic loops that replicate the architecture without producing self-reference, or clinical disruptions of self-modeling—would be decisive rather than merely awkward? Which conceptual objections—say, that analog neural computation escapes Gödelian constraints, or that self-reference is epiphenomenal—would defenders of the view themselves concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as missing the point? Is there a live risk that the account can be qualified so that any complex predictive system eventually spawns such a loop, and if so, does that insulation count as a strength or as unfalsifiability? Rank the defeaters by how much they should move someone who holds this view, and say whether the position genuinely risks empirical or conceptual refutation or has been protected into safety.
