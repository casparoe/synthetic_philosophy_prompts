I serve on our city’s Civic Autonomy Board, and we must rule on a proposed five-year pilot called the Tranquility Covenant for District 7. Residents may voluntarily enroll to receive guaranteed material security, algorithmically optimized daily schedules, and the replacement of local electoral politics by expert administration, retaining only a formal right to exit after the term ends. The architects design the program around three named pillars—Miracle (predictable, technology-guaranteed welfare outcomes), Mystery (deliberately opaque governance presented as a benevolent enigma), and Authority (centralized decision-making that removes deliberative burden from citizens). Data from a voluntary cohort of four hundred show sharp gains in reported life satisfaction and reduced decision fatigue, yet enrollees rarely invoke exit mechanisms even when dissatisfied. Half the board reads this as legitimate welfare maximization; the other half sees it as institutionalizing the Grand Inquisitor’s argument that people naturally prefer comfort and certainty to the burdens of freedom, so that offering them freedom is itself a kind of cruelty. Draft the board’s formal decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—addressed to the mayor and the research oversight office. Lead with a single-sentence bottom-line recommendation. Then present reasoning that honestly engages the philosophical tension, including the strongest consideration on the side you reject; do not request outside citations or bibliographies, and work only from the conceptual structure of the case and the Inquisitor’s logic as applied here. Keep the entire memo to no more than 400 words, in formal institutional prose.
