I'm currently working through some ideas about moral responsibility and interpersonal blame, and a friend offered an argument that seems to dissolve the practice of blame entirely. I want to know precisely where the sleight of hand occurs, because the premises feel individually plausible yet the conclusion—that literally nobody ever has standing to blame anyone for anything—seems clearly unacceptable.

Here is the argument, laid out as precisely as I can manage it:

(1) A person has standing to blame someone for a particular failure F only if they are not hypocritical with respect to F.
(2) To be hypocritical about F is not merely to have done F oneself, but to blame another for F while being disposed, in relevantly similar circumstances, to do F oneself.
(3) Blaming someone for F involves publicly holding that F is wrong and that the agent is properly subject to censure for it.
(4) Publicly holding that F is wrong, and acting on that judgment by censuring others, creates a standing commitment to avoid F oneself—otherwise one's censure is not genuinely normative but merely performative.
(5) Moral failure is pervasive: for any serious failure F that people actually blame (dishonesty, cruelty, selfishness, and the like), every adult has done something belonging to the same family at least once.
(6) Therefore, anyone who blames another for F is disposed, in relevantly similar circumstances, to do F (because they have done it before and human dispositions are stable across contexts).
(7) So every blamer is hypocritical about F.
(8) Hence no one ever has standing to blame anyone for anything.

Please tell me which step I ought to reject. Explain why that step looks convincing on the surface, and then price the alternatives: does giving it up force us to weaken the non-hypocrisy condition, to deny that blame involves any kind of normative commitment, or to accept that hypocrisy is unavoidable and therefore not disqualifying? Briefly indicate how the main positions in this debate—those that treat hypocrisy as fully undermining standing, those that treat it as merely diminishing the weight of the blame, and those that deny that blame requires moral purity at all—would each handle the rejection of that particular premise.
