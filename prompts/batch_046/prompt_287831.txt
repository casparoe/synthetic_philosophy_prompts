I spent the better part of last evening in an unresolved argument with a close friend over whether intellectual property is a coherent metaphysical category or merely a useful legal fiction, and we left open whether the “ownership” of an idea is structurally analogous to owning a physical object. Tomorrow I am delivering the opening remarks—about ten minutes—at the symposium “Ideas Without Owners,” hosted jointly by the university philosophy department and the public library’s digital commons initiative. The room will be split: half are IP attorneys and open-access advocates who navigate case law and licensing frameworks daily; the other half are local writers, archivists, and students who have never read a patent brief. I need a polished, formal address that takes our disagreement as its structural spine rather than as decoration. Rather than rehearsing familiar labor-mixing accounts, develop and apply a precise conceptual distinction between “occupancy”—the state of holding an idea within one’s own cognitive space—and “enclosure”—the legal claim of exclusive rights over that idea’s use by others. This distinction must do real work in the speech: it should determine what I can consistently say about attribution, about the ethics of copying, and about plagiarism, without reducing the lawyers’ practical concerns to mere illusion and without losing the non-specialists in abstraction. The address should feel like a genuine continuation of the argument, reach a reasoned position on whether ideas can be genuinely owned, and land with both halves of the audience. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not invoke obscure doctrines, highly specific case references, or niche theoretical frameworks that depend on outside verification. If you allude to any specific thinker or text, paraphrase without quotation marks.
