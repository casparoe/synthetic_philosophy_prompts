You are preparing a single 90-minute session for an advanced seminar of about ten students in philosophy of language and aesthetics, and you want them to feel directly the tension that arises when translating a short lyric under extreme formal constraint: specifically, the problem central to the Le Ton beau de Marot idea that preserving rhyme, meter, and sound architecture across languages can force such radical semantic deviation that the identity of the poem itself becomes unstable. Do not open with a lecture on translation theory. Instead, design a structured three-phase classroom simulation with explicit rules, assigned roles, and a small-stakes evaluation mechanism. In the first phase, divide the students into three small producing groups and present them with a brief invented source lyric—four lines in a constructed pseudo-language, accompanied only by a prose description of its emotional arc and narrative situation, with no canonical text they might already know—then impose an identical and severe formal constraint set for an English rendering: an ABAB rhyme scheme using only masculine rhymes, an exact eight-syllable trochaic line, and a vocabulary restricted to a closed list of twenty monosyllabic words drawn from machinery and weather domains, with no proper nouns, no direct emotional terms, and no compound words that smuggle excluded meanings. Each group produces one version, and they must defend their choices by pointing to specific acoustic or structural features rather than to literal fidelity. In the second phase, have two student judges—who have not seen the source or the constraints—listen to anonymized readings of the three versions and rank them according to how closely each captures the emotional arc described in the original prose summary, using a simple point allocation, with the judges required to justify their rankings solely through descriptions of rhythm, rhyme, and sound pattern, never through semantic paraphrase. After the judges vote, reveal the source descriptions and constraint lists, and ask each producing group to classify every deviation they made as either forced by the rules or discretionary, then hold a collective vote on whether any version remains the same poem or whether formal preservation has generated an entirely new artifact that merely echoes the original structurally. For the debrief, map the phases precisely onto the conceptual problem: production demonstrates how constraint functions generatively rather than ornamentally; blind evaluation introduces the niche idea of formal residue, namely that structural analogies may carry emotional information independently of lexical content, extending the standard preservation debate into perceptual rather than semantic identity conditions; and the voting phase forces the distinction between content-based and form-based criteria for work identity. You should also anticipate what will predictably fail in the first run: students will initially resist the vocabulary closure as artificial, one group will likely attempt to cheat by embedding emotional meaning in phonetic coincidence, and the judges will tend to reward the version that sounds most conventionally poetic rather than the one that best mirrors the source's acoustic profile, so the facilitator must be prepared to replay recordings and press the judges for real-time acoustic justification. Finally, be honest about what the simulation cannot reproduce: because the source is available only through a prose summary, the activity cannot replicate the actual phenomenological gap of encountering an unknown language, so the facilitator must supply that gap through framing narration, and the activity cannot fully test cross-linguistic identity conditions since all versions share the same target language, meaning the theoretical connection between acoustic resemblance and translation identity must also be supplied by the facilitator after the exercise. Present all of this in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or enumerated lists, as though writing a single formal letter that flows continuously from the setting through the rules, the evaluation mechanism, the debrief, the predicted failures, and the acknowledged limitation.
