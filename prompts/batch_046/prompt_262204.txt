I've been thinking on my own about Adam Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator—particularly his idea that we come to judge ourselves by imaginatively placing ourselves in the position of an outside observer. Most casual explanations I have seen treat that spectator as a kind of neutral, all-seeing mirror, but that does not match my own experience: keeping up with other people's perspectives feels effortful and selective, not unlimited. Please argue for the position that Smith's impartial spectator should be understood not as an unbiased judge with equal access to every viewpoint, but as a resource-limited mechanism operating with something like a "sympathy budget"—that it distributes limited emotional and attentional resources across cases according to familiarity, repetition, and proximity, and that this selective allocation is not a corruption of moral judgment within Smith's framework but a functional feature of it. In developing this argument, clearly separate what is settled in Smith's own writings—such as sympathy as an imaginative projection rather than direct feeling, and the spectator's role in moderating self-love—from what is genuinely contested or speculative, especially any claim about attention limits, emotional economy, or systematic bias that extends beyond what Smith explicitly states. Do not present a disputed interpretation as the scholarly consensus. Please avoid bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific secondary works; I want the reasoning to stand on its own conceptual footing without relying on outside verification.
