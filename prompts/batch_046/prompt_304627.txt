Please adopt the role of an expert in the history of moral philosophy with deep familiarity with Adam Smith’s account of sympathy and the impartial spectator, particularly as developed in the Theory of Moral Sentiments. Before constructing the deliverable, please take a moment to brainstorm the core conceptual distinctions—between direct sympathy and the generalized view of the impartial spectator, between propriety and merit, and between individual and collective moral agency—that must survive translation into practice. Think carefully about which distinctions are load-bearing for the procedure and which can be simplified without betraying the theory.

I am a senior philosophy major enrolled in a seminar on Adam Smith and practical reason, and I also serve as a graduate-student mediator for my university’s academic-conflict resolution office. In that practical role, I review proposed resolutions to disputes arising in research groups and classrooms—issues of misattributed credit, exclusionary dynamics, or dignitary harm—approximately twice per month. Each case reaches me with a written proposal from the involved parties (a remedy, an apology, a structural change, or some combination), and I must recommend whether the proposal is adequate, inadequate, or requires revision. I have about forty-five minutes per case, I possess no subpoena power, I must sign off on a recommendation that a faculty advisor then reviews, and I am explicitly not a judge: my task is to assess whether the proposed resolution adequately addresses the harm from the perspective of those affected, not to assign blame or determine legal liability.

The difficulty is that my direct sympathy for complainants often overwhelms my judgment, especially when harm involves sharp status asymmetry (for example, a junior researcher versus a senior figure). At the same time, I have noticed that my sympathy for institutional actors—departments, labs, committees—is almost always abstract and therefore weak, which risks making me overly punitive toward collective remedies that might actually restore propriety.

I want to operationalize Smith’s framework—specifically his claim that moral judgment requires both an immediate sympathetic response and the correction of that response through the perspective of an impartial spectator—into a reusable decision procedure that I can run on each proposal. To adapt this to institutional harm, I am injecting a specific modification: because direct sympathy with a collective institution is impossible in Smith’s terms (there is no single consciousness with which to change places), the procedure must construct what I will call a “representative spectator.” This is done by requiring me to sequentially adopt the perspective of each affected party (the injured party, any individual accused party, and relevant bystanders with direct knowledge), then integrate these perspectives through a weighted generalization that gives greater weight to the perspectives of those with less institutional power, on the assumption that the impartial spectator’s view should not replicate existing status hierarchies.

Your deliverable should be a step-by-step decision procedure: a checklist or flowchart in words, with questions asked in a fixed order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the precise point where the procedure issues a clear “no” (the proposal is inadequate), the precise point where it issues a “yes” (the proposal is adequate), and the explicit points where judgment cannot be eliminated and the decision must be handed to a human value judgment rather than a factual determination. The procedure should be honest about where it will misfire—particularly where status asymmetries corrupt the representative spectator, or where emotional proximity makes place-taking unreliable.

After presenting the procedure, apply it to three cases:

1. A senior professor proposes a private, written apology and a correction to the publication record for having presented a graduate student’s analysis as his own. The student accepts the correction but insists the apology must be public because the harm was public; the professor refuses, arguing that public acknowledgment would destroy the student’s future collaboration prospects with other labs.

2. A department proposes, after a documented pattern of exclusionary behavior in a large research group, a new mandatory authorship-policy workshop and a rotating “climate officer” role, but explicitly declines to identify or sanction any individual perpetrators, stating that the harm was “structural, not personal.”

3. A lab director offers substantial financial compensation (independent project funding) to a postdoc who was systematically excluded from grant applications, but frames the compensation as “a generous investment in your career” rather than as redress for wrong done, and requires the postdoc to sign a non-disparagement agreement.

Please include at least one case where the procedure produces a recommendation you suspect I will find unsatisfying or counterintuitive—perhaps where the representative spectator, once properly calibrated, approves a remedy that feels insufficient from the injured party’s direct perspective, or where it rejects a remedy that feels generous but fails on propriety.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: its boundaries, the kinds of cases it should not be used for, and the specific failure modes—such as emotional exhaustion, status corruption, or institutional capture—that will cause it to misfire.
