A design consultancy has adopted a hiring and promotion rubric that evaluates candidates solely on “cross-domain analogical fluency”: they are given an unfamiliar system—say, a fungal mycelium network, a 19th-century postal routing algorithm, or a children’s game with hidden rules—and must map its deep structure onto an organizational problem. The rubric awards no points for domain-specific expertise or causal explanation, explicitly rejects “surface feature matching,” and rewards only “structural alignment that produces new conceptual blends.” What philosophical commitments about cognition, knowledge, and professional competence does this institutional practice presuppose? In particular, does it require the view defended in Hofstadter and Sander’s Surfaces and Essences—that analogy-making is not a heuristic accessory but the core mechanism of all cognition? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology, distinguish what the rubric strictly presupposes from what merely fits it well, and say what would have to change in the firm’s other practices—training, project assignment, quality control—if it replaced this analogical model with the view that analogy is decorative overlay on underlying causal-mechanical understanding.
