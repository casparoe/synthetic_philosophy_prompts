Write a deadpan parody of a residential lease agreement—numbered clauses, legalistic fine print, approximately 650 words—drafted by an invented property-management firm, “Present-Tense Properties, LLC,” that takes a strict presentist (A-theoretic) ontology as its governing law. The venue is a bureaucratic contract, not an essay; the voice should remain dry, precise, and entirely in-character, never breaking the fourth wall to explain the joke.

The satire should target the practical incoherence that results when presentism is applied literally to obligations, property rights, and contractual continuity over time. The humor must arise from extending real metaphysical commitments to mundane provisions—security deposits, maintenance duties, renewal terms, eviction—rather than inventing absurd premises.

To keep the prompt conceptually dense without requiring outside sources, structure the lease around three precise distinctions from the philosophy of time, applied with obsessive legal rigor:
1. The rejection of B-relations (before, after, earlier, later) as ontologically binding, since they presuppose the equal reality of non-present times; the lease must therefore be rewritten entirely in terms of A-properties (pastness, presentness, futurity).
2. The internal presentist dispute between the growing-block model (past and present exist; the future does not) and the moving-spotlight model (only the present exists, though it successively illuminates different contents); include a clause in which the landlord reserves unilateral right to switch between these sub-models, with explicit provisions for how each affects tenant liability for damage that “was” done.
3. The problem of cross-temporal identity: address whether the entity that signs the lease is identical to the entity that pays rent in a subsequent present, given that on strict presentism there is no four-dimensional continuant spanning the interval, and specify the evidentiary standard for proving such identity in arbitration.

The piece should be readable to someone unfamiliar with McTaggart, but a specialist should recognize that the author understands exactly which commitments are being pushed to absurdity. Do not mention any named living philosophers; invented legal precedents and firm names are welcome. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Conclude with a “Tenant Acknowledgment” section in which the signer must initial boxes confirming they understand that, once the present advances, this contract—like all past objects—no longer exists, and therefore no retroactive dispute can be litigated. Maintain sustained irony throughout: the reader should never need to be told that presentism is being satirized, only watch it collapse under the weight of a security-deposit clause.
