I have been trying to understand why our welfare system treats different kinds of need so differently, and the pattern seems stranger the closer I look. If you claim unemployment benefits, you must submit weekly proof that you are applying for jobs, and investigators actively look for unreported cash work; being caught is treated as fraud, not just a mistake. But if you receive disability support, there is no parallel duty to show you are trying to recover or work within your limits, and earning extra money informally is usually interpreted as evidence of improved health rather than as deception. More puzzling still, there is now a pilot scheme for housing assistance that requires recipients to attend monthly financial-ethics workshops and submit to unannounced home inspections, while mortgage-interest tax relief for second homes involves no comparable ritual of accountability, even though both are state transfers that reduce housing costs.

What implicit theory of desert, or of what citizens owe one another, makes these distinctions hang together? Does the practice presuppose that some needs are morally earned and others are suspicious by nature, or is there a consistent principle—perhaps about choice, visibility, or tractability—that explains why unemployment requires active proof of virtue, disability does not, and housing aid now demands a kind of character verification? Reconstruct the underlying view, say whether the three branches actually share one philosophy or depend on incompatible ones, and tell me what a welfare system built on the idea that need alone creates entitlement would have to change: which audits, conditions, and moral categories would disappear, and which might remain for purely practical reasons?
