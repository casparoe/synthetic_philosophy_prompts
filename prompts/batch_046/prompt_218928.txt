Here is an argument I heard in a discussion about AI governance that now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion seems to dissolve the very problem it starts from. (1) The alignment tax is the reduction in capabilities that results from imposing safety constraints, value learning, or interpretability requirements on a model. (2) The competitiveness constraint holds that, in a competitive market or geopolitical environment, actors will deploy the most capable systems available, so any system bearing a non-trivial alignment tax will be outcompeted by less constrained alternatives. (3) Therefore, for alignment to be sustainable, the tax must be driven arbitrarily close to zero. (4) But any alignment mechanism—whether RLHF, constitutional AI, mechanistic interpretability, or formal verification—consumes compute, increases inference cost, or restricts the model's action space, all of which are performance costs. (5) If we call these costs "taxes" only when they are large, but rename them "overhead" or "infrastructure" when they are small, then the distinction between taxed and untaxed systems is purely semantic. (6) Consequently, every aligned system bears a tax by definition; the only question is whether the tax is visible in benchmark scores or hidden in latency, energy use, or development time. (7) From (2) and (6) it follows that no aligned system can survive competition with an unaligned system that avoids even hidden overhead. (8) But if unaligned systems always outcompete aligned ones, then deploying aligned systems is never rational for any competitive actor. (9) This implies that either alignment is impossible in practice, or the competitiveness constraint is false—but the constraint is supported by basic economics and the observed behavior of technology firms. (10) So we are left with the conclusion that alignment efforts are necessarily self-undermining: the more thoroughly we align a system, the more overhead we impose, and the more certainly we guarantee its replacement by an unaligned competitor. Where does this argument go wrong? Is the error in treating all overhead as an alignment tax, in assuming that hidden costs affect competitiveness in the same way as visible capability gaps, or in the slide from "actors prefer capable systems" to "actors will always select the maximally capable system"? And if I want to preserve both the reality of the competitiveness constraint and the possibility of sustainable alignment, which premise must I reject, and what does that rejection commit me to—perhaps that alignment can be a capability multiplier rather than a drag?
