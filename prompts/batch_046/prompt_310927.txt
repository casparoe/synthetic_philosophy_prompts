People defending deontology love comparing moral rules to load-bearing walls: knock one out to save a life and the whole house collapses, killing everyone inside. It's used to justify why you can't violate one right even to save five. I want a real audit of this, not just a vibe check. Map the parts precisely -- wall, roof, foundation, the person trapped, the person with the sledgehammer. Then show where the analogy leaks: buildings don't have rights, walls aren't agents, and modern architecture uses redundant supports so removing one wall doesn't always collapse anything. Crucially, ask whether the "collapse" part just preuspposes the paradox of deontology is real rather than explaining it -- if the roof falling is just more rights violations, then the analogy assumes that violating one rule triggers a cascade, which is exactly what's at issue. Also, does the analogy hide that in acutal moral dilemmas we often do have backup norms? Seperate the disanalogies that actually matter for the argument from the ones that are just noise. If there's a better analogy that doesn't bake in fragility, suggest one. Write like you're explaining to someone who took one ethics course and remembers half of it.
