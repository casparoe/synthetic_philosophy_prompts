I'm designing a 75-minute seminar session for sixteen advanced philosophy students on the mechanisms of moral revolution—specifically, Bernard Williams's skepticism that large-scale normative change is driven primarily by moral argument, and Kwame Anthony Appiah's complementary claim that practices like dueling collapsed when the social economy of honor shifted and the groups sustaining the code lost prestige, with anti-dueling rhetoric serving largely as post-hoc justification for interests that had already turned. I want an in-class simulation or structured role-play that makes students feel this dynamic from the inside, not through lecture. The setting should be a fictional aristocratic society in which public insult traditionally demands satisfaction by duel, and in which different student roles—hereditary duelists, merchant bankers, newspaper editors, reformist legislators, and foreign diplomats—have hidden payoff structures tracking honor, economic survival, and family reputation rather than moral conviction.

Please design the rules, roles, materials, and timeline so that over three or four rounds students must respond to staged insult scenarios by choosing to demand satisfaction, propose arbitration, apologize, or invoke a new moral principle. Crucially, provide each role with hidden honor-ledger and interest-reserve cards that change value between rounds—simulating, for instance, a banking crisis that makes aristocratic death in duels economically catastrophic for dependents, or the rise of a popular press that makes cowardice visible but not shameful. Moral-argument cards should be available but should have zero or negligible payoff effect unless they happen to align with the hidden shift in honor or interest costs. The collective decision to abandon or retain dueling should emerge from the payoff structure, not from the quality of reasoning, and students should discover this only in debrief.

Include a precise timeline for the 75 minutes, a payoff scheme using points or status markers rather than real money, and rules that prevent the facilitator from explaining the thesis until after the simulation ends. Then provide a debrief that maps specific rounds onto Williams's and Appiah's claims—when did arguments suddenly become convincing, and why? Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time: likely that students in high-honor roles will dominate speech, or that participants will moralize their roles and resist the payoff logic. Finally, be honest about what the simulation cannot reproduce—such as the multi-generational erosion of shame structures or the genuine psychological weight of dishonor—and state clearly what feature of the idea the facilitator must supply verbally.
