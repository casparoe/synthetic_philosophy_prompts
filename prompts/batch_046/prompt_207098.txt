I'm currently working through a graduate seminar on the justification of deduction, and we've spent several sessions on Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise—the classic regress in which every attempt to justify a logical inference requires a further logical premise that itself needs justification. Most of the discussion has circled around familiar responses: that the tortoise confuses using a rule with stating it, that logical knowledge is ultimately practical or procedural rather than propositional, or that the regress is benign because we can accept rules without accepting them as premises. But I'm struck by the sense that these positions have been rehearsed for decades without a clear sense of which unresolved issues actually matter for the broader philosophy of logic, epistemology, or even formal practice.

I'd like you to help me set priorities. Among the open questions and lines of work that grow out of Carroll's problem—such as whether the regress reveals a necessary non-propositional component to logical knowledge; whether distinguishing premise-acceptance from rule-acceptance genuinely dissolves the challenge or merely relocates it; whether the structure of the regress generalizes to normative domains beyond logic (for instance, practical or moral reasoning); and whether substructural or paraconsistent settings, where standard structural rules are restricted or absent, generate a genuinely distinct form of the tortoise problem that demands its own resolution—which should be treated as upstream and which as downstream? In particular, I'm interested in whether there is a defensible ordering that depends on tractability with current conceptual tools, on how many other debates (say, about inferentialism, epistemic circularity, or the foundations of automated reasoning) hang on the answer, and on whether resolving a given question would actually change how we think about justification in formal systems rather than merely adding another dialectical move. Please defend your agenda with explicit criteria—importance, tractability, dependency among questions, and what practical or theoretical difference the answer would make—rather than offering a survey of positions. Also indicate what evidence or conceptual distinction would cause you to reorder your priorities, and be honest about which lines of work you would deprioritize because their resolution seems unlikely to shift much downstream, even if they are intellectually interesting in isolation. I am not asking for a bibliography or for citations to specific papers; I want a strategic, dependency-aware prioritization of what is worth working out next in this area.
