As a retired physicist who has lately turned to philosophy of mind in an amateur capacity, I find that a domestic disagreement has taken on a structure I cannot resolve with the conceptual habits of my former discipline.

My mother, experiencing progressive cognitive decline, now depends upon a domestic recording system that captures discrete fragments of her day—spoken phrases, meals, movements between rooms—and each evening assembles them into a unified first-person narrative that she accepts as the authoritative account of her experience. She frequently rejects her own immediate impressions when they conflict with this synthesized record. One of my siblings maintains that the system performs an essential restorative function: it reconstructs the continuous personal history that her deteriorating neural processes can no longer sustain unaided. Another sibling contends that the narrative is an artificial imposition—an engineered continuity layered over genuine discontinuities—no more revelatory of an underlying self than a macroscopic temperature value reveals the particular velocities of individual molecules.

In statistical mechanics, we treated aggregate properties as indispensable predictive interfaces that did not correspond to any single microscopic configuration, yet we never concluded that the thermometer thereby invented thermal energy. Applied to my mother's situation, however, the analogy becomes ambiguous. If conscious experience is, according to certain philosophical accounts, itself an interface—a constructed narrative that conceals an underlying fragmentation of neural events from the system that must navigate them—then does the recording device extend a natural cognitive function, or does it substitute a manufactured interface for an already constructed one, thereby mistaking a useful fiction for recovered reality?

The question is not medical but conceptual. Is the disagreement ultimately about whether a synthesized narrative can constitute personal identity when the substrate processes have become disordered? And does the comparison to macroscopic physical variables genuinely illuminate the metaphysical status of such a narrative, or does it improperly extend a scientific framework into a domain where the notion of an interface carries different ontological weight?

Your guidance on the underlying philosophical structure would be most welcome.
