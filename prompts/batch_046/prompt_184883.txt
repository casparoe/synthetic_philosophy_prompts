In discussions about whether policing should be reformed, defunded, or abolished, interlocutors frequently invoke the claim that the state holds a monopoly on legitimate violence, often treating it as a knock-down argument: if the state necessarily monopolizes legitimate coercion, then abolishing the police is conceptually impossible, since any replacement security structure would either be illegitimate or simply recreate the monopoly under another name. Before I respond, I want to disentangle what that slogan could actually be claiming. Can you lay out the distinct precise readings—descriptive (a sociological observation about modern governance), normative (a justification for centralized coercive authority), definitional (a conceptual condition for statehood), and functional (a claim about conflict resolution and contract enforcement)—indicating for each whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false? For each reading, specify whether it genuinely supports the conclusion that police abolition is self-defeating, particularly when the abolitionist proposal involves decentralized, non-state restorative institutions rather than a mere renaming of the police. Also indicate which of these readings is likely being smuggled into the argument when someone uses the line to dismiss abolition without engaging its specific institutional designs.
