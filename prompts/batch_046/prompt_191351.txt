I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind working on theories of well-being, and at a recent workshop two colleagues—both competent but with different methodological leanings—got into a sustained disagreement about a modified experience-machine case that I think cuts deeper than the standard Nozick-style intuition pump. I am reporting the exchange as a bystander and do not know which side is correct.

Marcus proposed a hypothetical "domain-specific experiential overlay" (DSEO): rather than total immersion in a simulation, the device lets you live in the actual physical world but selectively replaces your phenomenological feedback in specific domains—say, your research achievements and your intimate relationships—so that you genuinely experience your work as groundbreaking and your partnerships as deeply reciprocated, even though externally your papers are rejected and your relationships are strained. Marcus argued that if hedonism (or even a mental-state-based objective list theory) is correct, refusing the DSEO is plainly irrational. He said the familiar "contact with reality" objection confuses a metaphysical preference for realism with a substantive contribution to well-being; since the mental states are phenomenologically identical and sustained, well-being is identical, and the only reason to reject the overlay is an unexamined bias in favor of causal history.

Elena replied that Marcus is treating well-being as a momentary mental-state snapshot and ignoring its diachronic structure. She said the real problem with Nozick's machine is not false belief per se but "practical self-constitution"—the idea that well-being for agents like us involves being responsive to an independent reality through our own practical reasoning. Even a domain-specific overlay undermines this, because it replaces responsive engagement with predetermined feedback loops: your beliefs about success or failure are no longer tracking anything you can act on or learn from, which collapses the distinction between achievement and fabrication that makes achievement valuable in the first place.

Marcus came back that Elena is either being circular—claiming real engagement is good because it is "real"—or she is smuggling in an objective-list condition without defending it. He pointed out that people regularly derive well-being from self-deception, religious frameworks, or fantasy that are not responsive to external reality in any robust sense. If the mental state is the same, and if the overlay is stable and transparent to the user, then Elena owes an account of why causal responsiveness is intrinsically valuable for well-being rather than merely instrumentally valuable for producing mental states.

Elena countered that Marcus conflates phenomenological identity with functional identity. She granted that fantasy and belief can produce mental states, but argued that sustained well-being requires coherence over time between your practical reasoning and the constraints you actually face. The DSEO creates an internal instability: eventually the mismatch between simulated feedback and physical reality—papers never actually published, partners never actually expressing affection—generates practical contradictions that undermine the mental state from within. Your planning, your emotional expectations, and your self-concept become decoupled from the environment in which you must actually act, so the well-being is not genuinely sustained but artificially propped up by an external mechanism that hides its own failures from you.

Marcus responded that this "instability" argument is empirical speculation, not conceptual analysis. He said Elena has now shifted from an intrinsic-value claim to an instrumental claim—that real engagement is better because it produces more stable mental states—which actually supports his point. If the DSEO could be engineered to be perfectly stable, her objection would dissolve, which shows that her real commitment is just to mental-state stability, not to causal authorship. He concluded that unless Elena can show why being the causal source of your achievements contributes to well-being independently of the mental states it produces, her objection remains a relabeling of the intuition Marcus already explained away.
