I spent much of yesterday evening in a long, somewhat circular argument with a close friend—someone I usually agree with—after a seminar on evolutionary approaches to culture, and I am trying to understand whether our disagreement was substantive or whether we were caught in a structural impasse that neither of us could escape. The topic was whether religious belief and practice can be explained as a natural phenomenon within a unified evolutionary framework. On one side are the adaptationists, who maintain that religious behaviors—costly rituals, collective worship, moralized supernatural monitoring—constitute a coherent functional system that was directly favored by selection, whether through enhanced within-group cooperation, costly signaling of commitment, or group-level competitive advantage. On the other side are the cognitive byproduct theorists, who grant that religious outputs are widespread and metabolically expensive, but argue that they emerge from the interaction of several independently evolved cognitive mechanisms—hyperactive agency detection, minimally counterintuitive concept formation, source-memory biases in ritual transmission—without any single selection pressure ever targeting religion as such. What frustrates me is that this debate has persisted for decades, yet both sides now work with essentially the same ethnographic corpora, the same experimental paradigms in the cognitive science of religion, and the same formal models of cultural evolution. The adaptationists continue to demand a selectionist narrative for the stability and cost of the phenomenon; the byproduct theorists continue to deny that stability of outputs implies unity of origins, and the exchange never advances.

My own suspicion is that the disagreement has stalled not because of missing empirical data, but because the two sides hold incompatible, largely tacit commitments about what counts as explaining the same mechanism—specifically, whether a cluster of culturally reinforced cognitive outputs can be said to have a unified evolutionary explanation in the absence of a single adaptive target, or whether such an explanation necessarily collapses into a category error that treats a family-resemblance cluster as if it were a functional kind like vision or digestion. But I want to test this hypothesis rather than simply assert it. Is this stall actually verbal, turning on ambiguous uses of terms like mechanism, function, or religion itself? Is there a hidden empirical disagreement—perhaps about the heritability of specific ritual behaviors versus general religious sentiment, or about whether multilevel selection models can be empirically distinguished from simpler group-benefit stories in practice—that has not been properly operationalized? Or are the sides answering genuinely different questions—one about evolutionary origins, one about cognitive maintenance—while using the same vocabulary?

Please diagnose the structure of this disagreement, drawing on what proponents of each view actually argue rather than on generic summaries. I want to know whether it is substantive, verbal, or a case of cross-purpose reasoning; whether a hidden formal or empirical question is doing the work; and whether anything—a new conceptual distinction, a specific kind of modeling result, or a carefully designed comparative study—could actually move it, or whether the impasse indicates that the framing question is itself ill-posed because religion is not the kind of thing that admits a single evolutionary explanation. I am not looking for a bibliography, a list of authors to read, or citations to specific works; I want an honest assessment of whether progress should be expected and, if not, why the field should accept that limitation rather than continuing to recycle the same arguments.
