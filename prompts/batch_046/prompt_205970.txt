I've been stuck in a grad seminar on genocide studies where we keep circling the same question: is the Holocaust uniquely incomparable, or can we place it alongside colonial genocides and other bureacratic mass killings without losing something essential? One camp keeps bringing up the Historikerstreit and arguing that comparison dilutes the specifc Jewish experience and risks normalizing it; the other camp points to Bauman's idea that modern bureaucratic rationality actually makes the Holocaust comparable to other modern systems of violence, not less special. We keep looping. I think part of the problem might be that we're mixing up ontological uniqueness — the idea that this event breaks all historical patterns and couldn't be reproduced — with what I'd call epistemic indispensability, meaning you can't understand modernity or state violence without using it as the central case. I might be making up that second phrase, but it feels like people slide between them without noticing. So is this actually a disagreement about historical facts and sociology, or is it a hidden normative fight about moral memory and who gets to claim victim status? And if Bauman is right that modernity structurally produces this kind of violence, does that undermine uniqueness or just shift where the uniqueness lives? I want a diagnosis of what's actually stalling us — whether it's verbal confusion, bedrock intuition, or something formal — plus an honest take on whether any conceptual distinction could actually move the debate or if we're just going in circles forever.
