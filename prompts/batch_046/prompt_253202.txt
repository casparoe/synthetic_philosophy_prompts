Design a 100-minute classroom activity for about 14 graduate philosophy students that makes them feel, rather than merely discuss, why Bernard Williams thought some moral norms collapse through the loss of what he called their "conditions of intelligibility"—when a practice becomes not just wrong but socially unthinkable—rather than through rational persuasion or a shift in material interests, and where Kwame Anthony Appiah's work on honor worlds suggests that such collapses often happen through strategic reframing rather than direct moral argument. Use a fictional 19th-century mercantile-aristocratic society facing an external shock (e.g., a new legal framework and economic structure), with students assigned roles that carry hidden "honor ledgers" tracking both public reputation and private shame using symbolic token mechanics. The rules should force real, small-stakes choices—challenge, apologize, withdraw, or reframe—so that individually rational moves gradually undermine the collective conditions that make honor meaningful, producing a discontinuous collapse that no single argument or cost change explains. Structure it in three phases: setup and role assignment (20 minutes), live deliberation with payoffs (50 minutes), and only then a facilitated naming of the Williams/Appiah distinction (30 minutes). Include explicit rules, the token/reputation payoff scheme, a timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments of play onto the three explanatory models. Predict what will go wrong the first time—likely premature consensus or students treating tokens as pure currency rather than social meaning—and say how to correct it. Be frank about which feature of historical moral revolutions the simulation cannot reproduce, such as multi-generational drift or state coercion, and note that the facilitator must supply that context. Finally, separate in your response what is settled in the literature (e.g., that interest and argument alone struggle to explain rapid normative collapse) from what remains genuinely contested, particularly whether Williams's intelligibility account can be fully disentangled from interest-based explanations and whether Appiah's honor-world framework extends beyond the specific historical cases he analyzes. Do not present either scholar's view as the consensus.
