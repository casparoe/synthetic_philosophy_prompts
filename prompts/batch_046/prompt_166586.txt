I'm working through G.E. Moore's proof of an external world for a seminar on anti-skeptical strategies, and my study partner has constructed what she calls a "Moorean cascade" that now strikes me as suspiciously powerful. The argument runs as follows. (1) Moore establishes that there is at least one external object by directly knowing a particular premise—roughly, that he has hands—whose truth entails the conclusion, even though the skeptical argument denies precisely that kind of premise. (2) The legitimacy of this move depends not on disproving the skeptical scenario in general, but on the epistemic priority of a direct particular knowledge claim over an abstract theoretical challenge directed against it. (3) If that priority genuinely holds, it ought to hold for any direct particular knowledge claim that is logically incompatible with a skeptical scenario. (4) Therefore, whenever I directly know some particular fact F, and there exists a skeptical hypothesis S that entails not-F, I can construct a valid Moore-style proof that S is false. (5) I directly know particular facts about my present conscious experience—its qualitative character, its temporal location, its specific intentional content. (6) Every global skeptical scenario—brain-in-a-vat, evil demon, Cartesian dream, simulation—entails that some or all of these particular facts do not obtain in the way I take them to. (7) Applying the cascade, I can generate a separate Moore-style proof for each particular fact, thereby refuting every global skeptical scenario not collectively but one by one, leaving no conceptual space for them. (8) But this result is implausible: skeptical arguments remain conceptually coherent and are not automatically dissolved merely by pointing at one's hands, one's thoughts, or one's coffee cup; if the cascade were sound, skepticism would be not just false but systematically unformulable by anyone with ordinary direct knowledge. (9) So one of the steps must fail. Which step should I reject—especially if I want to preserve Moore's original proof and the idea that particular knowledge can sometimes outrank theoretical doubt? And what does giving up that step cost: does it collapse the cascade back into a single, non-generalizable exception, or does it require a more fundamental revision of how direct knowledge relates to skeptical scenarios?
