As I'm drafting a chapter on institutional longevity, I've been applying reference-class forecasting—the outside view—to estimate the remaining lifespan of the current American-led international order. By grouping it with historical cases in the broad class of hegemonic empires since 1500, I derive a base-rate lifespan clustering near 180 to 250 years, but colleagues in my department object that this aggregates entities with incompatible fiscal, military, and ideological architectures. I think their objection presupposes something philosophical: that using such a broad reference class implicitly commits me to treating empire as a natural kind with shared causal dynamics, and that without that essentialist commitment the aggregation is merely statistical gerrymandering—what forecasters call reference-class tennis. Is that diagnosis correct? Can the outside view be defended without an ontology of empire as a natural kind, and if so, what principled constraint prevents me from narrowing the class ad hoc—say, to post-1945 nuclear-armed alliances—until the base rate becomes unfalsifiable?
