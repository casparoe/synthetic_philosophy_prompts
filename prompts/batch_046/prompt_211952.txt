I sit on our city's Public Space Governance Board, which sends reccomendations to the council about sidewalk vending rules, noise curfews, and park permits. I was discussing this with my roommate last night — she's in a sociology class — and she mentioned this whole tight vs loose culture thing, but I think she's mixing it up with something else. Anyway, we've been fighting over whether to switch from our loose 'principles-based' system, where inspectors use thier judgement, to a tight 'checklist' code that spells out exactly what's allowed in every zone. One of my boardmates, Marcus, circulated a summary he heard on a podcast: he claims tight societies only function because people are terrified of punishment, and that once you switch to checklists you kill internal motivation, which is why our downtown stays clean without much policing. He also says research proves tight cultures have worse mental health and less innovation, so he wants us to stay loose everywhere except emergency zones. I think he's conflating correlation with causation and overstating the mental-health claim, but I can't pinpoint exactly where he's wrong. Meanwhile our planning intern threw out an idea she calls 'normative viscosity' — rules that get stickier and harder to change the longer they're enforced — and suggested we build that into whatever framework we pick so we don't lock ourselves in. The council wants a memo by Friday with a clear recommendation, either one unified framework or a split-zone model, plus reasoning they can actually use. Please draft it: reccomend a specific path, take Marcus's worry about destroying internal motivation seriously, and tell us whether his summary of the culture research is fair or distorted. Also address the split-zone idea and whether it just creates boundary conflicts where people game the borders. No formal citations needed, just solid reasoning.
