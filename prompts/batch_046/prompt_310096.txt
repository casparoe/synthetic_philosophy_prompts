I seek counsel regarding a friend who recently entered a democratically administered cooperative that supplies housing and sustenance in return for collective labor. Members may depart with thirty days' notice, and the assembly has never actually constrained anyone's private pursuits. Yet the cooperative's founding charter preserves an unrepealed clause permitting a simple majority to reallocate any member's personal workshop to communal use without compensation or right of appeal. My friend reports that this dormant authority makes her feel unfree in a manner she cannot articulate, even though she has never been directly obstructed. Simultaneously, she admits she joined largely to escape financial precarity, and now finds herself too depleted by obligatory collective work to pursue the artistic practice that originally drew her there. She is weighing three courses: departing to live independently despite economic vulnerability; remaining while campaigning to excise the clause entirely; or remaining while negotiating to replace it with a supermajority threshold coupled with a compensation guarantee. She asks which path best honors her freedom. I am reluctant to answer, because each option seems to address a different, and perhaps incompatible, conception of what freedom demands—whether it is the absence of external impediments, the absence of arbitrary dominion, or the capacity for genuine self-direction. How ought I to advise her?
