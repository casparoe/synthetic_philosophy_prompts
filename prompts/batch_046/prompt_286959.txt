I was arguing with a colleague in our ethics seminar about Taurek's worry that saving five people isn't automatically better than saving one just because the number is bigger — you know, the classic rescue-helicoptor dilemma — and I realized nobody ever turns this into something a disaster cordinator can actually run in real time.

So here's my ask: build me a decision procedre for a regional emergency office that has to allocate one heavy-lift unit between two simultaneous flood zones, with a five-minut clock. I want it grounded in the idea that raw headcount shouldn't dominate, but also shouldn't be ignored automatically. Invent a framing they can use: call it the 'comparability audit', where the first step is checking whether the two groups sit in the same moral category — similar risk profile, no pre-existing special duty, comparable agency — before numbers are even allowed into the calculation. The checklist should move in order, have a hard stop where the answer is 'do not deploy on count alone', and flag the exact points where the decision stops being technical and becomes a value judgment the cordinator has to own personally. Apply it to three scenarios: (A) one stranger versus five strangers, all same risk; (B) two people with critical injuries versus four with minor ones; (C) one immediate family member of the cordinator versus six strangers with moderate risk. Make sure at least one case comes out with an answer the cordinator will hate, and be explicit about where the procedre breaks or invites bias. Also please say how confident you are in each step of the audit, and single out the point you're least sure about — I suspect it's drawing the line between legitimate partiality and plain unfairness, but I want you to name your own weakest link rather than just echoing me.
