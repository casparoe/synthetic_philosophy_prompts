You are a professional political philosopher with expertise in normative ethics and the philosophy of law. Before giving your main answer, take a moment to think carefully and briefly brainstorm what a normative analogy must preserve for its conclusion to survive intact. Then proceed with the audit.

In libertarian and classical-liberal discourse, taxation is routinely analogized to armed robbery: the state is the masked assailant, the tax code is the weapon, the citizen’s pre-tax income is the wallet, and democratic authorization is merely a majority vote among the robbers to continue the heist. The analogy is used to license the conclusion that all non-voluntary taxation is morally illegitimate, regardless of popular consent, because theft does not become legitimate if more people approve of it. I want you to audit this correspondence rigorously. First, present an explicit table mapping the source domain onto the target: what plays the role of the robber, the threat of force, the victim, the goods taken, the possibility of refusal or exit, and the use of the proceeds afterward? Next, separate the decorative disanalogies from the structural ones—specifically, consider whether democratic authorization, the non-excludable nature of public goods, the modern practice of employer-level withholding before a worker ever takes physical possession of gross income, and the absence of a clearly defined pre-political property right in gross earnings break the inference. Does the conclusion that taxation is morally equivalent to theft survive once these fractures are on the table, or was the analogy smuggling in an undefended premise about original acquisition?

Finally, assess whether a repaired analogy—say, taxation as compulsory membership dues in a territorial cooperative, or as conscription of labor-time rather than confiscation of goods—could do the same normative work without the property-rights baggage. Tell me whether the debate should be reframed away from robbery analogies entirely, and if so, what conceptual distinction (for instance, between pre-contractual holdings and post-institutional obligations) should replace them at the center of the argument.
