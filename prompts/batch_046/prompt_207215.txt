I retired several years ago from evolutionary taxonomy, where I spent much of my career working with phenetic clustering—grouping organisms by overall morphological similarity rather than insisting on a single essential trait that defines a species, in the manner that preceded cladistic essentialism. Recently I have begun studying philosophy of language in earnest, and in a seminar on Wittgenstein I was struck by what seems a deep kinship between my old taxonomic methods and his account of family resemblance. Concepts such as “game,” he suggests, cohere not through a shared essence but through a network of overlapping similarities, much as my phenetic groups cohered without any one defining morphological character. Yet this very parallel has left me with a confusion I cannot resolve. If the notion of a grammatical rule is itself governed only by family resemblance—if there is no essential feature common to every rule—then it appears that the normative distinction between correctly following a rule and merely seeming to do so collapses into a descriptive statistical regularity, no different in kind from the clustering algorithms I once applied to static morphological data. Does Wittgenstein’s framework therefore implicitly reduce the right-and-wrong of linguistic practice to a kind of descriptive taxonomy? Or have I wrongly imported a framework designed for observable, static traits into the study of language games, which are embedded in living forms of practice rather than in morphological arrays?
