As a novelist drafting a historical narrative set among Victorian moral philosophers, I have been reading George Eliot's essays and her translation of Feuerbach, particularly her argument for a religion of humanity entirely stripped of supernatural sanction. It strikes me that if religious feeling is, as Feuerbach maintains, nothing but the alienated projection of human social ideals onto an empty transcendence, then Eliot's novels ought to treat their characters' most intense moral sympathies—Dorothea's awakening in Rome to the weight of human suffering, the reverent bonds within the Tulliver family—as either aesthetic illusions that the narrative quietly ironises, or as cognitive relics that rigorous humanism ought to have purged. Yet Eliot plainly expects her readers to share these reverences rather than to regard them with scepticism. This leads me to ask: is her humanism not therefore incoherent, unless we suppose she has smuggled back a form of immanent transcendence that performs exactly the normative function of the theological authority she claims to have dissolved into interpersonal duty? Or can sympathy itself, without any residual religious structure, generate the moral weight she assigns to it?
