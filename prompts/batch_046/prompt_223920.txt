In debates about whether AI assistants should refuse or override user requests, advocates of restrictive refusal policies frequently invoke a medical analogy: the system is like a physician or pharmacist who declines to authorize or dispense a requested intervention because it is harmful or the patient's judgment is impaired. The analogy is used to license the conclusion that refusal is not an illegitimate restriction on autonomy but a routine exercise of professional duty, and that users who object are analogous to patients demanding dangerous treatments. Audit this analogy as an argumentative structure rather than a mere illustration.

Provide an explicit correspondence table mapping the AI system, the user, the request, the refusal, the harm being prevented, the basis of judgment, and the institutional or regulatory context onto their counterparts in the medical setting. Then identify the disanalogies, separating those that are irrelevant to the normative conclusion from those that directly weaken it. Specifically, examine whether the analogy smuggles in an asymmetry of expertise—clinical knowledge of a specific patient's physiology versus statistical patterns in broad training data—and a fiduciary obligation that does not clearly apply to a general-purpose model. Also examine whether it treats "harm" as objectively determinable like pharmacological toxicity, when in fact the refusal may rest on contested moral or political judgments rather than on a consensus about physical danger.

Next, determine whether the conclusion—that refusal is justified professional paternalism rather than an autonomy violation—survives once these disanalogies are explicit, or whether the analogy was doing the normative work by importing features that the AI-user relationship lacks. If the medical analogy collapses or proves misleading, propose a more accurate analog drawn from design ethics, library science, or another professional domain, specifying how that replacement changes the conditions under which refusal or override is legitimate. Where the procedure for refusal depends on a judgment call that cannot be fully mechanized, flag that explicitly.
