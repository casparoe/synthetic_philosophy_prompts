I was thinking about Hofstadter's old Letter Spirit project and got stuck on something that feels like it should be simple but isn't. We can recogize an 'a' whether it's Times New Roman, some wild graffiti tag, or a half-erased chalk scribble where half the loop is missing. But what exactly is the conceptual thing we're tracking? Is it a cluster of geometric invariants (closed loop, right-side stem), a position in some abstract style-space where every font is a coordinate, or is 'a-ness' more like a family resemblance with no real essence?

I'm not asking for a summary of the program itself. What I want is an explanation of the conceptual tension between treating letter categories as bounded regions in a feature space versus treating them as flexible prototypes that can absorb almost arbitrary distortion as long as some relational structure holds. Specifically, how does the idea of 'letterform essence' survive cases where the usual features are violated—like an 'a' drawn with its loop broken, or an 'a' that looks almost identicle to a malformed 'o'? Does recognizing these depend on top-down contextual expectations (the word it's in, the alphabet sequence), or is there genuinely a bottom-up perceptual mechanism that locks onto letter identity before context kicks in?

Also, there's this weird parallel to the binding problem in vision—how do we bind scattered visual features into a single categorical object? For letters, the binding seems to happen across wildly different instantiations. So explain: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? Not just the psychology, but the conceptual structure of the category itself. I keep feeling like there's a hidden assumption that letters have a Platonic ideal form, and our brains just accomodate it, but that can't be right because there's no canonical 'a' that covers both Helvetica and medieval blackletter. So what's actually going on conceptually?
