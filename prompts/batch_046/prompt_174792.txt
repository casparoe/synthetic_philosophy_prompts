Below are the notes and central argument for an essay tentatively titled “Predictive Weighting and Mary’s Room: An Architectural Reply to the Knowledge Argument.” The essay aims to show that Mary’s post-release color experience can be fully accommodated within physicalism once we distinguish propositional knowledge from indexical updates to an embodied generative model.

The core argument runs as follows. In the standard formulation, Mary is a scientist who knows every physical and functional fact about color vision but is confined to a black-and-white environment. Upon leaving and seeing a red tomato, she allegedly learns something new—what it is like to see red—thereby undermining physicalism. The familiar physicalist replies invoke the ability hypothesis, phenomenal concepts, or direct acquaintance, but each leaves an intuitive residue: Mary’s experience still feels like an ontological discovery rather than a mere cognitive reorganization.

My proposed reply injects a predictive-processing framing. Imagine Mary not as an ordinary biological human but as an optimally rational predictive system that has acquired every statistical regularity governing chromatic processing, yet has never instantiated the specific precision-weighting profile that accompanies embodied chromatic prediction error. When she first encounters red, her generative model does not acquire a new world-directed fact; rather, it updates its self-model with first-person precision weights tied to chromatic channels. The “new knowledge” is therefore indexical and architectural: Mary learns how her own system assigns salience to a particular class of prediction errors. The feeling of revelation is explained not by the intrusion of a non-physical property but by the sudden high-precision mismatch between her prior (no chromatic salience) and her new sensory state.

The essay then considers an objection: if the update is fully physically implemented—merely a change in algorithmic parameters or neural weights—why does it present itself as knowledge about the world rather than about the self? The reply distinguishes between the content of the update (world-model) and the mode of access (self-model restructuring). Mary’s mistake, on this view, is a kind of epistemic misattribution: she takes an internal calibration event to be an external ontological disclosure. Once this misattribution is corrected, the knowledge argument collapses into a claim about the structure of embodied cognition, not about the limits of physical explanation.

Finally, the essay asks whether this reply concedes too much. By allowing that Mary’s cognitive architecture genuinely changes, do we grant that physicalism must include dynamic, first-person architectures that are not reducible to static propositional knowledge? The tentative conclusion is that this is not a concession to dualism but a refinement of physicalism: physicalism should be understood not as the thesis that all facts are statically propositional, but that all facts—including indexical, precision-weighted updates—are ultimately grounded in physical processes.
