I have been turning over a line of reasoning—presented to me recently in a seminar discussion—that seems to demonstrate that moral evaluation is impossible in any collective context, and I cannot locate the precise misstep. The argument proceeds as follows.

(1) An agent is morally accountable for an outcome only if there exists an accessible possible world in which that agent acts differently and, holding every other actual condition fixed, the outcome in question does not occur.

(2) In large-scale collective harms—whether anthropogenic climate damage, industrial animal agriculture, or democratic elections in populous states—no single agent's different action can alter the aggregate outcome, given that the behavior of all other participants remains fixed.

(3) By parity of reasoning, in collective goods—such as ten individuals jointly pushing a boulder away from a victim—holding nine constant, the tenth is never necessary; hence no individual can claim moral credit for the rescue.

(4) Therefore, in all collective contexts, individual actions are neither morally required nor morally forbidden, and no agent bears either blame or praise for the aggregate result.

(5) But this conclusion is intolerable: it strips climate responsibility, dietary ethics, and democratic participation of all normative force, and it denies that joint rescuers act commendably.

(6) Hence at least one premise must be rejected.

The reasoning strikes me as valid on its face, yet I am reluctant to abandon a counterfactual analysis of moral responsibility altogether, since it seems indispensable for distinguishing culpable harm from mere accident. I am also unwilling to concede that collective action lies entirely outside the scope of morality. Where does the argument go wrong? Does (2) misapply the accessibility relation by illicitly fixing the conduct of others? Or does (1) conflate causal contribution with moral accountability in a way that only becomes visible under conditions of overdetermination? Which step should be rejected, and what does that rejection cost for our account of individual obligation within collective harm?
