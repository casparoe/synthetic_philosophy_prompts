Question: Norman Malcolm contends that describing a dream as an experience occurring during sleep is conceptually incoherent, because there is no criterion—available to the sleeper or to any observer—by which mental content could be verified at the time it is supposedly occurring. Daniel Dennett replies that this charge only targets occurrent phenomenal states, and that we should instead understand dreams as narratives assembled and loaded into memory during sleep, then retrieved upon awakening. Evaluate Dennett’s reply: does it actually answer Malcolm’s concern about the absence of verification criteria, or does it merely relocate the problem to the waking moment? In your answer, clearly distinguish between denying that dreams are phenomenal experiences and denying that they are experiences altogether.

Student answer: Malcolm’s core objection is that there is no verification procedure for mental states during sleep, so calling a dream an “experience” that took place while unconscious involves a category mistake. Dennett accepts this diagnosis but limits its scope: on his cassette model, the brain generates a narrative during REM and stores it; upon waking, the subject accesses this trace and produces a report. This means dream reports are verified not by inspecting sleep, but by examining the coherence and detail of the retrieved narrative against the stored trace itself. So Dennett saves the truth of dream reports—they accurately describe present memory contents—without claiming there was conscious experience during storage. Still, Dennett insists that the retrieval event carries phenomenal consciousness: the “feel” of recalling a dream is a genuine experiential quality in the waking moment. From this it follows that Malcolm’s incoherence charge is dissolved, since the report concerns a present memory-state with full waking criteria rather than an unverifiable sleeping state. Thus Malcolm and Dennett converge on denying phenomenal consciousness during sleep, and Dennett’s contribution is to show that reports can be truthful descriptions of memory without being descriptions of prior occurrences.
