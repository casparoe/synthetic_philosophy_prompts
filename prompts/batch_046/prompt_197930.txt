I'm revising my graduate seminar on practical reason, and I'm struck by how little progress we've made since Buridan's original formulation: students can recite the parable of the ass, but they treat the paralysis as a psychological quirk rather than a deep tension between different norms of rationality. To move past this, I want an engineered scenario—concrete, institutionally grounded, and free of any hidden asymmetry—that forces two accounts of rational choice into direct conflict over what an agent should do when preferences are genuinely flat.

Please construct a one-paragraph thought experiment, realistic and not sensational (imagine a hospital allocation board or a university hiring committee), in which a decision-maker must choose between two candidates or treatments that are identical in every relevant respect—no tie-breaking feature, no probabilistic distinction, and no appeal to external randomness. Engineer the case so that a strict maximization account, which permits only choice guided by a pre-existing preference ordering, delivers the verdict of paralysis or mandatory deferral to an external mechanism, while a dynamic-commitment account, which allows an agent to generate and bind itself to a non-arbitrary decision rule at the moment of choice, delivers the opposite verdict of decisive, justified action. The asymmetry must not come from any hidden feature of the options themselves.

After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it meets these specifications: state the exact choice situation and the method each theory employs; specify the relevant nearby worlds or counterfactual conditions in which the symmetry is broken differently; explain why each theory reaches its conclusion; and identify the precise structural feature—whether it is the absence of a prior ordering, the institutional mandate to decide, or the possibility of self-modifying rules—that does the work. Then explain what collapses if that feature is removed. Finally, provide an honest residue report: how would a defender of the maximization view attack this case, particularly by arguing that the dynamically generated rule is not rational but merely arbitrary? Do not cite specific philosophers by name, do not include bibliographies or reading lists, and keep the analysis within the vocabulary of decision theory and practical reason rather than appealing to exotic metaphysics or empirical literature I would need to verify externally.
