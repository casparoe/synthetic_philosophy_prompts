At a pub quiz night, two friends of mine — Leo and Mara, neither of whom studies philosophy formally — got stuck in a disagreement about the doomsday argument. They had both read a popular science article explaining that, if you treat yourself as randomly drawn from all humans who will ever live, being born near the hundred-billionth human suggests the total human population is unlikely to reach the trillions, which in turn suggests that extinction is not far away.

Leo defended the argument. He proposed a refinement he had thought up himself: instead of sampling from all biological humans, we should restrict the reference class to observers who are actually capable of understanding and posing the doomsday question — people with sufficient education, literacy, and access to the idea. He argued that this makes the sample more precise without breaking the logic. If we are among the first hundred billion such cognitively equipped observers, he said, then the total pool of them probably is not enormous either, and the near-extinction conclusion still follows.

Mara objected that this is circular in a subtle way. By defining the reference class around the ability to reason about population statistics, she said, Leo is smuggling in assumptions about technological and educational development that are themselves correlated with population size and historical epoch. She also invoked what she called the self-indication assumption — the idea that worlds containing far more observers are a priori more likely to contain you — and claimed that this cancels the doomsday update entirely.

Leo replied that Mara is mixing levels. He said the self-indication assumption only affects the prior probability over world sizes: it makes large worlds more likely before you observe anything. Once you are actually in a specific world, he argued, the self-sampling assumption still governs your expected position within that world's population. Therefore, he claimed, the two assumptions operate at different levels and do not cancel; at most, they combine in a way that still favours large worlds, which undermines rather than saves the doomsday reasoning.

Mara countered that Leo has not shown any formal interaction between the two assumptions — he is merely asserting that they work at different levels without demonstrating that they do not interfere. She also pressed her technological-dependency point: because Leo's capable-observer class depends on institutions that only arise in certain kinds of societies, the sampling frame is unstable across the very worlds being compared. She said this instability breaks the statistical analogy on which the argument rests.

They asked me to referee. I could not, since I have no academic training and am not sure which parts are standard objections and which are Leo and Mara's own inventions.
