I've been thinking about the unilateralist's curse — the situation where anyone in a group can take an action that effects everyone, so even if most people want to be cautious, the fact that one actor can defect and grab the benefits makes the cautious outcome fall apart. I want to apply it to a pretty specific corner: archiving and publication norms in tiny scientific subfields, like comparative mythology or paleobotany, where there isn't really a gatekeeper and any single prominent researcher can drop a controversial dataset or interpretation that reshapes the whole field's reputation. My position is that in these micro-disciplines the curse is basically unavoidable without formal acredation bodies, because voluntary norms always collapse once one lab decides the payoff from being first outweighs the shared cost.

What would actually count against this? Not just generic "people can cooperate" — I want the real defeaters. Would long-term stability in these fields count, or is that just selection bias? Would showing the payoff structure is symetric — that being first doesn't actually confer much advantage in such a small audience — break the analogy entirely? And I want to check whether I've qualified the view into unfalsifiability: if every norm collapse gets explained away as "the enforcement wasn't formal enough," is that insulation a real problem for a claim like this? Also, are there small-group dynamics from other domains, maybe open-source software governance or local fishing cooperatives, that would show the comparison is broken from the start?
