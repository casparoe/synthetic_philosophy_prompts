Next week I defend a modified non-aggregative position on rescue obligations at a moral-philosophy seminar. The audience is composed of analytic ethicists steeped in Scanlon’s contractualism and Kamm’s non-consequentialist arguments, one decision theorist who thinks natively in expected-utility terms, a legal philosopher who works on collective harm and tort, and graduate students who have just read Taurek and Parfit on the numbers problem. They are skeptical of anti-aggregation but not hostile; they care especially about consistency, about ad hoc distinctions, and about whether a view can be formalized without collapsing into either pure pairwise comparison or hidden utilitarianism.

My thesis is this: in rescue dilemmas, the moral weight of saving more lives is not generated by cardinal aggregation of individual claims, but by the presence or absence of what I call structural co-exposure—a relation among potential victims such that the risk they face is institutionally or causally unitary rather than distributively independent. Consequently, saving five co-exposed passengers is not “saving five times as much” but honoring a single, more stringent obligation that arises only when vulnerability is jointly structured; saving one of five independently endangered strangers, by contrast, generates no stronger duty than saving any one of them, and selecting among them is permissible. This preserves Taurek’s core anti-aggregation intuition while allowing that numbers sometimes matter indirectly, through the ontology of the risk structure rather than through summation.
