I have been preparing a long-form essay for a science-and-ideas publication on what I am calling the "competence-comprehension inversion"—the strange parallel between Darwin's demonstration that exquisite biological design (the vertebrate eye, the avian wing) arises through processes entirely devoid of foresight or understanding, and Turing's proposal that we should attribute comprehension to a machine precisely when its behavioral outputs are competently indistinguishable from ours, regardless of whether internal understanding is present. In conversation with colleagues, I realized that most readers hold implicit positions on these two inversions without recognizing that the commitments do not line up neatly: some people think Darwin shows that nature is full of intelligent design without an intelligence, while others think he merely describes efficient causation that never deserved the name "design" in the first place; similarly, some read Turing as dissolving comprehension into behavior, others as establishing a pragmatic criterion that leaves real comprehension untouched but hidden.

For this piece, I need a formal decision tree—presented as a taxonomy with branching questions rather than a list of named schools—that allows a reader to locate their actual commitments. Each node should pose a concrete claim or scenario answerable without prior knowledge of philosophical labels. The root should split on Darwinian adaptation: does blind natural selection producing complex functional structure constitute competence in the absence of comprehension, or is "competence" here only a misleading metaphor? From that branch, introduce Turing's behavioral criterion: if a system produces conversational or problem-solving outputs matching those of a comprehending agent, does that render internal comprehension explanatorily redundant, epistemically inaccessible, or conceptually irrelevant? Include a further split on whether biological and artificial cases are symmetrical—specifically, whether genetic information or evolutionary history supplies a minimal form of encoded comprehension that distinguishes Darwin's inversion from Turing's, or whether both cases share the same structure of output without inner life.

The leaves must carry named positions where they exist, or be marked "unnamed" if the combination is unoccupied, each accompanied by one sentence stating its most awkward philosophical commitment (for example, being forced to deny that evolutionary adaptation produces anything genuinely design-like, or being forced to accept that a system lacking all subjective experience nonetheless possesses linguistic comprehension). Please flag any pair of positions your tree genuinely cannot separate. To prevent the taxonomy from collapsing into a familiar survey of philosophy of mind, inject one niche analytical distinction—such as whether competence should be understood dispositionally (as a stable capacity across counterfactual environments) or actualistically (as successful performance in the specific context at hand)—and place it as an explicit branching node with its own logical consequences shown. Write the whole instrument in a polished, formal register appropriate for an intellectually ambitious magazine audience, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citation; the goal is a self-contained conceptual map.
