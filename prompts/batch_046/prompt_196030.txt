Consider the recurring pattern in debates over events that occupy the boundary between legend and documented history—such as the Trojan War, the Arthurian period, and the Exodus narrative. In each case, a textual tradition describes a discrete, transformative occurrence compressed within a narrow temporal window and often anchored to a specific leader or coalition. Archaeological and comparative-historical investigation, by contrast, typically yields a distributed picture: not an absence of evidence, but a diffusion of demographic, material, and textual indicators across broader regions and longer durations, with no single stratigraphic or institutional breakpoint that maps cleanly onto the narrative's dramatic structure. The notable move is not that defenders of the textual tradition deny this diffusion. Rather, they frequently accept the distributed material data as accurate background conditions or long-term structural trends, while insisting that the narrative nevertheless isolates a singular causal, symbolic, or covenantal rupture that the archaeological record—by its very nature—cannot register or falsify. They do not treat the gradual substrate as disconfirming; they relegate it to a preparatory or consequential role, preserving the discrete event as an irreducible historical core.

This maneuver seems different from straightforward denial of counterevidence, and different from the claim that myth symbolically encodes historical memory. It is a specific epistemic posture: the explicit acknowledgment of a continuous material substrate combined with the assertion of a discontinuous interpretive frame that the substrate is said to be constitutionally incapable of adjudicating. Does this posture have an established name in the philosophy of history or in historiographical theory? How does it differ from an ordinary auxiliary hypothesis, from the Annales-school distinction between event and long-term structure, and from theories that treat narrative compression as a purely literary or mnemonic device? If no precise term exists, what would be the appropriate definitional boundaries—would the concept apply only when the discrete frame functions as an identity-constitutive commitment for the community preserving it, or also in purely academic disputes about periodization and causal attribution?
