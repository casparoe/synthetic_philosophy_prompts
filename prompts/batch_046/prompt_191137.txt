I'm a copy editor for an international magazine and I keep getting copy that slips in genralisations about whole peoples — "the Japanese are reserved", "Nigerians are entrepreneurial", that sort of thing. I want a reusable decision procedure my writers can apply in about ten minutes before filing, grounded in whatever philosophy of social kinds and ethics actually says about generalising, but stripped down for people who haven't read the literature. It needs ordered tests: first check if the claim is distributive or modal, then whether it survives counterfactual population mixing, then insider versus outsider voice, then institutional versus dispositional framing. For each test say what the writer should do — add a qualifier, rephrase, flag for debate, or kill the line. Apply it to three worked cases: a statistical claim about punctuality, a reclaimed insider stereotype, and one where the checklist says delete but the writer insists it's the paragraph's whole point. Be explicit about which steps are just judgment calls in procederal clothing, and say what the procedure can't settle at all. Please write the answer in British English — and don't worry if there's a typo or two in this prompt.
