I am an independent compliance auditor for a multilateral development fund, and every quarter I must sign, personally and without legal staff, a binding determination of whether a recipient state has entered “systemic democratic backsliding” that mandates immediate suspension of disbursements. I need a reusable assessment procedure—ordered tests with explicit decision nodes, documented in under forty-eight hours—that applies a specific structural theory: backsliding should be declared only when the collapse of horizontal accountability (independent courts, audit bodies, anti-corruption agencies) interacts with competitive asymmetry (practical, not merely formal, barriers to media access, campaign conditions, and administrative resource neutrality) to produce constitutional self-entrenchment (legal or procedural changes that bind future governments regardless of electoral outcomes). Routine partisan governance, temporary emergency measures, or isolated institutional weakness must not trigger a positive finding.

The procedure must specify what evidence counts for each mechanism when formal rules remain technically intact but informal norms have eroded; it must flag every point where judgment is unavoidable and must be recorded rather than disguised as measurement; and it must say what to do when quantitative indicators are unavailable. Apply it to three cases: (A) a government that preserves judicial tenure statutes and electoral law on paper, but replaces all audit-body leadership with party loyalists, restricts broadcast licenses to allied outlets, and amends the constitution to require a two-thirds supermajority to alter its economic framework; (B) a popular administration whose climate and labor reforms I support, which expands court membership, directs regulators to deny licenses to outlets it designates as spreading “corporate disinformation,” and embeds its regulatory framework behind the same constitutional supermajority lock; and (C) a regime I oppose that openly suppresses opposition rallies, manipulates voter rolls through local militias, and intimidates journalists, yet leaves judicial review statutes, audit-agency charters, and media-licensing procedures formally untouched and makes no constitutional amendments that bind successors. One of these applications must yield a verdict I will dislike—either a backsliding finding against the government in case B, or a negative finding against the regime in case C—so that the procedure’s independence from my preferences is visible.

Conclude by stating exactly what this procedure cannot determine: whether the observed interaction reflects malicious intent or mere institutional incompetence; whether democratic recovery is probable once funding is suspended; and whether direct, non-structural repression that bypasses institutional channels should be classified separately. The deliverable is the procedure itself, not a narrative about the countries.
