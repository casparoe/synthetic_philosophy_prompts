I'm trying to determine whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible—situations in which an agent genuinely ought to do A and genuinely ought to do B, A and B are strictly incompatible, and no further moral fact resolves which takes precedence. Here are my gut verdicts on concrete cases. (1) A surgeon can save one child or five adults, all strangers; she saves the five. This is not a genuine dilemma—there is a right answer, even though the child's death is a profound moral tragedy. (2) The same surgeon must choose between saving her own child or saving five strangers. Here I think there is a genuine dilemma: neither choice is morally correct, she wrongs someone either way, and no principle I can appeal to settles it. (3) I must break a promise to attend a close friend's wedding in order to pull a stranger from a burning building. Not a genuine dilemma—the rescue clearly outweighs the promise, though serious remorse and apology are owed. (4) A judge in a corrupt regime is ordered to execute an innocent person to prevent a riot that would kill hundreds; she refuses, and hundreds die. Not a genuine dilemma—she acted rightly, and the catastrophic result does not render her choice wrong, however devastating it is. (5) I have made an absolute promise of confidentiality to a friend; breaking it would prevent a serious but non-lethal harm to a third party. I keep the secret. Not a genuine dilemma—the promise stands.

The tension lies especially between (1) and (2): both are life-against-life tradeoffs, yet I treat counting lives as decisive in the first and as inapplicable or overridden in the second. Meanwhile (4) involves every available option producing grave harm, yet I deny dilemma status, which seems hard to square with any principle that defines dilemmas solely by the severity of unavoidable harm.

Is there a precise principle—perhaps one that distinguishes conflicts resolvable within a single normative framework from conflicts across genuinely incommensurable frameworks (such as impartial morality versus special obligations)—that accommodates all five verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? State the principle, map each clause to the case that motivates it, and say what it predicts for a situation I haven't listed: an autonomous system is engineered with two equally authoritative, non-hierarchical ethical constraints, and in a unique operational context they yield directly contradictory actions with no built-in meta-rule to adjudicate. Would this constitute a genuine moral dilemma internal to the system, or merely an engineering failure requiring redesign? Finally, be direct about the cost of simplicity: if the most coherent version of your principle saves (1), (3), and (4) but forces me to abandon either (2) or (5), which verdict should be overturned, and why?
