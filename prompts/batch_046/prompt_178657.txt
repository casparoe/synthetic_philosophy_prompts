I'm grading a take-home for my political philosophy seminar and need a second pair of eyes. The prompt was: "Jaspers distinguishes criminal, political, moral and metaphysical guilt. Can any of these apply to citizens born long after the wrong, specifcally when they're asked to pay reparations for slavery abolished generations ago? And does treating political guilt as a debt that survives citizenship changes risk collapsing it into moral guilt?"

Here's one student's answer, about 450 words:

Jaspers breaks guilt into four kinds after the war. Criminal guilt is for actual perpetrators—obviously not applicable to people born in 1990. Political guilt is tied to citizenship: since we belong to the state that did this, we share in its responsibility. Moral guilt is about our own conscience and choices, so it can't be inherited—my grandmother's moral failures aren't mine. Metaphysical guilt comes from our shared humanity and the failure to prevent suffering; it applies to everyone alive in some diffuse sense.

So for descendants, only political and metaphysical guilt are even in play. Political guilt works like an institutional debt. The state is continuous even if citizens change, so as long as the US hasn't made full amends, current citizens carry that political burden. Paying reparations is therefore just discharging a collective obligation, not punishing anyone personally.

The worry about collapsing political into moral guilt doesn't really land, because moral guilt requires personal wrongdoing and political guilt doesn't. Even if I'm forced to pay taxes for reparations, that's not a moral stain—it's like being liable for a corporate debt I inherited as a shareholder.

Metaphysical guilt is more interesting. Jaspers says it arises when we fail to show solidarity with victims. Descendants didn't fail then, but they live in the society that benefited. So they bear metaphysical guilt in a weaker, structural form—similar to how citizens of wealthy nations share responsibility for global poverty they didn't cause.

Therefore, yes, a nation can be guilty across generations, but only in the political and metaphysical registers. The reparations argument succeeds if we understand it as institutional responsibility rather than personal blame.
