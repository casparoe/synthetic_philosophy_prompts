Read George Orwell's 1984 through the lens of privacy as "ontological opacity" — the idea that privacy is not merely the concealment of information but a structural condition that keeps certain mental states from being rendered transparent to institutional interpretation and predictive control. Winston's relationship with Julia and his hidden diary are often read as acts of resistance through secrecy; read them instead as attempts to maintain this opacity. Take a definite position: does the novel ultimately treat ontological opacity as an inalienable feature of personhood that the Party violates, or does the final breaking of Winston show that such opacity is always a contingent social achievement that collapses under sustained surveillance? Be faithful to both the philosophy and the novel: identify where the framing illuminates a specific scene (the telescreen, the room above Mr. Charrington's shop, or the Ministry of Love) and where the novel resists it — for instance, where Orwell suggests that privacy was never a right but a historical luxury. What does the novel contribute to the philosophical debate about privacy that a purely conceptual argument misses?
