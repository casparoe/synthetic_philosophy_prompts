I've been playing with this idea about slurs — not the usual expressivism vs descriptivism thing, but a kind of 'projective presuppositon' view. The idea is that when you drop a slur into conversation, you're not just signaling contempt or loading in some hidden description; you're actively projecting onto the common ground that the target group is naturally subordinate, and that projection sticks unless every single person in the exchange explicitly rejects it. So even if I'm in a room where someone uses a slur and I immediately push back, the damage isn't just social — the discourse is contaminated unless the rejection is unanimous and explicit. I think this explains why slurs feel different from other insults in group settings, and why reclaiming them is so hard: you're not just flipping the valence, you're trying to dismantle a presuppositon that was structurally embedded.

But I want to know what actually counts against this. Not just 'people reclaim slurs successfully' — I want the hard cases. What linguistic data, what pragmatic experiments, what cross-cultural patterns, or what philosophical results about presuppositon failure should make me drop it? Which parts are just awkward adjustments (like adding conditions for partial rejection) and which are fatal? Also, be honest: has this been qualified into unfalsifiability? If every counterexample can be explained away by saying 'the rejection wasn't explicit enought,' is that a bug? And please — if the whole framing is confused, say so. I don't need polite compliance; tell me if I'm mixing up presuppositon mechanics with social dynamics in a way that makes the view incoherent.
