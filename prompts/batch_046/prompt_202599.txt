As a novelist currently drafting a long work about societies that have traded autonomy for engineered contentment, I have been returning to Huxley's Brave New World and to Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor episode in The Brothers Karamazov, not as opposing pamphlets but as two dramatizations of the same wager: that human beings, when left to choose, will surrender freedom for relief from suffering. I want a philosophical reading of the novel through the lens of the Inquisitor's claim that freedom is essentially a burden people will abandon in exchange for miracle, mystery, and authority. Focus especially on the confrontation between John the Savage and Mustapha Mond, but do not treat Huxley's text as merely illustrating Dostoevsky's theology.

Specifically, I am interested in whether the novel's narrative voice is genuinely divided. The Inquisitor argues that Christ overestimated humanity's capacity for liberty and that the masses crave the abolition of painful choice. Mond seems to confirm this by describing the social machinery that replaces tragic autonomy with stable happiness. Yet the novel clearly mourns John's anguish and the destruction of high art. Does this sympathy for suffering actually vindicate the Inquisitor's accusation that humanity demands too much of itself, or does Mond's calm administrative logic expose a hidden complicity in the reader's own desire for narrative resolution and moral clarity? I want a reading that asks what fiction adds to this debate that philosophical argument alone cannot—particularly whether representing suffering aesthetically ever functions as an argument for preserving it, or whether such representation inevitably becomes the spectacle of misery that justifies its administrative abolition.

Please respond in no more than 300 words, leading with your bottom-line judgment about whether Huxley's novel ultimately sides with the Inquisitor's diagnosis or resists it, and then developing the reading with reference to specific scenes; treat both the novel and Dostoevsky's argument seriously without reducing either to allegory.
