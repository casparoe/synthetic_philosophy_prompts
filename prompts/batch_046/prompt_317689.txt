I am a doctoral researcher in comparative sociology, and I sit on a small advisory panel for a ministry of education in a country with very strong gender-equality legislation, universal childcare, and extensive anti-discrimination enforcement. We have been reviewing longitudinal data on career entry over the last fifteen years, and we keep encountering the same pattern: as structural barriers have fallen and family policies have become more generous, the gender split between technical-engineering tracks and care-oriented or humanities tracks has actually widened, not narrowed. Meanwhile, in partner countries with weaker institutional support and more traditional family structures, the gender gap in these sectors has slightly compressed, largely because economic necessity pushes people into whatever work is available.

Within the panel, three interpretations dominate. One member, a behavioural economist, argues that once coercion and constraint are removed, revealed preferences align with innate dispositions, so the widening split is evidence of natural difference. Another member, a critical sociologist, insists that hidden cultural scripts—norms about suitable work that operate below the level of law—must have intensified to fill the vacuum left by formal equality, meaning culture is still the driver. A third member, a political philosopher, claims that justice requires us to treat any persistent statistical disparity as a failure of equality of opportunity, and therefore we should redesign guidance programmes and even consider targeted incentives to achieve representational parity.

I find each of these conclusions unsatisfactory, but I struggle to articulate why without simply asserting that the data are ambiguous. I need your help to think through the conceptual structure of the disagreement. You are an expert philosopher of social science with deep familiarity with theories of preference formation, adaptive expectations, and the normative interpretation of distributive patterns. Please think carefully, perhaps beginning with some brief conceptual brainstorming that maps the inferential moves each panel member makes, then identify the hidden philosophical presuppositions—particularly about the relationship between freedom, culture, and statistical outcomes—that allow them to jump from the observed pattern to their explanatory or normative conclusions. Then advise me, concretely: if I am drafting a minority memo for the panel, what distinctions should I insist on preserving so that the ministry does not confuse a descriptive trend with either a biological destiny, a hidden injustice, or an automatic mandate for redistribution? In particular, address how one should treat preference-endogeneity under expanded choice sets, and flag where the counterfactual baseline problem makes it impossible to read aggregate divergence as direct evidence of either autonomy or its absence.

Please write your response in British English, using clear sections, and avoid citing specific papers or offering a reading list; I need arguments I can evaluate and deploy without library access. Make sure the memo framework is something a civil servant without philosophical training could follow, but do not dilute the conceptual rigour.
