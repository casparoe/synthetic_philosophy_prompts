Two senior engineers on my team, Alex and Blake, disagree about how our technical review board should aggregate expert judgments on whether to release an AI feature. I am reporting their exchange as a bystander and want a point-by-point adjudication rather than general advice.

(1) Alex proposes "premise-based aggregation": each reviewer independently scores the feature on three binary criteria—safety, fairness, and interpretability. Release requires a majority positive on all three. He argues this forces transparent reasoning and prevents a reviewer from endorsing release without specifying which criteria justify it.

(2) Blake counters that this invites the discursive dilemma. She describes a concrete three-reviewer case: Reviewer A approves safety and fairness but rejects interpretability; Reviewer B approves safety and interpretability but rejects fairness; Reviewer C approves fairness and interpretability but rejects safety. Each criterion individually receives two approvals out of three, so premise-based aggregation approves all three and therefore release, yet no reviewer endorses the full package, and a direct majority vote on "release" might reject it. Thus the collective judgment depends arbitrarily on whether we aggregate premises or the conclusion.

(3) Alex responds that Blake's example relies on perfectly cyclic preferences, which is an edge case in practice. He proposes retaining premise-based voting but adding a tie-breaker: if the premise rule yields an outcome that conflicts with a direct vote on release, fall back to the direct vote.

(4) Blake says the tie-breaker concedes her central point—once direct voting on the conclusion can override the premises, the logical coherence that justified premise-based aggregation is abandoned. She then introduces a variant she calls "abstention-weighted aggregation": reviewers may abstain with a confidence weight between 0 and 1, and judgments are aggregated by linear expectation. In her variant, even direct voting on release can become cyclically unstable if weights are distributed asymmetrically, because linear expectation over logical propositions need not preserve majority consistency. She claims this shows the problem is not premises versus conclusions, but that any linear aggregation rule over interdependent judgments risks incoherence.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say which party is closer to being correct overall about how the board should aggregate judgments, and identify what—if anything—both are missing, particularly regarding Blake's abstention-weighted variant and whether it genuinely extends the paradox or introduces an irrelevant complication.
