I am drafting the closing passage of a novel set in a decaying mining town, where one character—formerly an archivist—has begun to understand her grief not as a private wound but as a rearrangement of relationships she still inhabits: among the living, the dead, and those not yet born. I want to close her chapter with a poem in the strict sestina form, thirty-nine lines total: six stanzas of six lines, followed by a three-line envoi that gathers all six end-words, two per line.

The end-words for the first stanza must be, in this order: person, other, ancestor, soil, debt, mirror. Their rotation through the subsequent stanzas should do philosophical work rather than serve as ornament. Specifically, each recurrence of "person" should occupy a shifting grammatical position, so that by the envoi the word is syntactically dependent on the remaining five—never standing as an autonomous subject—to enact the Ubuntu idea that personhood is constituted through relation rather than possessed in isolation. The poem should take seriously the claim that the dead and the unborn are among those who constitute the living self, should treat the soil not as mere setting but as an active relational term, and should leave open whether the mirror offers genuine recognition or a dangerous misrecognition. I want the tension unresolved: the archivist should sound as though she has not decided whether she is remembering an actual community or inventing one to survive it. The verse should be accurate enough that someone familiar with relational conceptions of personhood recognizes the argument being made in the structure itself, yet it should read as a poem rather than a thesis set to meter. No citations or scholarly apparatus are needed; let the form carry the idea.
