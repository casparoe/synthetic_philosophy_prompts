I'm writing a feature on how hospital governance in parts of East Asia encodes a philosophy of the person that differs from the individual-autonomy model dominant in Western bioethics. In several Chinese, South Korean, and Japanese institutions, informed-consent protocols for life-sustaining treatment are structured around family-unit authorization: a competent adult patient's dissent can be overridden by collective family decision, physicians are shielded from liability when they follow the family's directive, and some hospitals now mandate an internal family-harmony review by an ethics committee before any withdrawal of care. The practice is defended not as cultural preference but as a clinical and ethical standard. What theory of moral agency and value does this institutional architecture strictly presuppose? Must it assume a Confucian role-ethical view that the self is relationally constituted, or could a liberal theory of relational autonomy license the same rules? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments, separate what the practice genuinely requires from what it merely accommodates, and say precisely what in the consent forms, liability rules, and committee procedures would have to be redesigned if the underlying view were replaced by a strongly individualist account of patient rights.
