I'm preparing a seminar on moral revolutions and have become stuck on what I take to be the convergence between Appiah's honor-account—particularly his treatments of dueling and Atlantic slavery—and Bernard Williams's claim that thick ethical concepts function as deontic filters: they do not merely evaluate actions but determine which interests may be cited as reasons inside a social practice. If both views are correct, then the disappearance of dueling looks less like the triumph of a moral argument over honor and more like the collapse of a filter that once made lethal satisfaction intelligible as a reason-giving move. By the same token, should we say that chattel slavery ended not because people came to recognize its wrongness, but because the thick concept of honorable mastery underwent a semantic contraction that excluded slaveholding from the domain of reason-giving practices? My students keep pressing me on whether this reduces every moral revolution to a redistribution of honor-credit, and I am unsure how to answer without either conceding that interests drive all normative change or importing a normative premise that the framework itself seems to exclude. Could you help me identify the precise confusion—especially whether it is coherent to hold that thick concepts filter reasons without treating the abolition of slavery and dueling as mere linguistic recalibration?
