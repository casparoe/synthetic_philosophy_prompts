I'm preparing a discussion for an undergraduate philosophy of art course and want to test whether Tolstoy's criteria for genuine art can survive contact with a modern historical film without being reduced to a simple moral checklist. Please read Steven Spielberg's Schindler's List through the lens of Tolstoy's aesthetic theory—particularly his insistence that true art must be sincerely felt by its creator, must transmit that feeling directly to the audience in a kind of emotional infection, and must serve the moral purpose of promoting universal brotherhood, while false or calculated art relies on isolating devices to manipulate spectators into superficial responses. Focus specifically on the film's decision to shoot almost entirely in black and white except for the brief appearance of the girl in the red coat, and ask whether that visual symbol operates as Tolstoyan infection or as precisely the kind of emblematic, controlling gesture he associates with bad art. Then consider Schindler's own arc from profiteer to rescuer: does the narrative structure, which centers a single heroic figure rather than depicting anonymous collective suffering, genuinely advance Tolstoy's demand for art that dissolves barriers between people, or does it substitute aesthetic control and individual heroism for the communal feeling he requires? Where does the film illuminate Tolstoy's theory by dramatizing moral awakening, where does it resist the theory by substituting spectacle for sincerity, and what does this particular tension reveal about whether Tolstoy's standards are even applicable to commercial cinema? I want to avoid treating either the film or the philosophy as merely illustrative of the other.
