I'm taking a seminar on free will and moral responsability and we got into Dennett vs Caruso last week, and I stayed up too late sketching this argument that's now bugging me because the ending is clearly wrong but every step looks okay.

Here's the chain, with steps. (1) Caruso argues that without libertarian free will, retributive punishment is unjust — we should treat offenders like quarantine cases, isolating them for public safety, not because they deserve to suffer. (2) Dennett pushes back that compatabilist free will is enough for genuine just deserts: moral responsibility works like a game, and you can deserve a penalty inside the rules even if the rules are deterministic and there's no metaphysical deep freedom. (3) But if desert is just a functional feature of a rule-system, as Dennett seems to say, then there's no deep reason it has to attach to completed harmful actions rather than to risky internal states. (4) Meanwhile Caruso's quarantine logic cares about future dangerousness, not about past choices. (5) So put them together: if we're managing people like public-health risks, and if desert is just whatever assignment of rewards and penalties best stabilizes the system, then society should reward people who have dangerous impulses but suppress them — they're lowering risk — and punish people with safe dispositions who suddenly act out. (6) In fact, if brain scans could show agression potential, people with high-risk brains who never offend should get tax breaks or bonuses for performing the social labor of inhibition, while people with low-risk brains who commit one crime should face the full quarantine.

That feels totally backwards — it means just desert tracks risk profiles, not actions, and that we should pay potential criminals for staying good. So either Dennett's game analogy actually blocks this slide somehow (maybe because the game only assigns penalties to actual moves, not to dispositions?), or Caruso's quarantine model isn't compatible with any talk of desert at all, and mixing them creates a monster. Where does the argument actually break? I'd rather hang onto Dennett's claim that compatabilist desert is real, but I need to know what I'm sacrificing if I reject step five or six.
