I'm developing a framework for the ethics of dramatizing real deceased individuals in historical fiction and biopics, and I want to stress-test it with an engineered case rather than another analysis of royal chronicles or recent musician biographies. I'm particularly interested in obligations that persist after death when the dramatization commits no factual error but instead produces what I'll call affective misalignment: attributing to the subject an emotional or moral stance that is consistent with the documented record yet arranged in the narrative to generate a meaning the subject never endorsed. Please construct a realistic thought experiment—set in an ordinary workplace or family context, not a court or battlefield—involving a dramatic adaptation of a real, non-public figure who died within the last century and left private written materials. The scenario must be designed so that two positions reach opposite verdicts specifically on this feature. Position A, a posthumous-autonomy view, holds that the dead retain a residual interest in the coherence of their life-narrative and therefore condemns the adaptation because the affective misalignment fractures that coherence, even though every individual claim is documentable. Position B, a cultural-inheritance view, holds that once private materials enter the archive the living possess full narrative sovereignty, so the adaptation is permissible precisely because no factual falsehood occurs and the emotional framing serves a legitimate public theme; on this view, any remaining "harm" is purely symbolic and therefore dismissible. The case should be one paragraph, should avoid perfect information, hypothetical technologies, or exotic legal doctrines, and should clearly specify both the exact documented behavior and the dramatic rearrangement that creates the misalignment.

After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it satisfies these specifications: state the verdict each theory delivers and the reasoning behind it; isolate the structural feature doing the work—the gap between the chronological and contextual sequence in the documents versus the sequence imposed by the dramatic arc; explain what happens if that feature is removed, specifically whether the dispute then collapses into an ordinary defamation or privacy claim; and show how a defender of the cultural-inheritance view would attack the autonomy view's appeal to narrative coherence as a metaphysically suspicious projection onto a subject who no longer exists. The scenario should not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and it should be fully evaluable by someone with standard philosophical training and no specialized archival knowledge.
