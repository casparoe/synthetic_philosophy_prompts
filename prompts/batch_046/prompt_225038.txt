I am a novelist who often constructs protagonists who maintain a moral or aesthetic conviction even after acquiring what they take to be higher-order evidence that the conviction arises from contingent psychological conditioning—imagine a character who continues to judge a particular ambiguous ending as ethically necessary despite recognizing that her judgment tracks her own unresolved grief rather than any feature of the narrative structure. From sustained reflection on such cases, I have come to hold what I call the irreducible first-person valuation thesis: that epistemic akrasia—believing P while believing one's total evidence, higher-order included, does not fully support P—is not a rational failure but a structurally appropriate response whenever P concerns values inseparable from the subject's lived perspective, such as the moral weight of a particular suffering or the aesthetic integrity of an unresolved plot. What would genuinely count against this view? I want the potential defeaters mapped and ranked from fatal to merely awkward, covering at least: formal results in epistemology that require higher-order evidence to always defeat first-order judgment; empirical findings about expert disagreement that undermine claims of privileged phenomenological access; conceptual arguments that dissolve the boundary between cognitive bias and legitimate first-person valuation; and narrative or phenomenological cases in which maintaining belief despite recognized bias produces not integrity but self-deception. For each, indicate whether defenders of the thesis could concede it and survive through qualification, or whether it forces abandonment of the core claim. Be honest about whether the view risks unfalsifiability—if the scope of 'irreducible first-person valuation' can be adjusted post hoc to exclude any counterexample, is that insulation a vice for a thesis of this kind, or does its purpose, analogous to a novelist's commitment to a character's inner coherence, justify such protection?
