I'm a journalist who covers philosophy and ideas, and I'm trying to work through the Austrian philosopher Moritz Schlick's argument that asking for "the meaning of life" usually smuggles in a false premise: that meaning must come from purpose, from some result outside the activity itself. Schlick proposes that meaning actually lives in what he calls "play"—not recreation in the casual sense, but any activity carried out with an attitude of present absorption and freedom from instrumental goals. He contrasts this with "work," which is governed by a purpose-attitude aimed at a future payoff. I'm testing whether my own gut judgments about concrete cases line up with that distinction, or whether they force a revision.

Here are my verdicts. A marathon runner who trains for six months with a stopwatch, tracking splits to qualify for Boston, then misses the race through injury: the whole enterprise feels hollow, as if the time were wasted. The same runner jogging through fog at dawn with no watch, no goal, and no plan to stop: that feels like it genuinely matters, even though nothing comes of it. A surgeon performing a technically perfect but routine operation to save a life: meaningful. The same surgeon spending an hour refining a single suture motion that does not improve the patient's outcome, purely because the gesture itself feels right: meaningful too, but in a way that makes me uneasy. A historian writing a definitive study of an obscure fourteenth-century trade route that perhaps twelve specialists will ever read: meaningful. The same historian writing the identical study only to complete a contractual trilogy for tenure: hollow. A parent waking at 3 a.m. to comfort a sick child: meaningful. The same parent doing it with a conscious mental checklist—"I must maintain family harmony and model responsibility"—feels less meaningful, though the act is identical. A stonemason carving a hidden chair-back ornament that no buyer will see, just to see whether he can do it: meaningful. The same mason carving the identical ornament because the contract requires it and the fee is good: not meaningful.

Yet one pair resists reconciliation. A scientist working for thirty years on a cosmological theory she knows she will not live to see verified feels, to my intuition, deeply meaningful—yet it is entirely oriented toward a future result she will never possess. Meanwhile, the marathoner training for a race she will miss feels hollow, though it is equally future-oriented. I keep going back and forth on a further case: a teacher who spends an entire semester preparing a student for an exam, not because she enjoys the teaching in the moment, but because she believes the exam result will change the student's life trajectory. Sometimes that strikes me as profoundly meaningful; sometimes it strikes me as pure instrumentality, no different from the tenure-driven historian.

Is there a precise principle—or a small family of competing candidates—that captures the pattern? For each principle, tie every clause to the specific case that motivates it, state its verdict on the cases I haven't settled (the teacher; also a musician practicing scales for a concert she will play; a monk meditating in order to reach enlightenment versus meditating because the sitting itself is absorbing; a volunteer building houses for a distant community she will never visit), and say honestly which of my original verdicts it must overturn—especially the scientist and the marathoner. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Lead with the bottom-line principle in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
