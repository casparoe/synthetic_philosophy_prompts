hey, i'm a phd student in political sociology and i've been fiddling with this take on Hobsbawm and Anderson: sure, nations are mostly modern inventions, but i think the imagined-community framing overstates imagination and understates the filing cabinet. my position is that national identity sticks mainly because state bureacratic slots — census boxes, conscription lists, tax categories — force people to inhabit them. the imagination is real, but it's scafolded by paper trails; pull the scaffold and the nation dissolves faster than pure culturalist models predict. so it's not just invented tradition, it's administratively locked-in tradition.

what would actually count against this? i want the arguments and evidence sorted by how decisive they'd be — knockdowns versus merely awkward fits. are there cases where strong national feeling existed with almost no bureaucratic infrastucture? or heavy bureaucracy that failed to produce any national feeling at all? what does my view predict that a pure Anderson-style imagination story doesn't — maybe that nation-building always lags behind census infrastucture? and have i already qualified myself into unfalsifiability by saying the bureaucracy was just hidden? if so, is that a problem for this kind of claim? also, what would defenders of the pure imagined-community view themselves concede as damaging to my scaffold argument, not just what critics say? be honest about whether this position risks anything real or if it's been insulated.
