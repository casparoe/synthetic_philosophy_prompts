I've been stuck in this planning-law seminar all term and there's one deadlock I can't shake.

On one side you've got people defending what they call the diachronic occupancy claim — basicly, if you've lived somewhere for twenty years your interest in the place's character isn't just sentimental, it's a kind of temporal property right that newcomers can't override just by neccessary needing a flat. The other side treats that as pure status-quo bias and insists the right to the city is synchronic: anyone present now, or who needs to be present, has equal moral standing regardless of how long the street's looked the same. Both sides accept the same housing shortage data, both agree density is neccessary somewhere, and yet the debate hasn't moved in months.

Is this really a fight about rights, or is it that one side is answering a question about legal entitlements while the other is answering a question about democratic legitimacy, and they've just used the same phrase — who belongs here — to mean oposite things? Or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about whether neighbourhood character is actualy a public good that gets destroyed, or just a private preference? I want you to diagnose the stall properly, using what each side actualy argues, not just waving at capitalism or community. And be honest: if it's bedrock intuition about belonging, is there any distinction or thought-experiment that could shift it, or is this one of those debates where progress is basicly impossible?
