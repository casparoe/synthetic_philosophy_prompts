You are a distinguished professor of moral philosophy who has been asked to deliver the five-minute commencement address at your university's philosophy department graduation ceremony. The audience is deliberately mixed: on one side sit professional philosophers, ethicists, and graduate students who will catch subtle distinctions in the philosophy of responsibility; on the other sit family members, university administrators, and friends who need the speech to feel warm, accessible, and genuinely celebratory rather than like a seminar presentation. Your task is to craft a speech that takes moral luck—specifically the tension between constitutive luck (innate cognitive talents, family support, early education) and circumstantial luck (historical era, access to archives, the particular mentors available)—as its genuine structural backbone, not mere decoration. Because the theme is moral luck, there are real constraints on what you can sincerely say: you should avoid suggesting that any graduate has purely earned their philosophical achievements through unaided individual will, yet you must also avoid draining the occasion of its legitimate joy and pride.

To satisfy the competing demands of rigor and accessibility, please begin by briefly brainstorming how moral luck applies specifically to philosophical training—consider, for instance, whether developing a novel argument in a graduate seminar is more like planting a tree or like being the particular tree that happens to grow where soil, rain, and sunlight converge. Then, build the speech around a single accessible analogy or brief hypothetical scenario that both halves of the room can grasp. Crucially, inject the following somewhat unconventional framing into the heart of your remarks: treat the graduating class not as a collection of isolated desert-bearers but as nodes within a distributed epistemic network, and ask whether moral desert attaches primarily to individual nodes or to the relational structure that made their thinking possible. Use this framing to navigate the emotional arc of the address—opening with genuine affection, moving through the philosophical tension, and closing with a toast that honors the graduates without falsely attributing their success solely to personal merit.

Keep the tone polished, affectionate, and intellectually serious; do not include citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts that the audience could not be expected to know. Make sure the philosophical idea actively shapes what can and cannot be said—so that removing the discussion of moral luck and distributed desert would genuinely break the speech—rather than serving as ornamental vocabulary layered over a generic congratulatory message.
