I am presently in the late stages of revising a literary novel structured around three interwoven timelines that lack explicit chronological markers, and over the past fourteen months I have developed an idiosyncratic method for deciding when a draft is complete. After finishing each version, I set the manuscript aside for exactly three weeks, then read the entire text aloud in one sitting, noting only those moments where my attention fractures rather than deepens; by this internal measure, the work has achieved stability across two successive cycles, yet my literary agent urges me to continue refining, proposing that I benchmark each chapter against the structural rhythms of recent prize-winning fiction and iterate until the variance in sentence length, emotional cadence, and thematic density falls within an optimal statistical range. A fellow writer with whom I discussed this over dinner argued that my reluctance is precisely the failure mode of an artist who confuses comfort with quality, insisting that true mastery demands relentless optimization regardless of subjective fatigue.

What gives me pause is not mere exhaustion but a specific pattern I have observed across my previous drafts: after approximately the seventh or eighth round of revision, individual sentences grow more polished and locally coherent, yet the narrative as a whole begins to suffer from an accumulated micro-inconsistency, as though each precise adjustment introduces an almost imperceptible shift in tonal gravity that eventually destabilizes the voice I originally sought to preserve. Compounding this is the sense that the evaluation framework itself mutates with every comparison; when I measure a passage against an external benchmark, my internal criterion for what the book should accomplish subtly reconfigures, so that I am no longer assessing the manuscript on its own terms but chasing a moving target that may have no fixed endpoint. I find myself wondering whether there is a principled, rational basis for adopting a stopping rule that accepts a sufficiently coherent result rather than pursuing an ever-receding ideal of perfection, especially when the very process of optimization appears to alter both the object being judged and the standards by which it is judged.

Could you help me understand whether my intuition reflects a genuine philosophical insight about decision-making under constraint, or whether I am merely dressing up an unwillingness to undertake the harder labor of exhaustive refinement? I am not looking for generic encouragement or a list of authorities on the subject, but rather for an analysis of whether a rational agent in my position—possessing limited cognitive endurance, facing shifting evaluative criteria, and working on an object whose global integrity is fragile—ought to prefer a satisfactory stopping point over an unending search for an optimum that may not exist.
