I have been thinking about split-brain cases and whether they genuinely fracture the unity of consciousness, and I find my own judgements pulling in inconsistent directions. In the classic experiments with patients who have undergone complete corpus callosotomy, where an image is shown to the right visual field and the left hand selects a matching object while the speaking left hemisphere denies any awareness of the image and invents an explanation, I am inclined to say that there are genuinely two streams of conscious experience, not merely one mind with divided access to information. Yet in alien hand syndrome, where one hand acts against the patient’s explicit intentions and is described as having a will of its own, I feel quite differently: despite the rhetoric of autonomy, I take this to be a single consciousness suffering a local breakdown in agency, not the presence of a second subject. Then there is the puzzling case of a fully disconnected patient whose left and right hemispheres, when presented separately with a moral dilemma, each favour a different course of action, yet the patient reports feeling only one settled preference with no sense of internal conflict; here I am unsure whether the left hemisphere’s narrative simply dominates and suppresses the other, or whether some deeper integration persists. This uncertainty is reinforced by the gradual fibre-sectioning thought experiment: if the callosal connections are severed one by one, I do not imagine consciousness snapping in two at a precise threshold, but rather fragmenting gradually, with intermediate stages of partial unity that resist a binary classification. But this sits uneasily with another observation I have encountered, in which split-brain patients with apparently complete disconnection nevertheless show smooth bimanual cooperation on complex tasks that seem to require information exchange, with neither hand complaining of interference and both contributing seamlessly. I feel that this cooperation points towards preserved unity via subcortical pathways, yet it conflicts with my willingness to see the classic divided-field case as genuinely dual. So I am left with a cluster of verdicts that do not obviously cohere: full disconnection can yield two streams, yet also seamless cooperation; alien hand involves autonomy without duality; narrative dominance may mask or may constitute unity; and fragmentation seems gradual rather than abrupt.
