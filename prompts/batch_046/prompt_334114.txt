You are a senior philosopher of aesthetics and an experienced grant writer for humanities fellowships. Before drafting, spend a few sentences thinking carefully about what makes a conceptual research proposal compelling: the question must be precisely stated, genuinely open given current interpretations, and tractable within the scope; the method must be philosophical rather than archival or empirical; and the failure conditions must be explicit. Then write the proposal.

I need a formal, polished two-page research proposal for a six-month fellowship project on W. E. B. Du Bois’s philosophy of art, specifically his defense of art as propaganda. Here is the half-formed idea to develop. Most interpreters treat Du Bois’s notion of propaganda as a single, undifferentiated function—art serving a political cause by persuading or mobilizing an audience. The proposal should ask whether this monolithic reading obscures a deeper bifurcation. Specifically, inject and work with the following distinction: on one side, “testimony-propaganda,” in which art’s primary political work is to render visible the interior life, suffering, and dignity of an oppressed group—a kind of collective self-assertion that does not necessarily target an external audience; on the other side, “strategic-propaganda,” in which art is deliberately engineered as rhetorical design to alter the beliefs, emotions, or moral dispositions of a hostile, indifferent, or powerful outside audience. The core question: does recognizing this bifurcation resolve the standard objection that Du Bois reduces aesthetic value to mere instrumental political value? And can the distinction be sustained conceptually, or does it collapse upon inspection?

Structure the proposal with these sections:

1. A precise statement of the research question, framed so that current readings of Du Bois genuinely leave it unsettled.

2. A brief diagnosis of why existing answers do not settle it: explain how the standard interpretation treats propaganda as monolithic, and identify the conceptual cost of that simplification—such as whether it forces Du Bois into an implausible denial of aesthetic autonomy.

3. The proposed approach: a conceptual reconstruction and analysis of Du Bois’s arguments, comparing them with contemporary philosophical discussions of testimony, rhetorical design, and aesthetic autonomy—without relying on a bibliography, citation list, or external literature review. Explain why this philosophical approach is preferable to an archival historical study or an empirical audience-reception study for this specific question.

4. A realistic month-by-month timeline for six months, specifying the conceptual work in each phase (for example, reconstruction of the bifurcation, testing it against standard objections, integrating the interaction between the two modes, drafting the framework).

5. A clear account of success and failure: what a successful result looks like (for instance, a defensible conceptual framework distinguishing the two modes and showing how they interact without collapsing) and what counts as failure (for instance, Du Bois’s texts consistently resist the bifurcation, or the distinction proves conceptually unstable).

6. A brief statement of the deliverable—perhaps a conceptual map or position paper—and why it matters for scholars working in African American aesthetics, philosophy of art, or theories of political rhetoric.

Constraints: Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or requests for citations. Do not ask for web access or external sources. Do not quote any specific passage in quotation marks; paraphrase Du Bois’s positions without verbatim quotation. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate for a fellowship committee, and keep the scope realistic for a half-year conceptual project. Start with the brief brainstorm on the distinction, then draft the proposal.
