I write about conceptual issues at the intersection of philosophy and emerging technology for a science and ideas publication, and I am currently helping a university colleague design an assessment for an upper-level course on ancient economic thought. We want to avoid questions that reward mere summary of Aristotle's familiar distinction between oikonomia and chrematistike, or his observation that money is a conventional measure of reciprocal need (chreia) that is itself barren and therefore should not breed more of itself through tokos. To push students into genuinely analytical territory, I want the exam to present a constructed but precise scenario: a synthetic unit of account whose supply expands algorithmically in direct proportion to the volume of exchange it mediates, and which yields to its holders a return drawn exclusively from facilitation fees rather than from productive labor or risk-bearing investment. The essay prompt should ask candidates to determine whether Aristotle's critique of interest applies to this mechanism, and to do so they must disentangle three possible grounds of condemnation — that surplus is generated from barren matter, that use-value is confused with exchange-value, or that proportional justice in exchange is violated — and apply them rigorously to the algorithmic case.

Please draft the exam question (aimed at a 90-minute undergraduate essay, approximately 1,200 words), a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme organized around five must-hit analytical moves, assigning specific marks to each for a total of 25. For each move, include a brief one-line note identifying the typical student error — for instance, conflating the conventional nature of money with a license for unlimited generation, or treating algorithmic automation as if it removes the moral structure of exchange altogether.
