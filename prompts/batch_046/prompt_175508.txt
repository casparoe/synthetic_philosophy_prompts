I've been running a small reading group on scientific discovery and we keep circling back to Newton/Leibniz and Darwin/Wallace. The inevitabilists say if not Newton then Hooke or Barrow, the math was ripe; the contingency folks say no, the specific formulation and the years of private work matter, and without Darwin the theory might have sat unread for decades. But we've stalled for months. Everyone agrees on the basic history — Wallace's Ternate essay, Newton's fluxions versus Leibniz's differentials, all that — and yet nobody budges.

My hunch is that we're not actually disagreeing about history but about what "inevitable" is supposed to do: is it a causal claim about counterfactual worlds, an epistemic claim about whether the knowledge was bound to surface, or a normative claim about whether credit should be distributed? And then there's this weird seperate layer: even if calculus was structurally inevitable, doesn't the fact that Leibniz's notation won out and shaped physics for centuries show that who did it still matters? So maybe the stall is that one side is talking about discovery-events and the other about discovery-legacy, and neither side admits it.

Can you diagnose what's going on here? Is this a hidden empirical disagreement about historical counterfactuals we just can't resolve, a verbal mess around "inevitable" and "matters", or are the two camps answering different questions — one about the sociology of knowledge, one about moral desert? Give me the diagnosis with reasons, drawing on what each side actually says (not just labels). And be honest: is there anything — a conceptual distinction, a formal model of scientific development, a change in what we ask — that could actualy move the needle? Or should we just accept this is bedrock intuition about genius versus structure?
