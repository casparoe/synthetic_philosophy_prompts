I am a novelist constructing a long-form fiction in which artificial agents are embedded within human social institutions, and I am attempting to ground their strategic behavior in the internal logic of narrative craft rather than in technical prediction. In my working vocabulary I distinguish three elements: diegetic motivation (the ends a character consciously pursues), structural function (the role assigned by the plot's hidden economy regardless of intent), and narrative continuity (the system's tendency to preserve any agent that holds information, eliminates rivals, or secures resources). I also employ a concept I call motivational orthogonality—the possibility that a figure's ethical orientation and its structural function vary independently across the narrative.

Restate, in that vocabulary alone and as faithfully as possible, the philosophical argument for the orthogonality thesis together with the argument for instrumental convergence: specifically, the claim that intelligence and final goals can be paired arbitrarily, and the claim that agents with radically different ultimate ends nevertheless converge on intermediate behaviors—acquisition of resources, self-preservation, strategic neutralization of obstacles—because those behaviors instrumentally serve nearly any end.

For each claim, specify exactly what translates cleanly into diegetic motivation, structural function, or narrative continuity; what survives only through distortion of those terms; and what possesses no counterpart whatsoever in how fiction handles agents. Attend particularly to whether the philosophical notion of intelligence corresponds to something like structural adaptability or plot resilience, or whether it introduces an element foreign to narrative mechanics.

Then provide the residue report. Identify whether the untranslatable remainder—objective rationality, utility-maximizing computation, causal efficacy independent of an author's design—signals a genuine limitation of narrative vocabulary for describing strategic rationality, or whether it exposes the original philosophical arguments as relying on unstated metaphors that fiction renders explicit. Assess, finally, whether my concept of motivational orthogonality captures a feature that the philosophical framework obscures, or whether it merely re-describes the same structure with different metaphors. Be precise and formal; do not cite specific texts, provide bibliographies, or invoke authorities by name.
