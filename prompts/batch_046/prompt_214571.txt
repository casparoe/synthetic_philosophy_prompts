I'm working through the wrong kind of reasons problem for a seminar on moral psychology, and I'm particularly stuck on a tension that standard treatments tend to glide over. The classic cases involve agents who do the right thing for selfish or prudential motives — returning a wallet to avoid legal trouble, for instance. But I'm interested in a structurally more complex variant: someone who performs a morally required action because they treat a moral authority's testimony as an exclusionary reason that preempts their own first-order moral reasoning. On one view, this remains paradigmatically the wrong kind of reason, since the agent hasn't grasped the moral feature that makes the action right; on another view, deferring to moral testimony can be rationally appropriate and even virtuous, provided the agent maintains a second-order disposition to reject that testimony should it conflict with considerations she can directly recognize as weighty. The dispute seems to turn on whether the wrong-kind critique targets the causal origin of motivation, the logical form of the reason within deliberation, or the agent's higher-order attitudes toward her own reasoning.
