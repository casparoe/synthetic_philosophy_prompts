In a recent philosophy reading group on secular morality, a participant invoked George Eliot's well-known closing reflection in Middlemarch — that the growing good of the world depends in part on unhistoric acts, on lives lived faithfully but hidden and unvisited — and used it to defend the view that private moral virtue requires neither public recognition nor institutional scaffolding in order to count. I am skeptical that the line actually carries that weight, but I also do not want to dismiss it as mere sentiment. Can you disambiguate the slogan by laying out the precise, distinct theses it could be expressing? For each reading, specify whether it is about (a) the causal mechanics of moral action (whether hidden acts genuinely aggregate into historical good), (b) the epistemology of virtue (whether moral worth depends on being observed), (c) a secularized version of providence (whether history possesses an immanent moral direction independent of intentional design), or (d) the ontology of agency (whether individuals are genuine causal nodes or merely carriers of collective processes). Indicate which readings are platitudinous, which are substantive and defensible in Eliot's actual religious and philosophical framework, which are false, and — crucially — which one the reading-group argument needed to go through. Please answer in American English, and do not provide bibliographies or reading lists.
