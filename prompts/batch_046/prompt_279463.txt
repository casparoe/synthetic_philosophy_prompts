The debate between accuracy-first epistemologists and pluralists about epistemic utility has persisted for decades without visible resolution. The accuracy-first side holds that the only fundamental epistemic good is the accuracy of belief—typically formalized through proper scoring rules such as the Brier score—and treats values like explanatory understanding or cognitive tractability as instrumental byproducts of well-calibrated belief-forming processes. The pluralist side accepts that accuracy matters but insists it can be legitimately traded for genuinely epistemic goods: for instance, scientists often prefer a simpler explanatory model with slightly worse predictive fit over an intractable, maximally accurate black-box predictor, and they treat that preference as epistemically justified, not merely pragmatically convenient. Despite widespread agreement on the formal properties of scoring rules and on the descriptive psychology of explanation-seeking, neither camp has moved. Diagnose the structure of this stalemate. Is it a verbal disagreement about the scope of the term 'epistemic' versus 'pragmatic'? Are the two sides actually addressing different questions—perhaps one evaluating individual credences in isolation, the other evaluating the architecture of collective scientific inquiry? Or is there a hidden formal issue, such as whether any non-accuracy epistemic value can be incorporated into a generalized scoring framework without collapsing back into a measure of accuracy? In your answer, use the concrete case of model-choice between predictive accuracy and explanatory simplicity to illustrate what each side actually says, specify whether the disagreement is substantive, verbal, or a case of two questions running under one name, and say exactly what would count as progress—whether a formal representation result, a conceptual distinction, or an argument that the dispute is ill-posed.
