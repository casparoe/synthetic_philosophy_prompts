I've been thinking about the way suffering risks—scenarios in which the future contains astronomical suffering rather than outright extinction—are framed in parts of the longtermist literature, particularly the claim that such risks should dominate existential-risk prioritization because the expected disvalue of unmitigated suffering could vastly exceed that of human extinction. Rather than challenging the empirical likelihood of these scenarios, I want to press a conceptual tension in the argument's structure. The reasoning behind s-risk urgency depends on our ability to aggregate and compare suffering across possible worlds, yet if suffering reaches a scale that completely outstrips any remaining moral agency, compensatory goods, or evaluative perspective—if it eliminates the very conditions under which moral comparison remains intelligible—then the framework used to establish the urgency collapses precisely at the point where the risk is greatest. This is not merely an epistemic worry about measurement difficulty; it is the claim that there is a conceptual ceiling beyond which expected-value comparison becomes self-undermining, because the object of evaluation destroys the standpoint required to evaluate it.

So the position I am considering is that extreme-scale s-risk scenarios should not be treated as the strongest case for mitigation via comparative disvalue, but rather as falling outside the domain of coherent expected-value reasoning altogether; defenders of s-risk prioritization ought to concede that once suffering reaches this threshold, the argument shifts from "this world is worse" to "this world is no longer comparable within the same evaluative framework," which weakens rather than strengthens the practical urgency relative to existential risks that preserve the conditions for evaluation. What would count against this? I want an honest map of its exposure: is there a principled account—perhaps in formal value theory or in the philosophy of comparison—that allows evaluation to survive the elimination of all counterbalancing goods and moral agents? Which objections are fatal, which merely awkward, and which would a committed s-risk advocate themselves concede? In particular, does the position risk conflating an axiological boundary with an epistemic limit, and is there a live conceptual result that either supports or undermines the idea that moral comparison has such a ceiling?
