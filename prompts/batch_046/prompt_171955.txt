Below is a short essay sketch on whether moral expressivism can survive the Frege-Geach problem through a structured-attitude analysis. I would like comments focused on philosophical coherence rather than on scholarship or sources; please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.

Essay sketch: Standard expressivism treats moral sentences as expressions of non-cognitive attitudes—approval or disapproval—rather than as belief-like states with truth-conditions. The Frege-Geach problem arises because moral predicates embed in logical complexes: in a conditional such as "If lying is wrong, then inducing one's brother to lie is wrong," the predicate "lying is wrong" appears in an antecedent that is not itself asserted, yet it seems to contribute a stable semantic content. If the unembedded sentence expresses disapproval, it is unclear what it expresses inside the conditional, since no attitude is being expressed there.

The response I want to explore treats moral predicates not as expressing simple first-order attitudes, but as expressing second-order structured dispositions: a disposition to approve or disapprove of actions that fall under a cognitively grasped concept. In embedded contexts, the sentence expresses not the first-order attitude itself, but a structural relation between that disposition and other doxastic commitments. Thus the conditional above expresses a hypothetical framing—were one's disapproval-disposition toward lying activated, one would extend it to the act of inducing it. This aims to preserve semantic uniformity, since the predicate always concerns the same structured disposition, while allowing expressive force to vary with logical position.

My worries are twofold. First, if the antecedent must possess a stable propositional content capable of serving as the object of a hypothetical framing, then it behaves like a belief-content, which may collapse the view into cognitivism. Second, if moral disagreement is reconstructed as conflict over the structure of these dispositions rather than over beliefs, it is unclear whether the view retains the core expressivist claim that moral dispute is fundamentally non-cognitive.

Please comment on the following: (1) whether this framing of the Frege-Geach problem is accurate; (2) whether the structured-disposition move genuinely resolves the embedding difficulty or merely relocates it; (3) whether the view avoids collapsing into cognitivism or a hybrid theory; and (4) whether there is an internal tension between treating the concept as cognitively grasped and treating the overall attitude as non-cognitive.
