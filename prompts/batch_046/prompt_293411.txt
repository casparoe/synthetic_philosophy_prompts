Question: Dialetheism is the metaphysical thesis that some contradictions are genuinely true; paraconsistent logic is a family of formal systems in which the principle of explosion—ex contradictione quodlibet—fails. Focusing specifically on Graham Priest's Logic of Paradox (LP), address two issues. First, explain the technical mechanism by which LP blocks explosion while retaining a modified form of classical negation, and clarify how its three-valued semantics (true, false, both) differs from a simple rejection of the law of non-contradiction. Second, evaluate whether adopting LP as one's preferred logic necessarily commits one to dialetheism regarding the liar paradox ("This sentence is false"). In particular, consider whether a stable intermediate position—such as epistemicism about semantic vagueness or a purely instrumentalist reading of formal models—allows one to employ LP without accepting that reality itself contains true contradictions.

Student answer:
Dialetheism says that some contradictions are literally true in the world, while paraconsistent logic is only a formal tool that stops contradictions from collapsing your entire system. In LP, propositions can take the value "both true and false," which is called a glut, and the logic is arranged so that from A and not-A you still cannot derive arbitrary B; this is exactly how explosion is blocked. LP also keeps classical negation in a restricted sense: if a statement is false then it is not true, except in the glut cases where both values apply together, so outside those exceptions negation behaves classically. As for commitment, using LP does not automatically make you a dialetheist. You could be an epistemicist about the liar paradox—holding that "This sentence is false" is determinately either true or false, but that we are permanently ignorant of which—while treating LP as a convenient formal framework that tolerates temporary inconsistency during reasoning. In fact, Graham Priest himself argues that paraconsistent logic is mainly useful for philosophers who reject dialetheism, because if you already believe that contradictions are true in reality then you have no need for a special logic; you can just remain in classical logic and accept the contradiction directly. Applying this to the liar, LP assigns the sentence the glut value "both true and false," but that should be read as a formal representation rather than proof that the world itself is contradictory. So LP and dialetheism are largely independent, and in Priest's own view they actually stand in tension with each other rather than supporting one another.
