I am not a trained philosopher, but I have been reflecting on my own about the special composition question—specifically, on what conditions must hold for separate parts genuinely to form one thing rather than merely to lie near one another. I have arrived at a proposed criterion that I have not seen discussed in the usual debates, which I shall call the reciprocal identity condition. According to this view, parts compose a unified whole if and only if the qualitative identity of each part is modally dependent on the presence of every other part in the arrangement, such that removing any single part would not merely damage the whole but would alter what it is for each remaining part to be the particular thing it is. On this account, a living organism might satisfy the condition because the metabolic and structural role of each component depends on the presence of the others, whereas a scattered constellation of satellites or a heap of sand would fail it, since each grain or satellite retains its own qualitative identity regardless of the others. I would like you to argue in favour of using this reciprocal identity condition as the correct resolution to the special composition question. In doing so, please address why it should be preferred both to unrestricted universalism, which seems to allow arbitrary sums, and to nihilism, which denies that even organisms are genuine wholes. Please also discuss whether the condition generates unacceptable vagueness at the boundaries—for instance, in cases of gradual biological death or in distributed technological networks where interdependence is partial rather than total. Where your reasoning touches on matters that are broadly settled in philosophy, please distinguish those clearly from points that remain genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed claim as if it were the consensus view. Please write your response in British English, and keep the tone formal and precise.
