As a retired physical chemist who has recently begun studying metaphysics, I keep noticing laboratory configurations that seem to fall between the two accounts of persistence I have encountered. In a chemostat culture maintained at steady state for years, every original cell and molecule is eventually replaced, yet researchers treat it as one continuous biological system. In a tokamak plasma discharge, individual ions remain confined only for milliseconds before being replaced, yet physicists refer to a single sustained entity with a specific magnetic topology. In both cases there is no material substrate that endures the full interval; but the successive states are not arbitrary temporal slices either, since replacements occur only within a continuous organizational and dynamical constraint. The philosophical views I have read divide persistence into either an object being wholly present at each moment with its parts, or being nothing more than a sequence of instantaneous stages spread through time. Is there an established term or recognized intermediate position for cases where continuity is carried neither by shared matter nor by mere spatiotemporal adjacency, but by the persistence of a causal-functional form through complete material turnover? If not, how should such steady-state entities be classified relative to the two standard views, and what precisely distinguishes them from each?
