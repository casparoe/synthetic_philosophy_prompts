Two colleagues of mine, Maya and Julian, got into an argument after a talk on moral consideration of invertebrates. I was there and could not settle it, and I would like you to adjudicate their exchange move by move. They were debating whether the scale argument for insect welfare—multiplying vast population numbers by a low per-individual probability of sentience—actually establishes a large expected moral burden, or whether it rests on a conceptual mistake about how uncertainty aggregates across groups.

Julian opened by saying that even if we assign only a one-in-a-thousand chance that any given insect is sentient, the estimated quintillions of insects alive at any time make the total expected number of sentient subjects enormous, and therefore the expected suffering is enormous too. He presented this as ordinary expected-value reasoning, no different in structure from buying insurance against a low-probability disaster, except that here the disaster is widespread hidden suffering that we might be causing.

Maya replied that this treats moral patienthood like a lottery ticket: it assumes sentience is distributed independently across individuals, so that probabilities can be summed across the population. But in reality, sentience likely clusters by neural architecture. Either the basic insect nervous system supports subjective experience, in which case nearly all insects are candidates, or it does not, in which case almost none are. The framing of a tiny prior applied uniformly to each insect is epistemically misleading; our uncertainty is mainly about the kind, not about each individual being an independent coin flip. Multiplying a minute prior by a huge population therefore commits a fallacy of division, treating a collective uncertainty as if it were composed of separate personal uncertainties.

Julian countered that even if sentience clusters, we still face a decision problem under uncertainty about which cluster obtains. If there is a ten percent chance that all insects are sentient and a ninety percent chance that none are, the expected moral weight is still ten percent of the total population, which remains vast. The aggregation is not metaphysical but decision-theoretic: we should weigh our actions by the expected welfare of all affected beings, and that requires multiplying probabilities by populations regardless of how the uncertainty is structured.

Maya said this response smuggles in a controversial metaethical premise, namely that moral status is commensurable with expected utility in this way. She argued that being a moral patient is a threshold property, not a gradable one that can be weighted by credence. We do not treat human embryos as having a fraction of a right because we are thirty percent sure they are persons; we either extend protection or we do not, guided by precaution but not by computing fractional rights across a population. Applying expected-value calculus to the boundary of moral status collapses the distinction between risk to known patients and statistical speculation about unknown ones.

Julian answered that Maya’s threshold view cannot explain why we treat factory-farmed chickens differently from wild insects even when our confidence about chicken sentience is also less than absolute. We already operate with gradations of moral concern based on cognitive capacity and confidence, so refusing to aggregate across insects is an arbitrary line-drawing exercise that protects our intuitions rather than the animals.
