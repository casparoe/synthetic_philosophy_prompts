Analyze President Harry S. Truman's decision in August 1945 to authorize the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki through the lens of the dirty-hands problem in political ethics—the thesis that a leader may be required to commit acts that are morally wrong in themselves in order to secure a greater public good. First, map the case with discipline: identify the specific moral wrong involved (the deliberate use of weapons causing mass death among non-combatants), the goods at stake (ending the Pacific War and avoiding the projected enormous casualties of an invasion of Japan), the alternative courses actually available to Truman in that decision window, and the institutional role obligations that constrained his choice. Then apply the dirty-hands thesis: was the wrong genuinely unavoidable for a leader in that office, or was it an opportunistic wrong that exploited the crisis? Here, distinguish carefully between structural dirty hands—in which the wrong is inseparable from the decision-making structure of the office itself—and personal dirty hands—in which the agent could have fulfilled the role without committing that particular wrong. Assess which category, if either, fits Truman's authorization. Next, consider the concept of moral remainder: the idea that even a fully justified wrong leaves a residual stain or unpaid moral debt on the agent. Ask whether the dirty-hands framework can accommodate this remainder without undermining the justification, and whether the presence of such a remainder distinguishes a justified dirty hands case from mere rationalization. Finally, say what the episode reveals about the limits of the thesis: does it confirm that politics necessarily requires moral corruption, does it show that the thesis risks being expanded to excuse any leader-ordered atrocity, or does it reveal that the structural-versus-personal distinction is too unstable to do real work? Be explicit about where the theory is silent—particularly regarding the long-term institutional normalization of weapons of mass destruction that followed the decision.
