For my seminar on forecasting and institutional collapse, I am trying to apply reference-class reasoning and the outside view—not the usual inside-view narrative—to the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991. Rather than recounting Gorbachev’s reforms, nationalist movements, or economic stagnation as causal drivers, I want to treat the episode as a prediction problem faced by an analyst in, say, 1985 who knows only the base-rate properties of comparable classes.

Consider the reference classes an outside-view forecaster might construct: multi-ethnic continental empires, one-party communist regimes, nuclear-armed federations, and hydrocarbon-export-dependent states. The base rates for peaceful, rapid dissolution differ sharply across them—the Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman Empires fragmented violently; Britain decolonized over decades; Eastern European communist regimes collapsed quickly in 1989 but without custodianship of strategic nuclear arsenals. Meanwhile, the class “liberalizing nuclear federation” is essentially empty.

Please analyze the Soviet collapse through this framework. What should an outside-view analyst have assigned in 1985 to the probability of a non-violent, core-intact dissolution by 1991? Which reference classes are genuinely analogous, and which mislead because they lack the nuclear or federal dimensions? How should the forecaster weigh an empty or near-empty reference class—should it lower confidence, or does it signal that the outside view has reached its boundary?

Then reverse the analysis: what does the actual 1991 outcome reveal about the framework itself? Does the rapid, largely peaceful dissolution show that the outside view is systematically conservative when institutional change accelerates, or does it expose an error in class construction—specifically, the failure to include a class of “ideologically exhausted hegemonic powers undergoing simultaneous political and economic liberalization”? Be disciplined about the mapping—identify the forecaster, the evidence set available in 1985, the prediction options, and the resolution criteria—and indicate explicitly where the theory offers no verdict rather than forcing one.
