I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind and epistemology, currently working through problems in modal epistemology with colleagues in a reading group. We have been trying to formalize a tension between the KK principle and what we have taken to calling graded epistemic transparency — the view that knowing p does not uniformly put one in a position to know that one knows p, but does so only when one's confidence exceeds a context-sensitive threshold and the belief is available for explicit report. During our last meeting, one of us constructed the following argument, which appears to show that either all knowledge is fully explicit and hierarchically accessible, or that implicit knowledge is not knowledge at all. Each premise seems defensible in isolation, but the conclusion strikes us as either circular or skeptical in an unacceptable way.

The argument proceeds as follows:

(1) The KK principle holds without restriction: for any subject S and any proposition p, if S knows p, then S knows that S knows p.
(2) By straightforward iteration, if S knows p, then for every finite n, S knows that S knows ... (n times) ... that p.
(3) Luminosity, or epistemic transparency, entails that knowing p puts S in a position to access the epistemic status of p, at least under conditions of rational reflection.
(4) Human cognition is finitely bounded; no subject can simultaneously represent or access an infinite hierarchy of iterated knowledge states in working memory or explicit belief.
(5) Therefore, if KK applies without restriction to implicit or subpersonal knowledge states — for instance, to the tacit perceptual tracking that allows one to navigate a familiar room — then such states cannot genuinely count as knowledge, since they cannot sustain the required infinite accessibility.
(6) But cognitive science and ordinary epistemic practice treat implicit tracking states as genuine knowledge; they are distinguished from explicit beliefs precisely by their lack of hierarchical accessibility.
(7) Consequently, either we deny that implicit states are knowledge (which seems to collapse perceptual cognition into mere information processing), or we restrict KK so that it applies only to explicit, reportable beliefs.
(8) However, drawing the boundary in this way appears circular: explicit belief is defined as that to which KK applies, and KK is restricted to explicit belief in order to avoid the reductio.
(9) So if we wish to preserve both the reality of implicit knowledge and a non-circular distinction between implicit and explicit cognition, we must reject the unrestricted KK principle altogether.

What I find most suspicious is the transition from (3) to (5), because it seems to slide from a modal claim about being in a position to access one's epistemic status to a claim about the simultaneous representational demands of implicit cognition. At the same time, step (8) feels like it might confuse a definitional stipulation with a substantive theoretical boundary. I would prefer to keep the implicit/explicit distinction intact rather than abandon it, but I want to know precisely which step to reject and at what cost. Is there an equivocation between potential accessibility and actual simultaneous representation? Or is the argument valid, in which case the least costly move is indeed to restrict KK, and if so, does that restriction undermine the standard defenses of the principle in modal epistemology? Please diagnose the chain: name the specific step or transition that contains the error, explain why it initially looks plausible, and then price the alternatives — what does rejecting that step commit me to, versus keeping it and abandoning either the distinction or the principle?
