I'm writing a feature on conceptual consistency in bioethical reasoning, and I need an outside analysis of a specific institutional track record. Consider an interdisciplinary review committee that has published rulings on four prior cases.

First, in the classic unconscious-violinist scenario—where a person is involuntarily hooked up to save a famous musician's life, and disconnection means the musician's death—the committee ruled that unplugging is morally permissible.

Second, in a modified version in which the individual signed an informed contract to remain connected for nine months with minimal risk to herself, and now wishes to withdraw for purely personal reasons, the committee still permitted disconnection, holding that bodily integrity cannot be contractually alienated over that duration.

Third, applying a future-like-ours evaluation to a fetus at approximately ten weeks gestation—possessing complete genetic potential but lacking any formed neural tube or cortical structures—the committee held that terminating the pregnancy is permissible, on the grounds that the being does not yet exhibit the psychological continuity that the argument requires for protection.

Fourth, in a case involving a conscious, sentient fetus at thirty-four weeks with established thalamocortical integration, where termination was requested for non-medical, career-related reasons and would require direct fetal demise rather than delivery, the committee rejected the request. It ruled that the being now falls within the scope of moral protection and that bodily autonomy does not license direct destruction when independent survival via standard delivery is medically feasible.

The committee now faces a new proposal. At twenty-four weeks gestation—after thalamocortical connections have formed but before standard extrauterine viability without technological intervention—a pregnant person develops a severe, non-life-threatening autoimmune condition. Continuing the pregnancy causes debilitating but survivable symptoms; discontinuing it resolves them. Crucially, an experimental artificial-womb protocol is available that could extract the fetus and sustain it ex utero with an estimated eighty-percent success rate, though premature extraction carries a meaningful risk of neurological impairment. Once extraction is complete, the pregnant person bears no further physiological burden. She requests direct termination rather than extraction and artificial maintenance, citing profound psychological distress at the prospect of her genetic material producing a child she does not wish to parent, regardless of physical separation.

What should the committee decide if it wishes to remain consistent with its own precedents? Which of the four earlier rulings is the closest analog? If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, what principle would the committee be adopting with each possible choice? And what single further case—specifying gestational stage, available technology, and the precise nature of the burden—would most clearly distinguish the competing principles?
