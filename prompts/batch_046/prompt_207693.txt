I'm running a grad seminar on free will and moral responsability, about 10-12 people, 90 minutes. I want an activity that makes them actually feel Frankfurt's challenge to the principle of alternative possibilities — not just discuss it. Specifically, I'd like a staged deliberation or role-play where the group has to make a real collective choice with small stakes (maybe allocating a real pot of money or deciding a real rule for the rest of the session), but unknown to them there's a hidden "intervener" mechanism built into the rules: if they had gone the other way, the facilitator or a planted student would have forced the same outcome. They should come away convinced they made the call freely and are answerable for it, and then in debrief discover the blocker existed all along.

To make it less generic, inject the "flicker of freedom" problem into the setup: give them a brief, hidden window where they could have sent a prior signal of resistance (like writing a secret note or pressing a button), and tell them afterwards that even that flicker was being monitored and would have been suppressed had it actually appeared. So the debrief needs to map this onto Widerker-style objections — that there's still some tiny prior choice that grounds responsibility — and then show why Frankfurtians think that flicker is too small to do the work. The rules and payoffs should be arranged so they run into the tension themselves before anyone names Frankfurt.

Please include the setting, roles, timeline, how the hidden mechanism works without being revealed too early, the debrief structure that connects moments in the game to parts of the argument, and an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce (probably the full metaphysical determinism side, since they're still physically able to move). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when running it — like students feeling betrayed by the deception — and how to handle that. Don't ask for citations or a bibliography; I want this runnable for a model with no web access. Maybe one small typo is fine.
