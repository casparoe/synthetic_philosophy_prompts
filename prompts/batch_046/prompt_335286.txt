I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about determinism versus predictability by running my gut on concrete cases, and I'm hitting a wall.

Case one: I run a perfect simulation of a double pendulum twice with the exact same initial conditions, but in the second run I round the 20th decimal differently. After thirty seconds the two trajectories are totally uncorrelated. My verdict: it's deterministic, but prediction is brittle — destroyed by any practical measurment error, not impossible in principle.

Case two: I flip a coin, and somehow I measure every air molecule and the exact torque. In principle I can compute the outcome. Verdict: deterministic and predictable in principle; the unpredictability is just ignorance.

Case three: I run two climate models with initial conditions that differ by less than my thermometor can detect. By day twelve they're predicting opposite storms. Verdict: this feels different from the coin. It's not just that I'm ignorant; the system amplifies error faster than I can ever reduce it. So I'd call it deterministically unpredictable in practice. But I'm not sure I should say "in principle" too — and that's where I get stuck.

Here's the tension. There's this idea about computational irreducibility — some deterministic rules have no shortcut, you just have to run every step. If that's right, predicting the weather might require running a simulation that takes as long as the weather itself takes. That feels like a different failure mode than the coin-flip case. But I still want to say the coin is "predictable in principle" while the weather isn't, even though both are deterministic. So is the difference error amplification, irreducibility, or something else?

Also: I think a human brain trying to intuit a chaotic pendulum fails for the same reason the computer fails with rounding — it's an information problem, not metaphysical. But then I also think that if hidden-variable quantum mechanics is true, quantum unpredictability comes from lacking access to hidden initial conditions, which feels like ignorance again, not irreducibility. So I seem to treat chaos as a "fundamental practical limit" and hidden-variable unpredictability as "contingent ignorance," but I can't articulate why.

One more case: a deterministic pseudorandom-number generator. Given the seed I can predict every output; given only outputs I can't easily reverse-engineer the seed. Verdict: predictable with the right info, unpredictable without it. But this feels closer to the coin flip than to weather.

So is there a coherent taxonomy of why deterministic things resist prediction — measurment error, error amplification, irreducibility, hidden variables — and which ones I should treat as "predictable in principle"? Or do I have to drop one of these verdicts? And what does the principle say about a deterministic cellular automaton that has no sensitive dependence on initial conditions, just long computation paths that make shortcut prediction impossible?
