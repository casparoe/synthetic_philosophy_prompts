In comparative archaeology and historical sociology, the long-running debate over whether the Late Bronze Age palatial economies, the Classic Maya lowland polities, and Easter Island (Rapa Nui) underwent genuine societal "collapse" has stalled despite decades of new data. One camp points to the rapid abandonment of administrative centers, the breakdown of long-distance exchange, the loss of writing or monumental construction, and—according to some narratives—severe demographic contraction, treating these as objective indicators that a society has failed. The opposing camp emphasizes continuity in local settlement, agricultural practice, and material culture, arguing that decentralization, political fragmentation, or adaptive simplification are misread as catastrophe because modern observers implicitly value centralized state complexity; they absorb the same chronometric and survey evidence as proof of resilience rather than decline.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is structural rather than empirical: the two sides answer different questions—one about the survival of specific institutional forms (palace economies, divine kingship, long-range networks), the other about the welfare and continuity of human populations—while insisting they are delivering a single verdict on whether the society "failed." There may also be an unacknowledged normative layer in which the loss of literacy and monumental architecture is treated as degradation only if institutional complexity is taken as an intrinsic good.

Diagnose this disagreement. Is it verbal—turning on the extension of "collapse"—or substantive—turning on what counts as evidence for societal failure? Are the sides separated by hidden empirical questions about mortality and migration that could be settled, by differing standards of what counts as data, or by bedrock intuitions about whether simplification is necessarily decline? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: an explicit distinction between institutional discontinuity and demographic crisis; a formal, measurable model of societal resilience; or should the term be abandoned altogether because it is irreducibly normatively loaded?
