Here is an argument I constructed comparing climate emissions and democratic voting, and I suspect it proves too much but cannot find the weak step. (1) For an individual act to be morally assessable with respect to a collective outcome, it must be a non-redundant part of the causal chain that produces that outcome—what we might call a triggering cause rather than merely a structural condition. (2) Both an individual carbon footprint in a global economy and a single vote in a large electorate are causally redundant: the climate outcome and the election result would be essentially unchanged if this particular act were removed. (3) So neither emitting carbon nor casting a vote is morally right or wrong with respect to the collective result. (4) But if redundancy eliminates all individual moral assessment, then there is no moral difference between a society in which everyone emits and votes and one in which everyone abstains, since in neither case does any individual trigger the outcome. (5) That conclusion is absurd: we clearly think collective patterns matter and individuals participate in them. (6) Therefore either (1) is false, or moral evaluation applies only to collective agents and never to individuals within them. I want to preserve both the reality of redundancy and the moral significance of individual participation, so I am inclined to reject (1). But if I do, I need an account of how structural conditions carry moral weight without being triggering causes. Which step should I actually reject, and what does that rejection cost?
