You are a professional philosopher specializing in metaethics and the metaphysics of normativity. Before constructing anything, take a moment to think carefully and brainstorm the full logical space of answers to the question: can reasons, rationales, or normative requirements exist in the absence of any actual reasoner? I want you to design a classification instrument—specifically a branching taxonomy with labeled nodes and named leaves—for an upper-level graduate seminar of about twelve students. The topic is free-floating rationales: reasons without reasoners.

Build the tree from the logical combinations of answers first, then locate actual positions within that space. Each internal node must be a concrete case or claim on which views genuinely diverge, phrased so that a student can answer yes or no without already knowing which philosophical label it points to. Do not ask “do you accept agent-neutral realism?” Instead, pose diagnostic questions such as: “Consider a possible world that never contains any conscious agents but in which suffering is arranged in patterns we would call cruel. Is there, in that world, a reason—not merely a cause—for that arrangement not to occur?” Or: “If a reason to φ exists, must there be some particular agent for whom it is a reason to φ, or can it be a reason full stop?”

Your taxonomy must accommodate and clearly separate the following positions as leaves, or explicitly note where they fall outside the branching logic:
- Strict relationalism: every reason is essentially a reason-for-some-actual-agent, and in a world with no agents there are no normative facts.
- Modal relationalism: reasons do not require actual agents, but they do require that some agent be possible; normative facts depend on modal accessibility to reasoners.
- Standpoint-independent realism: reasons are properties of states of affairs that obtain regardless of whether any agent exists, has existed, or could exist.
- Constructivist anti-realism: reasons are constructed through the rational activity of agents and have no being independent of that activity.
- Eliminativism about normative reasons: apparent talk of “reasons” is always reducible to descriptions of psychological dispositions or evolutionary programming.
- The free-floating hybrid: normative properties can exist without current reasoners, but they carry no directive or prescriptive force unless a reasoner is present to be directed.

At each leaf, name the position and state in one sentence its most awkward commitment—the claim it must swallow that sounds worst to common sense.

Construct the tree so it has six to eight leaves total. After placing the named views, identify any unoccupied combinations of answers that your branching allows but that no named position occupies; for each empty slot, briefly diagnose whether the vacancy is best explained by incoherence, by extreme unattractiveness, or by the combination being merely unexplored. Also flag any pair of named positions that your tree cannot genuinely separate, explaining why they converge on every diagnostic question you have included.

Keep the instrument free of bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific texts. Do not invoke obscure theories or named scholars that a model without web access could not reasonably be expected to place. Start with a brief brainstorming outline of the logical space, then present the tree with its nodes, branches, leaves, empty slots, and inseparability flags. Think step by step.
