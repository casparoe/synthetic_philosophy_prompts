I've been trying to work out this view I'm calling syntactic-gap intentionalism, mostly for a course on philosophy of langauge. The basic idea is that a text's meaning isn't just the author's private mental content, and it isn't purely in the text either—it's specifically in the intentional architecture of the gaps between what the author meant to encode and what the syntax actually delivers. So if a sentence syntactically allows two readings but the author's mental plan only accommodated one, the correct interpretation includes that specific mismatch: not reading A, not reading B, but reading A-as-intended-with-the-syntactic-leakage-to-B. I think this saves intentionalism from the usual death-of-the-author objections because it doesn't require telepathic access to mental states, just to the intended structure of ambiguity.

But I'm not sure how solid this is, and I want to know what would actually count against it—not just the standard Wimsatt-and-Beardsley rehash about the intentional fallacy, but the deeper defeaters. Are there results in translation studies or computational syntax showing these gap structures are either undetectable or so multiply realizable that the intentional component becomes redundant? What about texts where the author clearly had no stable mental representation of the syntactic options—improvised oral poetry that gets transcribed, or wiki-style collaboratively edited documents? Would a defender of my view just bite the bullet and say those aren't texts in the relevant sense, and if so, is that a fatal qualification or just normal boundary-drawing?

Please rank the counter-arguments: which are genuinely fatal (the view collapses into rival contextualism or can't describe actual cases), which are merely awkward (it survives but looks ad hoc), and which ones a committed syntactic-gap intentionalist would honestly concede are problems. I'm especially curious whether the view risks being insulated—say, claiming the gap is always present even when we can't detect it—and whether that insulation is a theoretical vice. Also, do rival positions like distributed-cognition or radical contextualist accounts explain the same phenomena without dragging in authorial architecture at all? Oh, and my friend who studies comparative literature mentioned something about medieval scribal practices where copyists intentionally altered syntax but kept the gap structure intact—I'm not sure if that's a real historical pattern or just her impression, but if anything like that exists, does it help or hurt my position?
