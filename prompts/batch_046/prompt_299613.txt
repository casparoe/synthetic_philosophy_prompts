I was chatting with my friends last night about Garrett Hardin's lifeboat ethics and whether it actually explains real-world survival choices or just collapses into a biological metaphor. We got onto the Mignonette case -- you probably know it: the yacht sinks, four blokes in a dinghy, they draw lots then kill and eat the cabin boy Richard Parker after weeks adrift, get rescued, and Dudley and Stephens are tried for murder in England. The court rejected the necessity defence but commuted the sentence to six months.

I'd like you to analyse this through Hardin's lens, but with a twist that I think is a bit niche: treat the British court itself as a kind of lifeboat, a boundried moral space with scarce resources (in this case, legal legitimacy and public trust). Does Hardin's triage logic apply here? Did the court implicitly accept that one principle (the sanctity of life) had to be sacrificed to preserve the survival of the legal order, hence marking them guilty but sparing them execution? Or did it reject the lifeboat framing altogether by insisting on absolute rules regardless of scarcity? And where does the analogy break -- Hardin is obsessed with carrying capacity and reproductive rates, which clearly don't map onto jurisprudence. So is this a revealing failure of lifeboat ethics, showing it secretly depends on ecological metaphors it can't defend, or just a misapplication?
