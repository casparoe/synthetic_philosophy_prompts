The historiographical and philosophical debate about whether pathogens, climate regimes, and volcanic events ought to be regarded as 'historical actors'—possessing genuine agency—rather than as passive background conditions has persisted for decades without clear resolution. Proponents, drawing on traditions that extend material agency beyond the human, argue that entities such as the bacillus behind the Black Death or the climatic aftermath of the Tambora eruption actively reshape political and economic trajectories; opponents contend that attributing agency to non-human forces risks a pernicious environmental determinism that obscures institutional and intentional explanation. Despite a growing body of case studies and conceptual analysis, the dispute appears stalled: neither camp has persuaded the other, and the literature often repeats the same objections without advancement. Diagnose the nature of this impasse. Is it fundamentally verbal, hinging on competing definitions of 'agency' and 'actor'; is it a substantive disagreement about the standards of causal explanation in history; or are the participants addressing distinct questions—perhaps one about explanatory ontology and another about the distribution of moral and political responsibility—under a single heading? In your answer, set out what each side actually maintains, identify whether a hidden empirical or formal question is doing the work, and specify what form of progress—whether a refined conceptual distinction, a formal model of multi-causal explanation, or a reconceptualisation of historical narrative—would count as resolving the disagreement, or whether the question itself ought to be dissolved.
