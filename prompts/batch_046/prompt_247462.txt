I am working through Nietzsche's genealogy of morality in a seminar that asks whether his diagnostic framework can still illuminate moral institutions that arose well after 1900. One case that keeps recurring is the contemporary practice of institutional “harm auditing” for algorithmic systems—specifically, the evaluation of AI outputs not merely for statistical bias but for “representational harm” to social groups, coupled with a moral obligation to preemptively suppress content that might cause such harm even when no identifiable individual is wronged and no direct causal injury can be demonstrated. Defenders present this expansion as a more sensitive form of justice; critics treat it as ressentiment formalized into bureaucratic procedure.

If Nietzsche were alive today and had fully absorbed this practice, together with its philosophical justifications in terms of structural injustice and epistemic injustice, how would he analyze the moral psychology of its advocates and administrators? Would he classify their stance as a variant of slave morality—weak forces achieving domination through institutionalized guilt and predictive prohibition—or as a disguised master morality that deploys the rhetoric of care and protection to exercise a more totalizing will to power? In answering, apply his concepts of ressentiment, the ascetic ideal, and the transvaluation of values. Where precisely in the genealogy would he locate the shift from direct, personal grievance to this mediated, anticipatory, and procedurally enforced mode of moral condemnation? And what would he likely say to the claim that such auditing represents genuine moral progress rather than a more refined and invisible mask for the same underlying drive?
