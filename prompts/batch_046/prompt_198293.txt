You have been invited to give the opening address at the inauguration of the Municipal Bureau of Civic Translation in a European city that has recently adopted an administrative framework it calls “asymmetric visibility.” Under this design, every public official is bound by strict neutrality of dress, symbol, and institutional speech—aligned with the French tradition of laïcité—while citizens who engage with the state may elect to have their petitions, testimony, or appeals interpreted and recorded through culturally specific frameworks of meaning rather than rendered into a universalist bureaucratic idiom, a provision inspired by Anglo-American multiculturalist commitments to recognition. The audience is split: constitutional lawyers, political philosophers, and sociologists of integration sit beside local teachers, housing officers, and community organizers with no formal training in theory. Write a ten-minute speech in which the tension between assimilationist neutrality and multicultural recognition does not merely illustrate the occasion but structurally shapes it—determining what you can celebrate without contradiction, what you must present as an open institutional dilemma rather than a solved problem, and what you ought to leave deliberately unspoken. Ground the address in one concrete, plausible scenario drawn from the Bureau’s expected daily work—such as a school-curriculum objection or a public-housing appeal—making sure the example is fully intelligible to both halves of the room and shows exactly how the asymmetry operates in practice. End with a brief reflective note that clearly separates the claims in your speech that rest on settled distinctions in the literature on laïcité and multiculturalism from those that depend on genuinely contested questions about whether state neutrality and differentiated recognition can be stably combined; do not present any disputed interpretation as if it were the consensus view among specialists.
