I teach an upper-level philosophy seminar of about 12 students in a single 75-minute session, and I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of the idea that promises bind even when no reliance is generated and no identifiable party can be harmed. The niche twist is “radical anonymity”: promises are made to unknown future readers of sealed messages, so expectation-based and harm-based explanations are structurally unavailable from the start. Design a runnable game or structured deliberation—with clear roles or rules, a step-by-step timeline with minutes, small real stakes (tokens or actions with actual, if minor, consequences), and materials list—so that participants themselves make and then face breaking such anonymous promises before anyone names the philosophical problem. Arrange the incentives so that they run into the tension between “I said I would” and “no one will ever know or be affected” on their own. Then include a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the debate between expectation theories, practice theories, and normative-power theories of promising; predicts what will go wrong the first time (for example, students treating it as a game about lying rather than about obligation) and how to handle it; and frankly states which feature of promissory binding—likely its directedness toward a specific other—this setup cannot reproduce and must be supplied by discussion. Please say how confident you are in your design judgments and single out the point about which you are least sure.
