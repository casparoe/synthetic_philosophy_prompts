I spent much of last night in an exhausting but clarifying argument with a close friend about whether it is philosophically coherent to say that a nation is "to blame" for a war. We kept circling the Fischer controversy—not merely whether Fischer was right that the German leadership deliberately risked a continental conflict in July 1914, but what "holding a nation responsible" could mean when the decision-makers were a small circle of chancellors, generals, and a monarch, and when the state itself lacked anything like a unified democratic will. My friend pressed a specific objection: moral blame, he argued, requires collective intentionality of a kind that the Kaiserreich, with its split authority between civilian government and an autonomous General Staff, could not possess; without that, assigning blame to "Germany" collapses into a category error that confuses causal contribution with moral accountability. I could not fully settle the point, but the disagreement is not academic for me: I serve on the advisory board of the International Historical Accountability Commission, and at our March session we must vote on whether to endorse the proposed resolution that assigns "primary structural responsibility to the German Reich for the escalation of the July crisis." The drafters intend this as a historical correction, not a criminal indictment, yet board members are divided over whether the resolution implicitly commits us to a theory of collective moral agency that we have never articulated.

Please draft the formal institutional memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. The memo should recommend a concrete decision—endorse the resolution, reject it, or endorse it only with specific limiting conditions—and provide reasoning the board can use in deliberation rather than in a classroom. In particular, it must address: (1) whether collective moral blame requires collective intentionality, and whether the internal division of authority within the Kaiserreich undermines any such attribution; (2) how the historical evidence associated with the Fischer thesis—particularly regarding pre-war strategic planning and risk-acceptance—bears on moral responsibility rather than on mere causal or structural contribution; (3) the strongest argument against your own recommendation, treated with full intellectual seriousness and not reduced to a straw position; and (4) precise conditions, qualifying language, or procedural safeguards that should accompany whatever recommendation you make. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citation suggestions; I need a document I can defend orally without referencing sources the board does not have in front of it. The tone should be formal, precise, and respectful of the philosophical stakes, recognizing that our vote is about institutional judgment, not historical revisionism for its own sake.
