Consider a philosopher of mathematics with finitist sympathies who nevertheless accepts formal reasoning when it avoids ontological commitment to completed totalities. She has rendered five verdicts. First, she rejects the standard Hilbert's hotel resolution—infinitely many guests in infinitely many rooms, with a bus of infinitely more guests accommodated by shifting each occupant from room n to room 2n—because it presupposes that an infinite collection can be globally rearranged in a single act, treating the guests and rooms as a completed infinity. Second, she accepts Cantor's diagonal argument against any enumeration of the reals between 0 and 1, but takes it to show that "the complete set of reals" is not a well-defined object, not to prove the existence of an uncountable actual infinity. Third, she endorses mathematical induction for any particular natural number n, while denying that this entails the existence of "the set of all natural numbers" as a single completed entity. Fourth, she approves of a computer program that computes the nth digit of π for any queried n, so long as the program never claims to have generated all digits simultaneously; she regards this as a legitimate potential infinity that involves no actual infinite object. Fifth, she rejects appeals to Zorn's lemma or the axiom of choice in non-constructive existence proofs, on the ground that selecting an element from each member of an infinite family requires treating that family as an already-completed domain.

Now consider a proposed "potential Hilbert's hotel" implemented as a digital registry. The system never stores all assignments at once. It defines a computable pairing function—say, mapping guest n to room 2n−1—and materializes a record only upon a specific query. At every actual moment the database holds only finitely many entries, though there is no upper bound on how many it could hold, and the pairing rule is globally specified for every natural number. The system never asserts that all assignments exist together; it asserts only that for each n there is a deterministic procedure to produce the nth assignment.

Predict her verdict on whether this registry is legitimate by her own lights. Identify at least two distinct rules that jointly accommodate her five prior judgments yet diverge on this registry, stating precisely what each rule predicts about the case. Name the prior case that serves as the closest analog. Offer your best-guess verdict with reasoning, and propose one further hypothetical case that would decisively distinguish the two rules. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
