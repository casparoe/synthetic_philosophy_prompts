As a journalist covering the intersection of cosmology and historical theology, I am preparing a feature that asks how Isaac Newton’s particular theological commitments—his Arian rejection of Trinitarian co-eternity, his conviction that space functions as the sensorium of a unitary sovereign God, his belief that mathematical natural laws occasionally require direct divine correction, and his prophetic chronology insisting on a single linear history rather than branching worlds—would shape his response to a problem he never faced: eternal inflation and the string-theory landscape. Today cosmologists invoke a multiverse of perhaps 10^500 causally disconnected regions with varying physical constants, using anthropic reasoning to explain cosmic fine-tuning without recourse to design. Given Newton’s insistence on divine economy, on a God who establishes immutable order rather than proliferating unnecessary worlds, and on prophecy that reveals one purposeful timeline, how precisely would he evaluate this multiverse hypothesis? Would he treat the anthropic principle as a concealed admission of design, as a failure of mechanistic explanation, or as a metaphysical error that confuses mathematical possibility with actual creation? How might his view of Christ as a created mediator—rather than co-eternal—inform his judgment about whether an infinite multiplicity of worlds demands or excludes a single governing intelligence? And would his concept of space as the sensorium Dei allow him to accommodate regions beyond all causal contact, or would he see eternal inflation as a violation of the unity, transparency, and correctability of nature that his theology and physics both presupposed? I am looking for a rigorous conceptual reconstruction that specifies which of Newton’s theological and methodological arguments he would deploy, where he might distinguish between speculative mathematics and demonstrated mechanism, and whether his commitment to a linear prophetic history makes an eternal, cyclical multiverse not merely implausible but theologically intolerable. No bibliographies or citation lists are needed—just a precise reconstruction of the reasoning he would likely have advanced.
