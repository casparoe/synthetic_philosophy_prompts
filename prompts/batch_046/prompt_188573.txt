Consider the following exam prompt and a student’s written response to it.

Exam question: Max Weber’s The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism is often read as a straightforward causal claim—that Calvinist predestination theology produced modern capitalist conduct. Yet Weber frames his study around an “elective affinity” between the religious ethic and the economic ethos, and he insists that both are ideal types rather than empirical descriptions of actual mental states. He also distinguishes explaining action through its subjective meaningful coherence from establishing its causal conditions. Write an essay that addresses three things. First, explain why Weber thinks reducing the religious ethic to a psychological disposition—such as anxiety over salvation—misses the specifically sociological character of his explanation. Second, clarify what “elective affinity” denotes: is it a one-directional causal influence, a functional correlation, a mutually reinforcing selection process, or something else? And does this concept allow values to play a genuinely causal role without reducing them to material interests? Third, discuss the implication of the ideal-typical status of both terms. If neither the ethic nor the spirit is an empirical reality but a constructed heuristic, can Weber’s thesis be falsified, or does it collapse into an unfalsifiable narrative of interpretive coherence?

Student answer: Weber’s central argument is that Calvinists worked harder because they felt anxious about predestination and sought signs of election in economic success. This anxiety is a psychological cause, but Weber wants to avoid reducing religion to psychology. Instead, he uses “elective affinity,” which means the religious values and capitalist practices simply matched up well—like two compatible personalities—without either actually causing the other. Because both the Protestant ethic and the spirit of capitalism are ideal types, they are not real things in the world, so Weber is not making a real causal claim; he is only showing that, if you construct these two types, they fit together coherently. This means his thesis cannot be falsified, since ideal types are just tools for understanding. The values do not cause anything in a material sense; they provide a meaningful framework that makes capitalist behavior understandable to the actors themselves. So Weber avoids reducing values to material interests, but only by giving up on causation entirely and settling for interpretive coherence. The “spirit of capitalism” is therefore best understood as a post-hoc rationalization that people used to justify economic behavior they would have engaged in anyway.

You are grading this as an instructor or exam grader. Provide a specific grade (letter or percentage, with the scale made explicit), followed by point-by-point comments that justify it. Identify the visible correct points—where the student grasps the distinction between psychological reduction and sociological explanation, or recognizes the heuristic status of ideal types—without overstating them. Then pinpoint the one or two subtle errors: a conflation in how “elective affinity” is characterized, a misstep in what the ideal-typical construction implies for falsifiability and causation, or an unjustified inference about rationalization. Do not signal your own view of Weber’s actual thesis; assess only whether the answer accurately addresses the question asked. Keep the assessment precise, formal, and focused on the reasoning rather than on style.
