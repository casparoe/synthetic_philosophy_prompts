I have drafted notes for an essay on the ethics of retaining controversial street names, centred on a distinction between commemorative intent and what I call navigational inertia—the persistence of a name owing chiefly to the coordination costs of altering postal, emergency-service, and commercial databases rather than to any active honouring of the figure. My argument is that where retention is largely inertial, it lacks the expressive endorsement that makes commemoration morally weighty; nevertheless, outright removal erases an archival trace that ought to be preserved. I therefore propose a policy of dual signage: retaining the original name for practical continuity whilst installing explanatory plaques that explicitly disclaim honour, thereby separating navigational function from moral endorsement. I also address the objection that dual signage still compels residents—particularly in neighbourhoods where the commemorated figure holds no shared historical significance—to utter the disputed name in daily life, thus perpetuating harm regardless of disclaimers. Please comment on the coherence of this central distinction, on whether dual signage genuinely resolves the tension between archival preservation and moral distancing, and on whether I have neglected stronger objections concerning the semiotics of place-names in culturally heterogeneous districts.
