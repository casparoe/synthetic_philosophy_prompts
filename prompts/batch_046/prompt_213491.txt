I've noticed a recurring argumentative slide in debates about whether you could have come from a different sperm and egg and still be you. Call it the "origin-rigidity oscillation," though I don't know if it has a real name, and I want the carving, not just a label.

Here are two concrete instances. In one discussion, a speaker insists that because you are essentially the product of that specific sperm and egg, a psychologically continuous being arising from different gametes is not "you" but a duplicate; they then invoke this to block non-identity complaints, saying there is no harm in failing to bring "you" into being, since any actual child would be a different person. Later, in the same conversation, when asked whether you could survive gradual brain replacement or a teleportation accident, the same speaker appeals to psychological continuity to preserve identity, treating your biological origin as just one contingent property among others—quietly abandoning the metaphysical rigidity they just used to generate a moral asymmetry.

The pattern is not plain equivocation between origin-essentialist and psychological-continuity theories of identity. What is distinctive is the directional switch: origin essentialism is treated as absolute and identity-determining when it serves to defend the uniqueness of one's actual history or to produce normative asymmetries, but is downgraded to a contingent biological fact whenever psychological continuity is needed to preserve identity across counterfactual change. The two deployments share a slogan—"your origins matter essentially"—yet they pull in opposite directions, and the tension is never addressed.
