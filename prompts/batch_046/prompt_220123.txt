After a long and rather heated argument with my friend last night about whether humans have any reason to intervene in wild ecosystems, I found myself defending a position that I think is more nuanced than the usual binary of “eliminate predation” versus “leave nature alone.” The view is this: wild animal suffering is real and massive, but any serious intervention must contend with what I will call the “constitutive harm” problem—the idea that for many species, particularly predators, the practices that generate suffering (hunting, territorial conflict, the starvation dynamics of prey populations) are not incidental to their form of life but partly constitutive of the ecological and behavioral identity of the species itself. To eliminate predation globally is not merely to reduce harm; in a non-trivial sense, it dissolves the conditions under which those predator species exist as the kinds of things they are, transforming them into ontologically different entities—perhaps domesticated analogues or functional equivalents of extinction. This suggests that the ethical calculus is not a simple aggregation of suffering versus pleasure across sentient individuals, but a conflict between welfare maximization and the preservation of ecological identities that are themselves sources of value. Consequently, I hold that we should pursue targeted, localized welfare interventions—vaccination against specific diseases, managed feeding in extreme conditions, or reducing human-introduced suffering—while rejecting large-scale ecological engineering that eliminates predation, precisely because such engineering does not just reduce suffering but dissolves the very entities whose suffering we claim to care about.

But I am unsure whether this position is genuinely coherent, or whether it has been quietly insulated from refutation by a strategic ambiguity in what “constitutive” means. I want you to tell me what would genuinely count against it—not just the standard objection that suffering is bad regardless of source, but the deeper defeaters: which empirical findings about animal cognition, trophic dynamics, or evolutionary ecology would undermine the claim that predation is constitutive rather than merely characteristic? Which moral frameworks—perhaps those that deny any non-instrumental value to species-level persistence or that treat ecological roles as purely functional—would force the position to collapse into a more conventional utilitarian or rights-based view? Are there historical or analogical cases from conservation biology or domestic animal ethics where a similar “constitutive practice” argument was advanced and then failed under scrutiny? Please rank these defeaters from fatal—those that, if true, would require me to abandon the core distinction between welfare intervention and ecological engineering—to merely awkward—those that complicate application without breaking the structure. I also want to know which of these objections defenders of this kind of view would themselves concede as damaging, and whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability by retreating to a metaphorical reading of “constitutive harm.” Finally, is there a result in evolutionary biology or philosophy of ecology that, if accepted, would render unstable the very boundary I am drawing between reducing suffering and altering the ontological conditions of the sufferer?
