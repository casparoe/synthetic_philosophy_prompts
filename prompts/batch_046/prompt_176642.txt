I am a novelist working on a book about collective memory and defeat, and I have been invited to deliver the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary museum exhibition examining lost causes—specifically the ways the Confederacy, Masada, Kosovo, and Dunkirk have been transformed from failures of state or army into foundational stories for the nations that claim them. The room will contain academic historians who study myth-making, descendants and representatives of the affected communities, and a general public that knows these events mostly through popular narrative. I want a speech of roughly ten minutes that takes seriously the philosophical problem of negative foundation: a collective identity built not on triumph but on a curated loss, which must be kept in a precise tension between honest mourning and mythic redemption without ever resolving into either pure tragedy or pure glory. The idea should structure what the speech can and cannot say—there should be no false comfort, no algorithm for drawing the line between remembrance and romanticization, and no claim that the ambiguity can be settled—while remaining fully intelligible to listeners without specialist training. Please write this in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one writes a formal letter; begin with the single sentence you want every person in the room to carry away, then unfold the argument and the occasion together.
