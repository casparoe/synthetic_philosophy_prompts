I've been trying to reconcile etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function, and I've built an argument that seems to damage both at once by showing that each, pursued consistently, dissolves the distinction between proper function and accidental benefit. Here is the chain as I see it.

(1) If causal-role theory is correct, then a trait's biological function is determined solely by its current contribution to the organism's fitness. (2) So any effect that currently promotes survival or reproduction—however unintended—counts as part of that trait's function. (3) Consider a synthetic polymer heart valve engineered for implantation: it pumps blood by design, but its surface texture also promotes a specific bacterial colonization that outcompetes pathogens and improves host recovery. (4) On the causal-role view, both pumping and bacterial promotion are functions; on the etiological view, only pumping is a function, since only that was selected or designed for. (5) But now apply the causal-role reasoning generally: a heart also contributes to thoracic stability via its weight, generates acoustic and electromagnetic effects detectable by other tissues, and sequesters resources that might otherwise feed infection—indeed, indefinitely many marginal contributions. If all of these are functions, then there is no privileged "proper" function, and malfunction becomes conceptually impossible: a heart that stops pumping but continues to stabilize posture and emit detectable fields has not malfunctioned, but merely changed its functional profile. (6) Apply the etiological reasoning with equal consistency: the synthetic valve's bacterial promotion, though systematically beneficial, is not a function, and neither is any evolutionary novelty in its first generation or any exaptation, since none were selected for those effects. So the etiological view withholds the status of "function" from genuinely beneficial traits in engineered, novel, or repurposed systems. (7) Therefore both accounts, pushed to their logical ends, eliminate the normative gap between "doing what it is supposed to do" and "doing something else that happens to help"—either by multiplying functions without limit or by denying function to reliable benefits. (8) Since medicine, evolutionary explanation, and bioengineering all depend on that normative gap, something must be wrong with the argument, yet I cannot see whether the error lies in (1), in the inference from (5) to (6), or in (7)'s assumption that losing a unique proper function destroys malfunction altogether.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would prefer to preserve the intuition that a synthetic valve genuinely malfunctions if it stops pumping, even when its accidental bacterial promotion continues, but I am unsure whether saving that intuition requires restricting causal-role analysis to a specific explanatory context, accepting that some systematically reliable benefits are "merely accidental," or abandoning the idea that every trait must have exactly one proper function. Please explain why the step you target looked plausible, price the main alternatives—especially whether malfunction can survive without functional exclusivity—and tell me what case or argument would change your mind: for instance, would you be convinced that malfunction is indeed a conventional, context-relative judgment if shown that clinical practice already treats different functional failures differently depending on the patient's environment, or that synthetic traits genuinely lack functions until a selection history is established?
