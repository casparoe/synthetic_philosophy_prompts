I am drafting my view for an advanced seminar on pain, moral status, and suffering-focused ethics, and I want a consistency check before I present. Here is the package I have settled on:

(2) Pain asymbolia is the best clinical proof that this separation is real: patients retain intact sensory-discriminative capacities (they localize, rate intensity, describe burning or stabbing qualities) and report intense nociceptive experiences, yet they deny these are unpleasant, show no avoidance behavior, and do not evaluate them as harmful. From this I infer that the sensory-discriminative component, taken in isolation, is axiologically inert.

(3) So I hold that “pure sensory pain”—nociception stripped of affective valence and avoidance-motivation—is not intrinsically bad, though it may carry instrumental disvalue (for example, signaling tissue damage).

(4) At the same time, I am a suffering-focused negative prioritarian: reducing severe suffering is lexically prior to promoting happiness, and I extend basic moral consideration to any being capable of phenomenal consciousness, regardless of cognitive sophistication or preference-formation capacity.

(5) From (4) I draw a further claim: causing pure sensory pain in a being that possesses phenomenal consciousness but lacks affective architecture (imagine a hypothetical conscious organism with intact nociception but no valence system) is still a pro tanto moral wrong—not because the experience is severely bad, but because inflicting any experiential state on a conscious subject violates a duty of non-maleficence toward phenomenal minds.

(6) In clinical triage, however, I think we should systematically deprioritize asymbolic patients relative to patients with full affective suffering, since negative prioritarianism targets the worst kinds of experience and the sensory-only state lacks the feature that makes suffering morally urgent.

(7) Finally, I reject evolutionary debunking of pain’s normative status—the biological function of nociception does not determine its axiological properties—but I also deny that phenomenal properties alone can ground full moral status without some relational or functional component (for instance, a capacity for welfare-related harm or integration into a life trajectory).
