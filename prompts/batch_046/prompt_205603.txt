I’ve been preparing a unit on historical epistemology for an advanced seminar, and I’m struck by how Kurosawa’s Rashomon (1950) seems to stage almost every failure mode that source critics warn about. The film gives us four incompatible first-person accounts of a violent encounter in the forest—the bandit Tajomaru, the wife Masako, the dead samurai Kanazawa speaking through a medium, and the woodcutter who watched from hiding—while the framing narrative at the Rashomon gate offers only a skeptical commoner and a priest with no external arbiter to break the deadlock. I want you to read the film as a dramatization of source criticism itself: take seriously Herodotus’s methods (cross-checking oral reports, weighing self-interest, distinguishing direct observation from hearsay) and Thucydides’s stricter demands (preference for contemporary witnesses, exclusion of mythic embellishment, suspicion of narrative shaped for effect). Where does each account fail those tests?

But I want the reading pushed further into the conceptual weeds. Consider the hermeneutic circularity that modern historians face—that we understand any single testimony only by constructing a provisional whole, yet the whole is nothing but the assembled testimonies. Does Rashomon present this circle as vicious, or does the framing—the rain, the ruined gate, the commoner’s cynicism—function as a hidden meta-source that escapes it? I also want you to inject and examine the idea of negative criticism: asking not merely what a source asserts, but what its form, omissions, and internal contradictions make it structurally incapable of recording. Apply that to the woodcutter’s final version, which restores coherence by suppressing certain details; does it actually resolve the evidential problem, or does it merely salvage narrative unity at the cost of suppressing contradictions?

Be faithful in both directions. Do not reduce the film to an illustration of historiography, and do not distort source-criticism concepts to fit the plot. If the film resists the philosophical framework—for instance, by making the pursuit of objective truth appear morally destructive, or by suggesting that every reconstruction is an act of self-justification—say so explicitly. Finally, tell me what Rashomon adds to debates about knowing the distant past that formal source criticism, with its usual assumption that more evidence and stricter method yield closer approximation to truth, tends to miss.
