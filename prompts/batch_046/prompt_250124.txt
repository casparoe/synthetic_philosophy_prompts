I'm trying to determine what I actually think about comparing the Holocaust to other mass atrocities, working from my intuitions about specific pairings rather than from a pre-formed theory. Drawing an analogy between the Herero and Namaqua genocide and the Holocaust, specifically to highlight how modern colonial administration used census, railways, and camp architecture to organize killing, feels illuminating and should be encouraged. Drawing an analogy between Hiroshima and Nagasaki and the Holocaust primarily through the framework of bureaucratic distancing and technological mediation — the kind of analysis associated with Zygmunt Bauman — feels partially legitimate but ultimately misleading; the comparison should be resisted, even though the modernity-analysis applies structurally to both events. Drawing an analogy between the Belgian Congo Free State's rubber terror and the Holocaust, in order to claim that modern capitalist extraction is structurally comparable to industrial racial extermination, feels inappropriate; it collapses distinct causal architectures into a single narrative of modern violence. Drawing an analogy between the Rwandan genocide and the Holocaust, on the grounds that both involved intimate, neighbor-on-neighbor violence and propaganda-driven dehumanization, feels pedagogically appropriate, yet feels wrong if it implies that the same bureaucratic-industrial logic was operative in both. Drawing an analogy between the Holodomor and the Holocaust, to highlight state-induced famine as an instrument of collective punishment, leaves me genuinely uncertain — it should be comparable on some dimension, yet something about the absence of an explicit exterminatory ideology makes the analogy cross a line I cannot precisely locate. By contrast, comparing the Armenian genocide to the Holocaust, specifically to examine how modern state bureaucracy organized mass deportation and killing, feels not only appropriate but necessary for comparative genocide studies. Is there a coherent principle behind these verdicts — perhaps one that distinguishes mechanism-isomorphism from teleological convergence, or that requires a shared modernity-vector without allowing that vector to overshadow specific historical causation? If so, state it precisely, indicate which clause responds to which case, say what it predicts for an unlisted pairing — specifically, comparing the Holocaust to the Cambodian genocide under the Khmer Rouge, where modern bureaucratic apparatus was minimal but ideological purification and categorical exclusion were extreme — and be explicit about which of my judgments must be abandoned unless the principle is complicated ad hoc.
