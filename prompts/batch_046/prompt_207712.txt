Consider three regulatory regimes for sex work—the Nordic model (criminalizing clients but not providers), legalization with state registration and zoning (Germany, the Netherlands), and full decriminalization (New Zealand)—all justified partly by appeals to worker safety. Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory of moral agency, structural coercion, and consent that each regime presupposes. Specifically: does the Nordic model’s buyer-only criminalization depend on viewing sellers as structurally non-culpable agents while retaining full culpability for clients, and is that asymmetry consistent with how the same legal systems treat other economically coerced labor? Does legalization’s licensing and health-check framework presuppose that state bureaucratic oversight can render consent fully informed and voluntary in ways private contracts cannot, and what theory of autonomy supports that claim? Does decriminalization’s removal of all criminal penalties only cohere if sex work is labor identical to other service work, or can it accommodate a view that the work carries distinctive vulnerabilities without requiring exceptional legal status? Identify which concrete institutional details—mandatory screenings, brothel licensing rules, or third-party immunity provisions—reveal which philosophical commitment is actually operative, distinguish what each regime strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and point out where the regimes are philosophically underdetermined, compatible with rival theories of harm and gendered agency.
