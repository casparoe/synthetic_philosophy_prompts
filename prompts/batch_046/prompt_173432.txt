After a long argument with a friend about whether any history can be genuinely objective, I want to test five stances against the standard objections. Please rank the following in tiers by how well each survives both Carr's claim that historical facts are always selected and interpreted by the historian, and White's claim that every narrative necessarily employs a literary emplotment that blocks neutral representation: (1) Ranke's regulative ideal of recovering the past with minimal interpretive distortion; (2) Carr's qualified objectivism, treating objectivity as rigorous intersubjective validation of causal claims rather than mirror-like correspondence; (3) White's constructivism, redefining objectivity as internal coherence among narrative elements; (4) archival minimalism, the view that objectivity applies only to discrete documentary attestations and vanishes once they are woven into causal or narrative structures; (5) emplotment pluralism, the view that while all histories use narrative forms, some emplotments are more tightly constrained by the archival record than others, permitting comparative judgments of fidelity across rival accounts of the same events. For each, assign a tier—A (survives both critiques intact), B (survives one but is severely weakened by the other), C (fails both yet remains conceptually interesting), or D (internally incoherent)—and in one sentence say whether that stance can support a meaningful distinction between a good and a bad history of the late Roman Republic's political collapse. Keep it focused: I need the tier list with reasoning, not a bibliography.
