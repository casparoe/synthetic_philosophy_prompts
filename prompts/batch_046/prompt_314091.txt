I'm preparing a recorded colloquium Q&A for my historiography seminar's podcast series. The guest is an unnamed defender of a view I will call "diagnostic presentism": the claim that moral historians should stop trying to bracket their own normative commitments and instead treat present moral categories as deliberately calibrated instruments—using them not to condemn the past anachronistically, but to render visible the structural moral blind spots that past agents themselves could not articulate. She holds that this is different from ordinary presentism because it requires historians to document precisely where past normative frameworks diverge from ours and to explain why those divergences were functional for the societies that sustained them, rather than simply labeling them wrong. I need a sequence of interview questions that takes this position seriously, anticipates the standard replies she is likely to deploy, and makes each reply harder to reuse. Please structure your response as follows:

1. Brief framing of the interviewee's position and the stakes (~150 words)
2. Opening movement: three questions that establish the core thesis without letting her retreat to generalities (~200 words)
3. Second movement: four questions that anticipate her likely deflections—especially appeals to the hermeneutic circle, to the inevitability of presentist bias, or to the claim that all historiography is already normative—and include the specific follow-up that forces her to choose between her view and the deflection (~250 words)
4. Third movement: two questions on method—how she distinguishes diagnostic presentism from moralizing history, and what empirical or archival finding would actually undermine her approach rather than merely complicate it (~150 words)
5. Closing question: one final question designed so that if she answers with "that's a research program, not an objection" or an equivalent maneuver, the answer will visibly fail to address what was asked (~100 words)

The questions should turn on actual content: how she handles cases where present categories map poorly onto past practices, whether her method collapses into justification rather than explanation, and what would count as evidence that the past had genuinely incommensurable moral reasons rather than merely different ones. Do not include generic biographical questions or requests for reading lists.
