I am working on a feature about how forecasters and researchers negotiate confidence in their own judgments, and a philosopher I interviewed walked me into an argument that now strikes me as suspicious precisely because its conclusion seems to erase the very practice it defends. Here is the chain he laid out. (1) Epistemic modesty—taking the outside view—requires that whenever I form a belief B through some reasoning process R, I should reduce my confidence in B by the historical error rate of R within my appropriate reference class. (2) The belief that I ought to do this—the Modesty Principle—is itself the product of reasoning, specifically meta-reasoning about reference-class calibration, base-rate neglect, and second-order uncertainty; call that process M. (3) So, if (1) is applied without exemption, I must reduce my confidence in the Modesty Principle by the base-rate failure frequency of M among agents like me. (4) The available evidence on calibration suggests that second-order judgments about one's own reasoning are often less well calibrated than first-order judgments, which implies a severe discount factor for M—potentially driving confidence in the principle itself toward zero. (5) But if my confidence in the Modesty Principle is near zero, I lack any normative ground to apply the outside-view correction to B in the first place. (6) Therefore I face a forced choice: either I apply the principle inconsistently—correcting object-level beliefs while shielding the principle from correction—or I apply it consistently and it cancels its own authority, leaving pure inside-view reliance as the only stable position. (7) Hence consistent epistemic modesty is self-defeating. I want to preserve the core intuition behind (1) and the discomfort with (7), but I need to know which link to break and at what cost—whether it requires carving out a special exemption for normative epistemic principles, rejecting the universal applicability of reference-class reasoning, or accepting a more radical revision to how graded confidence works. Please tell me which step fails and why it looked plausible, then say what would change your mind: which specific distinction or empirical consideration—perhaps between empirical prediction and normative commitment, or a result about meta-calibration—would lead you to a different verdict, and flag the point in your reasoning that is most likely to be wrong.
