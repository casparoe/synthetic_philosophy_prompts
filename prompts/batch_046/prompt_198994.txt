I am drafting a novel set in a near-future reproductive medicine clinic that offers what it calls counterfactual origin profiling. Before conception, prospective parents are shown simulated developmental trajectories—not merely genetic risk profiles, but predictive psychological portraits—of the child that would result from several possible sperm-and-egg pairings, including combinations with donors they did not select. The clinic's ethical charter insists that these alternatives are non-actual possibilities with no bearing on personal identity: the child who actually comes to be is said to have an essential origin in the specific gametes that formed them, yet the clinic simultaneously treats adoption, surrogacy, and embryo donation as fully constitutive of parenthood, severing legal and social identity from genetic provenance without any philosophical explanation for why the gametes matter in one register but not in another. The staff psychologists further maintain that knowing one's counterfactual siblings—the simulated personalities tied to unchosen gametes—helps parents accept the child they do have, which seems to treat the actual child as substitutable among possible outcomes while also claiming they could not have originated otherwise.

What implicit view of origin essentialism does this institutional practice presuppose? Reconstruct the philosophical commitments that would make it coherent to hold that a person is essentially tied to the specific sperm and egg that formed them, while also holding that identity survives legal severance from those origins and that the parents might have received a different, equally valid child had they chosen differently. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accommodates it, and identify where its parts pull in opposite directions—particularly between the medical-genetic register, where origin appears fixed and identity-constituting, and the social-legal register, where it is treated as contingent and transferable. If the underlying view were replaced with a thoroughly anti-essentialist account of personal origins, what specific elements of the clinic's counseling protocols, consent forms, and family-law coordination would have to change, and which parts of the practice could remain intact?
