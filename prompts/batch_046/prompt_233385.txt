Several liberal welfare states select immigrants through points-based or employer-tied systems that explicitly weight projected fiscal contribution, yet grant admitted immigrants—including family-reunification dependents with initially low earnings—full access to universal healthcare, education, and income support. At the same time, these states impose mandatory language and civic-integration courses as conditions for permanent residency, obligations that do not apply to native-born citizens with comparable civic or linguistic deficits. What implicit theory of distributive justice and political membership makes this combination coherent? Reconstruct the view that allows fiscal contribution to govern entry but denies it internal standing, and that treats integration as a conditional burden for newcomers alone. Is the practice strictly committed to a two-tier conception—economic selectors at the border, equal moral persons inside—or is it compatible with a uniform social-contract or cosmopolitan view? Take a definite position: either defend the coherence of this design by identifying its necessary philosophical commitments, or argue that it presupposes an unstated distinction it cannot justify, and specify which institutional elements—selection metrics, benefit rules, or integration conditions—would have to change if that distinction were rejected.
