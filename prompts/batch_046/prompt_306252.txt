Having spent my professional life measuring decoherence times in superconducting qubits, I have recently turned to conceptual questions that seem resistant to laboratory resolution—specifically, whether so-called white lies are genuinely wrong, or whether they represent a structurally necessary calibration between what is said and what must be preserved in high-context social coordination. In a recent conversation with a colleague who studies linguistic pragmatics, I was struck by the possibility that Dutch directness and Japanese indirectness are not merely cultural preferences but instantiate different ontological commitments about whether an interlocutor’s autonomy is best protected by explicit disclosure or by the avoidance of imposition. I am convening a single ninety-minute workshop for approximately fourteen graduate students in philosophy and anthropology, and I want them to experience this tension from the inside, not through argument but through structured interaction.

Please design an in-class role-play or negotiation game with explicit rules, assigned communicative norms, and hidden roles: divide the participants into a low-context subgroup that must use only literal, direct assertions to complete a collective resource-allocation task, and a high-context subgroup that must preserve relational harmony by using calibrated indirectness, including a specific refusal protocol in which negative responses are conveyed through positive-form statements that require interpretive inference rather than explicit negation. The payoff scheme should not involve real money but should use reputation tokens and joint-completion points arranged so that literal truth-telling in the high-context condition collapses cooperative success, while strategic misalignment in the low-context condition generates a visible moral cost registered in the token ledger. Include the materials needed, the sequence of rounds, and a mechanism—perhaps a hidden face-cost tracker—that makes the price of direct refusal tangible within the game’s incentives rather than abstract.

After describing the rules, provide a debrief that maps specific moments of gameplay onto the conceptual distinction between propositional content and illocutionary achievement, and between protecting an interlocutor’s autonomy from imposition versus affirming their relational value; be frank about which feature of the debate—likely the claim that indirect refusal is not universal politeness but a specific indexical order tied to a relational ontology—this simulation cannot reproduce and must instead be supplied by the facilitator. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, perhaps an over-identification of the directness subgroup with moral virtue or a failure by participants to recognize that the high-context protocol is not deception but cooperative calibration, and explain how to handle those failures without undermining the activity’s force. I am especially interested in whether the design can make participants feel the asymmetry between judging a lie wrong because it misrepresents reality and judging it wrong because it violates a duty of disclosure, without my having to lecture on that distinction beforehand.
