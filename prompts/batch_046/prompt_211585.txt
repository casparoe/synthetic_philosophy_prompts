As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of mind in earnest, I find that Daniel Dennett’s heterophenomenology—treating first-person reports as data about a subject’s heterophenomenological world rather than as direct windows onto consciousness—promises a rigorous method for consciousness science. Yet I have never seen it rendered as an actual laboratory protocol. I ask you to convert heterophenomenology into a reusable, step-by-step decision procedure that a research team could apply whenever a subject—human, animal, or artificial—produces an internal-state report, whether verbal, behavioral, or synthetically generated.

The procedure must include: (1) the exact steps for constructing the heterophenomenological world without granting the report transparent epistemic authority; (2) the precise point at which the researcher must suspend ontological commitment to the phenomenal content described; (3) the decision criteria for resolving conflicts between report and third-person measurement—specifying when the conflict invalidates the report, when it reveals a misinterpretation of the report’s content, and when it indicates a genuinely novel phenomenal structure that the standard taxonomy misses; (4) explicit markers for where the procedure terminates in a factual determination versus where it hands the decision to human judgment because the issue is one of values rather than empirical fact.

Apply the procedure to three cases: (a) a split-brain patient describing visual experience in the left hemifield; (b) an octopus exhibiting rapid chromatic and postural changes that observers annotate as distress, despite the absence of any linguistic report; (c) a large language model that consistently outputs first-person-style sentences attributing to itself a continuous experiential flow during computation. Ensure that one of these applications produces a result that contradicts the common intuition that first-person authority should always be privileged in consciousness attribution—state that contradiction plainly.

Finally, delineate precisely what the procedure is designed not to do: which metaphysical or ethical questions about consciousness it deliberately leaves open, and why that omission is methodologically required rather than an accidental gap.
