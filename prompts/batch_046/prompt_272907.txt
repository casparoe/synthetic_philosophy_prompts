I'm a doctoral student in normative ethics, and in my advanced seminar on consequentialism we've reached an impasse that feels structurally familiar. The problem is consequentialist cluelessness: we generally lack the predictive capacity to know the long-run outcomes of our actions, yet consequentialism seems to make the deontic status of an act depend on those very outcomes. The standard responses—appeals to expected utility, the claim that all moral theories face parallel epistemic limits, and the distinction between objective and subjective rightness—have been rehearsed exhaustively. What stalls us is not disagreement about the severity of predictive ignorance, but a deeper divergence about whether moral reasons must be accessible to the deliberating agent. The consequentialists in the room treat reasons as world-guided: if an act produces better outcomes, there is a binding reason for it regardless of whether the agent can access that fact. The non-consequentialists treat reasons as agent-guided: a consideration can only guide action if it falls within the agent's epistemic reach. Both sides agree on every empirical premise about forecasting failure, yet neither has moved.

My working hypothesis is that the dispute is not primarily epistemological, but concerns what I would call the transmission structure of normative authority—whether a reason binds an agent by virtue of being true in the world, or only by virtue of being available in deliberation. If that is right, the disagreement may be substantive but hidden beneath shared vocabulary: both camps say "cluelessness," yet one refers to predictive failure and the other to normative inaccessibility. However, I also wonder whether a masked empirical disagreement is doing hidden work—specifically, about whether complex social systems are in principle forecastable at the level of granularity that consequentialist evaluation demands, or whether that granularity is a philosophical fiction. So I want a diagnosis rather than an adjudication: is this a verbal dispute about "reasons" and "guidance," a substantive metaphysical disagreement about how normative properties relate to agents, or bedrock intuitions about epistemic authority that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would actually constitute progress—an empirical result about long-range forecasting, a conceptual distinction that disentangles the two senses of cluelessness, or should we conclude that the framing itself is ill-posed?
