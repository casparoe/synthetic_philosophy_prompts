Here is the exchange as it occurred.

Maya began by laying out the argument in standard form. From the knowability principle—that for any true proposition p it is possible that p is known—together with the factivity of knowledge and the distribution of knowledge over conjunction, one derives that every true proposition is actually known. She took this omniscience result to be reductio ad absurdum and concluded that the knowability principle itself must be abandoned. She added that attempts to preserve the principle by weakening auxiliary premises usually look ad hoc.

Jonas granted the formal validity but argued that the real pressure point is the distribution principle. Knowing that p and q together, he said, does not automatically entail knowing p and knowing q separately, especially when the conjunction involves self-reference about ignorance. In the paradoxical construction—where one considers the conjunction of p with the claim that p is unknown—the content of the conjunction undermines the stability conditions required for knowing either conjunct. Thus the contradiction does not demonstrate that p is unknowable in general; it demonstrates only that self-undermining epistemic states cannot be knowledge states, which is already guaranteed by factivity.

Maya responded that Jonas had not rescued the principle but had restricted it. By exempting conjunctions that entail their own unknowability, she said, he introduces an implicit qualification without supplying a principled, non-circular criterion for which truths fall under the exemption. She pressed him on ordinary cases as well: if knowledge fails to distribute in this special case, what justifies trusting distribution elsewhere? She concluded that his move is patching the derivation precisely where it fails.

Jonas then proposed that the deeper mistake lies in the formalization of the modal operator itself. If the possibility in the knowability principle is interpreted not as bare logical possibility but as cognitive accessibility for a finite agent, then the premise that the self-undermining conjunction is accessible collapses. No stable cognitive architecture could maintain a state that asserts p while simultaneously representing that p is unknown. The contradiction therefore marks a boundary of cognitive feasibility rather than a failure of epistemic transparency. On this view, the knowability principle survives intact, but only when qualified by what is cognitively realizable.

Maya objected that this imports empirical psychology into a logical argument. Fitch's derivation, she noted, operates with normative and modal premises, not with claims about brains or computation. If whether a truth is knowable depends on contingent mental architecture, then the principle no longer expresses a fundamental relationship between truth and knowledge; it expresses a contingent limitation. She argued that Jonas has replaced a logical thesis with a psychological constraint, thereby sacrificing the normative ambition that made the knowability principle philosophically interesting.
