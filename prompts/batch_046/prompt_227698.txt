I am grading exam responses for a graduate-level seminar on the philosophy of emotion and art. The exam asked students to address the paradox of tragedy—specifically, why rational audiences seek out artworks that deliberately evoke grief, terror, or despair, emotions they systematically avoid in practical life. The prompt required students to present two competing explanatory strategies: first, an account according to which negative affect is aesthetically transfigured through a framing or distancing mechanism (sometimes characterized as meta-emotional purification or cathartic restructuring); second, an account according to which the negative affect remains genuinely painful but is compensated, integrated, or outweighed by independent cognitive, formal, or moral rewards. Students were then asked to evaluate whether either strategy genuinely dissolves the paradox or merely relocates it, and to identify any shared hidden premise about the structure of emotional experience that both might tacitly assume.

Please evaluate the following student answer. Assign a specific letter grade (A through F, with plus or minus distinctions if warranted) and justify it through point-by-point comments. Your assessment should distinguish clearly between what the answer gets right about the visible architecture of the debate—its characterization of the paradox, its mapping of the two strategies, and its identification of a common underlying commitment—and where it makes subtle but consequential errors: a misattributed view, a conflation between distinct mechanisms, or an argumentative step that does not actually follow from the premises offered. Do not provide only a general impression; I need a granular evaluation that an instructor could use directly for feedback. The student is not being tested on historical scholarship per se, but on conceptual precision, so penalize conceptual slippage even when the surface narrative sounds plausible.

Student answer:

The paradox of tragedy is that people enjoy watching suffering. One answer, linked to Schopenhauer, says that tragedy lets us step outside ourselves and see human pain as part of a universal aesthetic pattern. This framing transforms raw grief into a kind of contemplative pleasure, so the emotion we feel is not really negative anymore; the paradox disappears because there is no genuine suffering left in the experience.

The other answer, in the Humean tradition, says that the sorrow is real but is made up for by the pleasure we take in the artist's skill and by the moral understanding we gain. The bad feeling is present but smaller than the total reward, so the choice to watch is rational.

Both of these views, however, depend on the idea that an emotional state must be either positive or negative—unitary and weighable—rather than genuinely ambivalent. If we drop that assumption and accept that we can feel sorrow and enjoyment together without one canceling the other, then neither framing nor compensation is needed. So both accounts fail: they do not dissolve the paradox but only push it into a false psychology of emotion.
