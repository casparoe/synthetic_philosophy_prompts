I sit on the Regional Civic Integration Board, which must issue a binding recommendation by Friday on the proposed Symbolic Concession Accord for the Meridian Commons redevelopment. The site adjoins a secular civic memorial and an active religious sanctuary whose congregations hold structurally incompatible axiologies: Group A regards public religious visibility as a violation of equal citizenship that cannot be compensated, while Group B regards any reduction in sacred presence as an existential loss that material benefits cannot offset. The developers propose segmented spatial zoning with rotating temporal access—neither group’s first best, but structured so that Group A secures extended periods of neutral public space while Group B retains guaranteed sacred-use windows—on the theory that because the two communities rank dimensions of the space differently, a structured exchange of concessions can yield mutual protection of each side’s highest-priority commitments without requiring either to abandon its core normative framework.

Half the board treats this as routine practical compromise; the other half, led by a board member who works on value pluralism, warns that framing the dispute in terms of 'moral exchange rates'—treating symbolic concessions as fungible goods that can be priced and traded—does not respect incommensurability but dissolves it. Their strongest objection is that allowing 'axiological arbitrage,' where a community trades away low-salience commitments to secure high-salience ones, actually exposes minority value systems to majoritarian pricing: once concessions become calculable, the politically weaker group will eventually be compelled to trade away its non-negotiable core for temporary protection of peripheral goods. I need a written opinion recommending a concrete course—approve the accord with binding conditions, reject it outright, or approve a modified version with an enforcement timeline—that engages this objection directly rather than dismissing it, and that addresses whether structured moral compromise genuinely preserves both value systems or merely disguises assimilation under the language of mutual gain.

Please lead with your bottom-line recommendation in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the board’s charge. The memo should be suitable for circulation to members who have not read the philosophical literature on the case, so avoid unnecessary technical jargon, but do not oversimplify the exchange-rate objection.
