I'm taking this philosophy of history course and we're stuck on whether history actually has "hinges" — specific moments where everything swings on one tiny thing — or if thats just a narrative trick we play after the fact. Our prof floated a pretty specific framework: a real hinge needs four conditions to hold. First, there has to be an actual decision node where at least two paths were live options for the actors involved, not just counterfactuals invented later. Second, there needs to be serious path-dependency right after, so the choice actually locks in big outcomes. Third, there should be a retrospective illusion of inevitability, where people later tell the story like the result was obvious all along. And fourth, there's this "compression" effect, where a whole complex period gets collapsed into that one minute or one choice.

But I want to check if this is actually a hinge by those four parts, not just a cool anecdote. So: who are the real actors at the node? What were the live options in that exact room — was there a real path where he reads the memo correctly and the wall stays up for months, or was the Soviet withdrawal and the economic collapse already making the opening inevitable? Does the path-dependency actually hold if we look at the next 48 hours versus the next ten years? And where does the framework force us to pretend there's more contingency than there really was?
