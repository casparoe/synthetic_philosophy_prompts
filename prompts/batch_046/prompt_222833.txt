I'm grading papers for an informal philosophy circle I've been running with people from the rationalist-adjacent community, and I want a careful second opinion before I lock in a final mark. The assignment asked students to reconstruct the Athenian Stranger's argument in the early books of Plato's Laws for instituting regulated symposia—communal drinking parties governed by precise rules—as an instrument of civic education. Specifically, they had to address four things: (1) the psychological mechanism the Stranger thinks alcohol activates; (2) how this relates to Plato's distinction between true and false pleasures elsewhere in the dialogue; (3) whether the symposion is best understood as an early analog to adversarial stress-testing of belief-formation under cognitive load, a framing several of us have been using in our group; and (4) what pedagogical purpose is served when citizens fail the test, and whether direct rational instruction plays any role.

Here is one response. It strikes me as mostly competent, but I suspect it slips in a few subtle places that a hasty grader might miss, and I want point-by-point confirmation.

The Athenian Stranger proposes regulated symposia in Laws I-II because he believes alcohol strips away the social masks people wear, revealing their true moral dispositions. This is essentially continuous with the scene in Plato's Symposium where Alcibiades, after drinking, confesses his hidden desire for Socrates and exposes his own lack of self-control. In both texts, wine functions as a truth-serum that dissolves false beliefs about oneself.

The Stranger's view on pleasure is that it is generally harmful to rational governance, but he carves out an exception for the symposion because the pleasure of wine is uniquely controllable by law. By setting precise rules for how much each age group may drink, the lawgiver turns pleasure into a calibrated stress-test: citizens are placed under cognitive load and observed to see whether their beliefs about justice and moderation survive. This is structurally similar to modern adversarial evaluation, where a system is tested with edge-case inputs to expose hidden failure modes. If a citizen becomes violent or unjust when drunk, the lawgiver has discovered a flaw in that citizen's character that sober reasoning would have concealed.

The pedagogical purpose is therefore diagnostic and exclusionary. Those who fail are identified and removed from positions of authority. Those who pass learn nothing directly; rather, their success demonstrates that their rational beliefs are robust. The Stranger also implies that direct instruction is useless for moral education, since people only learn from observing failures in others. This makes the symposion an anti-rationalist institution: it relies on emotional shock rather than argument.
