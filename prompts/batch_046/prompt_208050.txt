Analyze the 1924 trial of Leopold and Loeb and Clarence Darrow's closing argument as a case study in moral luck. The defendants, both from wealthy Chicago families and students at the University of Chicago, kidnapped and murdered fourteen-year-old Bobby Franks in an attempt to commit the "perfect crime"; at trial, Darrow did not dispute guilt but argued against the death penalty by emphasizing psychological determinism, youth, and an emotionally stunted privileged upbringing—suggesting the defendants were products of forces largely outside their control. Apply the standard taxonomy of moral luck—constitutive luck (the kind of agents they were), circumstantial luck (the contingent presence of a suitable victim and opportunity), and resultant luck (the speed with which the body was discovered and conviction secured)—and introduce a fourth, less-discussed category: comparative moral luck, that is, the differential moral and legal assessment that arises when structurally similar wrong acts receive radically different social responses because of factors such as wealth, media attention, or access to elite counsel, rather than because of differences in the wrongness of the acts themselves. For each category, specify exactly what Darrow's argument claimed, whether it amounts to a denial of moral agency or merely to reduced culpability, and what the judge's decision to impose life imprisonment rather than death reveals about whether the court was implicitly applying a theory of moral luck, making a pragmatic calculation about public reaction, or drawing a principled distinction between moral responsibility and appropriate punishment. Finally, assess whether the episode confirms the common intuition that moral luck should not affect punishment, undermines that intuition, or reveals that the intuition was never consistently applied in practice.
