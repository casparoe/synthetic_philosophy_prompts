As a retired physicist recently drawn into philosophy of epistemology, I am leading a 75-minute graduate seminar of about 14 students. I want them to physically experience the tension between reductionism and anti-reductionism about testimony—specifically the collapse that occurs when every testimonial uptake is treated as requiring an independent, non-testimonial check on the speaker’s reliability, yet that check itself arrives only through further testimony. I have begun calling this an “epistemic impedance mismatch”: the more one tries to filter belief-transmission by reducing it to direct verification, the more noise is introduced, because each filter demands its own filter, until the signal either stalls or is accepted on unexamined trust.

Design an in-class simulation—using only paper roles, message tokens, and a point-based payoff scheme with no real money—in which participants must collectively determine whether a fictional industrial incident (say, a coolant breach) occurred, using only written messages relayed through an assigned chain of informants. Some students should be assigned a reductionist protocol: they may record a belief only if they first “verify” the sender’s credentials, but verification tokens are themselves messages that must be verified in turn. Others should follow an anti-reductionist protocol: they may record beliefs immediately upon receipt, accumulating “trust debt” points that can be suddenly audited by a hidden “corruption” event. Structure the payoffs so that reductionists rationally freeze in recursive credential-checking, while anti-reductionists reach consensus quickly but risk total point loss if a corrupt node was trusted.

Please provide: (1) materials and role assignments; (2) precise rules and the token/payoff scheme; (3) a debrief that maps specific moments—freezing, sudden convergence, audit collapse—onto reductionism, anti-reductionism, and the impedance mismatch; (4) an honest statement of which real feature of testimony this simulation necessarily distorts and how you will supply it verbally; and (5) what will predictably fail the first run and how to correct it. Write no more than approximately 450 words, using clear headings, and do not include bibliographies or citations.
