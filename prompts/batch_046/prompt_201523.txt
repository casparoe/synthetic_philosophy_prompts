I want to close a seminar on suffering-focused ethics with a short poem that takes seriously the core asymmetry of negative utilitarianism: that suffering is unconditionally bad, whereas the absence of pleasure is not a corresponding harm to anyone, and that this creates a tension between the urgency of alleviating existing suffering and the strange neutrality of never having created the sufferer in the first place. The poem should be exactly fifteen lines, arranged in three quintains with an ABCBA rhyme scheme in each, so that the enclosing rhymes (A and B) stand for the two poles—suffering and non-existence—while the central rhyme (C) holds the tension without resolving it. The middle line of each quintain must include either "absence" or "void." Between the second and third quintain there should be a volta that shifts from the reality of present suffering to the logical implication that preventing existence prevents harm only by eliminating the subject, not by conferring any good. The tone should be austere and unresolved; do not turn it into an argument for anti-natalism or for resignation. Let the form do the philosophical work: the rhyme scheme should feel like two voices that keep returning to each other but never merge, leaving the question of priority hanging in the final lines.
