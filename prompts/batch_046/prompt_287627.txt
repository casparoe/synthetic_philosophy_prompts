I'm giving a seminar next tuesday at the LSE development studies group — mostly institutional economists and one legal anthropologist who's actually sympathetic — defending the idea that our standard Western corruption metrics misclassify a lot of what happens in, say, rural Ghana or parts of Indonesia. My claim is that clientelism and ceremonial gift exchange aren't just corruption with local colour; they operate on a different logic of obligation that's not reducible to bribery, but I still want to hold the line that there IS a real distinction between reciprocity and outright extraction, and that Western frameworks aren't useless, just overextended.

The audience is about eight people: five economists who think culture is mostly a residual category, two lawyers who work on anti-corruption treaties, and the one anthropologist. They care about measurable outcomes, they worry about relativism being used to excuse kleptocracy, and they've definatly read more on patron-client networks than I have.

Can you draft my Q&A prep sheet? I want the objections ordered by how much damage they'd actually do — not generic philosophy objections, but the ones these specific people will lob. Each one should be phrased the way an institutional economist or a treaty lawyer would actually say it. For each: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, plus an honest note on where my reply is soft or concedes too much. Include the one question I'm praying nobody asks — probably something about whether I'm just giving cover to authoritarians, but I want it phrased precisely and with a reply that doesn't sound defensive.

Oh, and here's the argument sketch I want them to target: I distinguish three layers — (1) the act itself (giving a goat after a favour), (2) the social expectation of return (is it enforced by social shame or by state-backed law?), and (3) whether the exchange undermines public goods or just redistributes within a private network. I say corruption only happens when all three align in a particular destructive way, not when layer one is present alone.
