I work in software engineering with no formal philosophy background, and I have been trying to apply decision-theoretic ideas to deployment pipelines. I understand Good's theorem to say that, if you face a choice and can acquire further information at zero cost before deciding, then gathering that information cannot reduce your expected utility—it can only help or leave it unchanged.

This seems to push toward an absurd conclusion about engineering practice. Here is the argument I built:

(1) Good's theorem applies whenever an information-gathering step is genuinely free—no resource expenditure, no delay to the decision, and full availability of results before the choice is made.
(2) Before deploying a feature or selecting an architecture, we can almost always run a shadow deployment, a synthetic load test, or an additional simulation on spare compute.
(3) If these tests are arranged to use idle resources and not block release timelines, then by (1) they are rationally mandatory: we should always run them before committing.
(4) The same reasoning applies to the meta-decision about whether to run the test: we could gather information on whether the shadow deployment itself will yield useful data, and if that meta-inquiry is also free, we must perform it too.
(5) So rationality demands an infinite regress of pre-deployment information gathering.
(6) But actual engineering practice relies on bounded testing and heuristic shipping; no team accepts infinite pre-release probing.
(7) Therefore either Good's theorem is false, or every practical deployment decision is systematically irrational.

I am inclined to defend (1) and (6), but I cannot locate the slip between them. Is there an equivocation between "free" in an economic sense and "free" in the decision-theoretic sense required by the theorem? Does (4) misapply the theorem by treating the value of information about information as always non-negative in the same way? Or does the regress fail because at some level the prior is already sharp enough that new data has zero expected value? Please identify the faulty step, explain why it initially looked sound, and say what giving it up costs—particularly whether it forces us to admit that "costless" information is impossible in any real decision architecture.
