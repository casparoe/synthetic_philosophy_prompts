I hold that weakness of will—acting against one's own acknowledged better judgment—requires no separate failure of practical rationality; hyperbolic discounting explains it fully, since steep near-term weighting makes preference reversal predictable rather than paradoxical. I worry, though, that I may be treating a descriptive intertemporal-choice model as if it dissolved a normative problem. What would genuinely count against this reduction? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward: empirical findings that agents with flat or exponential discount curves still show classic akratic patterns; conceptual arguments that hyperbolic discounting cannot distinguish rational preference change from irrational reversal without importing the normative standard it claims to avoid; and cases of structured precommitment failure, where an agent deliberately dismantles a self-binding mechanism at the decisive moment with no shift in discount rates, seeming to require a volitional breakdown the model cannot capture. Which of these would defenders of the reduction concede as damaging, and which can be absorbed by refining the discount function or redefining better judgment? Also assess whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—e.g., by treating every counterexample as an unmodeled interaction of curves with context—and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a view of this kind. Say exactly what evidence or argument would change your mind about whether hyperbolic discounting genuinely explains akrasia or merely redescribes it, and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
