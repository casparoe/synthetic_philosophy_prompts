Two acquaintances of mine have been arguing about whether failing to act can ever be a genuine cause of harm, and they asked me to referee. The case they keep returning to is this: a municipal council votes not to repair a broken streetlamp on Oak Street, and two weeks later a pedestrian is assaulted there in the dark. Jordan says this is a straightforward case of causation by omission—if the council had funded the repair, the assault likely would not have happened, so their failure is part of the causal explanation. Riley objects that causation requires a positive event, force, or change; since the council literally did nothing, there is no causal link, only a background condition (the darkness) and the assailant's own action. Jordan replies that we routinely blame omissions—parents who fail to feed a child are said to cause starvation—so treating omissions as non-causal would break ordinary practices of moral and legal responsibility. Riley answers that in those cases we are really blaming a breach of duty, while the physical harm is caused by whatever process actually occurred; the omission explains why no one intervened, not why the harm came about. Jordan then raises what he calls the baseline problem: whether something counts as an omission depends on what we take to be the normal or expected state of affairs, but those expectations seem to be shaped in part by the very causal roles we are trying to assign, so defining causation without reference to omissions risks circularity. Riley concedes that this is a real puzzle for our language and for moral judgment, but insists it is a problem about norms and descriptions, not about the metaphysics of the world—we can keep all our practices of blame without pretending that an absence is a kind of event.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: rate Jordan's and Riley's specific arguments from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and say who is more right overall. Also, please separate what is settled in the philosophical and legal literature on omission and causation from what remains genuinely contested, and avoid presenting any one contested view as the consensus.
