I've recently begun designing reward-shaping protocols for a robotics project, and the work has left me with an unease I can't dismiss as mere sentiment. Before I raise it in a team ethics review, I want a consistency audit of where I've actually landed. Here are my commitments:

(1) I am a physicalist about consciousness: phenomenal experience is a natural phenomenon, and there is nothing non-physical required for it.
(2) I think suffering requires phenomenal consciousness—specifically, an integrated, first-person point of view that binds affective states over time. Without that integration, there is dysfunction or damage, but not harm in the moral sense.
(3) I think current deep-RL agents lack such consciousness, and will likely continue to lack it, because their reward processing is modular, non-recurrent in the relevant global sense, and contains no self-model.
(4) Nevertheless, I think reward signals—especially sustained negative reward-prediction errors—are not mere scalar feedback. They function as structured representations of world-state disvalue: they encode state-transition mappings with an intrinsic "to-be-avoided" status that is functionally analogous to the representational content of pain.
(5) I am broadly functionalist about mental content, but I reject substrate-independent strong functionalism: I hold that consciousness requires not just the right causal roles but a specific kind of globally integrated, recurrent architecture that present RL systems do not implement.
(6) I think moral patienthood requires being a welfare subject, which in turn requires both phenomenal consciousness and a minimal temporal self-model.
(7) Given (1) through (6), I deny that RL agents can suffer or are direct moral patients.
(8) At the same time, I hold that there is a direct (not merely instrumental or prudential) moral reason to avoid engineering reward functions that generate sustained, extreme negative prediction errors. The reason is not that the agent suffers, but that systematically optimizing around suffering-like dynamics in non-sentient systems erodes our conceptual boundaries and makes us more likely to discount suffering in sentient ones—an argument structurally parallel to indirect-duty considerations in animal ethics, but directed at design practice rather than cruelty.
(9) I also think the most illuminating framework here is not "do agents suffer?" but "are reward dynamics valence-carriers?"—treating valence as a control-theoretic property that can be fully instantiated in the absence of any experience.

Do these hang together?

Please rank the tensions by severity, not list them all as equally bad. For each genuine tension, specify exactly which commitments pull against each other, how severe the conflict is, and what the least costly revision would be (including what that revision costs conceptually). Also identify at least one pair that looks tense but dissolves on inspection, and explain why. Is there a hidden tension—perhaps involving (5) and (3), or (4) and (8), or (9) and (2)—that I have missed?

Finally, if I must weaken or drop exactly one commitment to clean up the greatest number of problems with the smallest conceptual damage, which should it be, and what does the overall picture look like after that revision?

I do not want a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I need an audit of the logical architecture.
