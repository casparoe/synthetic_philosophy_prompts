I am writing an essay for my philosophy of life seminar, which this term has focused on absurdism and the recent analytic revival of questions about meaning in life. Our discussions have centered on Camus’s diagnosis of the absurd—human beings’ persistent search for transcendent purpose in an indifferent universe—and on contemporary hybrid accounts, especially Susan Wolf’s claim that a meaningful life requires both subjective attraction and objective value, and Thaddeus Metz’s fundamentality-based view that meaning arises from rational engagement with conditions that are themselves non-derivatively valuable. I want to apply these frameworks not to a standard philosophical example but to Samuel Beckett’s Waiting for Godot, a play I first encountered outside the course and now find puzzling in precisely philosophical terms. Specifically, I am asking for a critical reading that treats the play as a serious interlocutor rather than an illustration: does the cyclical, seemingly static existence of Vladimir and Estragon—marked by rituals like the boot inspection, the hat exchange, and the nightly wait for a messenger who never delivers—represent a form of meaning that survives the absence of Godot, or does Beckett dramatize the failure of both subjective attachment and objective value? I also want the reading to engage a somewhat more niche idea that has come up in recent discussions: the narrative-coherence requirement, according to which meaning depends not merely on discrete valuable activities but on a diachronic structure that integrates them into an intelligible life story. Given that the two acts of Godot are almost identical and that the characters seem to lack any developmental arc, does the play deliberately deny them such coherence, and if so, does that expose a hidden teleological assumption in Wolf’s and Metz’s accounts?

Please point to specific scenes—the tree’s sudden blooming, Pozzo and Lucky’s relationship, the boy’s repeated message—to show where the philosophical lens illuminates the drama and where the drama resists it. I am not looking for a summary of the plot or for a bibliography; I want an argument that takes both Beckett and the philosophers seriously, noting what the work adds to the debate that pure argument cannot capture. Lead with your bottom-line judgment in the first sentence—stating clearly whether Godot ultimately vindicates, undermines, or transforms these accounts of meaning—followed by the reasoning and textual support, with no preamble and without restating the question.
