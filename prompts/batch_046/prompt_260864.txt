I have been thinking about the old philosophical idea that if two objects share every single property—shape, size, material, and even all of their relations to other things—then they are not genuinely two objects at all, but one and the same thing. The familiar counterexample imagines two perfectly identical spheres suspended in an otherwise empty universe, distinguishable only by occupying different positions. A defender of the identity principle might reply that being-to-the-left-of-the-other is itself a relational property, so the spheres are not truly indiscernible after all. I want to push this further with a twist I have not seen discussed in the usual treatments. Suppose the two spheres do not merely sit apart, but move in perfect lockstep, maintaining exactly the same distances and directional relations to each other and to any hypothetical coordinate system at every instant. Now imagine a single observer who can only ever encounter one sphere at a time—never both simultaneously—and for whom there exists no external landmark, background feature, or frame of reference whatsoever. In this setting, is there any meaningful difference between saying "there are two spheres, each observed once in sequence" and saying "there is only one sphere, observed twice"? Please compare how a strict defender of the identity of indiscernibles and a defender of primitive thisness—the view that an object can carry a bare individual essence entirely independent of all its properties and relations—would each analyze this sequential-observation scenario. Which of the two positions offers the more consistent account, and does the lockstep-motion condition genuinely undermine the relational-property defense, or does it merely relocate the problem to the observer's limited access?
