I am preparing the final module for my graduate seminar on rationalist ethics, and I want to move the discussion past the conventional summary of Leibniz's view—that justice is caritas sapientis directed toward the common good, and that moral truths are eternal verities of reason rather than contingent facts. To that end, I have been experimenting with a framing that binds Leibniz's metaphysics of expression directly to his moral theory: if each monad expresses the entire universe from an irreducibly singular perspective, and if pre-established harmony coordinates these perspectives without causal interaction, then the exercise of justice might be reconceived not as the adoption of a single universal viewpoint but as the calibration of one's partial expression so that it maximally contributes to the overall harmony of perfections. Given this framing, please describe the most important implications for Leibniz's account of moral disagreement, moral error, and the very possibility of moral progress. In particular, I want you to consider whether two rational agents with genuinely divergent moral judgments can both be expressing valid partial perspectives of a single moral reality—analogous to different geometric projections of one object—or whether divergence necessarily indicates a failure of charity or wisdom on at least one side. I also want you to assess whether this expressive model strengthens or undermines the normative authority of moral reasoning: does it risk reducing moral prescription to a descriptive account of how perspectives happen to align, thereby dissolving the critical force that allows Leibniz to condemn tyranny and injustice? Finally, rank these implications in terms of how damaging they are to the coherence of his broader rationalist ethics, distinguishing those that a committed Leibnizian could absorb through qualification from those that threaten to collapse his moral theory into a form of metaphysical determinism that leaves no space for genuinely normative judgment.
