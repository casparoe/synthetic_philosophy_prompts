I'm taking a seminar on nineteenth-century existentialism, and my friends and I have been arguing about whether Kierkegaard's critique of the press and the public has any purchase on today's algorithmic media environment. We understand his basic worry: the press creates an abstract, irresponsible crowd—the public—that lacks the passionate commitment of the individual, and that this leveling reduces qualitative distinctions to quantitative noise. But we want to push this further into territory that didn't exist in his time. Specifically, imagine a social-media ecosystem in which engagement is optimized not for human readers but for predictive behavioral profiles, and in which a substantial share of discourse is generated by automated agents that simulate user preferences without any inner life or commitment whatsoever. These agents don't form a crowd in the traditional sense; they are distributed, non-conscious proxies that collectively produce the appearance of public opinion.

Given this framing, what do you think Kierkegaard would say about such an environment? In particular, how might he extend his concepts of the public as a phantom, of leveling, and of the press's dissolution of individual responsibility to a system where the speakers are literally not persons and the audience is partly a statistical construct? Please be careful to distinguish between what is well-established in Kierkegaard's texts—such as his contrast between the reflective, disinterested present age and the passionate age of revolution, or his claim that the public is an abstraction allowing everyone to hold an opinion without being committed to it—and what is genuinely speculative extrapolation to algorithmic culture. Also, please note any points where Kierkegaard scholars genuinely disagree about the scope of his critique, and do not present one contested reading as if it were the consensus view. Write your answer in American English.
