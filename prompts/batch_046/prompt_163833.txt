I serve as the graduate student representative on our university’s Interdisciplinary Research Funding Board, which oversees grants at the intersection of cognitive science, philosophy of mind, and machine learning. At our last meeting, a senior board member proposed that we adopt a strict falsifiability criterion as a demarcation rule for all applications in consciousness studies and AI interpretability: any proposal whose central hypothesis can be protected by auxiliary adjustments—what the proposer terms immunizing stratagems—would be automatically ineligible. The motion was prompted by two pending applications: one that uses first-person phenomenal reports shielded by ad hoc appeals to access-interference effects, and another that calibrates emergent-capacity metrics in large language models by shifting evaluation thresholds after each model release. The board is divided between those who see this as necessary methodological hygiene and those who fear it will exclude legitimate theoretical work that proceeds by model refinement rather than by single-shot predictive risk.

For Thursday’s decision session, I need to circulate a formal memo recommending a concrete course of action—adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions—together with a clear time frame for implementation or review. In drafting it, I want to apply a distinction I have been developing in my own dissertation work: rather than treating falsifiability as a binary property of a hypothesis, we should assess what I call tiered predictive vulnerability, separating (i) core theoretical commitments that must be directly exposed to empirical risk from (ii) auxiliary modeling choices that are legitimately revised when instruments or architectures change. This framework seems particularly apt here. The consciousness proposal’s core claim—that there exists a specific neural correlate of phenomenal integration—does carry empirical risk, whereas its protective moves occur at the level of measurement protocol; conversely, the AI proposal’s central claim about emergent interpretability appears to be constituted almost entirely by its auxiliary calibration rules, with no independent predictive anchor that survives revision of those rules. The memo should recommend whether our board pilots this tiered assessment for the current funding cycle, reports back in six months, or rejects the proposal outright in favor of either the blanket criterion or the status quo.

Please write the memo I will circulate, addressed formally to the board. It should contain an explicit recommendation with a specific time frame—say, piloting the tiered framework for this cycle with a six-month review, or adopting or rejecting the blanket rule immediately. The reasoning must engage the philosophical demarcation problem seriously, applying the distinction above to both pending applications so reviewers know exactly what criteria to use. Crucially, it must treat honestly the strongest consideration on the opposing side: that without a hard falsifiability line, we risk funding pseudoscientific programs that endlessly accommodate anomalous data, and that theoretical sophistication should not become a license for unfalsifiability. Do not dismiss that concern; show why the tiered framework addresses it rather than evading it, or concede the point if the recommendation is to reject the tiered approach. The document should be actionable, polished, and suitable for circulation before Thursday’s meeting.
