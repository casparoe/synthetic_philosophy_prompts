I want to know whether resolving this changes how you would architect a pluralistic forum, or whether it is a conceptual puzzle with no institutional footprint.

Second, the compositional-stability problem: Rawls treats an overlapping consensus as something that can stabilize a society made of diverse, often incompatible worldviews. But there is an open line of work about whether this stability is compositional—whether it holds when interaction happens through mediated interfaces, algorithms, representatives, or protocols rather than through direct deliberation among citizens who share a common democratic culture. Does the consensus scale like a modular system with clean interfaces between subsystems, or does it require a shared base layer? I want to know if this is a real philosophical gap or just me projecting software architecture onto politics.

Third, the exclusion-boundary problem: There is debate over whether doctrines that reject democratic authority are outside the consensus by definition, or whether Rawls's framework can accommodate them only as a stable modus vivendi rather than a genuine consensus. The open question is whether drawing this boundary is a normative judgment disguised as a conceptual one, and what criteria should govern it.

Of these three clusters, which open questions and implications should I treat as genuinely upstream—meaning that answering them would reshape how I think about building pluralistic institutions—versus which are secondary, do not actually follow from Rawls, or are overrepresented in the literature relative to their practical weight?
