I have recently been appointed to chair the oversight panel for a long-running clinical registry, and I am struggling with a case that has forced me to reconsider what it genuinely means to know a procedure rather than merely to have internalised it. One of our senior coordinators, who helped design the registry protocol fifteen years ago, has begun to approve small but material deviations—accepting incomplete consent documentation, for instance—without any conscious awareness that she has done so, catching herself only after the fact. She defends her competence by appealing to the absent-minded driver: she knows every turn of the protocol, she argues, just as the driver knows the route; the lapse is purely in attention, not in knowledge. I am not convinced this analogy protects her professional standing, but I need help seeing exactly where the philosophical fault line lies. Could you advise me, in British English, on whether the absent-minded driver problem actually supports her claim that full procedural knowledge can coexist with systematic failures of application, or whether it instead demonstrates that a particular kind of self-monitoring awareness is constitutive of knowing-how in institutional contexts? I am especially interested in whether there is a meaningful distinction between possessing a capacity, being able to exercise it automatically, and being able to govern its exercise reflectively—and whether losing the last of these amounts to a loss of knowledge proper, not merely a lapse of attention.
