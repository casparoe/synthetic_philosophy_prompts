I'm working through the lottery paradox and the preface paradox in a philosophy of science seminar, and I think I have identified a unifying structure, though I'm unsure where the reasoning breaks down. In both cases, an agent seems rationally entitled to high confidence in each individual claim within a large set—each specific lottery ticket failing to win, each particular claim in a scholarly monograph being correct—while simultaneously being rationally entitled to high confidence that at least one claim in the set fails. The standard diagnoses treat this as a problem with closure: rational belief over single propositions need not extend to their conjunction.

Yet I wonder whether the difficulty runs deeper. If rationality genuinely permits belief in every member of a set together with belief in the negation of their collective conjunction, then rational belief appears compatible with logical inconsistency at the aggregate level. Extending this to scientific practice, a large research collaboration might rationally maintain belief in each of its component findings—each dataset, each statistical model—while the principal investigator rationally maintains that the final published synthesis contains at least one serious error, analogous to the preface admission. If these paradoxes demonstrate that such a distribution is not merely psychologically inevitable but epistemically sanctioned, should we conclude that institutional scientific knowledge is structurally self-contradictory—that the collective rationally believes the paper's conclusions and also believes those conclusions are false? Or is there a hidden category mistake in treating collective belief as nothing more than scaled-up individual belief, so that the paradoxes lose their bite once we move from single agents to research institutions?
