I'm preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on Mill's late political economy, and I want to move students past the standard "liberal socialist" label. Please construct a decision tree — not a list of named scholars, but a logical sorting instrument — that takes a student from concrete commitments about Mill's texts to an interpretive position on his socialism. The root node should ask whether Mill's turn is driven primarily by an empirical forecast about competitive capitalism's inevitable trajectory toward monopoly and class polarization, or primarily by a normative claim that liberty itself requires the abolition of material dependency — what I will call the structural-dependency thesis. From there, branch on whether the cooperative ideal requires collective ownership of capital and land, or whether worker-managed associations within competitive markets could satisfy it; on whether Mill's stationary-state economics functions as a distributive ceiling that renders capital accumulation morally obsolete, or merely as a growth-independent welfare metric; and on whether the harm principle permits coercive restructuring of property relations when holdings perpetuate structural dependency, or whether it protects existing economic liberties against socialist redistribution.

Each leaf should carry a descriptive name — for instance, "the cooperative-market liberal," "the stationary-state egalitarian," "the dependency-abolitionist," or "unnamed" — plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment, such as having to read Mill's praise for voluntary association either as rhetorical embellishment or as a concealed mandate for compulsory cooperation. The tree should flag any pair of leaves that the branching logic cannot genuinely separate, and it should diagnose unoccupied combinations in the logical space: are they incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Because the audience knows Mill's core arguments about liberty and utility but is not yet immersed in the secondary literature, ground every node in conceptual distinctions rather than in citations or named interpretations; do not ask students to identify scholars. Finally, please indicate what specific discovery — a textual passage, a consistency argument, or an empirical claim about nineteenth-century economic conditions — would lead you to collapse one of these branches or relocate a leaf, and identify where your classification is most likely to fail by imposing false dichotomies.
