Please argue that the extended mind thesis should be reformulated around a diachronic integration condition rather than the standard functional-parity principle. On this view, an artifact becomes part of a cognitive system not simply when it plays a role equivalent to an internal mental process, but only when it is embedded in a sustained feedback loop that continuously updates the agent’s dispositional states, with the agent treating the artifact’s outputs as default-trusted inputs that are not independently verified on each occasion of use. Defend the claim that this restriction successfully blocks the cognitive-bloat objection—preventing every accessible database, search engine, or cloud storage system from being counted as part of the mind—while still accommodating canonical cases such as an agent relying on a long-used notebook for biographical memory. Then address the apparent cost: this revision seems to exclude one-off uses of trusted external tools, such as consulting a calculator for a single complex computation that the agent accepts without re-deriving. Argue that these exclusions are actually a theoretical virtue, because they preserve a robust distinction between genuinely extended cognition and mere instrumental tool-use, and that any attempt to salvage the one-off cases by weakening the integration condition collapses back into the bloat problem. Be precise about what the diachronic condition requires, why functional parity alone fails to capture the relevant form of cognitive embedding, and what this implies for the boundaries of the mind.
