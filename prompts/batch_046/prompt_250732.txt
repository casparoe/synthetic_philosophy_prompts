There's this museum practice I've noticed — they keep victims' personal items locked away, don't put them in the main galery, but hang one blurred photo and a plaque saying the absence itself is part of the history. At the same time they run school programmes telling kids they have a duty to carry the absence forward. I'm a bit stuck: if hiding the objects is meant to stop voyeuerism and let some things fade, how does that square with telling teenagers they're obliged to keep remembering? What theory of remembrance does the display policy actually presuppose — does it need Margalit's distinction between ethical and moral memory, or is it closer to Rieff's worry that remembrance can turn into spectacle? And is the school programme consistent with the display logic, or are they smuggling in two incompatible commitments? I'd like to know what the practice strictly commits them to versus what merely fits well, and where it's genuinely underdetermined so that several theories could license it.
