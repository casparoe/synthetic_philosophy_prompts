I recently had a long argument with a friend about whether Theravada Buddhist ethics, with its canonical emphasis on cetana (intention) and the classification of mental states by the three roots (kusala and akusala), can be coherently reconstructed as a form of consequentialism. My friend proposed that the tradition is best read as root-modulated consequentialism: outcomes determine moral value, but only through their necessary mediation by mental purity, so that an action is right just in case it maximizes wholesome mental states across agent and recipient. I argued that this collapses the tradition's deontic structure, since Abhidhammic analysis treats an action with wholesome roots as kusala even when it accidentally produces suffering, and treats an action with unwholesome roots as akusala even when it maximizes aggregate welfare—suggesting that mental roots function as side-constraints rather than as weighting metrics. Write an essay that weighs these two interpretations. You must defend a definite verdict—either that Buddhist ethics is structurally consequentialist with a psychological metric, or that it retains an irreducible non-consequentialist core—without ending on an agnostic note. Apply your analysis to one hard case: compare a physician who performs a painful life-saving procedure with genuinely compassionate intention against a physician who administers a painless lethal intervention with genuinely compassionate intention to end suffering, and explain precisely what each framework predicts about the moral status of the two acts and why.
