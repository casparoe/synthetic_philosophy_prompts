I am a novelist, and in three weeks I am presenting a paper—derived from the ontology of character in my recent fiction—to the philosophy of literature workshop at the university. My thesis is that fictional characters possess genuine dispositional properties, but that these properties are systematically finked and masked by narrative architecture itself: plot necessity, generic convention, and authorial intervention function not merely as external constraints but as internal antidotes that prevent the manifestation of the very dispositions they sustain. I argue that this requires expanding the standard metaphysical taxonomy—beyond Martin's physical finks and the Lewisian counterfactual analysis—to include what I call "narrative masks," dispositional inhibitors that are constitutive of the fictional object rather than accidental to it. The audience is split precisely in half: on one side, analytic metaphysicians of language and mind who are fluent in the dispositions literature and deeply skeptical that abstract artifacts can bear real modal properties; on the other, narrative theorists and literary scholars who view characters as purely intentional or artifactual constructions and who regard the importation of metaphysical machinery into fiction as either redundant or imperialistic.
