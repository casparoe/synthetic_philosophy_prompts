I am a retired atmospheric physicist who, since stepping away from active research, has taken up philosophy with the same patience I once brought to long-wave radiation measurements—slowly, skeptically, and without formal training. Each month I host a small salon for former scientific colleagues, none of whom have read philosophy beyond occasional popular essays, and I have agreed to lead next week’s session on peer disagreement in epistemology, specifically the tension between conciliationism and steadfastness. I am not asking for a lecture on the literature; rather, I need a teaching plan: the precise dependency order in which the pieces must be introduced so that the philosophical tension feels inevitable rather than artificial, since my audience will arrive assuming that good scientists always converge with more data.

For the carrying example, I intend to build the discussion around a constructed scenario I call the upper-atmosphere divergence: two independent observatories, both with long peer-reviewed calibration histories and equivalent expertise, measure a subtle thermal gradient in the mesosphere using different but equally validated instruments, and after exhaustive cross-checking they still arrive at robust yet statistically incompatible values, with neither able to locate an instrumental fault. I want your assessment of whether this case possesses the right properties—equal epistemic standing, genuine conflict, no hidden arbiter—to carry the concept, and what adjustments it needs so that it does not collapse into a mere technical dispute about error bars. Alongside this, I need a map of the misconceptions my scientifically trained listeners are likely to import: that conciliationism requires abandoning one’s measurements, that steadfastness is just professional stubbornness masked as principle, that disagreement always signals an unresolved empirical gap rather than a rational divergence, and perhaps a more subtle error I have not named—together with the exact point in the explanatory sequence where each should be headed off before it takes root. I also want one diagnostic question I can pose at the end—using a brief variant of the scenario—to determine whether they have understood the philosophical structure or have merely adopted a conciliatory temperament, and an explicit statement of what I should refuse to explain at this level, such as formal Bayesian uniqueness conditions, named historical positions, or technical literature, since I wish to avoid both bibliographic recommendations and appeals to authority I do not possess. Please keep the plan free of citations or reading lists; the session must stand on reasoning alone.
