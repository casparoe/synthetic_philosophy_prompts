I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at tomorrow's memorial symposium for my doctoral supervisor, Professor Anjali Mehta, who spent her career arguing that Western epistemology's insistence on univocal, absolute truth systematically distorts Jain anekāntavāda. The room will contain both scholars of Indian philosophy, who will recognize any misstep in my handling of syādvāda, and her family members and non-philosophical colleagues, who need to understand why she believed that no single statement about a life, a death, or a legacy can be unconditionally true. I want the address to treat the seven conditional modes of predication—syāt asti, syāt nāsti, syāt asti ca nāsti ca, syāt avaktavya, syāt asti ca avaktavya, syāt nāsti ca avaktavya, and syāt asti ca nāsti ca avaktavya—not as decorative references but as a genuine structural constraint: every assertion I make about her work, her character, or what her absence means must be articulated through these seven conditions, so that I never pronounce an unqualified absolute. At the same time, the speech must actually console grieving people rather than collapsing into logical abstraction, and it must land as affection and coherence for the experts without leaving the family behind. Could you write a five-minute address that uses this many-sided framework to say something genuinely consoling about her absence, honoring both her philosophical rigor and the emotional needs of a mixed audience?
