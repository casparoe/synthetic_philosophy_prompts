I want to avoid reducing Wilde’s novel to a simple gothic morality tale about hedonism; instead, I need a reading that takes both the narrative architecture and Bentham’s ethical calculus seriously, asking whether Lord Henry Wotton’s doctrine of a new hedonism genuinely maps onto Bentham’s quantitative framework or whether it instead smuggles in an ascetic assumption that sensual pleasure of a certain kind corrupts the soul intrinsically.

Could you examine how specific scenes and character choices—Dorian’s seduction and abandonment of Sibyl Vane, his later exploits in the opium dens, the murder of Basil Hallward, and the novel’s climactic restoration of the portrait—interact with a Benthamite analysis? For instance, does the portrait function as a kind of hidden felicific register, tracking the cumulative disutility of Dorian’s actions across time, or does it represent a metaphysical rejection of Bentham’s secular, harm-based moral arithmetic altogether? Does the conclusion, in which physical corruption is reversed only through Dorian’s death, imply that certain erotic and aesthetic pleasures carry an intrinsic moral taint that no quantitative calculus could ever justify or redeem? Please treat the philosophical idea as an active interlocutor rather than a backdrop: where the text resists a utilitarian reading—particularly regarding whether pleasure can be morally neutral when it is intensely private and aesthetically refined—say so explicitly, and suggest what the novel adds to the philosophical debate about sexual morality that pure argument alone cannot capture.
