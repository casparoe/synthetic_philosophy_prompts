Here's an argument I cooked up about Franklin's virtue project and now it feels like a trap. (1) Franklin said moral perfection means leaving no fault uncorrected, and he tracked every slip in his little book with dots. (2) He also admitted he never got humility right—he'd feel proud of being humble, so the dot for pride never dissapeared. (3) If a tracking system is designed to reach zero dots, and one dot is permanently stuck, then the system can't fulfill it's purpose; it's like running an accounting ledger where one debt can never be cleared and eventually drowns all credits. (4) So Franklin should have burned the book the moment he saw humility was unfixable, since keeping it just documents a failed perfectionist project. (5) More generally, if you aim at total moral purity and allow one residual fault, that fault doesn't stay static—it compounds, because every other virtue you practice is tainted by the presence of the unremoved vice that occured in humility. (6) Therefore either you achieve flawless perfection or incremental virtue is not just useless but actively deceptive; you should either be a saint or stop pretending. Where does this slip? I kinda want to keep (1) because Franklin really did chase zero faults, but (6) sounds insane—like it proves Franklin should've been a hedonist instead of tinkering with his chart. Which step is the rot, and what's the price of cutting it out?
