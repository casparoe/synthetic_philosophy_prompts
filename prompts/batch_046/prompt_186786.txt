Wittgenstein's On Certainty describes propositions like "the earth has existed for many years" as hinges: they are not supported by evidence, but form the fixed background against which particular doubts arise. I understand this to mean that hinge propositions possess a special epistemic status that makes them rationally unrevisable—otherwise they would be indistinguishable from empirical beliefs we merely hold with exceptional confidence. Given that, two questions trouble me. First, if hinges are genuinely unrevisable, does that not reduce to dogmatism? And if they can be revised, as Wittgenstein suggests happens when an entire practice or framework shifts, then what structural feature distinguishes them from stubborn hypotheses, other than their centrality in our practice? Second, I am wondering whether an artificial reasoning system ought to encode certain claims—say, about the stability of physical constants or the reliability of its own sensors—as explicit "hinge-rules" that override contradictory inputs. Would that formalize Wittgenstein's insight, or does it mistake a practical, language-game-relative condition for an axiomatic constraint?
