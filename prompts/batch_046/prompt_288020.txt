Please describe the most important implications of the philosophical debate on individual contributions to collective harms — particularly the inefficacy objection as it arises in climate ethics, dietary choice, and democratic voting — for the design of internal norms within a small collective that has recently become my direct responsibility. Since being appointed chair of the climate-action working group for our residents' association of roughly forty households, I have found that members routinely invoke causal negligibility to resist binding commitments on gas-boiler replacement, reduced meat consumption, and strategic voting in local elections. I am not seeking empirical statistics or a bibliography; rather, I want an analysis of what the literature on collective action problems implies about the coherence of our group's reasoning. First, does the inefficacy objection genuinely defeat the moral rationale for collective rules that constrain individual behaviour when defection produces no detectable change in aggregate harm? Second, how ought we to weigh indirect normative effects — such as expressive solidarity, institutional signalling, or the maintenance of cooperative dispositions — against the direct causal impotence that critics emphasise? Third, if we treat the association not merely as an aggregation of private agents but as a miniature deliberative body with delegated decision weights (akin to a localised sortition mechanism), does that institutional framing alter whether individual abstention counts as a harm or as a permissible free-riding strategy? You may introduce a hypothetical or somewhat unconventional conceptual device — for instance, modelling our burden-sharing scheme as an indirect-normative structure in which members are required not to maximise direct impact but to uphold a shared deliberative standard — provided the argument remains accessible without reference to specialised empirical studies or obscure theoretical apparatus.
