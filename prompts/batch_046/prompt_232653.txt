Next month I present to a joint philosophy-of-science and philosophy-of-psychiatry seminar my argument that psychiatric nosology should be read through the lens of Wittgenstein's On Certainty as operating with a stratified hinge structure: deep hinges concern the reality of mental distress as a domain requiring clinical intervention, whilst surface hinges govern specific diagnostic criteria, and revisions to manuals proceed not by falsifying those surface hinges but by temporarily suspending them under a meta-hinge commitment to clinical utility — an analogy I borrow from legal theory's idea of suspended judgement. The audience includes analytic epistemologists who treat hinge talk as obscurantist coherentism, philosophers of psychiatry sympathetic to Wittgenstein but worried I conflate hinges with Kuhnian paradigms, and a clinical psychologist who will ask whether this improves actual diagnosis. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as each group would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my position — especially my dependence on that legal-theoretic suspension analogy — that I must defend rather than hope they overlook. Include the objection I most dread being raised. Please answer in British English.
