Consider the institutional design common to many contemporary democracies: legislative office is filled by competitive election and may be held indefinitely, allowing career politicians, while chief executive offices are often subject to strict term limits; at the same time, binding judgment over individual guilt is assigned to juries selected by lot from the general population, with no required expertise, and advisory deliberation on policy is occasionally delegated to citizens’ assemblies also chosen by sortition, yet the authority to enact binding statutes remains exclusively with the elected legislature. This arrangement treats random selection as legitimate for fact-finding and for non-binding counsel, but as illegitimate for lawmaking; it treats long legislative tenure as unproblematic, even as a source of experience, but treats long executive tenure as a corruption risk requiring forced rotation.

What philosophical commitments about political competence, democratic equality, and the nature of representation does this specific combination presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory that renders it coherent rather than arbitrary—distinguishing whether it rests on the idea that lawmaking requires a popular mandate that only election can confer, that judgment of particular cases needs only common reason while legislation requires specialized knowledge, that careerism corrupts unitary power but is diluted in collective bodies, or on some other partition of political knowledge and authority. Then say whether the practice genuinely requires that theory, or whether it is compatible with rival accounts—for instance, one that sees election primarily as a filter for ambition rather than for competence, or one that regards sortition as the only genuinely democratic procedure and election as an aristocratic residue tolerated for administrative convenience. Finally, describe what this institution would have to become if rebuilt on the rival view: for example, if binding legislative authority were assigned to rotating citizens’ assemblies with mandatory term limits, or if career paths in legislatures were abolished and replaced with short, non-renewable mandates filled either by lot or by election with strict rotation. Identify where the existing practice is philosophically underdetermined—where its parts pull in different directions—and specify which further institutional choice would settle the matter.
