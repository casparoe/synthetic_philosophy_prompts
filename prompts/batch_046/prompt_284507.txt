I've been turning over the problem of verisimilitude in my head—why we praise art, maps, or biographies for being "true to life"—and I constructed an argument that seems to show the whole idea collapses into absurdity. (1) If a representation is valuable in direct proportion to how closely it resembles what it represents, then perfect resemblance is the best possible representation. (2) The perfect resemblance of an oak tree is just an oak tree, or perhaps an artificial tree indistinguishable from it in every perceptible respect. (3) So, by that standard, the best artistic depiction of an oak tree is not a painting or a poem at all, but the tree itself, or a flawless replica that replaces it. (4) Extending this, the best biography of a life is that life lived over again, the best map of a territory is the territory at full scale, and the best photograph is the scene it captures. (5) But then every representational art form is permanently second-rate, always outdone by its subject, which makes it puzzling why we ever produce or value representations rather than the things themselves. (6) Yet we clearly do value some representations highly—certain photographs feel more "truthful" than others, some novels capture a mood better than direct experience—so resemblance must be doing some work. (7) Therefore the principle linking representational value to resemblance must be false, and "verisimilitude" is either a confusion or a misnomer for something else entirely, like selective emphasis.

I feel like something slipped between steps (1) and (4), perhaps an equivocation between looking alike and being alike, or between resemblance in every respect and resemblance in the respects that matter for a particular purpose. But I also worry that if I reject (1), I lose the intuition that realism in painting or fiction is genuinely a virtue, not just a convention. If I reject (3) by saying a perfect replica isn't art because it lacks intention or framing, then I seem to be sneaking in a non-resemblance criterion—like authorship or selection—to save the idea of representation, which makes resemblance itself redundant. If I reject (6) and say representations are always inferior, then I have to explain why we don't treat museums and libraries as consolation prizes for missing the real thing. Which step should I actually abandon, why did it look so plausible on its own, and what do the standard positions—say, the view that representation is about selecting relevant similarities, versus the view that resemblance is neither necessary nor sufficient—each have to pay to reject it? I'd especially like to keep the idea that good art can be "lifelike" without committing myself to the claim that the best art should try to disappear into its subject.
