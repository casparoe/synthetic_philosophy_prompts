I'm running a 90-minute graduate seminar in philosophy of language with twelve students who have already worked through the opening sections of Wittgenstein's later work and can recite the standard objections to essentialism about concepts, yet remain conceptually stuck—treating family resemblance as merely a failure of analysis rather than as an alternative form of order. I want a runnable in-class activity that forces them to inhabit that alternative form. Design a structured game, using only paper cards and small-group rules, in which participants construct a functioning micro-language-game for a fictional cooperative practice—say, coordinating the repair of an imaginary shared mechanism—without being given any explicit definition of success or membership conditions. Give them a deck of action-cards describing moves that clearly belong to the practice, plus one or two deliberately ambiguous borderline cards that share no single feature with the core set. The coordination rule should be arranged so that groups must actually deploy the ambiguous cards to complete a task, which will push them either to invent an ad hoc essential criterion and fail, to split into incompatible sub-games with different family-groupings, or to stabilize around a shared practice that cannot be justified by reference to a hidden rule. The facilitator should speak almost nothing until the debrief, so that the participants construct the sequence themselves and only notice where they have arrived at the end.

After the game, provide a debrief that maps each phase—search for essence, fracture or convergence, silent acceptance—onto specific moments in Wittgenstein's account of language-games and the craving for generality; predict what will go wrong the first time you run it, such as participants demanding that you reveal the correct classification; and be explicit about which feature of actual language-games the simulation cannot reproduce, specifically the historical and cultural sedimentation that makes real family-resemblances feel inevitable rather than constructed, stating exactly how you will supply that missing dimension verbally. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; do not invoke highly specialized secondary literature that a model without broad training might not have encountered; and if you reference any specific passage, paraphrase it without quotation marks.
