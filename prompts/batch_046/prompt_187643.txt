I read an online philosophy essay that connects Plato’s discussions of wine in the Symposium and the Laws to modern cognitive psychology. Its summary runs like this: in both texts Plato treats alcohol not merely as a vice but as a kind of cognitive stress-test—wine temporarily suppresses the rational part of the soul so that the lower appetitive parts surface, which is why the Laws describes intoxication as a playful basanos, a trial that reveals whether someone’s moderation is genuine or only performed for show. The Symposium dramatizes this by having Socrates remain fully rational while Alcibiades and the other guests progressively lose control, implying that true philosophical virtue is immune to chemical disinhibition. On this reading, Plato’s legislative proposal in the Laws is to institute regulated symposia for citizens over thirty, with magistrates observing behavior under controlled intoxication to assess fitness for office. The essay then claims that Plato essentially anticipates the modern psychological disinhibition hypothesis—the idea that alcohol lowers inhibitory control without altering underlying preferences—so his proposal amounts to an early form of behavioral stress-testing. From this it concludes that democratic assemblies should be kept sober, but that periodic state-supervised drinking tests could replace moral education for adults, since if virtue does not survive the wine-test, habituation was never going to succeed anyway.

The framing sounds sophisticated, but I am unsure how much of this survives contact with the texts. Is it a fair summary? Please audit it point by point: which claims genuinely reflect Plato’s arguments in the Laws and the Symposium, which import modern psychological language anachronistically, and which are straightforward misreadings—especially the claim that Plato thinks state-supervised testing should replace habituation, and the inference that he regards virtue as either innate or nonexistent with no middle ground? Also, does the comparison to the disinhibition hypothesis actually illuminate Plato’s position, or does it distort his account of education, pleasure, and the soul’s parts?
