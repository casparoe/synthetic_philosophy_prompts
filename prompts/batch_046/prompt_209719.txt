I’ve been turning over in my mind why hypocrisy feels like it cancels someone’s right to blame others. Most people I talk to seem to agree that if you condemn cheating on taxes while hiding income yourself, you’ve lost the standing to call anyone else out—not just because you’re inconsistent, but because your blame comes with a kind of authority you’ve shown you don’t actually possess. One friend argued it’s purely about logical consistency: if the rule applies to everyone, it applies to you, and breaking it while enforcing it is self-defeating. Another said consistency is a red herring; the real issue is that the hypocrite demonstrates they don’t take the norm seriously, so they’re not positioned to hold others to it. I find both answers partly right but still incomplete, because they treat all failures of self-application as the same kind of mistake.

So I want to ask you to argue for a more specific position. Consider a case I’ve been calling “delegated self-accounting”: a person knows they have a deep, persistent blind spot about their own conduct—say, they are reliably self-excusing about their temper—so they deliberately build an external mechanism (a trusted board, a binding contract with future penalties, even an explicit appeal to a hypothetical future version of themselves) that takes over judgment of their past failures, while they retain full present authority to blame others for the same fault. They are not hiding their flaw, nor pretending it doesn’t apply to them; they have institutionalized the judgment of it away from their present self. Make the case that ordinary hypocrisy undermines standing to blame precisely because it involves a present-tense refusal to apply the judgment to oneself, whereas this delegated structure does not destroy standing, since the refusal has been replaced by a separated, acknowledged system of self-accountability. You should address the obvious objection—that this is just hypocrisy with extra bureaucratic steps—and explain why the separation of judging-self from blaming-others matters morally, not just procedurally. I’m not asking for references to philosophers or formal theories; I just want the argument built clearly from the ground up, since I’m trying to figure out whether the core problem with hypocrites is really about being inconsistent, or about something closer to a live, present claim to moral authority that they haven’t actually surrendered.
