I am drafting a satirical bureaucratic memorandum — approximately 600 words, composed as deadpan numbered clauses — issued by the Ministry of Predictive Governance to citizens who have received an oracle identifying Damascus as the unavoidable site of their death. The piece should target the logical incoherence of treating fatalist prophecy as an administratively soluble decision problem: citizens pursue causal avoidance (fleeing Damascus) without recognising that successful evasion either falsifies the oracle or, through redirected causation, fulfils it elsewhere. The voice must remain strictly internal to determinism — never collapsing into compatibilism or soft determinism — and must exaggerate real features of the paradox rather than invent them. To prevent a generic retelling, inject one niche conceptual twist: frame the oracle as operating within a logical-induction framework, wherein the Ministry’s published confidence schedule updates on observed travel behaviour so that avoidance itself becomes evidential input that stabilises a self-confirming fixed-point prediction, irrespective of actual outcome. Include a no-branching clause addressing personal continuity should a citizen attempt to bifurcate their journey, readable to someone acquainted with Parfit-style continuity arguments but not requiring specialist expertise. Conclude with a brief author’s note identifying where the memorandum takes liberties with causal and evidential decision theory, and with contested interpretations of the fable’s provenance. In that note, separate what is settled about the logical structure of self-defeating prophecy from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of logic and decision theory, and present no contested view as the consensus.
