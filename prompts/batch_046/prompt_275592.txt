I've been trying to work out for myself what actually distinguishes a nation from a large regional culture or an empire's administrative division, and I keep finding explanations that treat nations either as purely modern fabrications or as ancient tribes that merely acquired flags. Before I settle on any account, I want the full specification. Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of nation-formation must deliver — the phenomena it must explain, the distinctions it must preserve, the cases it must correctly classify, and the historical or sociological results it must remain consistent with. For each requirement, indicate the case or consideration behind it, and mark which are genuinely non-negotiable and which reflect the preferences of particular camps.

In particular, the theory must address these tensions. It needs to explain why some rituals, symbols, and narratives invented by states are adopted sincerely into ordinary people's family and community life, while others remain transparent propaganda that nobody internalizes. It must keep separate the idea of an imagined community built on horizontal fellow-feeling from a vertical relationship of subjects to a ruler or patron. It must avoid collapsing a multi-ethnic state that shares only a bureaucratic language into either a pure nation or a pure empire. And it must account for people who still identify primarily as villagers, clan members, or regional inhabitants, yet reliably behave as nationals in specific contexts — paying taxes, answering conscription, or sharing grievance — without treating that behavior as proof that their local identity never mattered.

The theory also needs to explain how print literacy, radio broadcasting, and state television can all serve as media for national imagination without reducing the cause to a single technology. It must preserve the difference between a nation that claims sovereignty and an older regional culture with similar folk practices but no such claim. Finally, it must handle the fact that many so-called ancient nations contain populations that had no consciousness of belonging to that nation until relatively recently.

Then tell me which single requirement eliminates the largest number of popular accounts, and flag any pair of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable — for example, whether demanding both deep historical continuity and complete modern invention sets an impossible standard. Keep the focus on the criteria and their motivating cases; if you briefly note how common frameworks fare against the list — for instance, those stressing invented tradition against those stressing long ethnic continuity — do so only after the requirements are fully laid out. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I am looking for the conceptual map, not a survey of the literature.
