I've been thinking on my own about what actually makes consent valid, particularly in situations that aren't outright abusive but still feel murky—like agreeing to a medical procedure where every risk is technically listed yet the emotional reality of recovery is never conveyed, or entering a personal arrangement where no one lied but the framing made asking clarifying questions feel socially costly. From what I understand, the standard framework checks three conditions: the person was not coerced, they were not deceived about material facts, and they had the competence to understand what they were agreeing to. But I've started to wonder whether that misses something I would call "depth validity"—the idea that consent should remain sound not only at the moment of agreement, but also under reflective reconstruction, meaning a person could later revisit the full emotional and structural context and still feel the choice was genuinely theirs rather than shaped by hidden asymmetries in how information was presented. Could you compare the standard three-part model with this notion of depth validity? In particular, I want to understand whether deception should be defined narrowly—as only false statements about material facts—or more broadly, to include strategic framing that never technically lies but suppresses the kind of understanding that would change how someone weighs their choice. I am also curious about competence: if someone is technically competent at the time of agreement but is operating under unrecognized emotional pressure that they only identify later, does that retroactively weaken the consent, or should validity be fixed permanently at the point of agreement? Please treat this as a conceptual comparison rather than legal or medical advice, and do not feel any need to cite scholars or texts—I am just trying to think this through with some clarity.
