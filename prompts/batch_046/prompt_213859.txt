I'm trying to determine what actually distinguishes genuine joint action from mere aggregate or coordinated behavior, going by my judgments about concrete cases.

Case 1: Two friends carry a heavy table up a stairwell together; each continuously adjusts grip and pace to match the other, and each intends that they, as a pair, move the table to the landing.

Case 2: Two strangers, unaware of each other, both push a stalled car from behind; the car rolls forward. Each intends only to exert force on the car, not to act with anyone else.

Case 3: An orchestra performs a symphony under a conductor. Each musician intends to play their part correctly and knows the others are playing theirs, but none of the musicians intends the collective performance as such—only the conductor holds that broader intention.

Case 4: A corporate board votes to approve a merger. Every director intends that the board reach a collective decision, yet they disagree about whether the merger itself is desirable. The vote succeeds by majority.

Case 5: Two pedestrians walk in perfect lockstep for a full block, each merely adjusting speed to avoid collision, with no shared goal.

Case 6: A swarm of autonomous delivery drones reroutes around an obstacle through purely local interaction rules; no drone represents the swarm's goal or the presence of others, yet the group behaves as a coordinated unit.

My verdicts are: Case 1 is clearly joint action. Case 2 is not—it's just an accidental aggregation of individual efforts. Case 3 strikes me as joint action, though I'm uneasy about whether the musicians' lack of a shared collective intention undermines that. Case 4 is joint action, I think, because there is shared commitment to the decision procedure despite disagreement on substance. Case 5 is not joint action. Case 6 is the one I keep revisiting: functionally it resembles Case 2, but the emergent coordination is more structured; still, since there is no intentionality anywhere in the system, I lean that it is not joint action.

Is there a single principle—perhaps involving mutual responsiveness, shared intentionality, joint commitment, or a we-mode attitude—that captures all of these verdicts? If not, which of my judgments should I overturn, and why? Please also state what the best principle predicts for a further case I haven't considered: a human surgeon and an AI surgical assistant performing a complex operation together, where the AI continuously optimizes incision patterns based on real-time imaging but possesses no beliefs, intentions, or awareness of the surgical goal.

In your response, please clearly separate what is broadly settled in the philosophy of collective intentionality and joint action from what remains genuinely contested—for example, whether shared intentionality can be fully analyzed in terms of interlocking individual intentions, whether a we-mode is required, or whether joint commitment is an indispensable element—and do not present any one of these contested positions as if it were the consensus view. If the principle you propose cannot accommodate one or more of my verdicts, say so explicitly.
