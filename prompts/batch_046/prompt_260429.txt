I sit on the allocation committee for our regional health consortium, and we are drafting a binding recommendation on crisis triage protocols before the next fiscal quarter. The proposed policy allows clinicians to withhold intensive care from one patient with a very poor prognosis when the same resources could save three others with a reasonable chance of recovery. One committee member objects on contractualist grounds: she argues that following T.M. Scanlon, an action is wrong if it is permitted by a principle that the affected individual could reasonably reject, and that this framework treats harm to one person as never justifiable merely by aggregating benefits to others. She concludes that our protocol violates contractualism because the single patient could reasonably reject being sacrificed for the group, regardless of the numbers involved. A second member responds that Scanlon's account allows the numbers of affected people to enter into reasonable rejection, not through simple addition of welfare, but through the idea that a principle permitting sacrifice in these circumstances might be rejectable from an ex ante impartial perspective, and that Scanlon's own discussion of rescue cases does not categorically forbid aggregation.

Please draft the committee's written opinion recommending whether the protocol should be adopted, modified, or rejected, with a clear implementation timeline if adopted. The opinion should treat the contractualist objection with genuine seriousness rather than dismissing it, should explain whether Scanlon's view actually blocks the kind of aggregation our protocol involves, and should separate claims that are well-supported in Scanlon's published work from interpretations that remain genuinely contested among philosophers working on contractualism. Do not present any disputed reading as if it were the established consensus.
