Consider the interpretive track record of the High Court of the Republic of Meridian in statutory-construction cases decided between 2017 and 2023.

In 2017, interpreting a tax code that granted exemptions to “non-profit organizations dedicated to education or charity,” the Court denied an exemption to a for-profit certified B-corporation that contractually donated 100% of its net revenue to public schools. The legislative history contained several references to “entities serving educational ends,” but the Court held that “dedicated to” refers to organizational mission, not financial outcome, and declined to extend the exemption.

In 2018, interpreting a building-access law requiring ramps in “public buildings,” the Court applied the requirement to a privately owned museum that charged admission and was open to all, rejecting the owner’s argument that “public” meant government-owned. The Court cited the legislative purpose of universal physical access and said “public” should be read functionally.

In 2020, interpreting a consumer-protection statute requiring “written notice” for termination of leases exceeding one year, the Court held that an email containing a scanned image of a handwritten letter satisfied the requirement, noting that the purpose was durable, readable documentation and that digital transmission preserved that function.

In 2021, interpreting a workplace-safety law applying to “employees present on the premises,” the Court applied the law to a remote worker injured during a single-day visit to headquarters for a meeting, reasoning that the purpose was to protect anyone performing labor for the employer on-site, even if not regularly stationed there.

In 2023, interpreting a media-regulation statute governing “broadcasting,” the Court refused to apply licensing requirements to an on-demand podcast distributed via RSS feeds, holding that “broadcasting” denotes transmission over electromagnetic spectrum, and that extending it to internet-delivered audio would violate the technological specificity embedded in the statutory scheme, despite the legislative purpose of regulating mass-audience content.

Now the new case: In 2024, the Court must interpret a 2010 courtroom-conduct statute that prohibits “recording devices in courtrooms” to prevent intimidation of witnesses and distortion of proceedings. A litigant used a smartphone app that captures audio in real time, converts it immediately to text via an on-device language model, and displays the transcript without storing any audio file. The legislative history shows the drafters were concerned with “any hidden documentation that could be replayed or leaked,” but the word “recording” is not defined. The lower court excluded the device, treating it as a transcription tool, not a recorder.

If the High Court wishes to be consistent with its own precedents, what should it decide—should the app fall within the prohibition? In particular, be explicit about what principle the Court would be adopting if it included the device, and what principle it would be adopting if it excluded it.
