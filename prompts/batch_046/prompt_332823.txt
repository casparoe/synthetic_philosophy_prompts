Two friends of mine, Maya and Jordan, just finished a long argument about whether generational labels—Millennial, Gen Z, Boomer—denote real kinds or are purely administrative fictions. Maya argued that cohorts form genuine social kinds: people who experience the same macroeconomic and technological conditions during formative years develop stable, causally effective dispositions—particular debt structures, delayed life milestones, modes of digital socialization—that cluster together and do real explanatory work, even with fuzzy boundaries. Jordan replied that this bundles age, period, and cohort effects in a statistically inseparable way; within-group variance by class and geography dwarfs between-group differences, and the year cutoffs are marketing conventions, not discovered joints. Maya conceded the fuzziness but insisted social kinds like citizenship or currency also have conventional boundaries and institutional mechanisms, yet remain causally real; generations, she said, have softer mechanisms—labor-market entry conditions, educational tracking, technology adoption curves—that still make cohort membership explanatory. Jordan granted the analogy but argued generations lack any enforcement mechanism, remaining purely interpretive frames that become self-fulfilling through branding and marketing. Maya countered that being partly self-fulfilling does not make a kind unreal—gender categories operate similarly—and that cohort membership determines which age one is when a period event strikes, so the interaction is non-redundant with pure age or period effects. Jordan's final point was that the properties attributed to Millennials cluster far more tightly by socioeconomic status and region than by birth year, so treating the cohort as a kind misattributes structural economic effects to demographic destiny.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of Maya's and Jordan's main arguments from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justify each rating briefly, say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties miss—particularly whether there is a meaningful distinction between generations as predictive heuristics and generations as ontologically basic kinds.
