I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about the moral weight of suffering versus happiness, not by adopting any named school but by checking my intuitions against concrete cases and seeing whether they line up.

Here are my verdicts. If I can prevent one person from enduring severe, prolonged physical or mental anguish, that takes priority over giving ten other people moderate joy or satisfaction. The urgency feels asymmetrical.

When I compare whole possible worlds, I think a world with billions of people living genuinely fulfilling lives but with occasional serious illness or grief is worse than a world with no conscious beings at all. The absence of suffering seems to outweigh the presence of happiness, so the empty world is preferable.

But if the choice is between a world with a hundred people in intense bliss and one person in agony, versus a world with fifty people in calm contentment and nobody suffering, I choose the second. Even though total happiness is far lower, the presence of that single extreme suffering makes the first world worse.

Then there is this intervention: a drug that eliminates all capacity for pain, grief, and fear, but also eliminates all capacity for love, wonder, and deep satisfaction. If it were administered to everyone, I would regard that as a profound loss rather than an improvement, despite suffering being reduced to zero. So I do not think suffering is the only thing that matters.

Yet I also think it would be wrong to press a button that instantly and painlessly ends all conscious life, thereby guaranteeing zero future suffering. I am not sure why I reject this: perhaps because some lives are worth living despite risk, or perhaps because I am inconsistently smuggling in value for happiness.

On a smaller scale, I think it is right to tell a friend that their business plan is flawed if the painful truth spares them a greater collapse later. But I keep going back and forth on telling a dying patient who asks whether their estranged child called. Comforting them with a false yes spares them grief in their final hours, yet the comfort rests on a lie, and the lie seems to make the act wrong in a way that the spared grief does not excuse.

Is there a coherent principle that captures all of this? I want candidates that explain why suffering dominates in world-comparisons and in priority-setting, but that also explain why flattening all emotional variation is not good and why ending all life is not obligatory. For each candidate, please state which of my verdicts it accommodates, which case motivates each clause, and what it says about the dying-patient lie and about the button. Most importantly, be honest about which of my verdicts must be dropped if the principle is to remain simple, and whether saving every verdict is only possible at the cost of an implausibly complex rule.
