Schrödinger, in his later essays on Vedanta, argued that the multiplicity of minds is illusory and that consciousness is fundamentally one—individual awareness being comparable to different facets of a single jewel rather than separate entities. Imagine he is alive today and must account for two bodies of post-1961 evidence: the split-brain literature showing apparently independent centers of awareness within one cranium, and modern large-scale artificial architectures that integrate information globally without any localized “observer” or central bottleneck.

How would he likely read the split-brain cases? Would he treat commissurotomy as a literal bifurcation of consciousness that undermines Vedantic monism, or as a division of access and reportability that leaves phenomenal unity intact? And in the artificial case—where processing is distributed across submodules with no single global workspace—does his framework imply that such a system hosts one distributed consciousness, hosts none because there is no biological “subject,” or reveals that the very framing of “how many minds” is a category mistake?

In answering, distinguish carefully between phenomenal unity (the first-person sense of being a single subject) and functional integration (the causal coordination of information), and ask whether Schrödinger’s argument is best read as an empirical claim about neural organization or as a transcendental claim about the preconditions for any experience whatsoever. If it is the latter, does it extend naturally to silicon architectures, or does it covertly depend on biological embodiment in a way that would make his view empirically vulnerable once brains are no longer the only integrated systems? Keep the analysis disciplined: say where Vedanta is silent rather than forcing it to speak about architectures it never anticipated.
