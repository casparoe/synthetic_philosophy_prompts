I defend a strengthened reading of Parfit's hitchhiker case: that it reveals not merely a local paradox in intertemporal rational choice, but that any normatively adequate theory of agency must accommodate rational irrationality — the deliberate adoption of dispositions that are locally suboptimal — as a structural feature rather than an anomaly. Specifically, I claim that in any decision problem where (i) an agent's present preferences depend on anticipated future actions, (ii) those future actions are only credible if backed by dispositions resistant to cost-benefit revision, and (iii) the costs of maintaining those dispositions exceed their direct instrumental benefits, then rationality demands what looks like irrational commitment. What observations, formal results, or conceptual distinctions would count decisively against this claim, as opposed to merely being awkward for it? I want the counter-evidence sorted by fatality: for instance, a proof that credible commitment can always be achieved through external mechanisms without internal disposition-change; a demonstration that the hitchhiker's reasoning collapses into standard precommitment without paradox; or empirical findings about actual human intertemporal bargaining that contradict the model. Include what defenders of the standard interpretation themselves concede as damaging, distinguish predictions this view makes that its rivals do not, and assess honestly whether the position has been qualified — say, by restricting it to sufficiently similar future selves or to non-expected-utility frameworks — into unfalsifiability, and whether such insulation is a theoretical virtue or a defect for a claim about the architecture of rational agency.
