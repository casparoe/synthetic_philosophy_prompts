I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of mind working on personal identity and distributed cognition, and I've been trying to formalize a tension in Douglas Hofstadter's account of the self. Hofstadter treats the "I" as a strange loop — a high-level, self-referential pattern rather than a substance tied to a particular brain. I want to take this substrate-independence seriously, but a colleague pushed it to what looks like a reductio. The argument runs as follows:

(1) If the self is a strange loop, then its identity conditions are structural, not substrate-bound: what makes me me is the pattern of self-referential representation, not the particular neurons that realize it.

(2) Patterns can be partially or fully instantiated in multiple substrates simultaneously, provided each substrate realizes the same causal-functional loop with sufficient fidelity — call this loop-fidelity.

(3) So if my strange loop is realized with high loop-fidelity in another brain — call it Brain B — then Brain B literally contains my "I," either in part or in whole.

(4) First-person reference ("I") is ordinarily anchored to a singular locus of experience; but if the loop is distributed across substrates, there is no unique anchor, only a pattern that spans brains.

(5) Therefore, on this view, the uniqueness of first-person authority collapses: mental states attributed to "me" are also attributable to "you" in Brain B, and there is no principled boundary between self and other.

(6) Moral responsibility tracks the subject of the loop: if Brain B, running my pattern, performs a harmful act, then I — as the pattern — am responsible, yet Brain B also experiences the act as its own.

(7) The result is that personal identity becomes both non-exclusive and morally over-determined: one act, two bodies, one self, two experiences, with no coherent way to assign blame or ownership.

This seems to prove too much: it dissolves the very distinctions that make first-person thought and moral accountability intelligible. I am committed to (1) and (2) — the substrate-independence and distributability of the loop seem essential to Hofstadter's picture — but I cannot locate the error. Is the mistake in (3), conflating replication with co-instantiation of the same token self? Or in (4), by assuming that first-person anchoring requires uniqueness rather than mere primacy? Or must we reject (6) and accept that distributed loops fragment moral agency?

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — name the step you reject or the premise least costly to abandon — then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
