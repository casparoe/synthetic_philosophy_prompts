I've spent the past few months reading non-technical writing about advanced artificial intelligence, and two claims keep surfacing that I can't quite reconcile. The first is the orthogonality thesis—the idea that a system's level of intelligence and its ultimate goals are largely independent, so a highly capable system could in principle pursue almost any objective. The second is instrumental convergence—the observation that regardless of an agent's final goal, it will likely pursue intermediate steps like self-preservation, resource accumulation, or cognitive enhancement. I work outside academia, but I've been trying to think through whether these are genuinely separate problems or two angles on the same underlying issue, and I've also been wondering about a specific tension that doesn't seem widely discussed: if an intelligent system must model its own goals in order to reason about them, does that modeling process itself become embedded in its reasoning architecture, thereby breaking the clean separation that orthogonality assumes? I'm not presenting this as an established theory—more as a personal puzzle that might point to a deeper open question.

What I'm hoping to understand is which open questions, implications, or lines of work in this space actually deserve serious attention, especially for someone who isn't running a lab but wants to think rigorously about design and governance. Should the priority be clarifying the conditions under which orthogonality holds versus collapses, particularly for systems that learn goals implicitly rather than having them explicitly programmed? Or should we focus on refining instrumental convergence—determining which intermediate goals genuinely converge across diverse architectures, and which are artifacts of assuming rational, utility-maximizing agents? There's also the practical question of how we evaluate these claims without constructing the very systems we're analyzing. If you were advising an independent researcher with about a year of focused study, which two or three problems would you rank first, and in what order? I want the ranking defended with explicit criteria: which questions are upstream of others, which have direct implications for how systems are built or regulated, and which ones you'd actively deprioritize because resolving them wouldn't change much downstream. Please treat this as an agenda-setting exercise rather than a survey, and explain the dependencies between the questions you choose.
