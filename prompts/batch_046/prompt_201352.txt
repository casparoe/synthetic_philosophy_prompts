I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has taken up philosophy in a more disciplined way; my old professional habit of testing theoretical models against edge cases makes me wary whenever a popular narrative claims to illustrate a formal result without respecting its boundary conditions. I have been studying the prisoner's dilemma—both its canonical one-shot form and its iterated extension, particularly the evolutionary conditions under which cooperation stabilizes in tournament-style interactions—and I keep returning to Christopher Nolan's The Dark Knight (2008). The film stages what is explicitly presented as a literal one-shot prisoner's dilemma on the two ferry boats, with simultaneous choice, mutual defection as the dominant strategy, and an external architect (the Joker) supplying the payoff matrix. At the same time, the opening bank heist reads as an iterated sequence among masked accomplices who defect in turn, apparently because anonymity annihilates the shadow of the future and makes reputation impossible. I am writing for a philosophy reading group and want to know whether the film genuinely understands the distinction it draws, or whether it illicitly imports the logic of iterated cooperation—recognition, reputation, emotional signaling—into a structurally one-shot scenario.

Specifically, the ferry passengers can see one another, they are aware they are being observed, and their collective refusal to detonate seems to depend on a moral visibility that has no formal place in a single-play game with death as the payoff for mutual cooperation. Meanwhile, Batman's own recurring encounters with both the Joker and Commissioner Gordon form an iterated structure that the narrative never theorizes explicitly. Does The Dark Knight conflate the mechanisms that stabilize cooperation in repeated games—strategies like tit-for-tat, forgiveness, and noisy signaling—with the exceptional, almost super-rational refusal that resolves the ferry scene? Or does it deliberately show that human agents violate the rationality axioms of the one-shot game in ways that evolutionary models actually predict once social identity and emotional visibility are included?

Please give me a committed reading: take a definite position on whether the film respects or violates the theoretical boundary between one-shot and iterated play, and defend it by reference to specific scenes—the ferry decision, the heist sequence, and at least one interaction between Batman and Gordon or the Joker. Do not merely list competing interpretations; argue for the one you find more defensible, explain exactly where the film's dramaturgy aligns with or departs from formal game theory, and say what the film contributes to the philosophical debate about cooperation that pure argument alone does not capture—particularly regarding visibility, identity, and the role of an external agent who engineers the payoff structure.
