I am a retired biologist who has recently begun studying moral philosophy and the philosophy of cultural heritage. In debates over the restitution of the Benin Bronzes and the Parthenon Marbles, I have noticed two analogies doing heavy argumentative work. The first, common among restitution advocates, compares a museum holding contested artifacts to a fence holding stolen goods: just as legal possession of a watch that has passed through pawn shops does not confer moral title, centuries of curatorial custody do not erase an original act of plunder. The second, advanced by some retentionists, compares the artifact to a child adopted and raised in another family: prolonged care, scholarly study, and public display have transformed the object, so returning it is less like restoring property and more like disrupting an established nurturing relationship. Please audit both analogies systematically. For each one, lay out the explicit correspondences—what in the source domain maps onto the artifact, the museum, the source community, and the act of return—then identify the disanalogies that actually matter for the moral conclusion. Note which normative premises each analogy quietly imports, such as assumptions about the fungibility of objects, the moral weight of care versus origin, or whether an artwork’s identity conditions allow it to become “part of” its custodial environment. Finally, assess whether either analogy genuinely supports the conclusion it is invoked to defend, and if both are deficient, propose a more precise analogy drawn from conservation biology or ecology that might capture the relevant moral structure without smuggling in hidden commitments. Please write your answer in American English.
