I have recently been appointed to a local sentencing review panel, and I find myself returning to a problem I thought I had settled: whether my judgements about punishment and moral responsibility form a coherent whole, or whether I am caught in the kind of tension that separates Dennett’s qualified compatibilism from Caruso’s rejection of basic desert.

Here are my verdicts. A violent recidivist who expresses genuine remorse but remains dangerous: I support indefinite civil commitment for public protection, yet I resist saying he deserves to suffer; the measure feels like quarantine, not repayment. A first-time offender who stole medicine for a dying relative: I think criminal punishment is inappropriate, though restitution is owed; the wrongness seems to dissolve under extreme duress, but the harm remains real. A defendant who acted after being subjected to coercive neural manipulation in a clinical trial: punishment is clearly unjustified, yet I also reject the global claim that she lacks moral agency—only that this particular act was not truly hers. A legal system that imposes harsher sentences on randomly selected offenders to maximise deterrence: I find this abhorrent, even if crime falls. But when an agent freely chooses, without deprivation, to harm another, I think the state may impose a burden; I am unsure, however, whether that burden should be understood as deserved suffering or merely as necessary social repair.

Is there one precise principle—perhaps preserving a restricted form of backward-looking accountability, or eliminating basic desert entirely—that captures all of these? Or must I abandon one verdict? Please also say what this principle predicts for a case I have not considered: an offender who fully acknowledges her wrong, requests a specific punitive ritual as atonement, and then reoffends after release. Finally, state clearly what argument or empirical finding would change your mind—whether about the efficacy of deterrence, the nature of agency, or the dignity of the offender—and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Please respond in British English.
