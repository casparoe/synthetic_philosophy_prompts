I've been messing around with the boundary problem — who actually belongs to the demos — and I want to check if this line holds up. The standard answers are citizenship, affected interests, or being subject to coercive power. But I'm trying out a weirder criterian: membership should go to anyone whose interests are structurally inarticulable outside the collective decision-procedure they're excluded from. Not just that they're affected, but that the conceptual language they need to even state what's at stake is produced by the institution that leaves them out.

So migrant workers affected by labor policy might not need formal membership if they can articulate grievances through independent unions — they have external voice. But a future generation, or an ecosystem mediated through human proxies, might literally lack the framing to formulate a complaint except through the democratic structures that currently exclude them. That puts them inside the boundary, not as representatives but as structural members because of epistemic dependancy.

This feels like it dodges the "all affected interests" over-inclusion problem since it doesn't hinge on causal impact, just on framing dependency. But I'm worried it's circular: the demos produces the language, so defining membership by dependency on that language just bakes the boundary into itself. Also, am I accidentally smuggling in Habermasian discourse ethics here without noticing?

Can you comment on whether this distinction actually does work? Specifically, is there a non-circular way to cash out "structural inarticulability," and does it fare better than affected-interests? And where are the weakest points in the argument — the circularity worry, the risk of including too many non-human entities, or something else I missed?
