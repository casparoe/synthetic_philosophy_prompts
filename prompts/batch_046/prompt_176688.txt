I am a retired physicist now auditing philosophy courses, and I have been asked to assess an exam response. The question posed to students was this: some philosophers hold that a genuine moral dilemma exists only when an agent faces obligations that genuinely conflict, neither being overridden, leaving appropriate moral residue—guilt or remorse for the duty left unfulfilled—while others deny this, arguing that hierarchical ordering always resolves conflicts and that such residue is merely psychological regret. Assess whether moral residue can establish the existence of a genuine dilemma, and evaluate whether hierarchical resolution succeeds in dissolving the phenomenon without misdescribing our moral experience.

One student wrote the following:

A genuine moral dilemma occurs when two obligations conflict and neither can be satisfied. In Sophie’s case, she must choose which child survives, yet she feels lasting guilt regardless of choice. This moral residue proves the obligation to the other child remains binding and was not canceled. Critics invoke hierarchical ordering, but this treats moral norms like a system of linear equations with a unique solution vector. Moreover, since Sophie cannot save both children, the principle that ought implies can shows she is not obligated to save either, which is absurd; therefore the principle fails in conflicts. Hence imperfect duties can conflict irreducibly, and residue is acknowledgment of an unfulfilled duty rather than mere regret.

Please grade this answer with brief justification, then provide point-by-point comments distinguishing what is accurate from what is confused or invalid. In particular, address whether the student correctly distinguishes moral residue from psychological regret, whether the hierarchical view is characterized fairly, and whether the appeal to ought implies can is applied correctly. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
