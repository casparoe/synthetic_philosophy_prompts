Consider the following argument drawn from Jeremy Bentham's unpublished late-eighteenth-century manuscript on sexual offences, in which he examines acts then classified as unnatural and subject to severe legal penalties. Bentham observes that in private, consensual encounters between adults, no direct injury is inflicted upon either party; the only alleged harm is the disgust or antipathy experienced by outside observers. He compares this spectator revulsion to the aversion some people feel toward eating particular foods, noting that such subjective sensibility cannot by itself constitute a legal injury. At the same time, he allows that the law might legitimately intervene if an act produces a broader general mischief—for instance, by destabilizing institutions upon which social cooperation depends. I am trying to understand the precise boundary he draws between an act that is merely offensive to common taste and one that genuinely threatens indirect social harm. Is his distinction grounded in an empirical prediction about the social effects of private conduct, or is it a conceptual claim about what counts as an injury in utilitarian calculation? And if we transpose his reasoning to a present-day scenario in which a private, consensual practice becomes widely visible through digital media, generating legislative demands for suppression on the grounds that its public visibility erodes shared norms—would Bentham treat the technologically mediated visibility as transforming a harmless private act into a source of indirect harm, or would he insist that the harm remains confined to the spectators' own sentiments and therefore falls outside the proper scope of criminal law? Please help me understand the internal architecture of this passage and identify where, if anywhere, his argument is vulnerable to the charge that he has underestimated the social externalities of visibility itself.
