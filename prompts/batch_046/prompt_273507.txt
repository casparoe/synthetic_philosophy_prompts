I keep encountering clients who are subjected to rigorous conditionality—continuous job-search verification, mandatory training attendance, sanctions for missed appointments—in order to retain subsistence-level support, while citizens who benefit from far larger implicit state transfers, such as mortgage-interest deductions or inherited-asset protections, face no comparable burden of proof. I want to examine whether this disparity can be defended on genuinely normative grounds, or whether it represents what I will call contractualist contamination: the inappropriate migration of market-exchange reciprocity norms into a domain—basic needs provision—where they do not belong.

Please provide your credence, on a scale from 0 to 1, that the disparity is morally justified by a coherent reciprocity principle rather than by this contamination. Decompose your answer into at least three sub-estimates: first, the probability that a defensible reciprocity principle applies to welfare provision at all; second, the probability that, if such a principle applies, it selectively licenses the explicit conditions imposed on workfare recipients but not the implicit conditions attached to other transfers; and third, the probability that public moral psychology genuinely tracks this principled distinction, as opposed to tracking status-based, racialized, or simply visibility-based heuristics about who deserves support. Assign each component a rough numerical value with a brief line of justification, explain how they combine structurally, and state which single component your overall credence is most sensitive to. Finally, describe what specific empirical result—regarding either the administrative design of welfare regimes or the cross-cultural psychology of reciprocity—would most substantially move your overall number.
