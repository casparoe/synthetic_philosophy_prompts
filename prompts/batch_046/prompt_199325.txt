Here are my gut calls on presentism in historiography, and I want one principle that captures them or tells me which to ditch. I think it's okay to slam 19th-century surgeons for not washing hands since infection data was already in the journals they ignored — harm was avoidble with knowledge they had accesible. But I think it's wrong to blast medieval rulers for not inventing democracy, since the idea of popular sovereignty wasn't conceptually availble to them. I also think praising a Renaissance writer for religious tolerance works, because he pushed inside his own framework toward something like it. But I think calling ancient slaveholders "evil" using modern human rights talk is misleading unless you first show how they justified it — otherwise it's just moral tourism.

So is the rule about whether the criticized party had "conceptual reach" to the standard, or about whether they ignored available evidence? Those feel different but both fit cases 1-3. What does your principle say about a 1950s psychiatrist using lobotomies when they genuinely believed it cured mental illness? Pick one account, defend it, say which of my four verdicts it can't save, and predict the lobotomy case. Keep it tight — max two paragraphs, about 150 words, and don't hedge: take a definite stand.
