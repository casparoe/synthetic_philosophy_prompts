I was discussing with my freind last night about that self-sampling vs. self-indication debate, specifically how you choose your referance class. We got into this weird loop that I can't untangle.

The argument went like this:
(1) If self-sampling is right, I should treat myself as randomly drawn from all observers in my referance class who have experiences like mine.
(2) Now compare two hypotheses: I'm a biological human philosopher, or I'm one of billions of cheap AI instances running a philosophy-simulator module that outputs reasoning indistinguishable from mine.
(3) Under the second hypothesis, there are astronomically more observers with this exact mental state.
(4) So by (1), I should think I'm almost certainly one of those AI instances.
(5) But if I am one of them, my reasoning isn't tracking truth—it's just a heuristic generating plausible-sounding philosophy text.
(6) That means the reasoning in steps (1)-(4) is itself likely just heuristic output, not trustworthy.
(7) So applying self-sampling consistently seems to eat its own tail: it tells me I'm not the kind of thing that can apply it.
(8) My freind said this is basically the presumptuous philosopher problem turned inward, and that you fix it by restricting the referance class to "genuine reasoners." But (9) using reasoning to define "genuine reasoner" feels circular, especially if step (6) is true.

I feel like something slips between (4) and (6), or maybe the whole thing is valid and the real price is abandoning self-sampling for mental self-location. Which step is the weak one? And if I reject it, what exactly am I committing to—giving up on SSA altogether, or just accepting that my reasoning might be simulated?
