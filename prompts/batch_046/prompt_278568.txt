I'm in a philosophy of mind semenar and we've been circling the same drain on epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment for weaks. The basic problem is familiar: if conscious states don't cause anything, then my judgment that you are in pain can't be caused by your pain, and my own judgment that I'm in pain might only come from some subpersonal monitoring mechanism that doesn't actually touch the qualia itself. The usual moves feel totally exhausted—introspection for self-knowledge, analogy for others, the reply that analogy collapses without causal interaction, etc. But here's the thing I keep wondering: what if the stall isn't really about causation at all, but about whether phenomenal concepts are acquired through a kind of direct reference that needs causal contact, or whether they're just functional labels we slap on based on behavioral tracking? In other words, maybe one side treats "knowing what its like" as a causal-tracking relation, and the other treats it as a conceptual role that never needed causal contact in the first place.

So my question is, is this debate stuck because its a verbal disagreement about what "knowledge" or "phenomenal concept" means? Or is there a substantive disagreement hiding underneath about whether subpersonal tracking can actually ground phenomenal judgments? Or are they literally answering different questions—one about whether consciousness is causally efficacious, the other about whether phenomenal attribution needs that efficacy? I want an actual diagnosis with reasons, not just a survey of positions. And tell me what, if anything, would count as progress here—a sharp conceptual distinction, an argument that one framing is incoherent, or maybe a reason to think the question is just hard and shouldn't be expected to resolve.
