I'm preparing a consistency check for a philosophy-of-religion reading group and want to lay out my current commitments about Buddhist anatta and personal identity so the others can probe them. Here is the package:

(1) I accept the classical Theravada analysis: the person is reducible to the five aggregates (skandhas), and there is no permanent, unitary, self-sufficient essence (atman) underlying them.

(2) I defend what I call the skandha-binding-error view: narrative continuity over time is a real functional achievement — causal integration across temporal slices of aggregates — but it involves a misattribution of that integration to a single, enduring subject. The self is therefore not an ontological substrate but a binding error with practical reality.

(3) I hold that moral responsibility requires not a metaphysical self but only a sufficient degree of skandha-binding across time. Retributive punishment is thus conceptually coherent — there is an integrated narrative to hold accountable — but it becomes unjustified whenever binding is severely disrupted, as in advanced dementia or dissociative fragmentation.

(4) I think that when narrative-binding collapses yet bodily and relational continuity remain — for instance, a patient in a persistent vegetative state with family present — our obligations are not owed to the patient as a narrative agent but to the relational network (the sangha-context), and they are primarily negative (do not harm, do not abandon) rather than positive (promote welfare, extend life).

(5) I maintain that suffering (dukkha) is genuinely real but ownerless: anatta applies to suffering just as it applies to the self. There is no owner of pain, yet pain is not illusory and carries direct moral weight.

Please audit these for joint tenability. Which pairs pull against each other, and how severely? For each genuine tension, indicate the cheapest revision that would restore consistency and what that revision costs me in terms of my other commitments. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad. In particular, I want to know whether there is a hidden tension involving (5): if suffering is ownerless and morally weighty, and if the vegetative-state patient in (4) might still suffer despite collapsed binding, does my restriction of obligations to negative duties in (4) become unstable? And is there an apparent tension between (1) and (2) that dissolves once the two-truths distinction (paramartha vs. sammuti) is properly applied?
