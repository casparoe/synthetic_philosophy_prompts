I'm trying to identify a specific argumentative pattern I observe in practical reasoning with vague predicates—say, in administrative law or hiring standards involving terms like "substantial burden" or "qualified candidate." The pattern runs as follows: a decision-maker first treats a borderline case as falling on one side of a sharp but unknown boundary, behaving as though there is a correct answer they are approximating. When pressed on why that particular cutoff was chosen, they retreat to saying the predicate is genuinely indeterminate, that any precise line is conventional, and that demanding exactness misses the point—essentially adopting a supervaluationist stance. Once the pressure subsides, however, they revert to treating their original cutoff as tracking the real, unknown boundary rather than as one permissible precisification among many. It is not quite motte-and-bailey, since neither stance functions as an obvious defensible core; both appear available as interpretations of the same standard, and the shift tracks whether the speaker needs to defend a specific outcome or deflect criticism of arbitrariness. Does this oscillation have an established name in the vagueness literature? If not, can you characterize precisely what distinguishes it from simple equivocation, from strategic ambiguity, and from a stable epistemicist or supervaluationist position? Please separate what is settled about the relationship between epistemicism and supervaluationism from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed view—such as whether vagueness is fundamentally semantic or epistemic—as if it were the consensus.
