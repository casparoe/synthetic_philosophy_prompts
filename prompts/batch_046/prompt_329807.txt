I'm working through Augustine's De mendacio (composed around 395, before he became bishop of Hippo) for a seminar on speech-ethics, and I've reached a conceptual knot in the second chapter that I can't untangle without importing modern assumptions about mental unity that Augustine might not share. The passage runs, in part, as follows:

"Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind. Whence also the heart of him who lies is said to be double; that is, there is a double thought: the one, of that thing which he either knows or thinks to be true and does not produce; the other, of that thing which he produces instead thereof, knowing or thinking it to be false. Whence it comes to pass, that he may say a false thing and yet not lie, if he thinks it to be so as he says although it be not so; and, that he may say a true thing, and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, although in reality it be so as he utters it. For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie."

My difficulty is this. Augustine seems to make lying depend on a mismatch between inner mental content and outer utterance, coupled with what he calls a "desire of deceiving in the uttering of his mind." But I cannot tell whether the "double heart" is meant to describe a single divided consciousness—someone who simultaneously holds contradictory beliefs—or rather a unified will that knowingly substitutes one representation for another. If the former, then cases of severe self-deception or dissociative belief states might not count as lies for Augustine, since there is no single "mind" that knows the truth and suppresses it. If the latter, then even an automated system that outputs false statements without any mental content at all would fall outside the category, not because it is innocent, but because it lacks the very structure of a "heart" that can be double. Could you help me see which reading the surrounding argument supports, and whether Augustine's framework can consistently handle a speaker who genuinely lacks a belief about the matter—say, someone repeating a rumor they neither credit nor discredit—without collapsing his distinction into a purely volitional account?
