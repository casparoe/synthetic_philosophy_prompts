I work as an ethics compliance auditor for a multinational manufacturing firm, and every month I must review employee objections to mandatory protocols—refusing to sign off on an audit with one skipped step, refusing to purge records the protocol mandates deleting, or refusing to enforce a check deemed redundant. I need a reusable thirty-minute decision procedure that requires no philosophy background or outside counsel, operationalizing the conceptual distinction between German-style folk morality, in which the moral subject is the role-occupant and wrongness is primarily a destabilization of institutional order (a kind of systemic betrayal), and American-style folk morality, in which the moral subject is the whole person and wrongness is primarily self-betrayal through violating one's own conscience. The procedure should be an ordered sequence of tests with clear stop points for sustaining the objection or overriding and requiring compliance, explicit flags for unavoidable judgment calls, and a way to separate role-capacity reasoning from personal-capacity reasoning without pretending the line is always visible. It must handle objections with no legal backing, objections that would cause serious organizational harm if sustained, and objections that conflate dignity with efficiency. Apply it to three cases: (1) an engineer refuses to approve a bridge design that meets every regulatory code but relies on a material she personally distrusts, knowing the board will be furious about delay; (2) a data manager refuses to purge old customer records, admitting he has no legal claim but arguing they might document a future injustice; (3) a factory supervisor refuses to run a safety check she considers redundant and insulting to worker professionalism, where compliance saves no time but she claims it undermines the dignity of the role. Include at least one case where the procedure overrides conscience despite my intuition that individual conscience should usually prevail. Finally, state clearly what the procedure is not designed to do—for example, resolving conflicts between two valid institutional rules, or determining whether the underlying protocol itself is unjust.
