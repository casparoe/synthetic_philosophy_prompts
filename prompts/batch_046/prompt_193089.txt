Write an essay on whether biography can serve as a genuine instrument of moral education, or whether its necessary acts of selection, pairing, and narrative closure inevitably collapse into either hagiography or debunking. Focus on Plutarch's Parallel Lives, in which Greek and Roman figures are matched not for strict historical parallelism but to invite the reader to measure his own character against an exemplary life. Weigh the objection that this practice assumes a "mirror" model of moral learning—simply beholding virtue in narrative form cultivates it—which contemporary moral psychology treats with deep skepticism. Defend a specific verdict on whether a sustainable middle path exists: a form of "exemplary" biography that highlights moral salience without suppressing flaws, and that resists forcing culturally incommensurable virtues into analogical pairs. In justifying your conclusion, address whether the narrative demand for coherence and closure itself distorts moral reality, and whether Plutarch's comparative structure solves that distortion or deepens it by implying that virtue is universally legible across historical contexts.
