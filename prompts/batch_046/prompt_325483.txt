Write a sestina on Amotz Zahavi's handicap principle, using as its six rotating end-words—repeated in the canonical sestina rotation—tail, cost, signal, burden, honest, waste. Let the form do philosophical work: the poem's own structural burden, the relentless repetition and rotation of these terms across six stanzas before the three-line envoi, should analogize the costly signal itself—ornamental difficulty not as decorative excess but as the very mechanism by which fitness is demonstrated. In the content, trace the logic of indexical signaling (that only a genuinely high-quality bearer can sustain the handicap without being destroyed by it) and specifically resist reducing the principle to conventional signaling or to pure evolutionary efficiency: the waste must remain irreducible, functioning as the guarantee of honesty rather than as a defect to be optimized away. The envoi, compressing all six end-words into three lines, should enact the payoff—the moment the burden finally reveals its credibility. Render the argument with enough precision that a philosopher of biology recognizes Zahavi's specific claim and not a generic "survival of the fittest" gloss, yet make the poem fully legible and affecting to a reader who has never encountered the handicap principle.
