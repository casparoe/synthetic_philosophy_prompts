I am drafting a novel that circles around the fragility of remembered love, and I keep returning to Michel Gondry’s Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind as a kind of shadow-text I need to understand philosophically rather than only emotionally. I want to read the film through the Buddhist doctrine of anattā—not as a decorative label, but as a rigorous analytical position—specifically the Abhidharma decomposition of the apparent self into the five skandhas, none of which owns or persists as an essence, together with the Madhyamaka claim that even the mental continuum (santāna) is only conventionally, not ultimately, real. Could you trace where Joel’s journey through the erasure of his memories dramatizes this bundle-theory dissolution of personal identity: for instance, does the Lacuna procedure, which targets relational memories as if they were removable files within the skandhas of saṃskāra (mental formations) and vijñāna (consciousness), implicitly treat the person as an editable narrative without destroying any underlying substrate, and is that precisely the illusion of selfhood that anattā seeks to expose?

At the same time, I need to know where the film resists or even contradicts this reading. The narrative insists on a destined reunion between Joel and Clementine, and on Joel’s final choice to begin the relationship again despite knowing the inevitable pain—does this smuggle back a substantial, romantically essential self that Buddhism would deny, or can it be read as something closer to the bodhisattva’s compassionate return to saṃsāra despite knowing its suffering? I also want you to consider the embodied, spatial texture of memory in the film—the way erasure fails because the body, the world, and unconscious traces retain what the procedure deletes—and ask whether this failure reveals something about personal identity that abstract philosophical argument misses, perhaps that continuity is not located in any single skandha but distributed through environment and habit in ways that make the “no-self” view harder to live than to state. Please do not provide bibliographies or citation lists; I am writing fiction and need a precise, honest critical reading that does not reduce either the film or the doctrine to an illustration of the other, but ends by saying what the work genuinely adds to the philosophical debate about whether we are anything more than the stories we can be made to forget.
