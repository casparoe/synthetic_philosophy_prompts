You serve as the senior decision analyst for a regional public-preparedness grant board that evaluates unsolicited proposals for preemptive interventions—wildlife vaccination, structural retrofitting, stockpiling, rescue protocols—where benefits are probabilistic and long-term while costs are immediate and certain. The board meets monthly, has no in-house philosopher or statistician, and must issue a yes, no, or refer-to-council recommendation with a one-page justification within ten business days. The board has adopted expected-value reasoning as its formal framework, but members are explicitly worried about expected-value fanaticism: the tendency for astronomically large but highly uncertain payoffs to dominate every other consideration and justify disproportionate or unlimited expenditure. Design a reusable procedural checklist that translates this framework into a sequence of ordered questions any board member can apply without advanced mathematics. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a recommendation, and where it must stop and escalate because the conflict is between competing values rather than resolvable by facts alone. The procedure must include explicit “fanaticism brakes”—conditions that flag when a pure expected-value calculation is producing a result that demands resources wildly out of scale with the board’s mandate and that should therefore be handed to democratic deliberation rather than to arithmetic. Then run the procedure on three proposals: (a) a ten-year wildlife vaccination program to prevent a zoonotic spillover estimated at a 0.02 percent probability, with potential regional mortality near 40 percent; (b) mandatory seismic retrofitting of all public schools based on a 0.003 percent risk of total structural collapse over the next century; and (c) redirecting 15 percent of the annual municipal budget to an asteroid-impact mitigation research consortium citing an annual extinction-level probability of one in ten million. Include at least one case where the procedure yields a recommendation the board is likely to find counterintuitive or unwelcome, and conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot adjudicate and must leave to elected judgment.
