I am preparing a philosophical profile on personal identity and origins for a metaphysics reading group, and I would like a consistency audit before I present it. Here is where I have landed. First, I endorse a form of origin essentialism for human persons: necessarily, if I exist, I trace back to the particular sperm-egg fusion that actually produced my zygote, and a being with a different causal origin—even one with identical DNA, identical psychology, and an uninterrupted mental life—is not me. Second, I analyse personal identity over time in terms of psychological continuity rather than bodily or biological continuity; if my consciousness were preserved through a brain transplant into a clone body, I would survive, despite the complete change of biological substrate. Third, I evaluate counterfactual claims about myself using counterpart theory rather than transworld identity: to say I could have studied medicine is to say that in another possible world there is a counterpart sufficiently like me who does so. Fourth, I hold that qualitative identity is not numerical identity for gametes; two sperm cells with exactly the same genetic sequence are still distinct entities, so substituting one for the other changes the origin. Fifth, I apply this consistently to a niche scenario I have been considering: if scientists constructed an embryo de novo in a laboratory, replicating my exact genetic and epigenetic profile without using my parents’ actual gametes or reproductive process, then the adult who developed—psychologically continuous with me—would nevertheless not be me, because the causal origin in my parents’ reproductive event is absent.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict, and how severely. In particular, I am unsure whether my acceptance of psychological continuity undermines my origin essentialism, whether my use of counterpart theory sits comfortably with the claim that I could not have originated differently, and whether my verdict on brain-transplant survival conflicts with my verdict on the synthetic-zygote case. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging, and for each genuine tension say what the least costly revision would be and what it costs. Also, identify the single revision that would clean up the most problems, and say whether any apparent tension dissolves once the distinction between persistence conditions and origin conditions is kept clear.
