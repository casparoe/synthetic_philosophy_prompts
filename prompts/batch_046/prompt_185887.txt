Exam question: In her work on transformative experience, L.A. Paul argues that rational decision-making breaks down for choices that are both epistemically transformative and personally transformative: the agent cannot know in advance what the experience will be like, and the experience may alter the very preferences by which outcomes are evaluated. Consider the following modified case. A research institute offers you a safe, fully reversible one-month neural interface that allows you to live with the complete first-person phenomenology and embodied skill-set of a professional concert pianist. At the end of the month you revert to your prior mental state, though you retain accurate autobiographical memories of the trial period. You must then choose whether to undergo a permanent, irreversible version of the procedure and commit to a life in music. Does this temporary “borrowed” experience resolve Paul’s epistemic problem? Does it resolve her personally transformative problem? In answering, distinguish carefully between knowing-what-it-is-like for a limited duration and knowing-how-to-evaluate a lifelong commitment from one’s present value perspective.

Student answer: Paul’s core claim is that transformative choices undermine standard rational-choice models because we lack first-person access to the post-transformation state and because that state may change our core values. In the neural-interface case, the epistemic problem is clearly solved: the one-month trial gives the agent direct phenomenal knowledge of being a pianist, so she now knows exactly what it is like to inhabit that identity. She can therefore assign an accurate subjective utility to the permanent option.

The personally transformative problem, however, remains partly intact. Even after the trial, the agent returns to her pre-musician value structure, so she still evaluates the lifelong commitment using her old preferences rather than the ones she would actually hold after permanent transformation. Here Paul draws on a continuity-of-identity constraint: because permanent transformation creates a discontinuity in personal identity, the pre-transformation agent is essentially choosing for a different person. Thus rational choice is still blocked—not by ignorance, but because the chooser and the beneficiary are not the same self.

That said, the reversibility of the trial weakens Paul’s argument in one important respect. Since the agent can sample the experience without committing, the choice is no longer genuinely transformative in Paul’s technical sense, because transformative experiences are by definition both epistemically closed and irreversible. Once reversibility is introduced, the decision reduces to a standard risk-assessment problem: weigh the remembered utility of the trial month against the opportunity costs of permanent conversion. So Paul is right that value-change creates difficulty, but she is wrong to think that temporary access fails to restore rationality; it restores rationality precisely by converting an epistemically closed choice into an empirically informed one.
