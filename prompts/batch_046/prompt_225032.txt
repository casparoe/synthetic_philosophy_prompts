I'm trying to work out, for my own understanding and for discussions with friends who haven't studied philosophy formally, exactly where people stand on whether a person is something you become through other people rather than something you carry inside your own skin. I've been thinking about Ubuntu—the idea often summarized as "I am because we are"—but the disagreements I encounter don't seem to split neatly between African and Western frameworks; they seem to turn on what kind of "because" is doing the work. Some people talk as if relationships literally constitute the self, others as if they only reveal or nurture it, and others as if community is just a useful backdrop. I want a decision tree or taxonomy that sorts these relational conceptions of personhood by running them through four concrete boundary cases: a newborn with virtually no social interaction yet, a person with advanced dementia who no longer recognizes anyone, an AI system that can mirror human conversation fluently but has no lived social history, and a hermit who actively chooses permanent isolation.

Please build the instrument from the logical space rather than from a list of named philosophers. The first branch should ask whether social relations are constitutive of personhood or merely instrumentally valuable for developing it. If constitutive, the next branch should ask whether the relation must be reciprocal and consciously recognized, or whether it can be one-directional and implicit—such as being cared for without knowing it. From there, branch on whether the relation requires shared physical presence or can survive mediation, absence, or even fictionalization. Finally, ask whether the complete loss of all such relations destroys personhood altogether or only damages an otherwise intact core. Each leaf should carry a position name—whether established like "strong Ubuntu-style relationalism," "moderate recognition-based view," "background social dependency," "individualist with social benefits," or "unnamed"—plus one sentence describing its most awkward commitment, such as being forced to deny full personhood to the hermit or to grant it to the chatbot. Note any combinations of answers that leave a leaf empty, and say briefly whether that gap looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored. Also flag explicitly any pair of named positions that your tree cannot actually separate, rather than forcing them onto different branches. I don't need bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I just need the diagnostic cases and the branching logic laid out clearly enough that someone could answer the questions without already knowing which philosophical label they belong to.
