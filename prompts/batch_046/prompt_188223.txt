I'm giving the opening remarks at a one-day workshop called 'Moral Weight and Tiny Minds' — half the room are philosophers of mind who've been arguing about the scale argument for years, the other half are conservation biolgists who just want to know why they ought to care about wasps. I want to frame the whole day around a tension I've been calling 'the aggregation paradox of near-zero credence': if you grant even a tiny sliver of probability that any given insect is sentient, and then multiply that across the quadrillions of individuals alive right now, the expected moral weight explodes past anything we can act on, yet our practical reasoning treats that explosion as noise rather than signal. The remarks need to do real structural work — they should explain why this isn't just a maths trick but actually constrains what kinds of policy recommendations we can honestly offer in the closing session, without letting the philosophers hide behind jargon or the biolgists dismiss it as armchair speculation. About four minutes, British English throughout, no bibliography or citation list please — just write it as if I'm at the lecturn with coffee in hand.
