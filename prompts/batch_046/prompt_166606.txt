I'm currently taking an upper-division seminar on analytic and existentialist approaches to the meaning of life, and we're working closely with Moritz Schlick's argument that life acquires significance not through instrumental labor but through activities that are ends in themselves—what he characterizes as play, marked by absorption, freedom from external purpose, and a kind of youthful spontaneity that modern civilization tends to suppress. Alongside my coursework I volunteer with a community hospice that receives a steady stream of proposals for patient activities—guided memoir-writing, virtual-reality travel, intergenerational storytelling, structured art therapy, and unstructured social gatherings—and the coordination staff currently evaluates these by intuition alone, with no shared framework. I would like you to operationalize Schlick's core distinction between work (goal-directed, instrumentally valued) and play (intrinsically valued, self-contained) into a brief decision procedure that a non-philosopher staff member could actually run in about ten minutes per proposal. The procedure should list the questions to ask in sequence, specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, indicate exactly where it issues a clear approval or rejection, and mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated because the issue turns on values rather than descriptive facts. Because Schlick himself tends to treat artistic creation and the vitality of youth as paradigmatic cases, the procedure needs to remain workable when applied to elderly patients and to non-artistic activities, which constitute the bulk of real proposals. Once you have built the procedure, please apply it to three cases: (1) a structured life-review program with explicit therapeutic targets for depression reduction; (2) an unstructured painting session with no evaluation, documentation, or outcome tracking; and (3) a virtual-reality visit to a patient's childhood home that requires significant technical setup and staff assistance. In each case, identify one result that the staff might find unwelcome or counter-intuitive, and conclude by stating precisely what kinds of proposals or value conflicts lie beyond the procedure's reach.
