Compose a poem in unrhymed iambic pentameter, divided into exactly seven stanzas, each spoken by a different witness observing one and the same concrete event—perhaps a fire breaking out in a crowded market, or a tree splitting in a thunderstorm. Frame the poem through Jain anekāntavāda, the doctrine that reality is irreducibly multi-faceted and that no single judgment is unconditionally true. Structure the stanzas according to the sevenfold scheme of syādvāda (conditioned predication): every stanza must open with the phrase “In some respect...” and embody one mode—(1) the event exists; (2) it does not exist; (3) it both exists and does not exist; (4) it is inexpressible; (5) it exists and is inexpressible; (6) it does not exist and is inexpressible; (7) it exists, does not exist, and is inexpressible. Do not arrange the voices as a dialectic that builds toward synthesis; let them accumulate without resolution, and let the recurring phrase “In some respect...” function as a refrain that binds the poem together while constantly undermining any claim to final authority. In the closing lines, have the seventh speaker observe that even this sevenfold testimony leaves the event intact but uncaptured in its full complexity. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with Jain epistemology recognizes the saptabhaṅgī schema, yet work as standalone poetry for a reader who knows nothing of the tradition—no external citation, bibliography, or scholarly apparatus should appear.
