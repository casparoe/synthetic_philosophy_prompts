I work as a software engineer without formal training in philosophy of mind. In a technical discussion group about cognition and AI, a colleague offered this summary of what he called the causal exclusion argument, associated with Jaegwon Kim: "If mental properties are just higher-level patterns realized in physical processes, then the underlying physical processes do all the causal work. The mental patterns are like software running on hardware — they don't push the hardware around; the hardware pushes itself. So in an LLM, any 'belief' or 'intention' is just a pattern over matrix multiplications, which means those mental states are causally inert. Only the tensors cause the outputs. Therefore, attributing mental causation to an AI is a category error, and any theory that lets mental properties cause physical changes violates the causal closure of physics."

He drew from this that mental causation is impossible in artificial systems. I am unsure which parts of this are standard philosophy and which are his own extensions. Please evaluate the summary claim by claim: indicate what is a fair representation of Kim's exclusion problem, what subtly misrepresents it (for example, by conflating non-reductive physicalism with epiphenomenalism, by treating functional patterns as necessarily causally redundant, or by importing a software-hardware analogy that the argument does not require), and whether his conclusion about LLMs and the "category error" actually follows once the argument is corrected. In particular, address whether the exclusion problem applies specifically to distributed representational properties in transformer architectures, or whether it presupposes a form of property dualism that a physicalist about AI cognition could consistently reject.
