After a long and somewhat frustrating argument with a friend last night about origin essentialism—specifically whether I could have come from a different sperm and egg—I have tried to organize my own intuitions into concrete verdicts. I am not looking for a literature review, bibliography, or citations; I want to know whether my judgments cohere into a defensible principle, or whether theoretical simplicity forces me to overturn some of them.

Here are my verdicts on eight cases. In each, assume I actually exist and originated as described, and ask whether I could have existed with the modified origin.

1. Baseline: I come from sperm S and egg E. Could I have come from sperm S' (a different sperm, same parents) and egg E? Verdict: No.

2. Mitochondrial replacement: Sperm S fertilizes egg E, but E's mitochondria are replaced with those from egg M before conception (three-parent IVF). Verdict: Yes, still me; cytoplasmic origin is inessential.

3. Pre-fertilization sperm editing: Sperm S is gene-edited in vitro to correct a lethal mutation before fertilizing E. Verdict: Yes, still me; it is the same sperm, repaired.

4. Post-zygotic editing: The zygote formed by S and E is edited in the first hour, before any cell division, to correct the same mutation. Verdict: Yes, still me.

5. Identical twin split: The zygote from S and E splits; Twin A is me. Could I have been Twin B? Verdict: Yes—we share the same origin zygote, so this is not a different origin, merely a different developmental branch.

6. Synthetic genome fusion: The nuclear genomes of S and E are fully sequenced and then chemically synthesized de novo; the synthetic genomes are fused in vitro and develop into me. Verdict: No. The causal-biological pathway is broken, even though the genome is identical.

7. Morula fusion (chimeric origin): Two embryos, one from S/E and one from P/Q, are merged at the morula stage. The resulting person carries about 85% S/E cells and 15% P/Q cells, and develops with my continuous psychology and memories, tracking the S/E lineage. Verdict: Leaning yes—it is still me, because my psychological and dominant cellular continuity tracks S/E, despite the intrusion.

8. Artificial gamete reprogramming: Skin cells from my parents are reprogrammed into gametes Gs and Ge with nuclear genomes identical to S and E, then fused. Verdict: No—the origin is not the actual sperm and egg, even if genetically identical.

The pattern is not obvious. Cases 2–4 suggest that modifications preserving the nuclear-causal identity of S and E are permissible. Case 5 suggests that sharing the zygote is sufficient. Case 6 suggests that breaking the biological causal chain destroys origin, even with perfect genetic replication. Case 7 is the hard case: if my origin is essentially the S/E pathway, then the P/Q intrusion should matter; yet my intuition is that I survive the merge because my continuity tracks the dominant lineage. Meanwhile, Case 5 says I could have been Twin B, but Twin B has a different psychological continuity from me; so if psychological continuity matters, Case 5 should be false, yet I think it is true.

Please address the following in this order and with approximate lengths indicated.

Section 1 — Reconstruction of the pattern (about 150 words): State precisely what principle or small family of principles captures Cases 1–8, noting which clauses are motivated by which cases. If more than one principle is needed, present them as competing candidates.

Section 2 — Predictions for unlisted cases (about 200 words): Apply your leading principle(s) to at least three further cases I have not listed: (a) cryopreservation of the S/E zygote for fifty years before implantation; (b) a case in which the egg's nuclear DNA is replaced with that of egg E but the cytoplasm comes from egg M, and the sperm is replaced with a synthetic copy of S; (c) a case in which my brain is gradually replaced with synthetic neurons that preserve my psychology, while my body remains biologically continuous with the original S/E embryo.

Section 3 — Which verdicts must be overturned (about 150 words): Identify which of my eight verdicts your leading principle cannot accommodate. Be explicit about whether you overturn the chimeric-merge verdict (Case 7), the twin-branch verdict (Case 5), or another, and defend the choice on grounds of theoretical simplicity rather than ad hoc saving.

Section 4 — The niche twist: cytoplasmic versus nuclear essentialism (about 150 words): I have been treating cytoplasmic origin as inessential (Case 2) but treating synthetic replication of nuclear DNA as essential-breaking (Case 6). Explain whether this distinction is stable, or whether a defender of nuclear essentialism must also reject mitochondrial replacement, and whether there is a principled middle ground that allows Case 2 but blocks Case 6 without invoking psychological continuity.

Section 5 — Settled versus genuinely contested (about 150 words): Separate what you take to be relatively settled in the philosophy of origin essentialism (for example, the basic modal claim about gametes) from what is genuinely contested among philosophers who work on this (for example, the role of causal continuity versus genetic identity, the status of cloning, or the relevance of psychological continuity to origin questions). Do not present a contested view as the consensus.
