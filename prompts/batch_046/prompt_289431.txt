I am a junior in a political philosophy and public policy seminar (approximately 20 students, 90-minute session) working on our unit about workfare, benefit fraud, and the moral categorization of claimants. For our final meeting, I need you to propose a structured classroom simulation—not a lecture or reading discussion—that makes participants feel how conditionality regimes actively construct the figure of the "undeserving poor" rather than simply detecting pre-existing fraud. The activity should have explicit rules, assigned roles, and small stakes: divide the group into claimants (each given a hidden profile pairing a genuine need level with an observable work-search behavior score), caseworkers operating under a fixed audit budget, and an appeals board with limited review capacity. Claimants receive full benefits only if they pass either a low-cost behavioral audit or a high-cost, noisy needs assessment; the group begins with resources split evenly between the two protocols, then votes mid-game to tighten conditionality by shifting funds from needs assessment to behavioral monitoring. The mechanics should be arranged so that this shift causes several claimants with high genuine need but weak observable behavior to be reclassified as undeserving and sanctioned, even though the underlying distribution of need has not changed—participants should discover this pattern through the payoff structure and record-keeping, not through prior explanation. Before the simulation begins, do not reveal the conceptual thesis; name it only in the debrief. After the game, provide a structured debrief that maps each phase—budget allocation, audit selection, appeals outcomes—onto a corresponding feature of welfare-state conditionality; predict at least two predictable misfires in actual classroom use (for example, role confusion or emotional defensiveness) and specify how a facilitator should handle each; and be explicit about which dimension of the problem—perhaps the macro-political economy of stigma, the lived phenomenology of applying for aid, or the long-term fiscal feedback loop—the rules cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator verbally. Do not include a bibliography, assigned readings, or citation list in your design, and avoid asking participants to consult specific texts during the exercise.
