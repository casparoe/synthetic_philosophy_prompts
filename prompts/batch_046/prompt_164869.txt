I am a high school student writing an independent study essay on the philosophy of medicine, specifically on whether disease is purely a matter of biological dysfunction—what philosophers call naturalism—or whether labeling something a disease necessarily involves a value judgment that it is harmful or undesirable, as normativists claim. Rather than asking for a bibliography or a survey of authors, I want to see whether my own intuitive verdicts about particular cases can be unified by a coherent principle, or whether some of them must be dropped.

Here are my judgments on six concrete situations:

1. Athlete's heart: an endurance athlete develops a resting heart rate near 40 bpm and mild cardiac enlargement. Function is enhanced, not impaired, and the person reports excellent health. Verdict: not a disease.

2. Congenital insensitivity to pain: an individual is born with a mutation that eliminates nociception. They report high autonomy and life satisfaction, yet they suffer repeated undetected injuries. Verdict: this is a disease—a failure of a species-typical protective mechanism—even though subjective well-being is high.

3. Menopause at age 52: complete, permanent cessation of ovarian function occurring well within the typical age range. Verdict: not a disease; it is a normal life-stage transition, not a dysfunction.

4. Severe myopia (approximately −8 diopters) in a population where over 85% of young adults share it, requiring corrective lenses for standard tasks such as driving. Verdict: I lean toward calling this a disease, because the visual system fails to perform a standard human function, but I feel clear tension with Case 3, since both are statistically common within their reference groups.

5. Aphantasia: a lifelong inability to form voluntary mental images, present in a small minority, causing no physical harm and only mild personal frustration. Verdict: not a disease—merely a cognitive variant, not a harmful dysfunction.

6. Hypothetical microbiome-null state with synthetic replacement: imagine a person born with zero gut bacteria, but engineered synthetic vesicles fully replicate all digestive, metabolic, and immune-modulatory functions. The individual thrives, reports normal well-being, and shows no inflammatory markers. Verdict: not a disease, despite being a radical departure from universal human biology.

The pair that troubles me most involves Cases 2, 5, and 6. In Case 2 I invoke dysfunction of a protective system despite well-being; in Case 6 I deny disease despite the complete absence of a universal biological system, apparently because replacement is complete. In Case 5 there is no replacement—only absence of a cognitive capacity—yet I still deny disease. So is my underlying principle that disease requires (a) failure of a species-typical function, (b) absence of adequate internal or external replacement, and (c) either objective harm or significant functional impairment? Or is there a simpler, more consistent account?

Please propose one or more precise principles that capture as many of my verdicts as possible. For each principle, state which clause is motivated by which case, predict what it would say about a further situation I have not considered—say, a society that deliberately induces the microbiome-null state in healthy infants for environmental reasons—and honestly state which of my six verdicts it cannot accommodate without revision. Also, please say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
