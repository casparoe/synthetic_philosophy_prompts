For a philosophy of mind seminar, I have been working out my interpretation of Descartes's cogito and want to check whether the pieces actually fit together before I present them. Here is the profile: (1) I take the cogito to establish only that there is an occurrence of thinking; it proves the existence of a particular conscious state, but it licenses no inference to a persisting mental substance, an immaterial soul, or an essentially non-physical thinker—on my reading, the argument is compatible with my being a temporary, physically realized configuration that happens to be thinking right now. (2) The move from thinking to existing is not an enthymeme with a suppressed major premise (all thinking things exist); rather, existence is co-given with the act of thinking in an immediate intuition, which is why skeptical scenarios do not block the inference. (3) I think the cogito remains robust even in radically deceptive conditions—if I am a Boltzmann brain, a brain in a vat, or an artificial simulation—because the argument requires only the subjective occurrence of seeming-to-think, not any reliable connection to an external world or a stable substrate. (4) I also hold that the first-person term in the cogito functions purely as an indexical marker rather than as a proper name or a referring expression that picks out a diachronic entity; there is no enduring ego revealed here, only a sequence of discrete cognitive events each carrying a momentary self-reference. (5) Despite this, I want to defend a robust, irreducible first-person authority about mental states, and I want to ground it precisely in the kind of immediate self-presence that commitments (2) and (3) describe. (6) Yet I worry that (4) undermines (5): if there is no persisting subject—only disconnected momentary thoughts—then there is no unified proprietor of mental states, and first-person authority collapses into a series of isolated glimpses without an owner who could possess knowledge across time. Do these commitments form a coherent package, or am I smuggling a substantial self back in through (5)? Please rank the tensions by severity. In particular, is the conflict between (4) and (5) fatal, costly but manageable, or only apparent once we distinguish different kinds of authority? Is there also a hidden tension between (1) and (3)—for instance, does the robustness of the cogito in deceptive scenarios depend on precisely the minimal, non-substantial reading I give in (1), or does (3) secretly require more? And if I must revise exactly one commitment to restore coherence with minimal damage, which should go, and what does giving it up cost me?
