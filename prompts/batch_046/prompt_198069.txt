Following a long and somewhat circular argument with a friend last night about whether linguistic meaning can be anchored in private mental states without public constraint, I have volunteered to lead a single session for a philosophy reading group—first-year graduate students in epistemology who have never taken philosophy of language but are fluent in basic formal logic—on Davidson's account of radical interpretation. I want a teaching plan, not the explanation itself. Specifically: lay out the dependency order in which the pieces must be introduced so that the interdependence of belief and meaning feels necessary rather than contrived; identify the one carrying scenario that should run through the session, which must be an invented "synthetic triangulation" case—an interpreter decoding an artificial agent's outputs solely through behavioral correlation and environmental interaction, with no shared evolutionary history—rather than the standard field-linguist example, and specify both what this scenario must contain and what it must deliberately lack; map the misconceptions this audience will predictably form or bring with them—such as taking "radical" to indicate a political position, treating the principle of charity as a moral prescription to be generous, conflating the thesis with Quine's indeterminacy of translation, or reading Davidson as a behaviorist—and indicate exactly where in the sequence each should be defused; provide one check-for-understanding question for the end that reveals whether they have grasped the simultaneous assignment of beliefs and meanings rather than merely memorized a slogan; and state clearly what should be omitted at this level—such as the formal Tarskian truth-theoretic apparatus or decision-theoretic refinements—while explicitly noting the omission. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
