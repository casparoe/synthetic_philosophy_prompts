Analyze Einstein’s 1915 recovery of Mercury’s anomalous perihelion precession—the 43 arcseconds per century that had been documented since Le Verrier’s 1859 orbital calculations—as a case study in Bayesian epistemology’s problem of old evidence. The salient historical facts are these: Einstein already knew the Mercury value when he formulated general relativity; in 1913 he and Besso had applied the Entwurf field equations and obtained only about 18 arcseconds, which he treated as a decisive failure of that theory; in November 1915 he applied the corrected field equations and derived 43 arcseconds exactly, taking the match as a profound confirmation. Through the lens of Bayesian confirmation theory, address the following: first, map the evidential structure—what counts as the prior for GR in late 1915, what counts as the old evidence (the Mercury anomaly), and what counts as the competing hypotheses (Newtonian mechanics with an undetected intra-Mercurial body, or the discredited Entwurf theory); second, explain how a subjective Bayesian, in the manner of Howson, should treat the fact that Einstein knew the target value before deriving it—does prior knowledge of the evidence diminish or alter its confirmatory force, and how should the agent’s confidence in GR be updated; third, examine whether Glymour’s bootstrapping worry applies here, given that Einstein used the same gravitational-field equations both to derive orbital dynamics and to account for the anomaly, and assess whether the 1913 Entwurf miscalculation functions as an independent check that blocks bootstrapping; fourth, identify what the episode reveals about the limits of Bayesian analysis—for instance, whether Einstein’s strong prior commitment to mathematical coherence and simplicity should be modeled inside the Bayesian framework or treated as an external heuristic that confirmation theory cannot properly capture; and finally, state whether the Mercury case ultimately supports the view that old evidence can genuinely confirm a newly proposed theory, undermines that view, or forces a revision of how Bayesians distinguish the context of discovery from the context of justification.
