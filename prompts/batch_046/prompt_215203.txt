You sit on your university's Committee on Named Endowments and Archival Ethics. A donor has offered to fund a permanent gallery, "Rational Immortality," displaying the private notebooks of mathematician Kurt Gödel and endowing an annual lecture series. The notebooks contain an unpublished modal-logic sketch—building on Gödel's well-known ontological argument—to claim that personal identity is necessarily preserved across all possible worlds in which consciousness exists, making an afterlife logically obligatory rather than merely probable. The donor's family insists this is rigorous formal philosophy, not religious advocacy, and requires that any display present the sketch as an active research program; they further stipulate that the lecture series invite only scholars who take the modal premises seriously. Your institution's secular charter and its commitment to open, pluralistic inquiry are in tension. Please draft the short written opinion you will circulate before Thursday's meeting: it should recommend a concrete course of action—accept, reject, or restructure the gift, with a specific 90-day implementation timeline—and it should engage the family's position seriously rather than dismissing the argument as pseudoscience. In particular, address the strongest consideration on the losing side: whether treating Gödel's afterlife reasoning as theology misclassifies a formal proof, or whether institutionalizing it violates neutrality.
