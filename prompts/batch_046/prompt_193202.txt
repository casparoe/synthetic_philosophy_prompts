I am trying to understand a passage from Immanuel Kant's Critique of Pure Reason, in the Transcendental Dialectic's treatment of the ontological argument. Kant argues, in substance, that being is not a real predicate: if I form the complete concept of a hundred thalers with all their internal determinations, and then add that they actually exist, the concept gains no new content; existence is not an additional property within the concept but rather the positing of an object corresponding to it.

This raises a difficulty for me when I turn to Gödel's modal version of the ontological argument, reconstructed from his unpublished notes. Gödel does not append bare existence to the concept of God. Instead, he defines God as that being which possesses all positive properties, demonstrates that necessary existence is itself a positive property, and then employs modal logic—specifically the system S5—to show that if God is possible in at least one world, then God exists necessarily in every world. The existence established is therefore not an external predicate but a modal status internal to the closure of positive properties.

Here is where I need help. First, does Kant's claim that being is not a real predicate apply with equal force to Gödel's argument? On one hand, necessary existence is embedded in the concept of God via the closure of positive properties, which might seem to make it part of the concept in a way bare existence is not. On the other hand, one might reply that Gödel's proof still requires the premise that God is possible—that is, that there is at least one world in which all positive properties are co-instantiated—and that this premise smuggles in an existential commitment that Kant's objection was designed to block.

Second, I want to examine a more specific structural feature. Gödel's argument depends on an axiom about positive properties: for any property P, either P or its negation is positive, but never both. Suppose we weaken this axiom and allow what I will call overlapping modal properties—properties that can be co-instantiated without contradiction, but whose negations are not positively excluded. In particular, imagine a scenario in which God possesses every genuinely positive property, yet lacks necessary existence because necessary existence is not itself in the base set of positive properties but is only derivable under the strong exclusivity axiom. If we construct this twin-modal ontology, does Gödel's proof collapse entirely, or can it be repaired by strengthening the modal axioms—for instance, by adding a principle that possibility entails actualization across worlds? And if it collapses, does that show that Gödel has not so much answered Kant as relocated the ungrounded existential assumption into the modal framework?

Please explain the central claim of the passage about concept-formation and existence, then address whether Gödel's apparatus genuinely evades Kant's critique or merely disguises it, paying particular attention to whether necessary existence functions as a real predicate within Gödel's property-based ontology. Work through the modal reasoning directly, without appealing to secondary literature or providing reading lists.
