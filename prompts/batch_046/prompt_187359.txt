Here are my intuitive verdicts on whether different institutional arrangements solve, sidestep, or succumb to the economic calculation problem, and I should like to know whether one principle captures the pattern.

When Oscar Lange's trial-and-error method is applied only to consumer goods, with capital goods allocated administratively, I think the static allocation problem is largely solved but the dynamic restructuring of capital remains broken. When a Soviet-style material-balances board updates input-output tables quarterly using factory self-reports, I think the failure is not mainly computational but motivational: managers systematically distort capacity figures to secure easier quotas, so prices never converge on real scarcity. If we imagine a planner with perfect omniscience about every preference ordering and production function, running on unlimited computing power, I still think economic coordination fails, but now for an epistemic reason: the competitive process through which preferences are discovered and revised is eliminated, not merely accelerated. By contrast, a market-socialist system in which worker cooperatives trade intermediate goods at negotiated prices but the state fixes all capital budgets and wages strikes me as eventually self-defeating, because correct pricing of intermediates depends on upstream capital valuations that the state has distorted. Yet a small face-to-face commune allocating resources by direct deliberation without money does not seem to suffer the calculation problem at all; it sidesteps it by operating within a single mind-like community rather than solving it at scale.

Is there one account of why planning fails that delivers all five verdicts—distinguishing informational, motivational, and discovery-based failures without collapsing them into one another? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and what does the surviving principle predict for a case I have not listed: a digital planning architecture that uses differential privacy to aggregate real-time consumption data and convex optimisation to adjust prices continuously, but leaves capital investment to algorithmic forecasting rather than entrepreneurial bidding?

Please state your principle precisely, identify which clause explains each of my verdicts, note any tension between them, and reply in British English.
