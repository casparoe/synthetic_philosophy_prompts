I serve on our university's Public Humanities Funding Committee, which must decide whether to fund a proposed permanent exhibition, "Famine and Agency: From Bengal to Anhui." The exhibition applies Amartya Sen's entitlement approach to frame the 1943 Bengal famine, the Great Irish Famine of the 1840s, and the food crisis during China's Great Leap Forward not as natural disasters but as politically constituted collapses of distributive entitlement—mediated, respectively, by wartime price controls and administrative failure, colonial extraction coupled with export policy, and local cadre incentives together with statistical suppression.

The committee is divided. One faction supports funding but insists the curatorial narrative must distinguish whether Sen's framework genuinely treats these three cases as instances of the same philosophical kind—namely, failures of distributive justice rather than ecological misfortune—or whether applying it uniformly flattens structurally different mechanisms into a single moral verdict. The opposing faction argues that labeling any famine a natural disaster is a category error that obscures agency, and demands an unambiguous normative framing without hedging.

Please draft the committee's written opinion recommending whether to approve the proposal, approve it with revised curatorial conditions, or reject it. Your memo should assess whether the entitlement analysis can coherently distinguish famine from starvation and whether it applies without anachronism to pre-democratic or colonial contexts; it should include a concrete recommendation with specific conditions or a timeline for revision; and it should treat the strongest objection to your position with intellectual honesty rather than dismissal.
