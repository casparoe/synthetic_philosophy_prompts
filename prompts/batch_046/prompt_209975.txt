Please describe the most important implications of Hofstadter's view that the self is a distributed, substrate-independent pattern—capable of persisting across brains through shared symbolic loops—for evaluating personal identity in progressive brain-division scenarios. Consider cases in which split-brain patients maintain continuous narrative coherence across hemispheres via artificial interhemispheric protocols, allowing mnemonic and symbolic access to remain partially shared rather than fully partitioned. Address whether the persistence of an "I" in another brain requires identical neural substrate, shared mnemonic access, or only isomorphic loop structure; what this implies for the moral and legal status of the resulting divided individuals; and whether partial persistence should be understood as dilution of a single strange loop or as the emergence of genuinely distinct but overlapping loops. Focus on the conceptual structure of Hofstadter's framework rather than empirical neuroscience, and do not provide bibliographies or reading lists.
