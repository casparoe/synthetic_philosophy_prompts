I am currently studying moral philosophy and have become particularly interested in the boundary between strict duty and acts that seem to go beyond it. During a recent seminar, my tutor introduced William James’s idea of a moral holiday—the notion that morality might permit occasional exemptions from constant self-sacrifice—which I had not previously considered in any detail. This led me to reflect on whether supererogation can be coherently accommodated within a Kantian framework, specifically in relation to the universalisability tests.

My confusion centres on the logical status of maxims underlying supererogatory action. Consider a physician who, having completed her contractual shift, voluntarily remains for an additional twelve hours during a mass-casualty event despite having no institutional obligation to do so. If we formulate her maxim as something like I will provide care beyond contractual obligation when extreme need arises, it appears to pass both the contradiction-in-conception and contradiction-in-will tests without difficulty. Yet if a maxim can be universalised in this way, does it not generate a perfect duty for all similarly situated agents, thereby eliminating the very optionality that makes her act supererogatory rather than obligatory? Conversely, if the maxim fails universalisation, does her action become morally impermissible rather than merely praiseworthy?

I wonder whether this tension reveals a structural limitation in Kant’s account—namely, that universalisability cannot distinguish between obligatory and excellent-but-optional conduct—or whether I am conflating the formal test of a maxim with the quantitative demands it imposes. Does the distinction between perfect and imperfect duties resolve this, and might James’s concept of a moral holiday help clarify why some acts remain genuinely optional despite being morally excellent? I should be grateful if you could identify precisely where my reasoning has gone astray and address these points in British English.
