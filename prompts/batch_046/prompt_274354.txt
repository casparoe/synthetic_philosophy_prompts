I've been feeling pretty lost lately since my freelance editing work dried up and now I spend half my day prompting AI to write things for me, which makes me wonder if meaning is just something we outsource now. Anyway, I was reading about Camus and his whole thing with the absurd — I think he died in the 60s? — and I started wondering what he would make of language models that can churn out endless coherent stories about rebellion and purpose without ever actually caring. Would he say the absurd has just gotten deeper, or that these machines actually prove his point that meaning is totally constructed?

There's also this idea that occured to me, kind of a niche twist on his framework, that I call recursive absurdism: not just recognizing that life lacks inherent meaning, but recognizing that the very act of recognizing it can now be automated and perfomed by systems that don't experience anything. So the rebellion Camus talks about — like, choosing to keep pushing the rock up the hill anyway — gets turned into a kind of performance that an algorithm can simulate definatly. Would Camus think that kills the human part of it, or would he say it just shows that happiness was always a kind of roleplay? I know he never saw LLMs, but I'm trying to figure out if his philosophy can even handle a world where that struggle is basically done by code.
