Write a deadpan bureaucratic memo of about 550 words in the voice of a senior administrator at the fictional Ministry of Ontological Integrity, announcing the full enforcement of the “Aarhus Compromise” regulations on mereological composition. The piece should read as a genuine internal government circular—dry, formal, preoccupied with compliance forms and inspection schedules—while satirizing the special composition question itself: the problem of when parts make a whole, the arbitrariness of any boundary drawn between them, and the counterintuitive fallout of both unrestricted universalism (an explosion of arbitrary objects) and strict nihilism (the denial that tables or people exist). As part of the invented regulatory framework, specify exactly seven recognized “bonding categories” through which parts may legitimately compose an entity; include familiar ones like functional integration and gravitational binding, but also inject genuinely strange ones such as “thermal sympathy” and “narrative continuity.” Detail the practical administrative consequences: citizens must file registration forms declaring which category licenses their composite objects, inspectors are overwhelmed by backlogs of disputed entities, penalties apply for “illicit fusion,” and exemption boards must adjudicate whether a given coffee table or human body qualifies. Let the standard philosophical objections emerge only as bureaucratic nightmares—complaints about ad hoc lists, protests that the rules generate absurd entities, and confusion over whether denying all composition eliminates the ministry itself—without ever naming them as philosophical arguments and without naming any real philosophers. The target is the debate and its characteristic moves, not any person. Keep the irony sustained and deadpan: the reader should recognize the satire without any winking. Do not include headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or other formatting devices; use continuous prose only, as in a formal letter or email. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
