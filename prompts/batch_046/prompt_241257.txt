For a reading-group consistency audit, I want to lay out my commitments on future contingency and logical fatalism—centered on Aristotle's sea-battle problem but pushed into territory where standard labels do not quite fit. Here is my profile:

(a) I maintain bivalence for all future-tensed statements: for any event E and future time t, either E will occur at t is true now or E will not occur at t is true now. I reject restricted-bivalence solutions because they seem to create an unexplained epistemic asymmetry—we can verify structurally identical past-tensed claims but are told to suspend judgment on their future analogues solely in virtue of temporal location.

(b) I understand contingency not as the absence of a true future-tensed proposition, but as the absence of present causal determination. So a sea battle tomorrow is contingent not because there is now no fact of the matter, but because present conditions do not necessitate it, even if one of the bivalent statements happens to be true.

(c) I treat high-confidence probabilistic forecasts—say, a meteorological model assigning a 70 percent probability to weather conditions that would trigger a naval conflict—as describing objective chances distributed over branches that are all metaphysically real, rather than as measures of our ignorance about a single predetermined future.

(d) I want to defend a compatibilist line about foreknowledge: if an omniscient being knew now that the battle will occur, that knowledge would not cause the battle or undermine its contingency, since knowledge is not causally efficacious.

(e) At the same time, I insist that if determinism is false, then some present truths about the future must be grounded in present dispositions or tendencies that fall short of necessitation—otherwise I cannot see what makes one branch more probable than another.

Please trace the tensions. Which pairs are genuinely in conflict and which only look like it? Rank them by severity, and for each identify the least costly revision and exactly what it costs me. In particular, examine whether (a) and (b) together collapse into a disguised determinism: if there is now a true statement about tomorrow's battle, and contingency is only the absence of causal necessitation, what work is left for the true statement to do? Also examine whether (c)'s branching realism is compatible with (e)'s grounding in present dispositions—does treating all branches as real prevent those dispositions from being genuinely probabilistic rather than deterministic? And check whether (d) is consistent with (b): if causal determination is the only factor that threatens contingency, and knowledge is not causal, then my reason for worrying about determinism seems to disappear, leaving my defense of foreknowledge unmotivated.

Finally, note any hidden tension I have missed—especially one involving whether my view permits present knowledge of genuinely free future actions without implicitly reducing freedom to present causal tendencies.
