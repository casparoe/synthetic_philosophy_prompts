I am working through Tolstoy’s well-known essay on art and have gradually come to accept a strict version of his infection theory: art succeeds when it transmits the artist’s feeling directly to the recipient, and genuinely good art must be that which promotes universal human brotherhood rather than division, elitism, or particularistic devotion. From this it follows that much technically accomplished work—Shakespeare’s plays, Wagner’s operas, certain forms of decorative painting—is not merely inferior art but corrupt art, because its emotional effect either divides audiences or appeals only to a privileged sensibility. I find this compelling as an explanation for why some celebrated works leave me unmoved despite their evident craft, yet I worry that the moral criterion is doing all the work and that the infection mechanism is merely decorative. I want an honest assessment of what would count against this position. Beyond the standard objection that it collapses aesthetic value into moral value, which specific cases or categories of art generate internal contradictions? For example, does Tolstoy’s own admiration for certain religious narratives sit uneasily with the universal brotherhood criterion, since religious feeling is often sectarian rather than universal? Are there works—abstract compositions, purely formal music, or silent visual pieces—that seem artistically significant yet transmit no identifiable emotional content at all, forcing the theory either to deny their status arbitrarily or to expand the idea of infection beyond recognition? Furthermore, if the defender responds by refining either the transmission mechanism or the moral condition, at what point does the view become insulated against counter-example? Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which ones a committed Tolstoyan would themselves concede as damaging rather than dismissing, and say whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability. Please answer in British English.
