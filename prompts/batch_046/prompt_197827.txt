I am currently studying George Eliot's 1856 Westminster Review essay 'Silly Novels by Lady Novelists' and have become particularly stuck on a passage that seems to intertwine religious sociology with an emerging aesthetic theory. The passage reads as follows: "The real drama of Evangelicalism—and it has abundance of fine drama for any one who has genius enough to discern and reproduce it—lies among the middle and lower classes; and are not Evangelical opinions understood to give an especial interest in the weak things of the earth, rather than in the mighty?" I am unsure whether Eliot intends a descriptive claim about where evangelical belief actually takes root, a normative claim about which social strata serious fiction ought to represent, or whether the rhetorical appeal to "the weak things of the earth" is meant sincerely or with an ironic distance from evangelical self-understanding.

What further complicates my interpretation is my attempt to relate this moment to her later Feuerbachian humanism—specifically her conviction that moral progress depends upon human sympathy rather than supernatural sanction—without anachronistically importing her mature "religion of humanity" into this early journalistic work. Would you help me determine whether the passage is best read as a proto-secular critique of evangelical fiction's class blindness, or whether it retains a genuinely religious (if heterodox) appreciation for the moral dignity of ordinary lives? I should also be grateful for any observations on whether her appeal to "genius" here anticipates her later theory of artistic sympathy, or whether it remains confined to a more conventional Romantic aesthetics. Please reply in British English.
