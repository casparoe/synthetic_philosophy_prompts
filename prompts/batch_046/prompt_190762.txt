I was sitting in on a graduate seminar on modal metaphysics, and after the session two students—call them A and B—got into an extended exchange about counterpart theory and transworld identity that went well beyond the standard textbook comparison. I want you to adjudicate their disagreement move by move, treating each contribution as a specific argumentative step rather than a general stance. Here is the substance of what was said.

A opened by arguing that transworld identity is straightforwardly incoherent: if one and the same object exists in world W₁ with property F and in world W₂ without F, then it genuinely instantiates both F and not-F, and world-relativizing properties is merely an ad hoc labeling device that avoids contradiction without resolving it. B replied that this mischaracterizes modal discourse: to say that Obama could have been a philosopher is not to ascribe contradictory intrinsic properties to a single object, but to claim that there is a world in which an object—namely Obama—possesses that property, with properties being implicitly world-indexed all along. A then pressed a more targeted objection involving personal fission: consider a world in which a person undergoes a perfect physical and psychological split, yielding two successors each retaining roughly half the original’s memories and psychological traits. On the transworld identity view, the original person must be identical to both successors, violating the transitivity of identity; on the counterpart view, the original simply has two distinct counterparts in that world, with no contradiction arising. B conceded that fission is awkward but argued that it can be deflected by denying that such branching is metaphysically possible for persons—perhaps because personal identity requires a form of psychological continuity that cannot genuinely diverge. B then turned the argument around: counterpart theory renders modal properties extrinsic and relational, so whether I could have been a musician depends not on anything intrinsic to me but on whether some other-worldly individual stands in the appropriate similarity relation to me. This seems wrong—our modal capacities ought to depend on our own natures, not on the existence of surrogates. A responded that this extrinsicness is precisely the theoretical payoff: modal claims are not about what happens inside me across worlds, but about which other-worldly individuals represent my possibilities, and that weighting similarity by causal origin together with qualitative continuity preserves ordinary intuitions without ontological commitment to my literal existence elsewhere. B’s final move was that the similarity metric is hopelessly indeterminate: if causal origin is weighted heavily, counterparts become near-duplicates and the view collapses back into transworld identity; if qualitative similarity dominates, then in worlds with radically different causal histories I might have a counterpart who shares none of my origins, making “I could have been a musician” depend on the existence of what is essentially a stranger.

Please go through the exchange step by step. For each of the four moves—A’s initial incoherence charge, B’s world-indexing reply, A’s fission objection, and B’s extrinsicness-and-vagueness counter—rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then state which side has the stronger overall position, identify what both parties are missing—particularly whether a graded or asymmetric counterpart relation could split the difference without collapsing into either extreme—and assess whether the fission case genuinely decides between the views or merely exposes a shared problem about the conditions of diachronic identity.
