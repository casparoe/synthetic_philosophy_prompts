I direct a small research laboratory, and a senior technician—previously meticulous—recently ruined an irreplaceable batch of samples through a procedural error. The damage is serious but recoverable. When I spoke with him, he was visibly distressed and explained that the mistake occurred during an episode of severe cognitive fog caused by a new medication for a recently diagnosed neurological condition. He had not disclosed the diagnosis or its side effects to our safety office, fearing immediate removal from sensitive projects. I now find myself caught between two unstable responses. When I focus on the concealed risk and the tangible harm, I feel a genuine impulse to hold him strictly accountable—perhaps by revoking his lab access—because someone who knowingly hides a dangerous impairment seems to forfeit the ordinary protection from anger and blame that we extend to one another as fellow agents. But when I consider the involuntary nature of his medical state, his obvious shame, and the institutional pressure that made concealment seem rational, that anger dissolves entirely into sympathy; I feel that reprimanding him would be like reprimanding a malfunctioning instrument, and I want to restructure his duties to accommodate him rather than punish him. The difficulty is not practical but conceptual. I sense that my attitude ought to track whether he is genuinely answerable for what happened, not merely whether harm occurred or whether he now suffers. At the same time, I worry that if I allow the medical explanation to fully dissolve my resentment, I may be shifting from relating to him as a fellow agent—someone with whom I can genuinely be angry, forgive, or seek reconciliation—to treating him merely as an object to be managed, repaired, and monitored. How should I understand this tension? Is there a principled way to determine when a hidden, mitigating cause should modify responses like anger and blame without eliminating the kind of interpersonal standing that makes those responses appropriate in the first place? And does the fact that he actively concealed the risk—rather than being unaware of it—restore a form of answerability that a purely causal explanation of the error would otherwise erase?
