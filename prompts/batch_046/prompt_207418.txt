I've been working through my position on the special composition question for a graduate seminar I'm convening next term, and I want to make sure the package I'm presenting is internally coherent rather than an eclectic collection of intuitions. Here is where I stand. First, I reject universalism: there is no object composed of my left shoe and the moon, because unrestricted composition violates any plausible constraint on genuine unity. Second, I accept restricted composition, but I want the restriction formulated in functional-structural terms rather than biological ones: parts compose a whole when they are arranged so as to realize a unified causal profile or organizational scheme, which allows artifacts and perhaps certain social entities but excludes scattered aggregates. Third, I deny mereological essentialism; a table can survive the gradual replacement of its legs, provided the replacement preserves the relevant organizational scheme and spatiotemporal continuity. Fourth, I take persistence conditions for composites to be continuity-based rather than part-based: the same chair persists through gradual part-replacement so long as functional and spatiotemporal continuity are maintained. Fifth, I also want to maintain that composite objects possess genuinely emergent causal powers—not reducible to the powers of their parts—since without this there seems no ontological motivation for preferring restricted composition over nihilism. Sixth, and here is where I feel the strain: in a hypothetical case of gradual biological fusion, where two organisms are slowly integrated into one continuous functioning system with no sharp boundary, I want to say that the original two do not survive, even though each maintains spatiotemporal continuity with parts of the resulting system.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe is each conflict? I am particularly concerned that my continuity-based persistence conditions in the third and fourth commitments may be incompatible with the determinate non-survival verdict in the sixth, since the fusion case seems to preserve exactly the kind of spatiotemporal and functional continuity that I elsewhere treat as sufficient for survival. At the same time, I worry that the second commitment together with the fourth commits me to vague boundaries—if organizational schemes admit of degrees, there may be no fact of the matter about when the table ends and a numerically distinct table begins—while the sixth presupposes a sharp answer about survival in fusion. Is there also a hidden tension between the fifth and the second, or does the appeal to emergent powers dissolve once we recognize that arrangement-dependent emergence is still genuinely novel? Rank the tensions by severity, specify for each what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs me, and tell me which single change would clean up the most problems without abandoning the core anti-nihilist, anti-universalist stance I want to preserve for the seminar.
