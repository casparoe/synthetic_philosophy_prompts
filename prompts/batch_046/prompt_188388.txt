I'm writing a paper for my sociology of gender class, well, trying to, and I'm stuck on something that feels more like philosophy than policy analysis. So in a bunch of countries with generous leave — Sweden, Canada, parts of the EU — you have these gender-neutral systems with shared months plus reserved "daddy months" that expire if not used. The goal is clearly to push fathers into care work. But at the same time, the childcare subsidies and tax breaks are mostly built for dual-earner households where both parents work full-time, and there's basically no support for families where one parent wants to stay home longer or work part-time without losing benefits. Even worse, the income replacement during leave is calculated on your salary in the 12 months before birth, so if you worked part-time after your first kid, your second leave is smaller — which basically penalizes anyone who steps off the full-time career track.

So the whole cluster seems to presuppose this very specific vision: equality means two identical career trajectories interrupted only briefly, care is a temporary deviation from market work, and the good family is one that returns to full-time dual employment as fast as possible. What philosophical commitments about gender, welfare, or the family does that pattern actually need? Could you justify the exact same policy structure with a totally different theory — maybe one that cares about children's attachment or about reducing total work hours across the household? Or is there something in the design — like the non-transferable quotas combined with the childcare gap — that only makes sense if you think sameness of roles is the core of justice? And if we replaced that implicit view with, say, a capabilities approach that treats care work as valuable in itself, what parts of the leave and subsidy system would have to change? I'm especially curious whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined or whether it's locked into one specific theory of equality.
