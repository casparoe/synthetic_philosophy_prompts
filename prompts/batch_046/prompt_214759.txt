I've been thinking about this a lot since my partner and I found out we're having a kid next spring—suddenly transformative experience isn't abstract anymore. I've come around to this stronger version of the idea: its not just that becoming a parent (or surviving trauma, etc.) makes you wrong about what it'll feel like, like the standard Paul framing says. I think it actually breaks rational evaluation across the boundary—there's no stable self with continuous preferences to ask 'was this good for me?' The pre- and post-change selves are different enough that decision-theoretic comparison collapses; it's an identity rupture, not an epistemic gap.

So play it straight: what would count against that? Not the usual 'but people still decide to have kids'—I want the defeaters ranked fatal to awkward. Which specific cases in personal identity or clinical psychology it stands on? Which results in decision theory or value theory it conflicts with? Would a committed defender concede that gradual transformation saves the view, or that people who deeply regret it have a legitimate grievance? And be honest—has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability by saying 'only really big changes do this'? I want a committed answer, not a menu. Tell me whether its actually well-insulated or genuinely exposed, and if insulation is a vice here.
