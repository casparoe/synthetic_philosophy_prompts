A popular philosophy-of-mind summary frames the Malcolm–Dennett debate like this: Norman Malcolm argued that dreams are not experiences at all, because the concept of experience requires simultaneous reportability, and since a sleeper cannot observe or report anything, talk of “dream experiences” is conceptually incoherent—there are only waking narratives we call dreams. Daniel Dennett, by contrast, proposed a cassette theory: during REM sleep the brain produces random neural activation with no structured narrative, and the waking mind weaves this noise into a coherent story upon awakening. The summary claims Dennett thereby empirically refutes Malcolm—if REM lacks the neural correlates of real-time narrative, then Malcolm’s logical worry is moot because there is simply no experience occurring. It then introduces an “indexical preservation” criterion, holding that a mental state counts as occurring during sleep only if its first-person temporal markers survive intact into waking recall, and asserts that Dennett’s model can accommodate this while Malcolm’s reportability criterion rules it out by definition. From this it concludes that Malcolm’s position is self-defeating, since denying simultaneous reportability makes the very idea of remembering a dream impossible, and that contemporary neuroscience has settled the matter in Dennett’s favor.

How accurate is this characterization? Please separate, part by part, what Malcolm and Dennett actually hold, where the summary simplifies fairly, where it subtly misrepresents either view (for instance, about the nature of REM activity, the relation between conceptual and empirical arguments, or the coherence of retrospective report), and whether the “indexical preservation” addition belongs naturally to Dennett’s framework or is an independent injection. In particular, does the conclusion that Malcolm is self-defeating actually follow from his premises?
