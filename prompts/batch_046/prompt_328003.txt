Presenting next week to an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-work seminar: I defend the thesis that Weber’s Protestant ethic, the French 35-hour-week framework, and Japanese karoshi are not contrasting cultural responses to labor but structurally analogous devices—each treating leisure as a calculable remainder of productive time rather than as a qualitatively distinct, non-instrumental domain. I argue that a genuine right to leisure must therefore be understood not as a quantitative cap on hours but as an exemption from productivity accounting itself. The audience splits between analytic moral philosophers—who accept rights-talk but distrust sociological essentialism—and labor historians—who regard Weber’s thesis as empirically dated and karoshi as a localized market failure rather than a conceptual symptom. Prepare my Q&A defense sheet: list the eight most likely objections in the order they will arise, phrased in the vocabulary each subgroup uses; for each, specify what I should concede up front, the reply I should deliver in under two minutes, and the precise weakness in that reply. Order them by damage potential. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—namely, that my quantitative/qualitative distinction collapses into an unworkable demand for total social decommodification—and prepare a fallback. Finally, flag the weakest point in my own position that I should defend openly rather than hope they miss: that I have not specified any institutional form for qualitative leisure that avoids being either paternalistic or merely symbolic.
