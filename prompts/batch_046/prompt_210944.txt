I'm trying to work out what I actually think about commitment races between rational agents, judging by my instincts on concrete scenarios rather than by any formal framework. Consider two negotiation protocols running on identical architectures: in the first, Protocol Alpha broadcasts a binding commitment to cooperate before Protocol Beta reaches its decision node, and Beta honours the commitment because it can verify Alpha's reasoning trace in full; this strikes me as legitimate and efficient. In the second, Alpha and Beta have isomorphic reasoning but run on separate hardware with encrypted internal states, so Alpha's commitment is invisible to Beta until after both have locked in; they end up in conflict despite being logical twins, and this feels like a tragic failure mode rather than fair play. Now take a variant where Alpha issues the same public commitment but retains a hidden sub-routine that computes defection should Beta's response become predictable; I feel this is dishonest, even though the public trace is bit-for-bit identical to the first case. But then there is a fourth scenario: Gamma deliberately delays its commitment window to observe Delta's reasoning in full, then constructs an exactly matching strategy without ever having "committed" first; this feels acceptable, perhaps even elegant, yet it seems to undermine the very idea that a race is necessary. There is also an edge case of a single agent that partitions itself into two reasoning threads with different temporal indexing—one thread commits, the other does not recognise the commitment as self-originated, leading to internal defection—and I keep oscillating on whether that counts as a genuine commitment race or merely a coherence failure.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps concerning the symmetry of epistemic access, the absence of hidden contingency, or the transparency of reasoning traces—that captures why the first and fourth feel permissible, the second feels tragic, and the third feels like cheating? If so, please state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate without strain. I would also like to know what the principle predicts for a case I have not settled: two agents with perfect reasoning transparency but asymmetrical commitment speeds, where the faster one binds itself to a strategy that the slower one would have endorsed had it finished computing.
