I am writing to seek your judgment on a practical investment decision that has started to feel like a philosophical problem about what past experience can license. For the past four years, I have co-managed a small archival restoration studio specializing in analog tape recovery. Our record is unbroken: every tape manufactured before 1998 that shows a particular degradation pattern we call bronze flaking has been fully restored by our chemical bath process without any loss of fidelity. Relying on this, I am preparing to commit significant resources to scaling our bath capacity in order to process a large incoming archive from a defunct radio station, almost all of whose tapes fall within that pre-1998, bronze-flaking category.

A colleague with a background in materials physics recently challenged the rational basis for this move. He suggests that bronze flaking might be a disjunctive property: in tapes never exposed to a new stabilizer compound—developed only this year and absent from every sample in our history—the pattern indicates recoverability, but in tapes that have been treated with that compound, the identical visible pattern indicates irreversible molecular fusion. Since our entire dataset lacks the stabilizer, he argues, it is equally consistent with both the uniform always-recoverable rule and the conditional recoverable-only-when-untreated rule, and that my confidence in scaling up assumes a projection of uniformity that the evidence underdetermines. I recognize that this sounds like an abstract logical possibility, but I am genuinely unsure whether it exposes a real defect in my reasoning or whether it is the kind of skeptical move that, if taken seriously, would prevent any practical reliance on past patterns. I must make the investment decision within the month. How should I evaluate whether my observation history genuinely supports the prediction I need to make?
