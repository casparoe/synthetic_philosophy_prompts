Write a dialogue between Dr. L., a philosopher of mind who defends a Hofstadter-inspired view of the self as a distributed pattern of self-referential loops, and Dr. K., a critic who holds that phenomenal ownership requires a singular, substrate-anchored first-person perspective. The setting is an invited symposium on cognitive architecture. They are examining a hypothetical case they call the coupled-index scenario: two human brains are linked via a high-fidelity bidirectional neural interface that maintains one unified autobiographical memory store with no privileged access for either brain. Each brain can retrieve any memory in the shared index, and each generates first-person reports using the same pronoun “I” for the entire corpus. Over years, roughly forty percent of the index’s associative pathways come to reside in synthetic tissue distributed between both craniums. One brain is then irreversibly destroyed. The survivor does not report grief for “another person,” but rather a sense of having lost part of its own body, and continues to refer to memories that originated in the destroyed brain using “I.”

Dr. L. should argue that this demonstrates the “I” was genuinely distributed—that the pattern of self-reference, not the biological container, constituted the self, and that the survivor is the legitimate continuation of the same distributed “I,” now contracted but intact.

Dr. K. should counter that what remains is either a delusional fusion or a collapsed “we” speaking in monologue, and that phenomenal presence—what it is like to be this subject—cannot be bifurcated without being annihilated. Dr. K. should specifically press Dr. L. on the migration problem: if the self is merely a pattern, why does the survivor feel loss localized to “my missing half” rather than indifference, and does this not prove that a singular locus of embodiment was always performing the real work?

The dialogue must contain genuine back-and-forth: each speaker should respond directly to the other’s preceding point rather than talk past it. Both should be equally competent, with no strawman. Include at least one exchange in which Dr. K. challenges whether synthetic intermediate substrates break narrative continuity, and Dr. L. replies by appealing to the functional equivalence of loop structures rather than biological continuity. Conclude with the two identifying one precise point of agreement and one issue that remains genuinely unresolved. Keep the tone formal and analytical throughout.
