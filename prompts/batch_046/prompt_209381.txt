I'm trying to stabilize my views on the separateness-of-persons objection before drafting a longer essay, and I want a consistency check on the package. I hold roughly the following:

(1) The Rawls/Nozick objection succeeds: there is no "super-person" that encompasses individuals, so aggregating welfare across people to justify sacrificing one is illegitimate in principle.

(2) I reject metaphysical essentialism about personal identity and accept a reductionist, psychological-continuity view (broadly Parfitian). So whatever separateness amounts to, it cannot be grounded in distinct simple substances.

(3) Despite (2), I think separateness is normative rather than ontological—what I call "reason-isolation": an agent's reasons do not pool with others' into a single maximand, even if the boundaries between persons are partly conventional.

(4) I do not regard the killing/letting-die distinction as morally fundamental; what matters is whether one's own agency is the direct source of the harm.

(5) Given (4), I judge the standard trolley case (divert to kill one and save five) not merely permissible but required, while I still judge the transplant surgeon case impermissible.

(6) I take the explanation for the difference in (5) to be a directness or means-based constraint rather than separateness itself, though I have not fully formalized this.

Does this hang together? In particular: does (1) conflict with (5), since (5) permits exactly the kind of cross-person aggregation that (1) rejects? Is the move in (3) stable without the metaphysical backing that (2) denies, or is that tension only apparent? And is (6) compatible with retaining (1), or does it effectively replace the separateness objection with a different principle?

Please audit the tensions, rank them by severity, and for each specify the least costly revision—what I would have to drop or weaken. Also state how confident you are in your assessment overall, and single out the specific point about which you are least sure.
