I'm working on illusionist accounts of phenomenal consciousness for a chapter on self-modeling, and I've constructed an argument that seems to show illusionism is self-defeating rather than merely counterintuitive. Here is the version I keep returning to:

(1) Illusionists claim phenomenal properties are not instantiated; instead, the brain misrepresents its own first-order cognitive states as having qualitative character.
(2) Any misrepresentation requires a representational vehicle that gets things wrong about its target.
(3) Here the target is a first-order perceptual state, and the misrepresentation is second-order: the system represents that state as being "like something" when in fact it lacks qualitative character entirely.
(4) But second-order representation of a state requires access to that state's actual properties—otherwise there is no specific content to misrepresent.
(5) So for the illusion to arise, the system must genuinely access the absence of qualitative character in the first-order state.
(6) Accessing the absence of qualitative character requires representing that absence within a qualitative contrast space—otherwise the system could not discriminate "no redness" from "no greenness" or from "no state at all."
(7) Therefore, generating the illusion of phenomenal consciousness requires the system to already inhabit the phenomenal contrast space it allegedly lacks.
(8) Hence illusionism presupposes the very phenomenal consciousness it denies.

The conclusion is absurd if I want to keep illusionism intact, but each step feels defensible, especially (4) and (6). If forced to choose, I'd rather preserve (1) and (4) than (8), yet I'm unsure whether the error is a hidden equivocation between cognitive access and phenomenal access in (5)-(6), or whether the argument is valid and forces us to drop a premise about representational conditions.
