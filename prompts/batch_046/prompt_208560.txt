Ok so here's something that's been bugging me about hospital protocols. In the US, the Uniform Determination of Death Act says you're dead if either your whole brain is gone irreversibly or your heart and lungs have stopped irreversibly. But in practice, if you're a potential organ donor and your brain is toast, they declare you dead right then and there while your heart is still being pumped by a machine. If you're not a donor candidate with the excat same brain damage, they usually just pull the plug, wait for your heart to actually stop, and then call it circulatory death. So the same body, same brain state, gets sorted into different death-categories based on whether there's an organ procurement team in the room. What view of death does that institutional split presuppose? Does it only make sense if you think death is basically a legal switch rather than a biological fact? Or is there a coherent ontolgy where being dead is relative to what technolgy is sustaining you and whether anyone wants your kidneys? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the practice actually needs that weird relativism or if it could be explained by practical logistics alone, and tell me what a death-certification system built on a single, technology-independent criterion would have to change about organ donation and family consent.
