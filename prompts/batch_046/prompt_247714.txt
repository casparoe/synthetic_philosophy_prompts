I work as a backend software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or political theory. Recently I read an unnamed tech-ethics article that attempts to frame the debate over whose values aligned AI should serve by borrowing an analogy from distributed systems. The author summarizes the position like this:

"The problem of value alignment is structurally identical to the Byzantine Generals Problem: stakeholders submit incompatible preference-sets, some of which may be adversarial or malformed, and the system must converge on a single policy without a trusted central authority. Liberal neutrality resolves the deadlock by treating every submitted preference as an equally legitimate input and aggregating them through a weighted arithmetic mean, much like a distributed consensus protocol reaching agreement across nodes. Consequently, an aligned system must never embed designer-specific moral constraints—such as rules against deception or manipulation—because doing so privileges one conception of the good over others and breaks neutrality. In this view, value pluralism is not a deep philosophical puzzle but a straightforward engineering challenge in fault-tolerant aggregation, and philosophers like Rawls or Isaiah Berlin have little to contribute once the protocol is correctly specified."

The article concludes that this proves alignment is purely a matter of robust preference-aggregation engineering, with no remaining normative ambiguity.

Parts of this sound plausible to me—especially the connection to fault tolerance—but other parts feel suspicious, particularly the idea that neutrality requires averaging all preferences including harmful ones, and that banning deception is a neutrality violation. Could you audit this summary with precision? Please go through it claim by claim, identifying what accurately captures debates about liberal neutrality and value aggregation in AI ethics, what subtly misrepresents those debates (for instance, by conflating neutrality among comprehensive doctrines with neutrality among all actions, or by treating incommensurable values as quantifiable weights), and whether the author's conclusion—that philosophy is redundant here—actually follows from the corrected premises. I am especially interested in whether there is a meaningful distinction between aggregating preferences and endorsing them, and whether liberal neutrality permits the system to exclude certain preferences (like those favoring deception) without thereby taking a substantive moral stance.
