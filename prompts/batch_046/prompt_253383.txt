I am drafting an essay for my advanced history seminar on whether Fernand Braudel’s tripartite model of historical time—the slow rhythms of geography and climate, the intermediate structures of society and economy, and the rapid surface of events and individuals—can be applied meaningfully beyond early modern Europe, specifically to the development of artificial intelligence since 1950.

My working outline is as follows. First, I briefly set out Braudel’s layers as they appear in his well-known study of the Mediterranean: geographical time measured in centuries, social time in decades, and individual or event time in days or years. Second, I apply this framework to AI history: the “geographical” layer comprises silicon supply chains, energy grids, and the physical accumulation of digital text corpora; the “social” layer includes funding regimes, corporate research laboratories, and regulatory institutions; the “event” layer covers specific model releases, benchmark results, and the biographies of prominent researchers. Third, I argue that in technological history the geographical layer lacks the inertia Braudel attributed to mountains and climate—data centers and chip fabrication can scale within months rather than millennia—while the event layer is disproportionately celebrated in popular journalism. I conclude tentatively that this compression does not invalidate Braudel’s hierarchy but requires treating digital infrastructure as a temporal register with its own, faster form of inertia.

First, is my characterization of the three layers accurate enough for this level, or have I inadvertently conflated Braudel’s view of environment with a stronger geographical determinism he actually rejected? Second, does applying this framework to AI development genuinely illuminate how we narrate technological change, or does it feel like an artificial analogy? Third, in my third section I claim that digital infrastructure “lacks inertia” compared to climate and soil; is this philosophically coherent, or am I treating physical and informational substrates as comparable when they belong to categorically different orders?

I am working from lecture notes and one secondary summary, so I do not need bibliographic suggestions. I am mainly interested in whether the argument is structurally sound, whether the analogy holds, and whether my claim about compressed inertia is defensible without empirical data I do not have access to.
