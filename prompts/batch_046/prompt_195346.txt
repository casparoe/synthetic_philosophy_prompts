In a seminar on Enlightenment aesthetics and politics, you have been examining Jean-Jacques Rousseau's arguments regarding the Geneva theater—specifically his claim in the Letter to d'Alembert that theatrical representation corrupts civic virtue by substituting artificial sentiment for authentic moral engagement, his distinction between unifying civic festivals and divisive spectacles, and his suspicion that professional performers embody a dangerous form of systematic deception. You have also discussed his broader anxiety, expressed across the Discourse on Inequality and the Social Contract, that modern societies suffer when performative appearance supersedes genuine being. Now imagine that Rousseau is confronted with a contemporary phenomenon that has no eighteenth-century analogue: immersive, algorithmically personalized theatrical environments in which audience members wear biometric sensors that feed real-time emotional data into an AI system, which then dynamically rewrites both the narrative and the performers' actions to maximize individual arousal, while the audience is simultaneously encouraged to livestream and perform their own reactions for remote viewers mediated by predictive engagement algorithms. Rather than offering a generic anachronistic prediction, construct Rousseau's likely assessment by extending three specific conceptual commitments—(1) spectacle versus authentic presence, (2) the professional deceiver as a social threat, and (3) the civic festival as a unifying alternative—into this technological domain. Specifically, address whether he would regard the biometric feedback loop as an intensification of the theatrical corruption he condemned in Geneva, whether the audience's dual role as both spectator and performed object collapses his distinction between actor and citizen in a way he could not have anticipated, and whether any element of this system might accidentally realize his ideal of collective emotional unity rather than individual alienation. Your response should work entirely from Rousseau's own premises, should not appeal to secondary scholarship, and should not request bibliographic references or reading lists; instead, show precisely where his arguments commit him, where they leave him genuinely undecided, and what he would likely demand be abolished versus reformed.
