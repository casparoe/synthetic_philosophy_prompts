I'm drafting this novel that jumps across like three centuries and I got into a weird argument with my anthro friend M and my archival historian friend J about those big-picture history books -- you know, Harari's Sapiens, Diamond's Guns Germs and Steel, and that Graeber and Wengrow book The Dawn of Everything. We were trying to figure out if grand narratives are actually explaining anything or just telling really convincing stories with footnotes.

M said big history works because it gives "cognitive scafolding" -- Harari linking the cognitive revolution to agriculture to modern science makes broad human behavior predictable even when local details vary. She said it's not fiction because it's constrained by genetics and archaeology, but that the value is in the arc, not every causal arrow being proven. Then J said that's exactly the narrative fallacy -- Graeber and Wengrow showed the "stages" collapse when you actually look at archaeological variability, and if you can't specify the mechanism linking, say, writing to modern capitalism with rigor, you're writing just-so stories. M admitted variability is real but said macro-explanation always needs abstraction.

J pushed harder and said big history smuggles in teleolgy -- the progress story. M countered that Harari explicitly avoids teleology since he calls agriculture a "trap" not an improvement. J said even "trap" assumes a standard of wellbeing that isn't historically grounded. Then M brought up this idea she called "narrative friction" -- she said good grand narratives should generate productive anomalies rather than smoothing them away, and Harari's story actually does this, like why the cognitive revolution hit only one species. J said "narrative friction" is just special pleading with a fancy name, and asked for a criterion that distinguishes productive friction from ad hoc adjustments. Neither budged.

As a novelist trying to build a historical arc without lying, I'm stuck in the middle. Can you adjudicate? Tell me who's right about which specific point -- is narrative friction a real epistemic virtue or just special pleading? Is the teleolgy charge fair given the "trap" framing? And what are both missing about the difference between explaining at scale and telling a story? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
