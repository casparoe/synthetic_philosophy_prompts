You advise a strategic-risk team at a policy consultancy that must produce forty-eight-hour assessments of whether large-scale institutional arrangements—monetary unions, federal compacts, imperial trade systems—are likely to survive the next two decades, and if not, by what mode they will fail. The consultancy wants to institutionalize the forecasting principles usually summarized as the outside view, reference-class forecasting, and base-rate reasoning, but adapted to a context where historical analogies are scarce, the institution in question belongs to several reference classes at once, and the analysts have no time to build bespoke statistical models.

Construct a reusable decision procedure—ordered questions, criteria for a sufficient answer at each stage, explicit stop points where the answer is “no further institutional assessment possible without inside-view judgment,” and flags for where values rather than facts must take over—that a senior analyst can run in under four hours. The procedure must handle three specific pathologies that standard textbook versions ignore: (1) reference-class contamination, where an institution is simultaneously a member of classes with radically different survival curves (for example, a monetary union is also a treaty organization and also a redistributive fiscal pact); (2) structural dissimilarity, where a feature of the modern case is absent from every historical member of the class, requiring a weight or discount rather than a binary exclusion; and (3) inside-view capture, where the analyst’s access to current political narratives biases the adjustment away from the base rate.

Then apply the procedure to two live-form cases: (A) a contemporary currency union with a unified central bank but no common debt instrument and with exit mechanisms that are legally undefined; and (B) a federal compact in which constituent units retain formal veto power over constitutional amendments and over the appointment of the supreme arbiter. For each case, run the procedure through to its conclusion, including one step where the procedure yields a base-rate prediction that conflicts sharply with the intuitive inside-view assessment—explicitly mark that conflict and state how the procedure requires the analyst to report it rather than resolve it. Finally, state exactly what the procedure cannot do: identify the boundary conditions under which it should be abandoned in favor of expert political judgment, and note where the consultancy’s board must sign off on a values judgment rather than a forecast.
