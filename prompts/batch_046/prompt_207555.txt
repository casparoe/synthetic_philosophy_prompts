I recently read an essay—published in an unnamed interdisciplinary volume on material religion—that proposes what it calls an “indexical materiality” framework for interpreting ritual artifacts. The author applies this to Neolithic Çatalhöyük wall paintings and domestic bull-skull installations, arguing that because these motifs recur in fixed spatial patterns, they do not merely signal generic symbolic practice but directly index a shared, articulated theology of cyclical regeneration. The essay claims that modern archaeology has largely overcome the old “Pompeii premise” worries: since ritual objects are deliberately deposited rather than accidentally lost, they function like linguistic indices pointing unambiguously to specific mental states, so that differences in burial assemblages between regions map cleanly onto distinct theological doctrines. It further insists that when material records conflict with textual accounts—for example, the absence of weapons in burials attributed to warrior societies—the archaeological evidence should always take precedence, because objects do not lie, whereas texts are rhetorical constructions. From this, the author concludes that archaeology can now independently verify or falsify ancient religious claims without hermeneutic circularity.
