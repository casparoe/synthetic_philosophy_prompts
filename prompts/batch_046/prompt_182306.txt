I'm preparing a feature on whether large-scale historical outcomes—empires, revolutions, technological regimes—are genuinely vulnerable to micro-contingencies in the manner of the proverb about a lost nail, or whether that image is mainly a narrative convenience. Before I speak with historians and complexity scientists, I want the philosophical specification first. Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of macro-historical contingency must deliver—the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must preserve, and the findings from dynamics or social science it must not contradict. For each requirement, supply the motivating case or consideration behind it, and flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Specifically, the list must include: (1) a criterion separating deterministic chaos—sensitive dependence within fixed rules—from genuine historical branching; (2) a criterion explaining why some small events are amplified into structural change while others dissipate, without collapsing into post-hoc selection bias; (3) a criterion governing counterfactual robustness—how far back a micro-event can be altered before the macro-outcome is structurally fixed rather than genuinely contingent; (4) a criterion addressing scale—whether contingency operates at the level of individual actions, institutional rules, or long-term demographic and ecological trends; and (5) a criterion ensuring compatibility with retrospective explanation, so that successful historical understanding is not rendered impossible by the very openness the theory asserts. Briefly, score structuralist historiography, chaos-theoretic modeling, and counterfactual narrative analysis against this list, but keep the weight on the criteria and their tensions rather than on the scorecard. Conclude with a single line beginning Verdict: that names the most severe tension in the desiderata and states whether it appears resolvable.
