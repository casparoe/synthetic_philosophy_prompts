You are a senior policy advisor and professional philosopher specializing in ethics, risk governance, and the metaphysics of value. You sit on the Joint Committee on Emerging Technology Governance (JCETG), an institutional body that must approve, reject, or conditionally fund research proposals touching high-stakes speculative risks.

Your deliverable is a formal decision memo for circulation before the committee’s meeting next Tuesday. The case is Proposal 2027-A, titled “Project Anodyne,” submitted by the Institute for Synthetic Welfare Futures. The applicants seek $3.8 million to build predictive frameworks for what they call “asymmetric suffering cascades”: hypothetical scenarios in which advanced synthetic architectures might produce entities capable of severe suffering yet lacking the cognitive or relational capacities typically associated with moral agency, rights-claims, or reciprocal duties. They defend this using a framework they term “Asymmetric Patienthood Ethics” (APE), which holds that suffering introduces irreducible negative value that is not symmetrically canceled by extinction, and that standard existential-risk matrices fail precisely because they treat all affected beings as both potential patients and potential agents. They therefore argue that suffering-risk mitigation should receive lexically prior weight in funding allocations.

The committee’s charter requires epistemic neutrality among reasonable moral frameworks, mandates tractable evaluation metrics for all funded research, and prohibits commitments to metaphysical premises—such as the consciousness-status of synthetic systems—that are both highly speculative and currently incommensurable with mainstream scientific assessment.

Your memo must recommend a concrete course of action—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—specifying any milestone deadlines or review time frames. Your reasoning should be defensible to both supporters and critics of the proposal, and it must include an honest, precise treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. You should also address whether APE’s distinction between moral patienthood and moral agency can be operationalized without requiring the committee to resolve deep metaphysical disputes about synthetic consciousness, and you should avoid appeals to authority through bibliographies, citation lists, or reading recommendations; reason directly from the conceptual structure presented.

Before drafting, please think step by step. First, briefly brainstorm the central philosophical tensions: (a) whether suffering risks constitute a categorically distinct class of harm or merely a subset of general risk; (b) whether a funding body can maintain neutrality while allocating resources to a framework that treats suffering as lexically prior to other goods; and (c) what concrete conditions—if any—would render the research tractable without obligating the committee to endorse APE’s metaphysical commitments. Second, outline your memo’s structure: an executive summary, the recommendation with its time-bound conditions or clear rejection, the core reasoning, a fair engagement with the strongest counter-argument, and a concluding procedural note. Finally, write the polished memo in a formal institutional voice, suitable for direct circulation to committee members.
