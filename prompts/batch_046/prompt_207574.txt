Having spent thirty years as an experimental physicist before retiring, I have recently turned to philosophy of action and moral psychology with the same expectation of precise, falsifiable disagreement that I once demanded in peer review. In a graduate seminar I am auditing, we have been examining the clash between Daniel Dennett’s compatibilist defense of moral responsibility—according to which retributive just-desert is conceptually muddled and punishment is defensible solely through its instrumental effects on behavior, rehabilitation, and social signaling—and Gregg Caruso’s free-will skepticism, which holds that no human being exercises the kind of uncaused, self-originating causal power required for basic-desert blame, and therefore that retributive punishment is never morally justified. Rather than asking you to declare a winner, I want you to draft the document that a genuinely committed Dennettian and a genuinely committed Carusian could both sign after a rigorous, good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Please structure the statement as follows. First, open with a single bottom-line sentence that captures both the shared consensus and the exact conceptual point at which agreement ends. Second, provide a numbered section of agreed claims—covering the nature of the free-will problem, the distinction between basic and consequentialist desert, the empirical psychology of blame, and the legitimate institutional aims of punishment—written with sufficient precision that neither camp need append a qualifying footnote. Third, present a disagreements table with four columns: the crux (for example, whether compatibilist conditions for agency are sufficient for basic desert, or whether neuroscience can settle the causal-structure question); the specific observation, argument, or empirical result both sides agree would bear on that crux; the Dennettian prediction about what that result would show; and the Carusian prediction about what it would show. Include at least three such cruxes, and for each record in advance the outcome each side expects, so that the table functions as a registered prediction rather than a post-hoc rationalization.

Keep the entire response under 600 words, with the bottom-line sentence standing alone, the agreed-claims section not exceeding 250 words, and the table not exceeding 350 words. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific works; I am interested in the conceptual architecture and in the logical exposure of each view, not in source verification. Write formally, as if for publication in a philosophy journal’s collaborative forum, and avoid treating either position as a straw man: each formulation of disagreement should be phrased so that the side being represented would accept it as an accurate statement of its own view.
