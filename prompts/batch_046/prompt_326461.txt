Here are my commitments:

1. I accept Scanlon's contractualist core: an act is wrong if and only if it can be justified only by principles that at least one affected party could reasonably reject.
2. I think the aggregation problem is manageable if we distinguish sharply between instance-level principles (governing individual acts) and architecture-level principles (governing the design rules that permit accumulation), and I want to apply this modular distinction to moral reasoning generally.
3. I hold that reasonable rejectability must be assessed from an "each-we" perspective—asking whether any particular affected person could reject the principle—not from an aggregative "all-together" collective standpoint.
4. I maintain that contractualist verdicts should not depend on counterfactual predictions about what others will do, because that introduces a coordination fragility that makes moral assessment unstable.
5. I believe that in cases of collective harm, such as climate damage, no individual contribution may cross the threshold of reasonable rejectability at the instance level, and therefore no individual contributor acts wrongly; nevertheless, the architecture-level principle that permits unbounded, uncoordinated accumulation is itself rejectable.
6. I also hold that if an architecture-level principle is rejectable, then any agent who knowingly implements, maintains, or participates in sustaining that architecture acts wrongly, regardless of the negligibility of their particular contribution.

Please examine these commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which pairs only appear to conflict but dissolve on inspection, and how severe each genuine tension is. For each tension, identify the cheapest revision that would restore consistency and what that revision costs me. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally serious. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most conflicts overall.

In your response, please state how confident you are in your analysis and single out the specific point about which you are least sure.
