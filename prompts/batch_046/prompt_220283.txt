I'm drafting a long-form feature on epistemic dependence in professional life, and I've tried to codify the philosophical commitments that underpin my framing. Before I finalize the piece, I need a consistency audit—not a literature review, but a check on whether my own package hangs together.

Here are the commitments:

(1) AI-mediated belief formation is a form of distributed cognition rather than testimony: a user's iterative framing of prompts, rejection of incoherent outputs, and synthesis across sessions constitute reasoning-by-proxy that retains genuine first-person epistemic agency.

(2) Understanding a claim requires what I call reconstructive autonomy—the capacity to regenerate the supporting inference chain from first principles if every external source were removed.

(3) In high-stakes practical domains—clinical decision support, statutory interpretation, engineering risk assessment—professionals ought to act on AI outputs even when they lack reconstructive autonomy, provided institutional audit trails and verification protocols are in place.

(4) The boundary between using AI as a cognitive prosthesis and being epistemically replaced by it is purely sociological: it tracks institutional liability norms and accountability structures, not any internal feature of the user's mental state or cognitive process.

(5) There is no society-level erosion of human understanding caused by AI adoption, because understanding is modular: a deficit in reconstructive autonomy within one task-domain does not propagate into adjacent domains.

(6) The knowledge that results from well-designed human-AI interaction is first-personal, not second-hand, precisely because the selective judgment exercised in prompt design and output endorsement is an irreducible component of the belief-forming process.

Which of these are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision in each case? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging. For each tension, specify which commitment would have to be modified or abandoned, and what that costs conceptually. In particular, check whether (1) and (4) can both stand, whether (3) undermines (5) in the very domains where (3) applies, and whether the distinction I draw in (6) survives the claim in (4) that all such distinctions are conventional. Also tell me if there is an apparent conflict between (2) and (3) that dissolves once we separate justification for action from the conditions for understanding.
