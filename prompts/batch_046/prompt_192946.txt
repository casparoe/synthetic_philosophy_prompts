I am a retired experimental chemist who has only recently begun serious study of philosophy, and I have agreed to lead one session of a local discussion circle composed mainly of retired scientists and engineers—none with formal philosophical training—on al-Ghazālī's occasionalism and his denial that created things possess necessary causal powers. I do not want you to lecture me on occasionalism itself; I want a pedagogical plan for how I should present it to them.

Specify the exact dependency order for the explanation: which distinction—between correlation and metaphysical necessity, or between primary and secondary causation—must be secured first, and why the other concept cannot precede it without collapsing into confusion. For the carrying example, I wish to avoid the familiar fire-and-cotton scenario and instead build the session around enzymatic catalysis; tell me precisely what properties this example must possess, and what visible mechanism it must lack, in order to make the occasionalist denial of intrinsic causal power intuitively vivid for people accustomed to thinking in molecular terms. Then map the misconceptions I should expect, in the sequence they will likely arise: I anticipate confusion with quantum indeterminacy, then with Hume's constant conjunction, then with a global denial of scientific lawfulness; indicate the exact point in the explanatory arc at which each should be intercepted and defused, and how. Provide a single closing question that will reveal whether a participant has grasped that occasionalism preserves observed regularity through divine custom rather than through created causal efficacy. Finally, state explicitly what I should omit—perhaps the atomist metaphysics historically paired with this view, or modern mechanist philosophy of science—and how I should frame that omission so it reads as a deliberate pedagogical boundary rather than an evasion.
