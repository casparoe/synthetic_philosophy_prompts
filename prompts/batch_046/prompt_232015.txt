I'm a doctoral student in moral psychology and philosophy of punishment, and I'm working through a seminar paper on how literary narratives complicate Strawsonian accounts of moral responsibility. I want to read Anthony Burgess's novel A Clockwork Orange as a philosophical test case rather than a thematic illustration, specifically through the lens of reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, indignation, and their vicarious counterparts—without reducing either the novel or the philosophy to the other. The plot and major scenes are familiar enough that I don't need summary: Alex's gleeful ultra-violent autonomy, the Ludovico conditioning that renders him incapable of violence or aesthetic choice, his display as a rehabilitated object by state authorities, the prison chaplain's insistence that true morality requires the freedom to choose evil, and F. Alexander's turn from victim to political avenger who attempts to drive Alex toward suicide by exploiting his conditioning. What I want is a sustained critical reading that takes both Burgess and Strawson seriously and does not treat the novel as a mere dramatization.

My own framing introduces a tension I think standard discussions overlook. Strawson treats reactive attitudes as essentially interpersonal practices through which we hold one another to account within a shared moral community of participants. Once an agent is transformed into an object of behavioral management, however, the appropriate moral response may no longer be directed at the agent at all. Burgess's narrative seems to depict what I will call a displaced reactive economy: the resentment and indignation that should target Alex are redirected onto the institutions that engineered his passivity, producing not forgiveness but moral horror, administrative pride, and vicarious outrage on behalf of the damaged victim. At the same time, the novel introduces a temporal asymmetry that I think is philosophically under-examined. Before conditioning, reactive attitudes are backward-looking—society resents Alex for past violence. After conditioning, they become forward-looking—pride in rehabilitation, anxiety about relapse, satisfaction with control. Meanwhile, Alex is deprived of reflexive reactive address: he experiences nausea rather than guilt, and is no longer able to inhabit the attitudes that constitute moral membership in both directions, receiving and feeling.
