I've come around to thinking the best argument for sortition isn't descriptive representation at all -- it's that elections generate a kind of "mandate illusion" where politicians claim popular authority for policies that were never actually endorsed by voters, and this illusion makes retrospective accountability almost impossible. If you replaced the legislature with a sorted citizens' assembly that had real lawmaking power, that illusion would collapse, and accountability would have to run through direct oversight mechanisms instead of the ballot box. What would actually count against this? Not the standard "random people aren't competent" objection, but specifically: what institutional evidence or historical cases would show that the mandate illusion is either harmless or that sortition creates a worse accountability gap? In particular, has anyone demonstrated that randomly selected bodies are *more* susceptible to elite capture precisely because they lack an electoral base and party infrastructure to defend them? I want an honest assessment of where this position is exposed -- which defeaters are fatal, which are just awkward, and whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability if every failure of a citizens' assembly gets blamed on implementation rather than the design itself. Also, is there a descriptve representation argument that, if it turned out to be false, wouldn't actually hurt the mandate-illusion case?
