Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers—call them A and B—debating the Meno problem: why is knowledge more valuable than true belief? A should defend a position that combines the standard modal-stability insight with a specific twist: that the surplus value of knowledge consists in what she terms “diachronic authority,” the legitimate entitlement to rely on a belief in future reasoning without re-verification, and that this authority is lost even in cases of stable true belief formed through pure environmental luck. B should oppose this by arguing that diachronic authority is merely a pragmatic benefit, not the core value Meno sought, and should advance an alternative account in which the surplus value comes from “structural embedding”—the belief being situated in a network of supporting reasons that would survive targeted revision of background assumptions—even when that embedding is not accessible to the agent’s conscious reflection. Both characters must respond directly to each other’s arguments: B should challenge whether diachronic authority can distinguish knowledge from reliably formed true belief in cases of epistemic outsourcing, and A should press B on whether structural embedding collapses into a form of justification that is itself only instrumentally valuable for reaching truth. The dialogue should be substantive, with real back-and-forth rather than parallel monologues, and each speaker should commit clearly to their view with explicit reasons rather than merely mapping out the logical space. Avoid bibliographic references, citations, or appeals to named theories that require external verification; build the arguments from the conceptual claims themselves.
