Since receiving a diagnosis of a progressive neurological condition six months ago, questions about the moral limits of bodily autonomy and the refusal of life-prolonging treatment have become personally urgent rather than merely theoretical for me. I have therefore been reading Hume's essay Of Suicide, not as an antiquarian exercise but as an argument that might bear directly on contemporary debates about physician-assisted dying and the ethics of self-termination. I am particularly stuck on the passage in which Hume addresses the objection that suicide violates the order of Providence. He appears to contend that, because the Deity governs the world through general laws rather than particular interventions, a human being's decision to end his own life is no more an affront to divine authority than a farmer's decision to divert a river or a physician's administration of a medicine that unintentionally hastens death. The analogy seems to turn on the claim that local alterations within a system do not disrupt the overall governance of that system. Yet I do not see clearly how Hume derives a moral permission from this metaphysical observation. Is the implicit premise that human life lacks a specially designed purpose, and that cosmic indifference therefore removes any duty to preserve oneself? Or does the argument instead depend on a prior claim about the extent of human sovereignty over one's own body, with the theological framing serving only as rhetorical cover? I would appreciate help in understanding the precise logical architecture of this move—whether it is best read as an argument from the absence of teleology, from the proportionality of causes and effects, or from a different principle altogether. Additionally, I wonder whether the reasoning holds if one strips away its theological vocabulary and replaces Hume's conception of the self with a modern narrative-identity framework, in which ending one's life is understood not as an interruption of a biological process but as the deliberate closure of an authored story. Does that substitution strengthen the argument, undermine it, or leave its structure intact?
