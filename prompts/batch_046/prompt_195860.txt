Here are my verdicts on whether the "man of his time" defence excuses moral failures in historical figures, and I want to know whether a single principle can organize them or whether I must abandon one. Thomas Jefferson's slaveholding and his fathering children with an enslaved woman strike me as inexcusable: abolitionist arguments circulated in his own circle, he drafted language condemning slavery while owning people, and he could have manumitted them, so temporal context does little work. Winston Churchill's racial imperialism and his refusal to divert grain during the Bengal famine feel differently distributed: the racial hierarchy was structurally universal among British elites, but his specific suppression of famine warnings and refusal of aid was an agentic choice available only to him, so the defence applies to the racism but collapses for the famine response. Christopher Columbus's establishment of the encomienda system and initiation of mass enslavement in Hispaniola seems to me to exceed even the brutal norms of late-fifteenth-century Iberian expansion; he designed institutions rather than merely inheriting them, yet I hesitate to apply modern frameworks of genocide without accounting for his cosmological premises. Consider also an early-nineteenth-century anatomist who, in a context where dissection of unclaimed poor bodies was legal and standard, nevertheless arranged for executed prisoners to be displayed in theatrical demonstrations for paying audiences rather than for clinical training; I think he is partly excused by professional norms but not by the theatrical addition. Is there a coherent principle—distinguishing structural inheritance from agentic design, or available moral alternatives from epistemic blindness—that saves all four judgments? State it precisely, show which clause each case motivates, predict the verdict for Robert E. Lee, who resigned his U.S. commission to defend a slaveholding republic despite having expressed private reservations about slavery's morality, and say honestly which of my four judgments your principle cannot accommodate.
