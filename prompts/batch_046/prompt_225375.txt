I am preparing for a discussion in my political-philosophy seminar on communitarian critiques of liberal individualism, and I have been working through Tönnies’ classic distinction between organic community and associative society. I am focusing on the part of his argument where Wesenwille—the essential will rooted in shared blood, place, and mental life—is said to generate a form of social unity that is lived rather than chosen, in contrast to Kürwille, the arbitrary will that constructs instrumental associations through explicit agreement. The passage claims that this organic unity produces obligations that are experienced as necessary rather than contracted, but I cannot quite follow the inferential step. Does Tönnies mean that co-participation in a shared physical and psychological environment conceptually entails binding duties, or is there a hidden teleological premise—perhaps that human flourishing requires this kind of unity—that supplies the normative force? I am also trying to test whether his framework can illuminate digitally mediated groups that exhibit intense shared mental life despite lacking the biological and territorial substrates he appears to require. Could you walk me through the logic of the passage, distinguishing the descriptive sociology from the normative claim, and indicate precisely where a liberal critic would deny the inference from shared existence to non-consensual obligation?
