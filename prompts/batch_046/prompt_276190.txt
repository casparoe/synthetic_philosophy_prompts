After retiring from experimental physics and turning to philosophy, I have become preoccupied with Douglas Hofstadter's dialogue involving Aunt Hillary — the ant colony that behaves like a single organism in the Ant Fugue. I wish to press the boundary of collective cognition with the following argument, which seems to prove that either ant colonies are not minds after all, or far too many engineered systems are minds. (1) If a distributed system processes information through recursive feedback loops whose global state regulates local behavior and produces outputs that are not linearly decomposable into the contributions of individual nodes, then that system is a unified cognitive agent rather than a mere aggregate. (2) In Aunt Hillary, pheromone trails form layered counterpoint analogous to voices in a fugue, where trail reinforcement and caste-state transitions generate colony-level predictions about resource distribution that no single ant represents or computes. (3) Hence Aunt Hillary satisfies the condition in (1) and is a unified cognitive agent with a mind. (4) Yet a distributed climate-modeling cluster, or a market of algorithmic traders, also processes information through recursive feedback loops producing globally regulated, non-linearly decomposable outputs. (5) Therefore, if (1) holds, such engineered clusters also have minds, which proves far too much; so we must reject either (1) or (2). Which step is the weakest link, and why did it initially seem compelling? Specifically, does rejecting (2) conflict with empirical studies of ant colony computation, does rejecting (1) commit us to biological individualism about minds, and could a modified version of Hofstadter's symbol-level versus signal-level distinction salvage (1) without the absurd extension? Present your diagnosis as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
