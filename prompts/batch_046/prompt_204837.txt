I sit on a municipal labor-relations board and every quarter I have to sign off on multi-round collective-bargaining protocols before they go live. I’m not a game theorist — I was an organizer for years before I got appointed — and I keep seeing lawyers design protocols with ten or twelve rounds assuming more rounds means more cooperation, without seeing the induction problem staring them in the face. I want a reusable decision proceddure, like a checklist I can actually run in forty minutes, that translates what the backward-induction paradox and the centipede game actually imply for real-world negotiation design. It should ask ordered questions about whether the endpoint is common knowledge, whether exit costs are credible and visible, how payoffs shift round-to-round, and where common knowledge of rationality actually forces defection. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls in disguise, mark the hard stops where the answer is definitely reject or approve, and flag the grey zone where the checklist hands the decision back to a person because it’s about risk tolerance, not logic. Then run it on two proposals: Protocol A lets either side declare impasse after any round with zero penalty but allows talks to resume later, and Protocol B requires both sides to put funds in escrow that burn incrementally if anyone walks away, with reopening banned entirely. Include one case where the procedure tells me to approve something I’ll probably hate, and say clearly what the checklist can’t fix no matter how well I run it.
