I am a software engineer without formal training in philosophy or mathematical logic. I recently encountered a technical essay—aimed at people working on AI safety and formal verification—that summarized Gödel's incompleteness theorems and drew conclusions about the limits of mechanized proof. The summary went roughly as follows. Gödel's first theorem establishes that in any consistent formal system capable of expressing elementary arithmetic, there exist true arithmetic sentences that the system cannot prove. His second theorem adds that if such a system is consistent, it cannot prove its own consistency. The author then applied these results to modern proof assistants—specifically mentioning dependently-typed systems like Lean and Coq, which use the Curry-Howard correspondence to verify software properties. The claim was that because these systems are powerful enough to encode arithmetic, Gödel's conditions apply directly: no proof assistant can ever verify every true property of the programs it checks, and none can establish its own consistency internally. The essay went further, arguing that this creates an in-principle barrier to fully formalizing AI alignment: any safety guarantee expressed within such a system will necessarily leave some true safety conditions unproven. Finally, the author suggested that human mathematicians and engineers can overcome this limitation because human cognition is not restricted to a single formal system, allowing us to recognize truths that lie outside any particular mechanized framework.

I want to assess whether this summary is fair—both as an account of Gödel's theorems and as a philosophical argument about formal verification and AI safety. Please examine it point by point: which claims accurately reflect the theorems, which subtly misrepresent the scope or conditions (for example, whether being powerful enough to encode arithmetic is correctly applied to proof assistants, or whether there is a hidden slide from unprovable arithmetic sentences to unprovable program properties), and which conclusions depend on additional premises not contained in Gödel's results themselves. In particular, I am interested in whether the argument about human cognition accessing outside truths actually follows from Gödel, or whether it imports an unstated philosophical assumption. I also want to understand whether the distinction between a system's inability to prove its own consistency and its inability to prove arbitrary true statements about software has been collapsed, and whether that collapse matters for the essay's conclusion about AI alignment.
