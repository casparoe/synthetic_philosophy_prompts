I recently encountered an essay summarizing the exchange between Avishai Margalit and David Rieff on whether societies bear a duty to remember historical wrongs. The author writes that Margalit defends a universal moral obligation to preserve shared collective memory, arguing that a community's identity is literally constituted by what it remembers together, and that to forget is therefore to dissolve the moral bonds that make any claim of injustice coherent; on this view, remembrance is not merely therapeutic but structurally necessary for justice itself. Rieff, according to the summary, rejects this as dangerous essentialism, contending instead that remembrance is always therapeutically valuable for survivors and that societies ought therefore to institutionalize active forgetting—specifically through state-sponsored amnesty mechanisms modeled on the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission—so that the past does not poison present politics. The essay concludes that Margalit's position collapses into a form of identity politics, while Rieff's liberal neutrality requires the state to enforce collective amnesia, leaving private grief untouched.

Something about this characterization feels strained—both thinkers seem more nuanced, and the conclusions appear to outrun the premises. Is this a fair summary? Please separate, without relying on citation lists: (a) what each thinker actually holds, where the summary captures the core concern, and where it conflates Margalit's distinction between an ethics of thick relations and a universal morality of memory; (b) whether Rieff's actual skepticism about the therapeutic value of remembrance and his stance on state amnesia are misrepresented here, and whether either thinker makes remembrance constitutive of justice in the manner described; and (c) whether the essay's concluding contrast—Margalit as identity politics versus Rieff as liberal neutrality demanding state-enforced forgetting—survives these corrections, or whether it depends on the distortions. I am particularly interested in whether these errors are common popular simplifications or serious misreadings that would mislead someone approaching the debate without direct access to the original texts.
