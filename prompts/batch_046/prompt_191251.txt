I have been thinking on my own about whether an enormous number of barely perceptible harms can ever amount to something morally worse than a single extreme harm, and I have settled on a particular image to test my intuition. Imagine that one person suffers intensely for an entire year. Now imagine that every person alive experiences, once each day for a century, a fleeting itch so faint that it is forgotten almost immediately. Even if one tries to add up all that discomfort amongst these cases, I wonder whether moral weight might depend on a kind of resolution: perhaps harm only registers once it crosses a threshold of intensity or duration, rather like a picture that needs a minimum number of pixels before a shape becomes visible.

I am not asking for a list of philosophers or texts. Instead, I would like your actual credence — expressed as a number between 0 and 1 — that, once we reject simple additive counting, the aggregate of those trillions of tiny discomforts could genuinely be the morally worse scenario. Please decompose your reasoning into distinct parts and assign each a rough probability.

Specifically:
1. How likely is it that moral value is genuinely granular, with a floor below which individual harms do not register at all?
2. How likely is it that aggregation follows a non-linear rule (for instance, one that makes extreme harms disproportionately weighty) regardless of whether there is a granularity floor?
3. How likely is it that comparing these two distributions is conceptually coherent — that there is a determinate fact about which is worse — rather than being a category mistake?

Show me how these combine to yield your overall number. Then tell me which part you are least certain about, and what line of reasoning — not a reference to an authority — would most move that particular value.
