I am working through a problem in a seminar on institutional ethics and distributive justice, and I keep returning to a screening rule used—at least in principle—by several multilateral development and climate-adaptation funding bodies. The rule is that a proposed policy or investment is approved only if it counts as what internal evaluators call a safe Pareto improvement: there must be at least one credible socioeconomic scenario in which some affected party is made strictly better off, and there must be no credible scenario in which anyone is made worse off relative to the baseline. In practice, this means that projects with highly ambiguous distributional footprints—say, a large-scale irrigation scheme that benefits farmers under high-rainfall models but threatens pastoralist water access under drought models—are rejected not because the expected harm exceeds the expected gain, but because harm is not ruled out across the ensemble of forecasts. I find the rule puzzling: it seems to embed a particular philosophy of risk and moral standing, yet defenders present it as a purely procedural safeguard with no deeper distributive theory behind it.

What philosophical commitments about value, moral risk, and distributive justice does this institutional practice presuppose? Does it require a lexical priority of harm-avoidance over benefit-production, or can it be reconstructed within a contractualist framework in which coercive or redistributive authority is legitimate only when it does not worsen anyone's position under any reasonable conception of welfare? Reconstruct the implicit normative theory, distinguish strictly necessary commitments from ones that merely fit the practice well, and assess whether the rule is philosophically underdetermined—compatible, for example, with both a Rawlsian maximin principle applied under deep uncertainty and with a conservative status-quo bias that lacks any genuine distributive rationale. Furthermore, describe what a funding framework that abandoned the safe-Pareto constraint but retained a serious commitment to protecting the vulnerable from exploitation or domination would have to look like in terms of decision procedure, evidentiary standards, and institutional accountability.
