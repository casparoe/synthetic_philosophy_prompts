Here are my gut calls on whether a claim counts as american exceptionalism, and im stuck on the pattern. Case 1: a senator in the 1800s says we shouldnt join international courts because our common law is already more just than any treaty — yes, thats exceptionalism. Case 2: a demographer notes US urbanization was faster than france's and chalks it up to coal and railroads, not destiny — not exceptionalism, just history. Case 3: a government memo argues geneva conventions dont apply to detainees because american values are too unique to be boxed in by old european rules — yes, exceptionalism. Case 4: a textbook points out we have the oldest written constitution still running and frames that as proof of special national character — yes, but it feels different from case 3 somehow. Case 5: an NGO head says US charity should replace global aid structures because our civil society is more efficient — yes? Case 6: a philosopher says liberal democracy is universal, it just happened to be invented in philadelphia first, so the US has no special duties — not exceptionalism, just chronology. Case 7: a climate policy paper says developing countries should get looser emissions targets but the US should stay strict because american innovation will save everyone — i keep flipping on this one.

Is there one principle that sorts all these? Maybe something like exemptionism vs missionism, or descriptive vs normative? I want the account that saves the most verdicts, what it predicts for case 8 — a historian arguing US slavery lasted longer than brazils so american race relations are uniquely damaged — and an honest list of which of my gut calls it has to overturn. No need for sources or a reading list, just the principle and the repair job.
