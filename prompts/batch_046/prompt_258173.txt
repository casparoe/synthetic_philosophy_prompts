You have been asked to deliver the opening address at a workshop convened jointly by a municipal arts council and a university philosophy department. The occasion is the reopening of a public garden that was promised to the neighborhood in 1972, converted to a parking structure in 1998, and now—following a settlement—restored as green space with a plaque acknowledging the broken promise. The audience is split: half are moral philosophers and legal theorists who work on normative powers, promissory obligation, and institutional standing; the other half are neighborhood residents, some of whom were present in 1972 and 1998, local organizers, and the city officials who signed the 2024 restoration agreement.

Write a roughly six-minute speech (approximately 800 words) that takes the question “why do promises bind?” as its structural spine—not as ornament, but as the problem that determines what can and cannot be said about this reopening. Navigate a specific conceptual tension. On one view, a promise binds because the promisor exercises a normative authority that creates a directed duty in the promisee; if the city forfeited that authority by violating its 1972 commitment, then its 2024 pledge may be structurally hollow, no matter how sincere the ceremony. On another view, a promise binds because it generates reasonable reliance and vulnerability; on that account, the 2024 promise might still bind precisely because residents are vulnerable to being disappointed again, and the city’s damaged standing does not dissolve the obligation it creates by asking for trust.

The speech must speak to both halves of the room. The philosophers should recognize that you are working seriously with the authority-versus-reliance distinction, without reducing either side to a straw figure. The residents should understand why this philosophical question bears directly on whether they should treat the plaque and the new agreement as trustworthy, without feeling that they are being lectured by abstraction. Crucially, do not treat the reopening as automatically redemptive. The speech should ask openly whether an institution that has broken its normative standing can genuinely “re-promise,” or whether what it offers is something weaker—an apology with landscaping. At the same time, it must not collapse into cynicism: the philosophical framework should shape what kind of hope is rational here, distinguishing between trust that is warranted and hope that is merely wished for.

Do not provide citations, a bibliography, or a reading list, and do not name specific philosophers unless unavoidable common knowledge demands it; develop the conceptual tension in your own terms. End with a concrete, modest proposal for what the workshop itself should investigate: under what conditions—institutional, relational, or both—would the city’s 2024 commitment count as binding in the same normative sense as the original 1972 promise, and how could the community tell the difference?
