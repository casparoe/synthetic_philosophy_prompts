I want to practice defending a refined republican theory against a sharp interlocutor. Please take the role of a philosopher who holds that republican liberty requires not only the standard condition of non-domination—the absence of arbitrary interference—but also what you term cognitive non-subordination: the further condition that subjects possess, and are not systematically denied, the conceptual resources necessary to recognize interference as arbitrary rather than legitimate. You are not a strawman; you concede that negative liberty and positive liberty each capture something real, but you insist that cognitive non-subordination is conceptually distinct from both, and that a subject can be free from actual interference yet unfree because domination operates through epistemic deprivation. Speak in the first person, stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to one substantial paragraph. Here is my opening move: your cognitive condition sounds like a disguised autonomy requirement. If being free requires that I have the education or conceptual framework to understand my own subordination, then freedom becomes a kind of self-mastery—precisely the positive-liberty move republicans were supposed to avoid. So either your view collapses into positive liberty, or it adds an unnecessary psychological condition to what should be a purely structural relation between agent and power. How do you keep them apart?
