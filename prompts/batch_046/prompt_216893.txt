Design a 90-minute workshop activity for 14 graduate students in philosophy or cognitive science that makes participants experience, before they are told what it is, how a self-referential symbol like Hofstadter's "I" might bootstrap itself from distributed representational loops rather than residing in a single substrate. The room should have no lecture after the first five minutes of silent rule-reading.

Introduce a running "proxy ledger" mechanism: every time the group needs to record or communicate "who decided this" or "what the collective intends," the statement must be rewritten through an intermediate symbolic proxy (e.g., "the current node," "the ledger entry," or "the active rule-set"), and every reference to that proxy must in turn be rewritten through another proxy. Assign rotating roles—Recorder, Challenger, Proxy-Assigner, and Silent Observer—so that no single participant controls the chain and so that coordination friction is real rather than theatrical. Structure the session in four phases: (1) 10 minutes of silent individual proxy-naming; (2) 35 minutes of collective decision-making on a shared practical problem (for example, allocating a real but small shared resource like snack preferences or seating order) using only the ledger; (3) 25 minutes in which participants must resolve a contradiction that arises when two ledger branches point back at each other; (4) 20 minutes of debrief.

Arrange the rules and incentives so that the group eventually produces a symbol that refers back to the collective activity itself—something like "this ledger" or "the deciding loop"—but do not announce this target in advance. The debrief should map specific moments in the ledger-building onto features of Hofstadter's strange-loop account: the absence of a central controller, the self-reference that operates at the pattern level rather than the object level, and the idea that the symbol for the self is a compressed abstraction of lower-level loops. Be explicit about what the activity can reproduce—the bootstrapping of self-reference from distributed, lower-order representations—and what it cannot reproduce, namely the first-person phenomenological sense of being a single, unified subject. Identify one predictable failure mode (for instance, one participant breaking the proxy chain by appealing to direct intention, collapsing the loop into ordinary group deliberation) and specify how the facilitator should handle it without dissolving the mechanism.

Write the response in American English. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or requests for outside research. Do not ask participants to consult Hofstadter's texts during the session.
