In my advanced seminar on assertion and belief, I have been pressing my students on Moore-paradoxical utterances, and I find my own intuitions pulling in directions I cannot fully reconcile. I want to see whether there is a coherent principle behind the following verdicts, or whether one of them has to be surrendered.

Consider these cases, all sincere first-person present-tense assertions unless noted otherwise. First, the canonical utterance: a speaker says, "It is raining, but I do not believe it." My verdict is that this is not merely false but structurally absurd: it undermines the speaker's authority as an informant and defeats the very point of putting a proposition into the common ground. Second, a graded variant: "It is raining, but my credence that it is raining is only 0.4." Here I judge the absurdity to be equally severe, perhaps more transparent, since the speaker simultaneously invites uptake and explicitly disclaims the mental state that normally licenses it. Third, an evidential disclaimer: "It is raining, but I lack sufficient evidence to believe it." My verdict shifts sharply—this sounds like a candid admission of epistemic weakness, perhaps reporting a model output one does not personally endorse, and I do not find it paradoxical at all. Fourth, a delegated institutional case: a spokesperson for a climate-science body asserts, "Global temperatures are rising, but I personally do not believe the models are accurate," presenting the claim as the institution's position rather than her own. I feel this is incoherent in a way that parallels the canonical Moore case, even though her personal doxastic state is not directly contradictory. Fifth, the retrospective version: "It was raining yesterday, but I did not believe it at the time"—perfectly coherent, with no trace of absurdity. Sixth, what I have been calling the agnostic-commitment case: "It is raining, and I have no belief about whether I believe that it is raining." My instinct is that this belongs closer to the canonical absurdity than to the evidential disclaimer, though I am unsure whether the flaw is identical.

The difficulty is that the canonical case, the graded-belief case, and the delegated-assertion case all involve some fracture between what is presented for acceptance and what is personally endorsed, yet the evidential-disclaimer case involves a similar fracture without producing absurdity. Meanwhile, the retrospective case dissolves the tension through temporal separation, which is easily explained, but the agnostic-commitment case introduces a second-order gap whose status I cannot fix. Can you propose one or more precise principles that capture these six verdicts? For each, specify which clause is motivated by which case, predict how it handles further scenarios—such as a speaker contractually obligated to assert a proposition she believes false, or one who asserts with full belief but adds "though I could be wrong"—and state honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate without revision. I am especially interested in whether any principle can distinguish the evidential-disclaimer case from the graded-belief case without ad hoc stipulation, and whether the delegated-assertion case forces us to extend Moore's paradox beyond individual psychology into institutional epistemology.
