I'm working through a puzzle in the philosophy of social kinds and keep encountering the same argumentative loop in debates about race and gender, though I cannot tell whether philosophers have isolated it as a distinct move rather than a generic instance of social construction. Second, defenders reply by citing robust statistical disparities—gaps in accumulated wealth, morbidity rates, or predictive risk scores—that appear to track the category reliably across contexts and therefore seem to capture something real. Third, one notices that these disparities are themselves produced by the institutional machinery that applies the unstable classifications: census categories determine funding formulas and epidemiological sampling frames, gender markers determine clinical-trial eligibility and diagnostic algorithms, and risk scores are trained on data labeled by the same bureaucratic rules. Thus boundary fuzziness is answered by appealing to effects that depend on treating the boundaries as sharp, and those effects are then used to justify preserving the sharp boundaries.
