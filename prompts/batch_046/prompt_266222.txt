Consider the following line of reasoning, which I have been pressing in a graduate seminar and which now seems to overreach in a way I cannot precisely locate. (1) Darwin's account of natural selection demonstrates that adaptive design—eyes, wings, metabolic pathways—can emerge from a process that possesses no representational grasp of what it is building; the mechanism is competent without being comprehending. (2) Turing's analysis of mechanical computation demonstrates an isomorphic inversion: a system can execute arbitrarily complex symbolic operations—proofs, predictions, translations—without any semantic access to the meanings of those symbols; computation is competent without comprehension. (3) Human cognitive architecture is, by all available scientific accounts, the joint product of evolutionary selection (premise 1) and neural information-processing (premise 2). (4) If comprehension is not present at the foundational level of a system, no structural elaboration upon that foundation can conjure it ex nihilo; organizational complexity amplifies competence but does not generate a novel ontological category of understanding. (5) Consequently, the human capacity for theoretical reasoning—including our capacity to formulate Darwin's theory and Turing's model—is itself an instance of high-order competence without comprehension. (6) This collapses the distinction between a physicist who genuinely understands quantum mechanics and a hypothetical artifact that produces identical theoretical outputs with no inner grasp; the only difference is quantitative complexity, not qualitative presence of comprehension. (7) But that distinction is not merely intuitive—it is presupposed by the very practice of science, including the practice of arguing for premises 1 through 5, which we take ourselves to understand rather than merely emit. (8) Therefore, the reflexive application of the Darwin-Turing inversion to human cognition must be blocked at some point, though I cannot see where without damaging one of the premises I wish to preserve.

My question: identify the precise step at which the argument slips—whether it is an equivocation between biological and cognitive competence, an unjustified assumption about the non-emergence of comprehension, or a hidden modal error in the reflexive turn—and explain what rejecting that step costs. In particular, if we reject step 4, do we commit ourselves to an emergentist metaphysics of mind? If we reject step 3, do we abandon naturalism about cognition? And does the argument reveal a genuine tension in the concept of "competence without comprehension" when it is turned upon the agent who deploys it, or is the tension merely apparent?
