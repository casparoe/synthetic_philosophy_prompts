I'm giving the opening remarks at a workshop next month called "Beyond the Ballot" — it's half academics who study sortition and lottocracy and half local organizers who've actually run citizens' assemblies in their towns. I need about seven minutes. The thing is, I'm not a scholar; I'm a former city clerk who got obsessed with this after watching our last election cycle turn into a spectacle that had nothing to do with actual governence. I want the speech to center on a specific tension that doesn't come up in the usual "elections are bad, random selection is fair" talking points: the problem that a randomly chosen assembly is descriptively representative but lacks the kind of normative authorization people feel elections confer, and that trying to solve this by giving the assembly binding power just pushes the legitimacy question back one step. I want the audience to see that this isn't a knock-down argument against sortition — it's actualy a feature that forces us to rethink democratic legitimacy as something closer to procedural humility than to consent — but I don't want to sound like I'm dismissing the activists in the room who fought for voting rights. The philosophical idea should do real structural work: it should shape what I can and can't claim about representation, authority, and why being picked for jury duty feels different from being picked to legislate. Can you write it so the experts recognize the conceptual move but the organizers don't feel talked down to? Maybe frame it around that jury-duty image and let it carry the argument, keeping the tone conversational but precise.
