You are a professional moral philosopher specializing in collective action problems and applied ethics. Before giving your diagnosis, please take a moment to brainstorm the possible sources of philosophical deadlock—whether conceptual, empirical, normative, or methodological—and list them briefly, then proceed with your analysis.

The debate I have in mind concerns the so-called inefficacy objection to individual moral obligations in large-scale collective harm cases—climate change, industrial animal agriculture, and democratic voting. On one side, philosophers and activists argue that individuals have direct obligations to reduce carbon footprints, adopt plant-based diets, or vote, treating these as standard moral duties that survive aggregation. On the other side, critics invoke causal impotence: a single person's emissions, meal choice, or ballot is numerically negligible relative to the total, so the individual makes no difference to whether harm occurs. The discussion has been running for decades with familiar moves—expected utility adjustments, threshold models, Kantian universalization, and virtue-theoretic responses—yet neither camp has conceded ground, and recent literature seems to recycle the same objections. Here is the specific configuration that puzzles me. In climate and meat cases, critics often concede that collective action works but insist that this does not translate back into individual obligation because of what we might call structural redundancy: each agent's contribution is substitutable by others, so no particular agent is necessary. Meanwhile, defenders often reply by appealing to collective intentionality or joint causation, but they rarely address whether the moral force they invoke actually attaches to the individual agent or only to the group as an emergent entity. In voting, the problem takes a different shape—each vote is causally redundant in large electorates, yet democratic theory treats voting as a civic duty—suggesting that our intuitions about inefficacy may be domain-sensitive in ways neither side systematically explains. I want you to diagnose the stall: is this a hidden disagreement about whether moral reasons must be difference-making, a verbal dispute over 'causation' versus 'participation,' a clash between person-affecting and impersonal frameworks, or something else entirely? And given your diagnosis, what distinction, empirical finding, or reconceptualization—if any—would actually move the debate forward, or should we conclude that the disagreement tracks irreconcilable bedrock intuitions about individual agency within massive systems?
