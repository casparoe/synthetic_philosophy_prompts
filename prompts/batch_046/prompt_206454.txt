I am a software engineer with roughly eight years of experience in distributed systems and no formal background in philosophy, though I occasionally read about rational decision-making because it bears directly on how my team evaluates technical risk. Recently, my tech lead and I were deciding whether to migrate our primary datastore to a new graph database architecture. Before examining any data, we explicitly wrote down a shared prior: we both agreed there was about a twenty-five percent chance of a critical service outage within six months. We then spent two hours together reviewing identical benchmark reports, pilot-cluster incident logs, and vendor reliability statistics. At the end, I had updated my belief to roughly sixty percent risk, while she had updated to roughly fifteen percent. We cross-checked our arithmetic, verified that we had interpreted every chart identically, and even agreed to evaluate our forecasts with a logarithmic scoring rule to keep our confidence calibrated; yet we remain genuinely divided. Our engineering manager now wants to know which of us is being irrational. I understand that in practice our different histories with similar migrations may explain the divergence, but I wonder whether there is a deeper philosophical principle about rational belief, shared evidence, and agreement that applies here. Specifically, should two rational agents with the same prior, the same observed evidence, and verified reasoning necessarily converge on a single posterior belief? If they do not converge, does that indicate that one of us has made an error, or is persistent disagreement under these conditions philosophically legitimate? I would appreciate an explanation of what conditions would need to hold for convergence to be required, whether our situation subtly violates one of them—perhaps in how we weight the same facts rather than in the facts themselves—and what a fair resolution process looks like that avoids simply defaulting to seniority, authority, or a coin flip.
