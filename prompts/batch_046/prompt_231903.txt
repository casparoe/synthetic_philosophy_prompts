Read Mary Shelley's Frankenstein through the lens of material constitution — specifically the statue-and-clay puzzle, but with a twist: the constituent matter is not inert clay but fragments of dead bodies that were themselves once living, constituted persons. Focus on Victor's assembly and animation of the Creature, and on the Creature's subsequent narrative of awakening, education, and moral suffering. Is the Creature a single object constituted by those cadaverous parts, or is he an aggregate that merely appears unified? Does Victor's horror come from recognizing that he has made something with mental and moral properties that dead tissue cannot have — the analog of a statue that thinks — or from the more unsettling possibility that the parts retain residual histories and persistence conditions of their former owners, making the Creature not one person but a cluster of partial persons coincident in one body? Where does the analogy between biological assembly and sculpting illuminate the Creature's demand for recognition and a name, and where does it collapse because living parts carry modal and temporal properties that clay lacks? A faithful answer should not reduce the novel to an illustration of analytic metaphysics; it should also say what Shelley's dramatization of made-personhood adds to the debate about constitution that arguments about statues and clay alone do not capture.
