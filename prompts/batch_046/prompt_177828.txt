I'm drafting an essay on the epistemology of archival absence in the transition from parish-based poor relief to centralized municipal welfare administration around the late nineteenth century. Rather than treating missing records as mere data loss, I want to argue that bureaucratic form itself generated what I'm calling structured omission—a systematic non-inscription of certain populations—distinct from accidental lacuna. The core claim is that when relief administration shifted from face-to-face parochial knowledge to standardized intake forms, the mobile poor (those without fixed address, regular employment, or kinship ties within the parish) did not simply fall out of the record; they were rendered administratively illegible in a specific, predictable manner. The forms required fields—residence, occupation, family structure—that presupposed a settled, nuclear household, so applicants who could not supply these categories were either turned away unrecorded or shoehorned into false categories that erased their actual conditions. My tentative thesis is that this wasn't an incidental failure of coverage but a constitutive feature of the new administrative rationality: the archive didn't just forget the mobile poor; it produced them as a category of non-subject precisely through its classificatory architecture.

I'd welcome comments on whether this distinction between structured omission and accidental lacuna actually does explanatory work, or whether it collapses into a more familiar Foucauldian point about power and knowledge that I'm merely redescribing. I'm also unsure about a specific move in the middle of the argument: I claim that the forms' presuppositions were not merely epistemic but normative—that by requiring a settled address, the bureaucracy was implicitly enforcing a particular model of deserving poverty tied to domestic stability. Is that inference warranted, or am I smuggling in a normative conclusion that the institutional logic doesn't actually support? Finally, I'm considering extending the argument to suggest that the silences in these archives should be read not as absences demanding recovery but as positive traces of administrative violence. That feels like it might overreach, but I'd like to know whether the earlier premises genuinely license it or whether I need an additional bridge about intentionality or structural harm. Any suggestions on coherence, interest, or where the reasoning is weakest would be helpful.
