"Explain Rawls's separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism. Then apply it to a intergenerational case: suppose a climate policy forces severe, lifelong austerity on everyone alive now—no air travel, rationed heating, etc.—but this produces enormous welfare gains for people living 300 years from now, with no overlap in lives. Does the separateness objection say this tradeoff is unjust, or does it only apply within one generation? Assess both options."

And heres the student answer (they got about 15 mins on it):

"Rawls's separateness objection says that utilitarianism treats society like one big person, so it allows one group to suffer for the total good, just like one person might take pain now for pleasure later. But people are actually separate, with their own lives, so you can't justify making the present generation suffer just to help future people. This is basically Nozick's utility monster problem applied across time—if future people soak up all the utility, the present ones are just fuel for the calculus. Because of this, Rawls thinks we can't make interpersonal comparisons of well-being at all, which is why he uses the veil of ignorance to pick principles without knowing who we are. So in the climate case, the tradeoff is unjust: you can't sacrifice one generation for another, just like you can't sacrifice one person for another. Indirect rule-utilitarianism escapes this because it uses rules that protect everyone, so a rule against intergenerational exploitation would be adopted. Therefore the objection succeeds and blocks the policy."
