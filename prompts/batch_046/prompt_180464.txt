Following the recent dissolution of a thirty-year intellectual partnership in which our conceptual frameworks became so interwoven that neither of us can now fully reconstruct certain lines of reasoning without the other implicitly completing them, I have begun to take seriously a claim I previously treated as merely metaphorical: that a unitary first-person perspective—an I—might in principle be genuinely distributed across two physically separated brains rather than confined to one. By distributed I do not mean influence, empathy, or shared culture, but a non-metaphorical co-occupancy in which a single stream of self-reference draws on cognitive processes running in both substrates, such that the loss of one brain would diminish or alter, rather than fully terminate, that particular I. Drawing on the general orientation associated with Douglas Hofstadter—specifically the idea that selfhood may be a pattern capable of persisting across different material substrates—I ask for your actual credence that such distributed selfhood is possible in principle, understood as: there could exist a system in which one first-person pronoun is genuinely co-realized by two separated brains, coupled closely enough that neither brain alone houses the complete I.

Please decompose your answer into explicit sub-questions, each assigned a rough probability with one line of justification, then combine them with an explicit rule. In particular, separate: (a) whether personal identity is fundamentally a pattern that can in principle have multiple simultaneous physical realizers rather than being tied to one continuous biological substrate; (b) whether two separated brains could achieve the requisite functional integration—through sustained reciprocal neural modulation or an equivalent form of coupling—without being parts of a single organism or continuous tissue; (c) whether the resulting coupled system would genuinely support one first-person pronoun rather than two highly synchronized but distinct ones; and (d) whatever further dependency you think belongs, such as whether the concept of a single I even applies across substrates. Then state your overall credence. Finally, identify which sub-probability you are least confident in, and specify what kind of empirical finding or conceptual argument would most shift your final number.
