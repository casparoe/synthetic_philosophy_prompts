I have recently been appointed to a cross-border citizens’ council that advises on water-rights and climate-adaptation policy, and the boundary problem has become personally urgent: I reside in the jurisdiction, pay local taxes, and am directly affected by its decisions, yet I hold no citizenship, while some citizens live permanently abroad and are barely touched by the outcomes, and the policies we design will bind generations not yet born. Rather than asking for a bibliography or a survey of canonical texts, I want you to construct a diagnostic taxonomy—structured as a branching decision tree with concrete nodes and named leaves—that sorts the competing answers to “who belongs to the demos?” specifically in this kind of multi-level, intergenerational, and functionally differentiated governance setting.

The tree should be built from the logical space of commitments rather than from a list of pre-labeled theories. Start with a root question that a thoughtful non-specialist could answer without knowing democratic theory, then branch through diagnostic claims about whether membership requires being subject to coercive law, being affected by collective outcomes, possessing deliberative competence, or holding a pre-political status such as citizenship or territorial residence; whether the boundary ought to be uniform across all policy domains or should vary functionally; and whether membership obligations and rights extend diachronically to future generations or remain strictly synchronic to present residents. At each leaf, place a named position if one is genuinely defended—such as all-affected-interests democracy, stakeholder democracy, territorial citizenship, associative democracy, or intergenerational-covenant views—and for logically available combinations that remain unoccupied, briefly diagnose whether the gap stems from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Please flag any pair of named positions that your branching questions fail to separate, and avoid all requests for citations, reading lists, or source attributions.

To prevent the instrument from collapsing into a generic quiz, inject one somewhat niche conceptual distinction into the branching logic: treat “membership as authorization” (the standing to legitimately authorize collective decisions) and “membership as exposure” (the standing to be legitimately exposed to the risks and benefits of those decisions) as independent axes rather than as two descriptions of a single relation. This should yield hybrid or split-boundary leaves—such as non-citizen residents who are exposed but do not authorize, or distant economic stakeholders who might be said to authorize through investment without being exposed to local ecological risk—so that the taxonomy can locate positions that standard all-or-nothing accounts of the demos necessarily miss. Write in a polished, formal register appropriate for a handout in political philosophy.
