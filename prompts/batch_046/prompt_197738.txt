I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I am trying to think more rigorously about how the ontology-design work I do relates to scientific classification. I recently watched the film Arrival and want to read it through the philosophical question of natural kinds: do the sciences discover real divisions in nature, or do they construct useful ones? The heptapods' language seems to encode a reality structured differently from human taxonomies—particularly in how it treats time not as a linear container but as a simultaneous field. I keep returning to an idea that does not come up in standard philosophy-of-science discussions: that realism about carving nature at its joints might hold for static kinds, such as chemical elements or biological species, but collapses for processual kinds—entities like storm, war, or conversation that are defined by dynamics rather than stable properties. If the heptapods are carving reality along processual joints that human science ignores because our databases are built for static entities, the film might dramatize a failure mode in our ontology design.

Please give me a reading that takes both the film and the debate seriously. Where does Louise Banks's growing fluency in heptapod suggest she is acquiring not merely vocabulary but a different ontology of kinds? How do scenes in which the military insists on categorizing the alien presence as either a weapon or a tool illustrate the human tendency to force dynamic entities into static taxonomies? Where does the film resist this reading—perhaps in the way the narrative ultimately resolves around human emotional and political dynamics rather than ontological revelation? And what does the work add to the natural-kinds debate that pure philosophical argument misses? I do not need citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; I am working without ready access to academic resources and want to use this to clarify my own framework.
