I'm delivering the opening remarks at the launch of the Meridian Institute for Cross-Disciplinary Inquiry. The room will hold philosophers and logicians familiar with Russell's paradox and the limits of naive comprehension, researchers from the sciences and humanities who are not, and board members concerned with institutional identity. I want the philosophical idea to shape the speech structurally rather than serve as ornament: specifically, I want to use Russell's paradox — that unrestricted comprehension allows the self-referential set of all sets that do not contain themselves — as a live warning about how we define our own membership. The risk is that if we characterize the institute as the collection of all scholars who do not belong to a single discipline, we construct a description that undermines itself the moment the institute becomes a discipline in its own right. So the address must welcome people without claiming that interdisciplinary denotes a stable, self-inclusive set; it must concede that coherence requires restricted, iterative membership criteria analogous to Zermelo-Fraenkel comprehension rather than naive inclusion. At the same time, the speech should reassure non-philosophers that this failure of universality is not a failure of purpose. Write a polished five-minute address that lands for both halves of the room, avoids technical lecture, and never asserts false universality about who belongs. Then please indicate how confident you are in the institutional analogy — especially whether mapping set-theoretic self-membership onto disciplinary self-definition is genuinely illuminating or strained — and identify the single point in your response about which you are least certain.
