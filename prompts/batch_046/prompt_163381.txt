As a novelist whose manuscripts have been catalogued by publishers under successive generational labels—first "post-9/11," then "Millennial," now "Gen Z adjacent"—I have come to regard the literary industry's cohort-based framing as something more than marketing convenience. The practice is now institutionalized: prize committees request "representative" generational panels, university syllabi organize contemporary fiction by birth-decade brackets, and review outlets assign critics according to cohort identity rather than thematic expertise. I want to understand what ontology this machinery quietly presupposes. Does treating "Gen Z" or "Millennial" as curatorial categories commit us to the view that birth-year ranges form natural kinds with shared essences—some psychological or historical substrate that unifies individuals born between, say, 1997 and 2012? Or can the practice be sustained on purely operational grounds, as a heuristic for tracking period effects without ontological weight? I am particularly interested in whether the industry's framing implicitly resolves the age-period-cohort identification problem—treating observed differences as cohort properties rather than life-stage or historical-period artifacts—and whether that resolution is philosophically defensible. Reconstruct the implicit theory of what a generation is that makes this institutional sorting coherent. Is the practice compatible with a purely constructivist view of generations, or does it smuggle in essentialism about historical experience? And if we abandoned cohort-based curation, what alternative taxonomy—perhaps organized by technological mediation, economic precarity, or narrative form—would avoid the ontological commitments the current system makes?
