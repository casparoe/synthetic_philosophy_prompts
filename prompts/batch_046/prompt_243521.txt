I serve as a senior mediator for an intergovernmental board that resolves conflicts between indigenous governance councils and state regulatory agencies over land use, ceremonial access, and resource management. These disputes rarely involve simple factual disagreements; they involve incompatible normative frameworks—collective custodianship versus individual property rights, sacred non-commodification versus economic development—that cannot be merged into a single value system. I need a reusable decision procedure, expressed as an ordered checklist with explicit stopping conditions, that my team can apply in under ninety minutes per proposal to determine whether a proposed agreement represents a legitimate moral compromise—what we might call a gain from trade, in which each side secures a value it ranks highly in exchange for conceding one it ranks lower—versus a case of value corruption, coerced assimilation, or a structurally unfair bargain that should be rejected outright. The procedure must distinguish between constitutive commitments, those that if surrendered would dissolve the party's normative identity, and instrumental preferences, those that can be traded without such dissolution; it must include a power-asymmetry audit; and it must mark precisely which steps are mechanistic and which require an ethics-panel judgment call that cannot be automated.

Please construct this protocol step-by-step: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to proceed, the conditions that trigger an immediate stop with a recommendation against agreement, and the conditions that pass the proposal to the ethics panel because the issue is irreducibly one of values rather than procedure. Then apply the procedure to three concrete scenarios: first, a council agrees to permit limited archaeological documentation of a burial site in exchange for formal co-management authority and veto power over future excavations; second, a state demands that seasonal ceremonial closures be relocated and compressed into a two-week window to accommodate a hydropower timeline, offering in exchange enhanced funding for cultural preservation programs; and third, a proposal that appears symmetrical on paper—shared revenue from a mining operation in return for modified ritual access protocols—but involves one party with legal enforcement capacity and the other without, and in which the mediator's own preliminary assessment suggests the deal should fail despite its surface fairness. In your applications, include at least one case where the procedure produces a recommendation that conflicts with an intuitive political preference, such as rejecting a deal that looks equitable but violates a constitutive commitment, or approving one that looks like a concession but is actually a protected trade within instrumental preferences. Finally, specify the limits of the procedure: what it cannot settle, what kinds of errors it is prone to make, and under what conditions the mediator must override its output.
