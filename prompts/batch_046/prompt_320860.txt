A philosopher friend who writes on intertemporal agency has rendered four verdicts. First, a dieter who eats dessert while sincerely judging she ought not, feeling genuinely torn, is weak-willed. Second, a retiree who rapidly spends savings because she hyperbolically discounts her future self's needs, but feels no conflict and fully endorses each purchase, is not weak-willed—merely rationally time-biased. Third, an agent who takes a drug that eliminates future-oriented concern and then spends recklessly, with no competing judgment present, is not weak-willed either, since weakness requires a divided will rather than a unified but myopic one. Fourth, someone who installs a Ulysses contract—accepting present cost to bind a future self—displays diachronic agency, but this is the opposite of akrasia, not an instance of it.

Now consider this new case: a researcher knows that fabricating data now will trigger a painful ethical review later; she also knows her present self hyperbolically discounts that distant cost. At the moment of fabrication she explicitly judges "this is wrong, I must not do it," yet she experiences no emotional resistance—only a calm, recognized failure of her present executive will to align with her standing normative judgment. She proceeds.

Predict my friend's verdict—akrasia, mere hyperbolic discounting, or a distinct failure of diachronic authority—and lay out the competing rules that fit her four prior rulings (for example, a divided-judgment rule, an emotional-conflict rule, a preference-reversal rule, and a cross-temporal-authority rule). Say what each predicts here, give your best-guess prediction with reasons, identify the further case that would best distinguish the rules, and recommend the ruling that best coheres with her precedents, noting honestly what principle she would be adopting either way.
