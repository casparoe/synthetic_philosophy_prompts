I'm applying for a one-semester independent study grant in philosophy of physics and need a concise research proposal—roughly 2,000 words—on a reframed version of Einstein's hole argument. The standard presentation treats diffeomorphism invariance as a direct metaphysical lesson: if you smoothly drag the metric around inside a region where the stress-energy tensor vanishes, you generate a mathematically distinct but physically equivalent solution, which is taken to show that bare spacetime points lack the kind of identity needed to bear physical properties. The literature then splits between substantivalists, who either accept a limited indeterminism or try to restore determinism by enriching points with haecceities or primitive identities, and relationalists, who deny that points exist as independent entities altogether. I suspect both sides share an unexamined assumption: that the gravitational field must be represented as a point-valued assignment, so that if point-identity fails, determinism is genuinely threatened.

My proposal asks whether the hole argument's apparent failure of determinism is really an artifact of that representational choice rather than a deep ontological discovery. Specifically, I want to examine whether we can understand the metric not as a function g_ab that assigns curvature values to individual points, but as anchored to equivalence classes of local geometric properties—so that "dragging" the field does not produce a different physical state because the state was never indexed to bare points in the first place. This is not full relationalism, since the anchoring structures are still spacetime-internal and depend on local curvature invariants; nor is it standard substantivalism, since it denies that determinism requires point-identity. The precise question, then, is whether there exists a coherent representational framework for general relativity in which the hole argument's construction yields the same physical state by construction, without secretly reintroducing point-haecceities or requiring empirical modifications to Einstein's equations.

The proposal should explain why the existing positions leave this open: substantivalist responses typically treat the representation as fixed and debate what to add to points, while relationalist responses reject points but rarely ask whether the problem might dissolve at the level of formal representation rather than ontology. The approach would be purely conceptual, using only standard differential-geometric machinery—no new physics—to compare three representations (point-valued, fully relational, and equivalence-class anchored) and check whether determinism is restored in the third case without hidden structural commitments. A successful result would be a clear argument that at least one non-substantivalist representation blocks the hole argument's conclusion without collapsing into relationalism; failure would mean that every determinism-restoring representation either smuggles in point-identity or demands commitments beyond standard GR. Given the one-semester scope, the deliverable should be a structured conceptual analysis with formal illustrations but no claim to original mathematical theorems, and it should include an honest assessment of what would make the project too large—specifically, if anchoring equivalence classes requires a full reinterpretation of the initial-value problem in GR rather than a local representational shift.
