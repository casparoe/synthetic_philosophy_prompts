I'm an ethics compliance officer reviewing remote informed-consent recordings for decentralized clinical trials. Each session lasts about fifteen minutes, takes place over video call with family members sometimes present, and concerns protocols where the trial may be the patient's only access to an experimental therapy. I must decide, within ten minutes per case, whether consent is invalidated by deception, coercion, or incompetence. I need a reusable three-stage audit procedure—ordered checkpoints with explicit decision rules—that I can apply without philosophical training, but that preserves the conceptual distinctions that matter.

The procedure should treat deception as strategic framing rather than mere omission: specifically, flag when risk information is presented using only relative statistics or when benefits are described with certainty language that exceeds the protocol's actual confidence levels. For coercion, distinguish direct threats from structural pressure, with a specific node for cases where the trial is the sole available treatment and family members display visible anxiety during the call. For competence, use task-specific understanding rather than global mental status: require a structured teach-back covering purpose, risks, and right to withdraw, with modified rules when a family member serves as "communication support" (clarifying language) rather than proxy decision-maker.

Please construct this as a step-by-step flow in words, marking every point where judgment cannot be eliminated and explaining what error the shortcut risks. Then apply it to three cases: (1) a patient with mild cognitive impairment who completes the teach-back correctly while her adult child supplies technical vocabulary for her answers; (2) a patient shown only relative-risk figures for adverse events who demonstrates perfect recall of those figures but cannot convert them to absolute risk; (3) a terminal-illness patient for whom this trial is the only remaining option, with no explicit threat made but with his spouse repeatedly stating "we have to do this" on camera.

One of these should produce an answer that the procedure yields but that you expect I will find counterintuitive or uncomfortable to enforce. Finally, state clearly what this procedure cannot determine—such as hidden internal pressures, long-term stability of preference, or the ethical status of the trial design itself—and note where I must rely on clinical or legal consultation rather than this audit.
