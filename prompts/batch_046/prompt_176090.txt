I'm delivering the commencement address this spring at a university that, three years ago, formally acknowledged that its endowment and earliest buildings were substantially funded by profits extracted through forced labor in a colonial economy. Since then, the administration has established a restorative scholarship program for descendants of the affected communities, and campus debate has split sharply over the language of responsibility: some faculty and alumni argue that speaking of "our guilt" unfairly indicts present students and staff who committed no personal wrong, while others insist that refusing collective guilt is itself a moral evasion. The audience will include graduating students and their families, trustees, historians of institutional violence, and philosophers of collective responsibility who will notice any conceptual slippage.

Please write a five-to-seven-minute address that uses Karl Jaspers's taxonomy of guilt—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical—to do real structural work in the room. The speech must navigate Jaspers's core claim that moral and metaphysical guilt are strictly personal and non-transferable across generations, whereas political guilt attaches to membership in a collective and can be inherited as a burden of responsibility rather than as individual culpability. It should therefore reject both the false comfort of saying "no one here bears any relation to this harm" and the false accusation that individual graduates carry personal moral guilt for historical crimes they did not commit. To keep the framework from feeling like a rehearsed museum piece, introduce a specific tension that standard treatments often miss: the risk that metaphysical guilt, when transmitted intergenerationally, collapses into an aesthetic sentiment of sorrow that substitutes for concrete political obligation, or that institutional "acknowledgment" can become a ritual performance that absorbs guilt symbolically without redistributing actual power. The address must be fully intelligible to families without philosophical training, yet precise enough that experts recognize you are not blurring Jaspers's categories for rhetorical ease. Write it in American English, do not include a bibliography or suggested readings, and do not put anything in quotation marks that comes from an actual text unless you have verified the wording from a source.
