I'm preparing the opening argument for a graduate workshop on comparative historiography, and I've found myself stuck on what seems like a hidden contradiction in our efforts to move beyond Eurocentrism. The standard critique—that we wrongly apply categories like "feudalism" or "the Renaissance" to non-European pasts—is well rehearsed. But in my discussions with colleagues last week, I began to think the deeper problem is not categorical imposition but temporal imposition: specifically, the assumption that global history must be told from everywhere at once within a single, shared chronology. I want to call this "synchronic universalism," and I think it does a different kind of epistemic damage by forcing genealogical, cyclical, or relationally ordered traditions into a linear calendrical grid.

Please argue that constructing a genuinely non-Eurocentric global history requires abandoning this universal timeline—not to fall into relativism, but to make room for what we might call plural historical ontologies. You should directly address the objection that without a shared chronology we cannot meaningfully discuss cross-cultural causation or influence, and show why that objection secretly depends on the very linear model it claims to defend. Keep the tone suitable for an advanced seminar: precise, but accessible to historians who are not specialists in philosophy of time.
