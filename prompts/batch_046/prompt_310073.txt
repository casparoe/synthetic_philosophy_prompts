Write a dialogue between Maya and Jonah, two ethics grad students who both agree acting for the right reason is necessary for moral worth but cant agree on whether a "scaffolded" reason still counts. Theyre debating Eli, a surgeon who saved a patient during a blackout and says he acted out of respect for the patients dignity. The catch: Eli only formed that exact moral judgment after an intense "moral calibration" program in med school that literally reshaped his emotional responses — without it he'd be totally indifferent.

Maya argues Eli's reason is basically an implant. It exists only because a targeted intervention suppresses his natural self-interest, so his action lacks real moral worth even though he sincerely avows it. She floats a condition she calls upstream autonomy — for full worth, the reason has to be reachable through the agent's own reflective capacities without that specific scaffolding being causally necessary.

Jonah pushes back: every moral reason is shaped by upbringing, culture, brain development. If you demand causal purity then nobody gets praise. What matters is reflective transparency — Eli can fully endorse the reason, and if you counterfactually removed the training he'd still recognize it as correct once he learned about it. The reason isnt an implant, its a skill he aquired.

Maya replies that moral motives arent like arithmetic skills; if the compassion vanishes the moment the scaffolding is reversed, then it was never genuinely his. Jonah asks if shes smuggling in a hidden purity test, and Maya admits she kind of is, but claims its the right kind — not about origin, but about robustness: the reason must survive counterfactual debiasing.

Make both sides equally sharp, no strawmen, real back-and-forth where they actually answer each other rather than talk past one another. Keep the tone casual, like theyre arguing over coffee, and leave a typo or two in.
