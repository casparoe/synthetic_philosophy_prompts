Consider Aristotle's argument in Politics Book VI: democratic equality requires filling offices by lot, because election selects for superiority and therefore introduces an oligarchic principle; however, he allows election for roles that require specific expertise. Apply this distinction to a modern lottocratic proposal in which citizens are not sorted into a legislature but into small "epistemic panels"—bodies drawn by lot and charged with adjudicating contested empirical disputes (for instance, risk assessments underlying policy) after receiving expert testimony, yet retaining collective judgment. Does Aristotle's concession that technically demanding offices should be elected entail that these panels must also be elected, since their function is precisely expert-sensitive adjudication? Or can the lot be defended here on the ground that the panel's legitimacy comes not from individual knowledge but from the statistical representativeness of civic reasoning capacities—so that equality applies to the distribution of judgment rather than to the possession of expertise?
