I'm taking a graduate seminar on democratic theory and political epistemology, and I'm trying to work through John Dewey's later writings on democracy as a mode of social inquiry rather than as a formal governmental apparatus. In one especially dense passage, Dewey seems to argue—I'm paraphrasing here—that democratic politics ought to be understood as a form of collective experimental intelligence directed at shared social conditions. He suggests that individuals form a public precisely when they recognize that their actions produce indirect consequences extending well beyond their immediate intentions, and that this public must then organize itself not by applying fixed principles or by registering pre-existing preferences, but by treating proposed social arrangements as hypotheses whose validity is assessed according to their actual effects on the lives of those affected. He further implies that, just as in scientific inquiry, the experimenter and the subject of experiment cannot be cleanly separated in social life, since any political intervention reshapes the very community that designed it.

I'm having difficulty pinning down exactly how Dewey conceives the relationship between the emergence of the public and the experimental process itself. Specifically, I want to know whether he believes that recognizing shared indirect consequences is a preliminary cognitive achievement that must occur before democratic experimentation can begin—so that problem-definition logically precedes solution-testing—or whether he treats that very recognition as part of the ongoing experiment, meaning that the boundary between identifying a shared problem and testing responses to it is fluid and co-constitutive. I also wonder whether his analogy between social and scientific inquiry commits him to requiring something like controlled, reproducible conditions for legitimate democratic deliberation, which seems politically impossible, or whether he explicitly allows that social experiments are necessarily irreversible and that the community serves simultaneously as investigator, instrument, and object of study. Could you please help me understand what Dewey is actually claiming in this passage about the structure of democratic inquiry, particularly with respect to whether problem-definition stands outside or is internal to the experimental process?
