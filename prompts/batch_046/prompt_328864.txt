Read Ursula K. Le Guin's short story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas through the lens of T. M. Scanlon's contractualism, with particular attention to the aggregation problem. In the story, a city's prosperity depends on the perpetual suffering of a single child; nearly all citizens accept this arrangement, while a few reject it and leave. Rather than treating the narrative as a straightforward utilitarian calculus—many happy lives weighed against one miserable life—consider whether Scanlon's framework of principles that no affected party could reasonably reject offers a different diagnosis. Would the child have grounds to reject the principle that permits its confinement for the general welfare? Does contractualism's resistance to aggregating harms across persons illuminate why the citizens who stay are not merely making a quantitative trade-off but are endorsing a principle that fails the test of reasonable rejectability? At the same time, examine where the story resists this reading: the narrative seems to depend on the sheer scale of the beneficiaries, and the walkers-away appear moved by the magnitude of collective happiness rather than by the logic of justification to each individual. In preparing this for a philosophy seminar, I want a reading that takes both Le Guin's fable and Scanlon's non-aggregative moral logic seriously, asks whether the story dramatizes something contractualism gets right that act-utilitarianism misses, and honestly notes where the fictional setup forces contractualism into an uncomfortable silence about numbers.
