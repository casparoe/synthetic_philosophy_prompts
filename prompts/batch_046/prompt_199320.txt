ok so I'm trying to map out my view for a seminar on whether the agricultural revolution was actually a mistake, and I want to make sure these commitments don't secretly contradict each other before I write the paper.

I'm pretty bought into the Sahlins-type argument that forager life was relatively affluent—decent leisure, decent nutrition, low structural inequality—so for most people the shift to farming was a welfare loss, not progress. I also think evolutionary psychology gives us reason to treat prestige-seeking and coalition-building as deep, stable traits, which makes hierarchical organization a kind of natural attractor state whenever institutional conditions allow it to lock in. At the same time, I reject climate and population-pressure determinism; I think the transition was driven mainly by competitive feasting and big-man dynamics, where surplus was actively produced to build obligations and cement followings, not just to feed more people.

I think forager egalitarianism wasn't passive but an active reverse-dominance system—mocking hoarders, ostracizing anyone acting like a chief. And I think inequality specifically needs storable, inheritable surplus with excludable access; without that, farming alone doesn't institutionalize hierarchy. But I also think the storage revolution created exactly that kind of surplus—grain that can be held across seasons and passed down—so the leveling mechanisms collapsed once storage tech spread, not because people stopped trying to level but because the granary made ostracism ineffective. Here's where I feel like I might be pulling in two directions: if prestige psychology is deep and elites were actively driving surplus production for status, doesn't that make the shift kind of inevitable once storage existed? And if so, is calling it 'a mistake' the wrong framing—like, it was a collective mistake for the majority but basically a smart move for the elites? Plus, if reverse dominance worked for millenia, does that mean those prestige traits aren't actually that deep, or just that the leveling institutions were unusually robust?
