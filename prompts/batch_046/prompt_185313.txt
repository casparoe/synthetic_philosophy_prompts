Write an essay on the conflict between Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge and the principle of epistemic closure. Begin by outlining the tracking conditions—especially the counterfactual sensitivity requirement—and showing why they appear to fail for beliefs formed by valid deduction from known premises. Then weigh the standard resolutions: abandoning closure altogether, weakening the tracking conditions to accommodate entailment, or restricting closure to a narrower class of inferences. Next, introduce and critically examine a less familiar amendment: that tracking should govern not single propositions but sustained inferential pathways, so that a deductive conclusion is known if the entire reasoning chain, taken together, satisfies a global sensitivity condition—namely, that if the premises were false in nearby worlds, the reasoner would not have arrived at that conclusion via the same method—even though individual intermediate steps might not be sensitive on their own. Evaluate whether this pathway approach genuinely reconciles tracking with closure, or whether it collapses into a form of process reliabilism that sacrifices the distinctive anti-luck motivation behind Nozick's account. Reach a clear, defended conclusion about whether the two commitments can be jointly maintained, and specify which of the four strategies you regard as the least damaging revision to our ordinary epistemic practices.
