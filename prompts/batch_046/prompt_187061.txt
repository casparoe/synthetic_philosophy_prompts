I am preparing a fifty-minute session for first-year cognitive-science students on the problem of letterform essence—what makes every instance of the letter a an a across handwriting, printed fonts, and deliberate distortions—and on why feature-checklist accounts of recognition fail. To make the failure concrete, I have designed a hypothetical counter-style alphabet in which each letter retains all of its usual local parts—loops, stems, crossbars, ascenders—but rearranges their global spatial relations; for example, an a with its bowl on the left and its tail extending leftward, or an e whose crossbar sits at the bottom. Human readers usually identify these correctly, yet any algorithm that checks for local features breaks down. I want the session planned, not delivered: the exact order in which the pieces must be introduced so that the counter-style alphabet functions as a decisive problem rather than a curiosity; the one concrete example that should carry the entire explanation and why the standard Fregean analogy of Hesperus and Phosphorus is the wrong pedagogical tool here; the misconceptions the students will predictably form—I expect at least that style is merely noise around a canonical skeleton, and that recognition is just approximate feature-matching—and the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be headed off; a brief check I can use at the end to determine whether they have grasped that recognition is relational rather than checklist-based; and which part of the formal distinction between categorical recognition and analogical style-transfer should be left unnamed and unexplained at this level. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. I want the dependency structure of the explanation, the boundary conditions of the misconception map, and the rationale for what is omitted.
