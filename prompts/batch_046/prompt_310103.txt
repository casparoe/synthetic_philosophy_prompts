For decades, biologists and philosophers have argued over whether “species” denotes a real division in nature or merely a stable convention for sorting organisms. The biological species concept requires reproductive isolation, the phylogenetic concept requires monophyletic ancestry, and the evolutionary concept requires a shared evolutionary fate—yet every empirical advance, from well-documented hybrid zones to cryptic lineages revealed by environmental DNA, seems to furnish counterexamples for one framework and corroboration for another without ever producing convergence. My own working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not ultimately about organisms at all, but about whether a taxonomic category must play an explanatory role in evolutionary theory or only a predictive and communicative one; the opposing camps may therefore be answering different questions—one ontological, one epistemic—under the single heading “What is a species?” I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another survey of the concepts. Is the stalemate substantive, verbal, or driven by incompatible standards of evidence? Are there hidden empirical predictions being masked by ambiguous terms such as “lineage” and “isolation,” or are the sides anchored to bedrock intuitions about naturalness that no further data could dislodge? And given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—a decisive experiment, a formal criterion for choosing among concepts, a dissolution of the question itself, or something else?

The reason the philosophical stall feels urgent to me is practical: I am working on assigning species-level status to microbial communities detected only through metagenomic clustering, where reproductive isolation is unobservable in principle and monophyly depends on the phylogenetic model selected. In this setting, the classic concepts do not merely conflict; they become inoperable without arbitrary stipulation. Does this operational breakdown reveal a deep flaw in the concepts, or does it simply expose that taxonomy has always been an engineering discipline disguised as metaphysics? If the latter, then perhaps the debate has persisted because each side mistakes a tool-choice problem for a discovery problem. Please address this concretely: explain what each major position actually says about cases like model-dependent microbial clustering, identify which feature of the disagreement is doing the work, and tell me whether anything—conceptual, formal, or empirical—should be expected to move it, or whether the field should reconceive its aims entirely.
