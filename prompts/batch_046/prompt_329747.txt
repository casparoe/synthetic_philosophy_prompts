In debates about weakness of will and hyperbolic discounting, philosophers and behavioral economists often analogize the divided self to an intra-personal principal-agent relationship: a long-term "planner" (the principal) tries to contract with a sequence of present-biased "doers" (the agents) who share her identity but apply hyperbolic rather than exponential discounting. The analogy is used to license the view that akrasia is not primarily a failure of rational insight or virtue but a predictable contracting problem, and that remedies—Ulysses contracts, precommitment devices, environmental restructuring—are best understood as internal mechanism design rather than as direct cultivation of willpower.

Audit this analogy with precision. First, lay out the explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the principal, the agents, the contract terms, the hidden action, and the enforcement mechanism? Second, identify the disanalogies that bear specifically on the normative conclusion that commitment devices are the appropriate remedy. In particular, address the recursive problem: if the planner herself discounts hyperbolically, the analogy seems to generate an infinite regress of nested principals rather than a stable authority—does this collapse the principal-agent framing, or can it be repaired by indexing authority to a particular time-slice? Third, examine whether importing economic language smuggles in welfare-aggregation assumptions across temporal stages—treating intertemporal conflict as analogous to inter-personal bargaining—that a non-utilitarian or anti-reductionist view of personal identity might reject. Fourth, assess whether the analogy quietly imports the idea that the "contract" should maximize some integrated lifetime utility, and whether that presupposes a particular answer to the metaphysical question of what binds successive selves together. Finally, state clearly whether the "contracting failure" diagnosis of akrasia survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion by framing a normative conflict about the good life as if it were merely a technical problem of incentive alignment.
