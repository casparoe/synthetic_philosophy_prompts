I've been working through the Hart-Devlin debate on legal moralism for an advanced philosophy course, and I think I've built an argument that accidentally makes Devlin's position look unavoidable by pushing Hart's harm principle until it breaks. Here are the steps, numbered so you can track them precisely:

(1) Devlin argues that a society's shared morality is as vital to its survival as its legal and political institutions, which means private acts that corrupt that morality—such as acts that violate widely shared sexual or religious norms—constitute a genuine threat that criminal law may legitimately suppress.

(2) Hart responds that this confuses real harm with mere popular disgust, and that the law should only prohibit conduct causing direct, tangible injury to others, not conduct that simply offends the majority's moral sensibilities.

(3) If Hart's harm principle is applied consistently, it protects not only private consensual acts but also public advocacy for those acts, since speech by itself does not produce the direct physical or economic damage that Hart demands as a condition for legal coercion.

(4) But if a society permits both the practice and the open, organized promotion of acts that its members regard as deeply destructive to the moral bonds holding them together, that society may suffer a gradual erosion of cooperation and trust that is every bit as damaging as physical harm.

(5) Therefore Hart's principle either allows this erosion—undermining the very social stability it is meant to protect—or it must draw an arbitrary line between private behavior (fully protected) and public advocacy (potentially restricted), without any clear principle to justify the distinction.

(6) If that distinction is genuinely arbitrary, then Hart has not replaced moralism with neutrality; he has merely hidden the moral judgment inside the boundary he draws.

(7) So either we accept Devlin's claim that law may defend shared morality, or we accept a harm principle that is either self-defeating or secretly moralistic.

Which step is the weak one? I would prefer to keep Hart's intuition that the state should not police private morality, and I also want to avoid collapsing into Devlin's camp. But I need to know whether there is a principled way to restrict advocacy without abandoning the harm principle, or whether the flaw lies elsewhere—perhaps in my assumption that moral erosion counts as harm.
