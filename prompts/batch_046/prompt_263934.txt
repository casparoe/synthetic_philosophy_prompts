I'm enrolled in an upper-division seminar on ethics and artistic representation, and for the final project I am directing a short hybrid film about an intellectual disagreement between two deceased twentieth-century philosophers, both of whom left extensive audio archives. Rather than casting actors, I plan to extract vocal fragments from their actual recordings, run them through a voice-synthesis model, and use the reconstructed voices to deliver dialogue that I have composed entirely from sentences drawn from their published letters and lectures. The scene is counterfactual: it depicts the two sitting together in a seminar room in 1972, debating whether abstract objects exist independently of minds, despite the fact that they never met in life. The pedagogical justification is that audiences will hear the thinkers' own vocal cadences wrestling with the arguments; however, a fellow student contends that synthesizing speech this way violates a form of posthumous narrative integrity, because the voices are indistinguishable from authentic recordings yet the words are arranged into a confrontation that never occurred. I am trying to determine whether there is a principled distinction between dramatizing the dead through conventional acting and doing so through reconstructed vocal presence, and whether my obligation to these figures concerns factual fidelity, protection of their intellectual legacy, or a kind of dignity that persists after death regardless of harm. How should I evaluate whether the project is ethically permissible?
