I work as a software engineer on distributed ML pipelines and have no formal training in philosophy, but I have recently been trying to understand the literature on self-deception. The classical formulation strikes me as structurally analogous to a contradiction in a single-threaded system: if deception requires that the deceiver knows the true state and intentionally withholds it from the deceived, then applying this to one agent seems to require that the same subsystem both holds and lacks the same belief token. The standard responses—intentionalist accounts that split the self into quasi-independent subsystems, deflationary accounts that reduce the phenomenon to motivated reasoning or confirmation bias without any genuinely hidden knowledge, and split-self models that treat different cognitive modules as informationally isolated—have been debated for decades, yet in my reading the positions seem to recycle the same objections without convergence.

What I find puzzling is whether the impasse is genuinely about the nature of belief and agency, or whether it is largely an artifact of insisting on a unitary-agent framework that distributed cognition and modular architectures in cognitive science have largely superseded. In software, when a module operates on stale or filtered representations produced by an upstream process, we do not describe the system as deceiving itself; we describe an information-flow boundary or a consistency failure across components. Is the philosophical debate stalled because the two camps are actually answering different questions—one about the phenomenology of first-person denial, another about the functional architecture of belief maintenance—under the single label self-deception? Or is there a hidden substantive disagreement about whether intentionality must be globally available within a system for deception to occur? I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is: verbal, substantive about mental architecture, empirical in disguise, or grounded in bedrock intuitions about personal identity that neither side can argue for.

Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? Would it take a conceptual distinction that separates first-person phenomenological reports from third-person functional descriptions, an empirical result from cognitive neuroscience about modular information encapsulation, or is the question itself ill-posed because it imports a pre-scientific notion of a unified ego into a domain where distributed processing is the correct model? Please do not provide a bibliography or survey of interpretations; instead, engage the positions concretely—what intentionalists and deflationists actually commit to about belief access and agency—and tell me whether anything short of abandoning the unitary-self assumption could resolve the stall.
