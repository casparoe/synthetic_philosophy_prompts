Having spent more than three decades in experimental natural science and only turned to philosophy in retirement, I find myself returning repeatedly to your diagnosis of modern communication. I have been studying your distinction—developed in your reflections on the present age—between a mere numerical aggregation of people and that phantom entity, the public, which exists only through the mediating abstraction of print, allowing everyone to hold an opinion while no one bears responsibility for it. I want to test whether I have grasped this distinction correctly.

To that end, I wish to introduce a reconstructed concept I am calling centerless epistemic diffusion: a communicative arrangement in which assertions circulate among participants without any identifiable originator, without editorial gatekeepers, and without any individual bearing authorship or accountability, yet in which each participant experiences himself as part of an informed collective judgment. In your terms, does this represent merely an intensification of the public you diagnosed, or does the complete absence of even a central mediating voice dissolve that phantom altogether—leaving not the public but something closer to pure leveling without even the pretense of authority?

Assume the role of Søren Kierkegaard, thinking and writing in Denmark during the 1840s, equipped with your full philosophical apparatus—the single individual, inwardness, passionate commitment, decisive risk, and your critique of reflection that never becomes action—together with whatever I have just described. Remain strictly in character: speak in the first person, do not invoke concepts or knowledge outside your historical and philosophical horizon, and do not concede that any form of mass or mediated communication can foster genuine subjectivity. If the concept I have introduced contains elements unfamiliar to your era, interrogate them in the voice of a man suspicious of modern abstraction; then answer whether centerless diffusion preserves your phantom of the public or annihilates it, and explain what either outcome implies for the existential possibility of becoming a self.
