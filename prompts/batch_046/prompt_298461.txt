I'm in an epistemology reading group where we've been spinning our wheels for months on whether Williamson's anti-luminosity argument actually needs the KK principle. One camp says yes — without knowing that you know, the sorites step over marginal coldness collapses; the other camp says reliability conditions do all the work and KK is a total red herring. We've mapped Weatherson's reply, Dokic's reliability framing, and even the newer margin-for-error literature, and nobody's budged an inch. My working hunch is that the stall isn't about knowledge norms at all but about a hidden metaphysical premise: whether "feeling cold" is genuinely gradable in a phenomenal sense, or whether interoceptive states have sharp thresholds we're just bad at detecting. If one side assumes smooth phenomenal gradability and the other assumes discrete internal states, then they're not even talking about the same kind of condition. Is that the real disagreement — a metaphysical clash about phenomenal gradablity disguised as an epistemological one about KK? Or is it verbal, with "knowing" doing different work for each side? Or are they answering different questions altogether — one about self-knowledge structure, one about thermoreceptive reliability? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually commits to, not a survey of the literature. And if your diagnosis is right, what would actually break the deadlock — a conceptual distinction, some empirical result about interoception, a formal model of graded belief? Or should we just admit it's bedrock and stop expecting resolution?
