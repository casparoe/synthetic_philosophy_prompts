You are a professional philosopher of epistemology with experience designing diagnostic instruments for graduate seminars. I want you to construct a decision tree that functions as a “which position do you actually hold?” sorting instrument on the relationship between the KK principle and the luminosity of knowledge. The tree should be built for an audience of advanced philosophy students who understand basic epistemic logic but should not need to recognize theory names in advance to navigate it.

Please begin with brief brainstorming: list the logical distinctions that genuinely separate views on this issue—specifically, whether there exist genuinely borderline cases of knowing, whether the existence of such cases undermines the KK principle, whether luminosity fails globally or only in specific domains, and whether synthetic or algorithmically mediated evidence introduces a special exception. Then build the tree from those distinctions, not from a list of named positions. The tree must start from a concrete case commitment: present the Synthetic Indistinguishability Scenario (SIS). In this scenario, subject S examines a high-fidelity synthetic image that is algorithmically generated to be perceptually identical to a natural photograph. S correctly believes it is a real photograph, with 90% confidence, but has no access to provenance metadata and is unaware that a generative model produced it. The first node should ask whether S knows the image is real. From there, each branch should be a further diagnostic question—phrased as a claim about the case or a general principle that a student can accept or reject without knowing which theoretical label it corresponds to.

Your tree must include exactly the following five leaves, located in the logical space after the branching: (1) The Classical Cartesian Leaf—knowledge is fully luminous and KK holds universally; in SIS, S does not genuinely know because certainty is absent. (2) The Williamsonian Anti-Luminosity Leaf—knowledge exists in borderline cases but is not luminous; KK fails because second-order access is not required, following the margin-for-error reasoning. (3) The Contextualist Gradualist Leaf—luminosity and KK are context-sensitive; in low-stakes contexts S knows, in high-stakes or synthetic-mediated contexts she does not, without universal failure. (4) The Externalist Safety-Only Leaf—knowledge requires only that the belief be safely formed, regardless of second-order access; KK and luminosity are both rejected globally, including for synthetic evidence. (5) The Synthetic-Exception Leaf, a constructed position for this exercise—KK and luminosity hold for biological, direct perceptual knowledge but fail specifically when the evidential chain includes an opaque algorithmic layer; thus in SIS, S lacks knowledge not because of general anti-luminosity but because synthetic mediation breaks the transparency condition required for second-order knowledge. For each internal node, phrase the question so that it separates at least two of these leaves. After mapping the tree, provide a scoring key that translates answer paths to the five leaves above.

Crucially, you must also: note any combinations of answers that are logically possible but unoccupied by these five leaves, and briefly diagnose why (incoherence, unattractiveness, or unexplored status); explicitly flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate with the questions you have designed, explaining what further distinction would be needed; ensure that no node asks a student to identify a theory by name, so every node should be answerable by committing to a claim about the SIS case or about a general principle; and keep the tree to no more than five decision nodes, excluding the root case presentation. Please present the final deliverable as a structured taxonomy—root case, then the decision nodes with their branching answers, then the leaves with their labels, then the scoring key, then the diagnostics for unoccupied spaces and inseparable pairs. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Think carefully and work through the logic step by step before finalizing the tree.
