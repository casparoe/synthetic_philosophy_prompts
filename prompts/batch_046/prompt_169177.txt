I'm working through some problems in the philosophy of action for a graduate seminar, and I've become preoccupied with Buridan's classic paradox: the ass suspended between two equally appealing bales of hay, apparently unable to choose. Most standard treatments present Buridan's own resolution—an appeal to the will's sovereign capacity to break rational deadlock—as an early voluntarist intervention. But I'm trying to understand whether that resolution can survive contact with more recent formalisms that treat indifference as a modeling artifact rather than a metaphysical crisis.

Specifically, there is a somewhat niche intersection of computational decision theory and empirical psychology that frames the problem differently. In reinforcement learning, symmetric reward landscapes are often resolved through exogenous randomization—epsilon-greedy strategies that deliberately inject noise—while in social choice theory, tie-breaking is sometimes handled through endogenous lexicographic refinement, where a hidden asymmetry is revealed rather than invented. Meanwhile, behavioral studies suggest that human agents facing genuinely symmetric options frequently experience decision paralysis rather than exercising any volitional freedom to select arbitrarily. Given all this, I want you to predict how Jean Buridan himself might respond if he were alive today and thoroughly familiar with these modern frameworks.

Would he maintain that algorithmic randomization is not an exercise of freedom but its abandonment, insisting that the will must actively determine the rational appetite rather than delegate to noise? Or might he concede that what looks like volitional sovereignty is actually the discovery of a concealed preference ordering—perhaps a lexicographic rule buried beneath apparent symmetry? Please develop this prediction in detail, preserving his medieval commitments regarding rational appetite and volition, and address both the formal challenge from computational tie-breaking mechanisms and the empirical evidence that humans often freeze rather than choose when indifference is genuine.
