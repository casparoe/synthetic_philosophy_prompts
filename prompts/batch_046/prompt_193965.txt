Consider the problem of moral uncertainty: an agent assigns non-trivial credence to both a consequentialist framework and a deontological framework, and the two issue recommendations that are not merely different in degree but in kind—say, whether breaking a promise is permissible to avert a modest but certain harm. One proposed resolution is a two-layer model: at the first layer, the agent distributes credence across first-order moral theories; at the second layer, the agent assigns each theory an “authority weight” that determines how strongly that theory should govern action in cases of conflict, ostensibly without requiring direct comparison of the values or reasons internal to the competing frameworks. Explain the conceptual architecture of this two-layer model with precision. Then address the central tension: do the second-layer authority weights themselves presuppose a meta-ethical framework for comparing the normative force of competing theories? If they do, does the model genuinely dissolve the intertheoretic comparison problem, or does it merely relocate that problem to the meta-level? In particular, distinguish carefully whether these weights are best understood as empirical confidence measures (tracking the agent’s belief about which theory is correct) or as irreducibly normative weights (tracking how much each theory ought to govern action regardless of its likelihood of being true), and explain why that distinction determines whether the model collapses into a form of expected-value aggregation, into a maximin-style robustness rule, or into something structurally distinct from both.
