I'm a PhD candidate in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I'm preparing a 75-minute graduate seminar session for 10 to 14 students on the structure of first-person reference. I want a runnable in-class simulation—complete with assigned roles, restricted communication channels, physical tokens, and a collective payoff—that makes participants feel the force of the Hofstadterian idea that the symbol I is not an observed object in the world but a strange loop: a self-referential marker that refers back to the very cognitive system producing it, with no independent external anchor that could ground it from the outside. The structural problem should emerge from the rules and incentives themselves, not from any opening explanation I provide.

Please design the full activity. Specify the exact roles (with duplicates so every student participates), the interaction rules, the materials needed, and the payoff scheme—symbolic, involving no real money, but tied to collective success (for instance, a shared completion token or seminar participation credit). The core mechanic should be a hidden circular dependency: Perceptual Feeders can report only external stimuli via written cards; Memory Archives record prior messages; Narrative Synthesizers hold a physical token labeled I and can draft collective reports; Executive Controllers can approve a report only if it already contains the I-token, and they alone may summon silent Meta-Observers to apply validation stamps; but Meta-Observers are forbidden to speak or stamp unless directly addressed by an Executive Controller. Thus bottom-up attempts to build a chain of verification stall, and the group should experience the absence of any external entry point for self-reference. The resolution must come only when a Narrative Synthesizer arbitrarily places the I-token on the report without prior external validation, closing the loop so that Executives can then engage Meta-Observers and complete the form. Also include a debrief plan that maps each phase—linear search, deadlock, arbitrary insertion, loop closure—onto parts of the strange-loop structure; predicts what will predictably fail on the first run (Meta-Observers breaking silence out of frustration, dominant students trying to pass notes outside channels, premature abandonment); and explains how to correct these (strict channel enforcement, a brief system-reset protocol, explicit reminders that the deadlock is the point). Finally, be explicit about which feature of the idea the simulation structurally cannot reproduce—such as the evolutionary and neural emergence of the loop over developmental time, or the qualitative phenomenology of being an I—and that I as facilitator must supply that limitation verbally. Keep the prompt fully self-contained: no required readings, no citations, no bibliographies, and no dependence on niche formalisms that a small model might not reliably recall.
