John Locke famously distinguished between the identity of a "man"—the living human animal—and the identity of a "person," which he tied to sameness of consciousness extending backward through memory. Imagine a technology, unavailable in Locke's era, that gradually replaces every neuron in a human brain with a synthetic analog over several decades. The substitution is so slow—perhaps one ten-thousandth of the brain per month—that psychological continuity, including memory and self-awareness, is never interrupted; yet after fifty years, no biological neural tissue remains.

Given Locke's framework, how would he likely adjudicate the fate of the original person? Would he maintain that the same person survives in an entirely artificial substrate, since consciousness remains continuous? Or would he insist that the original "man" has perished, leaving only a numerically distinct person who inherits the consciousness? Furthermore, consider the challenge that, because the transition is continuous, there is never a discrete moment at which one can say the original person has died—only a gradual change in the underlying substance. Would Locke treat this continuity as proof that personal identity is genuinely substrate-independent, or would he argue that the absence of a sharp boundary undermines our ability to apply the concept of identity at all?

Please reconstruct Locke's likely reasoning from his account of personal identity, his distinction between substance and consciousness, and his treatment of continuity over time. In particular, explain whether his view commits him to accepting gradual substrate replacement as personal survival, or whether there is a tension in his system between the continuity of consciousness and the persistence of the original substance that this scenario exposes.
