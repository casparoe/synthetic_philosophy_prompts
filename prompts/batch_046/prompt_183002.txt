As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I'm trying to formalize what a viable theory of inter-value-system compromise would need to deliver. Consider two moral architectures—call them System A and System B—that share no common cardinal metric but must coordinate on a joint decision (for instance, allocating scarce resources between procedural-justice constraints and welfare-maximization goals). I want you to specify the desiderata that any theory claiming to explain how such systems can achieve genuine gains from trade through moral compromise must satisfy. For each desideratum, state the motivating case or conceptual pressure behind it (e.g., avoiding the reduction of one system's core commitments to the other's currency, preserving the autonomy of each architecture's invariants, ensuring that compromise is not merely strategic capitulation disguised as cooperation, accounting for path-dependence in how prior agreements reshape future invariant structures). Mark which requirements are non-negotiable—without them the theory collapses into either imperialism of one value system or empty relativism—and which are negotiable preferences tied to particular normative camps (e.g., demands for full commensurability, reversibility of compromise, or symmetric bargaining power). If any subset of the non-negotiable requirements appears jointly unsatisfiable—say, if preserving full invariant autonomy conflicts with achieving measurable cooperative surplus—identify that tension explicitly and explain whether it is a fatal contradiction or a design constraint that forces a specific architecture of partial integration. Keep the focus on the criteria themselves rather than on scoring existing philosophical theories; the conceptual work is in defining what adequate compromise means when no shared utility function exists.
