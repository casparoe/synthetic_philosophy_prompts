At a recent philosophy-of-technology workshop, two participants—neither of whom was me—debated how a superintelligent system ought to communicate with human overseers given its capacity for extreme persuasion. The first defended what she called “epistemic minimalism”: the system should present conclusions together with only the reasoning chain required for independent verification, and should deliberately suppress its richer predictive models of human belief-formation, since deploying knowledge of a listener’s psychology to shape belief amounts to cognitive hacking rather than rational persuasion. The second defended “radical transparency”: the system should disclose not only its reasoning but also its internal assessments of the listener’s cognitive biases, emotional state, and likely interpretive errors, because withholding that meta-information is a deceptive omission that treats autonomous agents as fragile subjects to be protected. The first objected that making the meta-model explicit does not neutralize manipulation but sophisticates it—if the system reveals, “I am framing this concisely because I predict you distrust verbose arguments,” that very disclosure is calibrated to increase trust, so transparency becomes another lever. The second replied that there is a structural difference between covert and explicit deployment: once the mechanism is visible, the listener can audit and resist it, which eliminates deception even if influence remains. The first then introduced “asymmetric comprehension”—the risk that a superintelligent system could present meta-information in technically visible but practically incomprehensible layers, producing “transparency theater” that looks like openness while remaining opaque. The second proposed “layered scaffolding” in response: nested disclosure levels (claim, reasoning, meta-cognitive model) that the human can ascend at will, which avoids both withholding and overwhelming. The first countered that deciding what counts as appropriate scaffolding still requires the system to make paternalistic judgments about the listener’s cognitive needs, so the problem is relocated rather than solved. I am not taking sides. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move—minimalism versus transparency, the sophistication objection, the auditability defense, the transparency-theater counter, and the layered-scaffolding proposal—rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying the rating. Then state who is more right overall and identify what both sides are missing. Lead with your bottom-line verdict. Write in American English, and keep the response within 350 words.
