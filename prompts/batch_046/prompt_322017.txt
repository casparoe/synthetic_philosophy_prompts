I've recently begun drafting a policy-oriented essay on automated welfare eligibility, and in doing so I've adopted a particular republican-liberty framing that I want to stress-test before committing to it. My position is this: domination should be understood as the presence of an arbitrary power of interference, regardless of whether that power is ever exercised, so that a citizen stands in a relation of subordination whenever an institutional agent possesses an unaccountable capacity to alter her legal or material status. Applying this to predictive risk-scoring—systems that continuously rank welfare recipients by fraud likelihood without automatically triggering sanctions—I hold that these algorithms constitute domination, because state agents retain an opaque, discretionary capacity to invoke sanctions based on the score, and the citizen lacks practical recourse to challenge her ranking or disable the system's authority over her. I further claim that conventional democratic safeguards—judicial authorization requirements, transparency reports, or elected oversight boards—do not eliminate this domination unless they include citizen-initiated mechanisms that can permanently suspend the algorithm's jurisdiction over the individual; mere procedural accountability, in my view, leaves the arbitrary capacity intact, merely adding a layer of intermediaries.

What would genuinely count against this view? I am not looking for the familiar negative-liberty objection that no actual interference has taken place; I want to know which conceptual distinctions within republican theory itself, which institutional designs, or which empirical developments should force me to revise or abandon the application. For example, if a risk-scoring system incorporated fully transparent criteria plus an individual audit right that could nullify the algorithm's applicability to that citizen, would republican theory classify that as the removal of arbitrary power, or does the collective authorship of the rules remain necessary for non-domination? More broadly, I want an honest assessment of whether my framing has become insulated: if every proposed safeguard is interpreted either as insufficient or as itself embedding domination, has the position been rendered unfalsifiable, and is that a defect for a normative framework intended to guide institutional reform? Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which ones committed republican theorists would concede as damaging, and specify whether there exists a live conceptual or empirical development that could overturn my claim that algorithmic risk-scoring is structurally dominating.
