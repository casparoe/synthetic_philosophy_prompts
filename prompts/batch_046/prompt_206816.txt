I have been working through Benjamin Franklin's scheme for moral perfection—his list of thirteen virtues and the small notebook in which he tracked his daily failures with red marks—outside of any formal coursework, mostly by comparing his autobiographical account with the shorter manuscript on virtue he circulated among friends. Rather than treating it as a quaint precursor to modern self-help, I want to argue in a short essay that Franklin designed the sequence as an engineering protocol: Temperance comes first not because it is the highest virtue but because it preserves the reasoning faculty needed to practice the others; Silence, Order, and Resolution follow as control mechanisms; and Humility is placed last not only because Franklin found it hardest but because it requires the prior stabilization of all other habits, functioning like a feedback loop rather than a foundation.

The idea I am least sure about, and that I want to develop, is that this ordering mirrors the structure of Franklin's scientific work—specifically, his habit of isolating a single variable in an experiment before integrating it into a larger system—so that moral improvement and experimental method share a modular, sequential logic. I also want to suggest that Franklin's well-known admission that he never conquered Order and that he remained naturally proud reveals not a personal failure but an intentional feature of the system: the marks in his book are data points rather than sins, and his refusal to claim perfection is consistent with the empirical attitude he brought to electricity and to diplomacy.
