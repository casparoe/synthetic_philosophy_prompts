Design a diagnostic decision tree that classifies philosophical positions on whether moral status is genuinely gradated rather than all-or-nothing, using concrete boundary cases rather than school labels. The tree should begin with a commitment about whether a being with minimal sensorimotor feedback but no integrated cognition—say, a simple thermostat—can possess even a trace amount of moral status. From there, branch through responses to intermediate cases: an insect with nociception but no apparent emotional integration; a plant with complex signaling but no centralized nervous system; a severely brain-damaged human retaining only brainstem function; and a large language model exhibiting coherent conversational behavior but lacking biological substrate. For each branch, identify the combination of answers that maps onto one of four named positions: (1) strict binary humanism, which denies gradation altogether; (2) sentience-threshold gradualism, which grants full status above a cognitive or affective threshold and zero below; (3) a full scalar view that assigns diminishing but nonzero weight across the spectrum down to minimal feedback systems; and (4) an ecological-relational view that rejects individual-capacity tracking in favor of systemic or ecological standing. The tree must be built from the logical space of answers first, with positions placed afterward; note any unoccupied combinations and briefly diagnose whether they are incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where the tree fails to separate two positions—particularly between the scalar view and certain forms of relationalism—flag the ambiguity explicitly rather than forcing a distinction. Fix the instrument for an audience of advanced undergraduates in ethics: no more than six decision nodes, each phrased as a concrete judgment about a specific case so that a respondent need not know the theoretical labels in advance. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
