I'm working out my intuitions about judgment aggregation across logically linked propositions—essentially applying the discursive dilemma to concrete institutional cases. Here are my verdicts.

A climate review board accepts P (temperature records are reliable) and Q (the warming trend exceeds natural variability), but rejects R (therefore current warming is anthropogenic), citing unmodeled solar forcing. I think the dissent on R should stand; forcing the conclusion via premise-based aggregation feels wrong.

A grading committee accepts S (the essay satisfies every rubric criterion) and T (satisfying all criteria yields an A), but rejects U (therefore the essay gets an A), citing discretionary authority. Here I think the opposite: the premises should bind them, so premise-based aggregation is correct and the dissent on U is incoherent.

A medical board accepts V (treatment X extends survival) and W (X causes severe side effects), but rejects Z (therefore X should be first-line). Conclusion-based aggregation feels right; the value judgment in Z isn't logically compelled.

But I'm genuinely torn on a statutory case: judges accept A (the ordinance bans vehicles in parks) and B (bicycles are vehicles), yet reject C (therefore bicycles are banned), citing legislative intent. This looks structurally like the climate case—an implicit exception—but institutionally like the grading case, where rules should constrain. Should I treat it like the first case or the second?

Is there a principle—perhaps one distinguishing purely logical entailments from those that depend on unstated contextual or normative premises—that captures my verdicts on the first three cases and resolves the fourth? Or must one of my first three verdicts be overturned for consistency? And what does the resulting principle predict for a committee that accepts "the contract was signed" and "signing creates obligation," but rejects "therefore the party is obligated," arguing that duress invalidated consent?
