For the past few months I have been working with a local emergency-planning group on triage protocols, and the moral arithmetic has begun to feel personally urgent rather than abstract. I have arrived at the following set of commitments, but I am unsure whether they form a coherent position.

First, I accept John Taurek's claim that in a straight choice between saving one stranger and saving five strangers, the fact that there are five does not by itself generate any stronger moral reason; the numbers do not count in that aggregative way. Second, I think this means that, absent other considerations, one should decide by lot—flip a coin—rather than automatically saving the greater number. Third, I hold that agent-relative reasons can justify favouring one's own child over any number of strangers, but I want to deny that this works by assigning the child greater weight; it is a special-relational permission, not a scaled-up value. Fourth, I think that public institutions, lacking agent-relative attachments, should apply the same pairwise logic and therefore ought also to randomise across lives rather than maximise survival. Fifth, I maintain a strict doing-versus-allowing distinction: actively harming one person to rescue five is impermissible, whereas failing to rescue five in order to avoid harming one is permissible.

Please audit these commitments. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict, and how severe is each tension? For each genuine conflict, indicate the cheapest revision that would restore consistency and what it would cost me philosophically. Finally, tell me which single change would clean up the most inconsistencies. Please write your answer in American English.
