Been reading about workfare and benefit conditionality and there's this move i keep seeing but i don't have a word for. A politician or pundit says strict job-search rules and sanctions are needed because fraud is rampant and taxpayers shouldn't fund laziness. You point out fraud rates are tiny and enforcing the rules costs more than they save. They don't drop the policy; they say okay, but it's still about reciprocity and keeping social norms alive. You show the norms are already strong and the conditions don't actually change anyone's behavior. They shift again: fine, maybe it's inefficient, but people need to feel it's fair, that nobody gets something for nothing. Each time the factual premise gets knocked out they swap in a different rationale, but the conclusion—harsh conditionality—never budges. It's not quite motte-and-bailey because these aren't a defensible core plus an extreme extension; they're separate arguments that just happen to share a slogan. Is there an established name for this kind of justification migration in welfare debates? If there is, how is it different from ordinary rationalization or value pluralism? And if there isn't, what should we call it, and where exactly are its edges—like, does it require that the rationales be mutually incompatible, or just that they take turns covering for each other?
