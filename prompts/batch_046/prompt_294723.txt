I've been debating with colleagues in a philosophy of technology reading group about whether autonomous systems genuinely generate responsibility gaps, and our discussions keep circling the familiar examples—autonomous vehicles and algorithmic sentencing—without resolving whether the gap is metaphysical or merely practical.

To move beyond this stalemate, I want you to write an essay addressing a more specific framing: consider a federated medical-triage network composed of dozens of independently maintained predictive modules. None is designed to deny care, yet their emergent interaction systematically excludes a particular demographic from emergency resources; no individual programmer, hospital administrator, or module possesses both the epistemic access to understand the exclusion mechanism and the causal authority to alter it. Weigh the arguments that this architecture produces a genuine, irreducible responsibility gap—drawing on the idea that moral accountability requires a unified locus of control and knowledge—against the responses that responsibility can be distributed across the network, relocated to institutional governance structures, or reconstructed as collective agency.

Your essay should reach and defend a clear verdict: either that such federated systems create an unavoidable gap demanding a new moral framework, or that the appearance of a gap dissolves once we correctly apply existing concepts of distributed and institutional responsibility. Do not conclude with an ambivalent summary; justify your position by showing precisely what the opposing view fails to capture about either the nature of agency or the demands of justice.
