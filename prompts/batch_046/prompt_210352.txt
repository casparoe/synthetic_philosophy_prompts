If Baruch Spinoza—who died decades before the modern historical-critical quest for Jesus emerged—were confronted with contemporary arguments that criteria of authenticity (dissimilarity, multiple attestation, and embarrassment) can finally settle the identity of the historical Jesus, how would he likely assess them? Reconstruct his position from his well-known hermeneutical commitments: the distinction between universal moral wisdom and particular historical narrative, the treatment of prophetic imagination as a psychological rather than metaphysical source of authority, and the claim that true religion is rational and universal rather than anchored in contingent biographical details. Would Spinoza regard these criteria as valid tools for stripping away later accretion and recovering a real person behind the texts, or would he see them as extending the very confusion between eternal message and narrative vehicle that he sought to dissolve? More specifically, consider whether his framework entails that asking who the real Jesus was is philosophically malformed—since the rational ethical core can be separated from the particular teacher—or whether historical recovery might instead serve a negative function: removing superstitious attachment to biography in order to reveal the universal teaching.
