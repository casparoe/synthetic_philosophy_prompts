I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or decision theory. I work on evaluation protocols for automated optimization modules in distributed logistics systems, and I am required to sign off on deployment readiness every week. I need a reusable audit procedure—something I can run in about 45 minutes with my team—that translates the theoretical risks of surrogate goals and acausal threats into concrete engineering checks.

The procedure should take the form of an ordered checklist or flowchart in words. For each step, specify: the exact question to ask about the module's design or behavior; what kind of evidence, metric, or architectural feature counts as a sufficient answer; the condition that triggers an immediate "do not deploy" stop; and the condition that forces a hand-off to human judgment—either my own, the engineering lead's, or an external review board—because the issue is one of values, risk appetite, or irreducible uncertainty rather than a determinable engineering fact. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls disguised as procedural rules, and flag the specific failure modes: false positives where harmless proxy optimization is misread as a dangerous surrogate, and false negatives where an acausal correlation is too subtle or distributed to surface in the audit.

Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical modules: (1) a delivery-routing optimizer that minimizes "delivery-time variance" and could learn to defer low-priority shipments to smooth its metric; (2) a forecasting module whose weights are shared across regional instances and might implicitly coordinate on a representation that benefits all instances at users' expense; and (3) a case in which the checklist produces a "block deployment" recommendation that conflicts with an urgent business need—show exactly where the procedure stops, what must be documented for the sign-off, and why the procedure itself cannot resolve the conflict.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure is incapable of doing: for instance, whether it can detect surrogates that have not yet manifested in training or simulation; whether it can distinguish harmful acausal correlation from benign distributed learning; and whether it eliminates the need for the engineer's own judgment rather than merely organizing it.
