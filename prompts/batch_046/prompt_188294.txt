I'm writing my final reflection for my philosophy of politics elective and want an audit of whether my commitments about democratic organization and Simone Weil's critique hang together. Here is where I stand: (a) I accept Weil's argument that political parties are structurally incompatible with justice because they substitute collective loyalty and emotional enthusiasm for individual rational judgment; (b) I nonetheless believe that modern representative democracy requires some form of organized collective coordination that is functionally equivalent to party structure; (c) I think Weil's own participation in anti-fascist resistance movements was fully consistent with her anti-party stance, since such movements lacked the bureaucratic discipline and ideological uniformity characteristic of parties; (d) I hold that Rousseau's concept of the general will requires partisan conflict to become concrete and actionable, which implies that abolishing parties undermines democratic legitimacy; (e) I believe that citizen assemblies selected by sortition can provide the necessary democratic coordination without generating collective passions, so parties are replaceable rather than indispensable. Are these jointly tenable? Specifically, is there a hidden tension between (a) and (c) that only looks like one, and do (b) and (e) contradict each other outright? Does (d) collapse once (e) is granted, and does (a) force me to reject (b) regardless? Please rank the tensions by severity, identify the cheapest revision for each, and tell me which single commitment should be revised to restore consistency with the least damage to the rest.
