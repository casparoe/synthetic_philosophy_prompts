I hold the view that W. E. B. Du Bois's claim that artistic production is necessarily propagandistic — especially as developed in his well-known 1926 essay on Negro art — should be interpreted not as aesthetic reductionism but as an epistemic thesis: that art created under racial domination functions as an irreplaceable mode of testimony, transmitting experiential knowledge that discursive or empirical methods cannot make available to outsiders, and that the aesthetic form is not merely a vehicle but a necessary condition for the credibility of that transmission. I want an honest map of what would count against this reading. Which features of Du Bois's broader philosophy — his theory of double consciousness, his later economic determinism, or his evolving positions on integration and Pan-Africanism — does it fail to accommodate? Are there works or practices from the Harlem Renaissance that resist being read as epistemic testimony? Does the interpretation risk unfalsifiability by treating every aesthetic failure as merely failed propaganda, or by redefining propaganda so broadly that no artistic practice could escape it? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a committed defender of this interpretation should themselves concede as damaging, and assess whether the view has been insulated by qualifications to the point that it risks nothing empirically or interpretively — and if so, whether that is a problem for an interpretation that aims to capture Du Bois's actual commitments rather than to serve as an independent philosophical claim.
