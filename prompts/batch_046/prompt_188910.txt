You serve as the ethics procedure officer for a university-affiliated hospital that reviews about fifteen research protocols per month involving vulnerable adults—psychiatric inpatients, people with mild cognitive impairment, and others whose decision-making capacity is context-dependent rather than globally absent. Your board has adopted a philosophical framework that treats valid consent not as a single signature event but as a three-layered authorization: an epistemic layer (the subject grasps what the study involves, including any non-therapeutic purpose), a volitional layer (the choice is free from controlling influence), and an executive layer (the subject can integrate the decision with their own values and life plan). Crucially, under this framework the three layers are evaluated independently: a deficit in one layer cannot be compensated by strength in the others, except through a narrowly defined supported decision-making pathway that itself must pass an independent coercion screen.

Your task is to turn this framework into a reusable, thirty-minute decision procedure—an ordered checklist with decision nodes, stop conditions, and hand-off rules—that you and a non-philosopher colleague could apply to a protocol document and consent form. The procedure must cover deception (omissions, framing effects, therapeutic misconception), coercion (institutional pressure, indirect rewards, family influence, the special vulnerability of inpatient status), and competence (task-specific understanding and value integration, not binary global capacity).

For each layer, specify the concrete questions to ask in sequence; what counts as a sufficient answer versus an insufficient one; the exact point where the procedure yields a clear invalid—do not enroll, valid—proceed with standard safeguards, or flag for full board review; and the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be documented. Include explicit red-flag tests you can apply directly to protocol language. Then apply the procedure to three protocols:

(a) A six-week placebo-controlled trial for chronic neuropathic pain in psychiatric inpatients, offering $600 compensation, with consent forms describing the study as an evaluation of a new pain management approach without mentioning the placebo arm or the non-therapeutic primary endpoint.

(b) A vaccination study for elderly residents with mild cognitive impairment, using a family-member-supported consent process in which the resident can assent or dissent, but family members receive a $100 gift card for each completed study visit.

(c) A low-risk observational study of sleep patterns in the same elderly population, with no compensation, no deception, and a brief capacity screen showing adequate task-specific understanding, but where the ward’s clinical culture is strongly pro-research and nursing staff routinely encourage enrollment.

In one of these cases your procedure should produce an answer the board is likely to resist—state that result clearly, explain why the framework forces it despite institutional pressure, and identify exactly what the procedure cannot do, such as resolving value conflicts about whether the research itself is morally permissible. Flag all hard boundary cases the procedure handles, mark every point where it must hand off to human judgment rather than algorithmic decision, and state its explicit limits, particularly regarding novel forms of influence that fall outside standard deception or coercion categories.
