In a working group on atrocity law, a colleague repeatedly deploys the phrase "genocide is the crime of crimes" to block two moves: comparing genocide's moral gravity with other mass atrocities, and arguing that legal classification alone creates a distinctive duty of intervention. I want to bring a precise disambiguation to our next session. Lay out the distinct theses that slogan could express—about lexical hierarchy in international law, about the irreducibility of group-targeted destruction, about rhetorical escalation, about the special wrongness of intent to destroy a protected group, and about the normative consequences of legal labeling. For each, indicate whether it is a near-truism about the Convention's drafting history, a substantive and defensible moral claim, or false/unsupported—particularly the inference from "crime of crimes" to an automatic trigger for military action. Identify which reading, if any, actually supports both of her argumentative uses without begging the question, and flag any reading that sounds profound but collapses into a tautology about how "genocide" is defined.
