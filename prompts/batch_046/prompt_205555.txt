Construct a decision-tree taxonomy for classifying ethical commitments within suffering-focused moral philosophy, with particular attention to what I will call the cross-ontological weighting problem: how an agent should compare, aggregate, or refuse to aggregate suffering across biological humans, non-human animals, potential artificial sentiences, and merely hypothetical future beings. The tree must begin with a diagnostic question about the asymmetry thesis—whether preventing suffering carries lexical or at least strict priority over promoting happiness—framed as a concrete choice between eliminating a single instance of extreme torture versus creating a billion lives of mild contentment. From that root, branch first on aggregation: does the agent permit interpersonal summation of suffering, or do deontic side-constraints or threshold effects block it? Second, branch on ontological scope: does the agent extend full moral weight only to entities with biological phenomenal consciousness, or also to functional analogues that lack subjective experience but exhibit suffering-correlated behaviors (the shadow-suffering boundary)? Third, branch on policy orientation toward high-risk populations: does the agent endorse non-creation or even active extinction for groups whose expected suffering exceeds expected well-being, and if so, does this apply only to wild animals, to potential artificial populations, or to both? Each terminal leaf should be labeled with the corresponding position in logical space—such as classical negative utilitarianism, suffering-focused prioritarianism with a lexical suffering-weight, abolitionist antinatalism extended to wild-animal and synthetic domains, shadow-sensitive functionalist ethics, and the null-comparison view that denies cross-ontological aggregation is coherent—while explicitly marking any empty combinations and diagnosing them as incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored. Every internal node should present a scenario answerable without knowledge of philosophical labels, so the tree sorts respondents by their intuitive commitments rather than by vocabulary. Limit the tree to at most seven decision nodes, and flag any pairs of positions that share identical answers across all nodes and therefore cannot be separated by this instrument.
