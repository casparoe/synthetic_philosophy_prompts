I'm writing a comparative essay for a jurisprudence seminar that focuses on the precise logical boundary between Hart's legal positivism and Fuller's natural law theory, specifically around the claim that law must contain some minimal moral content to be law at all. Rather than asking for a broad overview, I want you to compare two specific conceptual mechanisms in detail: Hart's argument that any functioning legal system must contain a "minimum content of natural law" rooted in basic truisms about human vulnerability, approximate equality, limited altruism, scarce resources, and limited willpower; and Fuller's claim that law possesses an "inner morality" expressed through procedural canons—generality, publicity, non-retroactivity, clarity, consistency, feasibility of compliance, stability over time, and congruence between official action and declared rule. The comparison should not merely list differences but should examine whether Fuller's procedural principles are genuinely non-moral in the sense Hart might accept, or whether they already smuggle in substantive moral commitments that Hart's separation thesis is meant to exclude.

To make this concrete, consider a hypothetical legal system—call it the Code of Meridian—whose rules are fully general, public, non-retroactive, clear, internally consistent, consistently applied by officials, and formulated so that compliance is physically possible, yet whose substantive content mandates the systematic deprivation of a particular group's property and liberty based solely on arbitrary characteristics, with no procedural avenue for those affected to alter their status. Applying both frameworks rigorously, would Hart be forced to concede that this system fails as law because it violates the minimum content—perhaps because such systematic arbitrariness undermines the very social survival and predictability that make law necessary—or could he maintain that it remains valid law, albeit morally odious? Conversely, would Fuller have to say that the Code fails as law because its procedural perfection is undermined by substantive evil—say, because the arbitrariness of classification violates the principle of congruence or feasibility of compliance in a deeper, moralized sense—or could he maintain that the inner morality is fully satisfied and the system is therefore law, just very bad law? In short, determine whether there is any logical space between Hart's minimal moral conditions and Fuller's procedural morality, or whether the two accounts collapse into one another once we examine edge cases where procedural perfection accompanies substantive atrocity, and identify which premise—Hart's separation of law and morals, or Fuller's identification of procedural morality with legality itself—bears the greater cost if we reject it.
