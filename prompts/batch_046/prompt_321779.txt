I serve on my university’s faculty appeals panel, and a case that arrived last month has made the philosophy of moral luck unexpectedly personal: I must help decide whether a colleague’s career should bear the full weight of an outcome she could not have foreseen. The case concerns Professor Elena Voss, whose lab published a high-profile study in 2022. In 2024 an audit revealed that the dataset had been silently corrupted by a third-party cloud-sync error that inserted duplicate records with altered timestamps; none of Voss’s protocols required manual verification against raw instrument logs, because automated pipeline integrity was the field’s standard practice and identical omissions existed in neighboring labs, including my own. The initial disciplinary committee recommended a one-year suspension without pay, framing her failure to catch the error as reckless disregard for data integrity, even though the corruption was invisible to ordinary review. Voss’s appeal argues that sanctioning her for the published falsehood—rather than for any culpable negligence—exemplifies resultant and circumstantial moral luck: she is being punished for an outcome partly outside her control, shaped by technical circumstances she did not choose. The panel must issue a formal recommendation memo to the provost—uphold the suspension, overturn it, or replace it with a specific lesser sanction—supported by reasoning that both the administration and Voss can actually respond to. In drafting this memo, please treat the control principle seriously, holding that institutional sanctions should track what the agent genuinely controlled, while also giving honest and substantial weight to the opposing institutional argument: that public trust and deterrence may require holding researchers accountable for published outcomes regardless of hidden technical failures, and that separating evaluation of character from evaluation of results may collapse in practice when the result itself is a false scientific claim. Do not supply bibliographies or reading lists; work directly from the conceptual distinctions. Specifically, address whether the counterfactual robustness of the sanction matters—would we still impose it if the sync error had not occurred but Voss’s verification practices were identical?—and whether an institution can coherently assess moral responsibility for a result without implicitly committing to a form of institutional moral luck of its own. Include an explicit acknowledgment of the strongest consideration on the losing side of your recommendation. End the memo with a clear bottom line.
