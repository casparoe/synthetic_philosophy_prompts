You are a professional philosopher of action and decision theory who regularly writes opening addresses for interdisciplinary workshops. Before drafting anything, take a careful moment to think step by step: first, outline the classic absent-minded driver problem and the specific twist described below; second, identify exactly how that twist reshapes the standard causal-versus-evidential framing; third, map that reshaping onto the workshop's mixed audience so that the philosophical idea does real structural work rather than serving as decoration.

The occasion and audience: You are delivering the opening remarks at a two-day workshop titled "Rational Planning Under Self-Ignorance." The room is split evenly. One half consists of decision theorists, philosophers of action, and logicians who know the absent-minded driver problem intimately. The other half consists of cognitive scientists, behavioral economists, and policy designers who have never encountered it and do not share the philosophers' vocabulary. The remarks should run about seven to eight minutes. They must be polished and formal, but a non-philosopher sitting in the back should be able to follow the stakes.

The philosophical idea that must shape the speech: The absent-minded driver problem. Present it in its familiar form—a driver wants to reach a destination with the best route, knows she is absent-minded, knows she cannot reliably distinguish the first intersection from the second, and must decide whether to take the first exit (safe, moderate payoff) or continue toward the second (better payoff but risk of missing both). However, you must inject and develop a specific, somewhat niche extension—call it the interpretive precommitment failure. In this variant, the driver anticipates her confusion and installs a dashboard reminder designed to activate only at the second intersection. The twist is that her absent-mindedness is not merely locational but hermeneutic: when she sees the reminder, she may misattribute it to the first intersection and treat it as confirmation that she has already reached her target, thereby turning a precommitment device into an instrument of self-deception. Use this twist to argue that the real puzzle is not just which decision theory (causal or evidential) gives the right prescription, but how an agent can design an informational environment that remains stable under her own predicted misinterpretation.

Your address must satisfy the following structural constraints:

1. Explain the basic problem without jargon, using concrete imagery, so that the non-specialists grasp why it is genuinely puzzling and not just a contrived paradox.
2. Introduce the interpretive precommitment failure as a genuine conceptual extension, not a gimmick. Show precisely how it complicates the usual framing: it suggests that even a correct ex-ante plan can be undermined not by weakness of will but by the planner's own anticipated confusion about the meaning of her cues.
3. Make the twist do real work for the occasion. Because this is an opening workshop address, you cannot resolve the debate; you must set it up as an open problem that justifies why philosophers and empirical scientists are in the same room. The twist should make it obvious why philosophical analysis of planning failure and empirical research on reminder systems, cue design, or cognitive load belong together.
4. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or appeals to "the literature"—assume the audience has no access to texts and the speaker has no web access.
5. Do not invoke super-niche named formalisms, unpublished technical frameworks, or obscure theorems that a well-trained model might not reliably recall. You may refer to standard distinctions such as causal decision theory and evidential decision theory, but present them conceptually rather than as named doctrines to be looked up.
6. Maintain a formal, polished register appropriate to an academic opening address, yet keep the language accessible enough that the emotional and intellectual stakes are clear to everyone in the room.
