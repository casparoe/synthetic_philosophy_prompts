I'm working through some questions at the intersection of philosophy of mind and fundamental physics with two colleagues, and we have reached an impasse I cannot resolve on my own. One of them, Maya, has become convinced that if panpsychism is correct—if experience is a fundamental feature of physical reality—then the moral landscape is vastly larger and more troubling than standard ethics assumes. She argues that fundamental entities, whether field configurations, spacetime points, or quantum states, might possess some minimal form of sentience, and that the sheer cardinality of such entities within observable space means that even an infinitesimal negative valence per entity could aggregate into suffering that dwarfs all human and animal pain combined. From this she infers that large-scale physical interventions—particularly those that rapidly alter quantum coherence across enormous numbers of particles, such as high-energy collider experiments or large-scale quantum computing architectures—might be generating hidden reservoirs of primitive distress that we have entirely overlooked.

The other colleague, Leo, rejects this inference on several grounds. He contends that suffering is not bare sentience but requires functional integration, a unified perspective, and at least some minimal self-model or global workspace; without these, fundamental entities may have experience in some attenuated sense, but they cannot suffer in any ethically relevant way. Maya replies that this is precisely the kind of biological chauvinism panpsychism is meant to overcome—if valence is fundamental, it need not depend on neural integration, and the absence of brains does not entail the absence of pain. Leo then presses her on the mapping problem: even if fundamental valence exists, she has offered no principled criterion for which physical configurations correspond to negative rather than neutral or positive states. At this point Maya introduces a specific hypothesis she labels perturbation-distress mapping: she proposes that rapid decoherence or entanglement-breaking in quantum systems corresponds to a primitive form of distress, analogous to a sudden disruption in a minimal cognitive architecture, and therefore that experiments inducing widespread decoherence carry a hidden ethical cost researchers have not acknowledged. Leo counters that this mapping is entirely ad hoc, unfalsifiable, and indistinguishable from the claim that all physical change is suffering; he further notes that if cosmic processes such as supernovae and black-hole mergers constantly produce far more decoherence than human technology ever could, then human actions are ethically negligible regardless, undermining her urgency.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each central move—Maya's scale argument, her rejection of integration requirements, her perturbation-distress mapping, Leo's integration criterion, his mapping objection, and his cosmic-scale rebuttal—assess whether the argument is clearly mistaken, partially sound but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then say which party is more right overall, and identify what crucial consideration both seem to be missing, especially regarding whether panpsychism combined with fundamental valence actually generates actionable ethical obligations distinct from standard environmental or technological risk assessment.
