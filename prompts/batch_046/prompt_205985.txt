There's this advisory panel my friend serves on that decides whether an atrocity gets the genocide label in legal briefs and museum copy. Thier track record is all over the map but not random:

- Herero and Namaqua, yes, because of explicit extermination orders against a defined people.
- Holodomor, no, they said the target was class with an ethnic correlation, not a group as such.
- Yazidi killings and sexual slavery by ISIS, yes, even though the body count was smaller than events they passed on.
- Cultural Revolution purges of specific clan and religious networks in parts of China, no, they called it politicide with cultural damage, not genocide.
- Forced assimilation and family separation in Indigenous residential schools, yes but only as cultural genocide in a footnote with different moral weight, not the full Convention category.

Now the new case: a non-state militia that shares the broad ethnicity and religion with a small dialect community is burning thier villages, banning the language, and moving children into dominant-group families, but avoids killing and calls it integration. The minority isn't a neat Convention box—it's a dialect cluster inside a larger nation. The panel has to recommend whether this counts.

What will they do? I'm less interested in the yes/no than in the rules that could fit thier past calls—maybe one about physical destruction being necessary, one about intent to erase group existence regardless of method, and one about whether the victim has to be a politically recognized minority. Which rule best predicts this call, and what's the closest analog from thier record? If they say yes, are they stretching Lemkin's original idea past breaking, or just being consistent? And what case—say, mass killing of that same dialect cluster by an outside army—would actually separate these rules?
