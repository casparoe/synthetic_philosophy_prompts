After a long and inconclusive argument with a friend about whether any principled line separates acceptable from unacceptable deployments of racial epithets, I have tried to stabilize my own intuitions by testing them against concrete cases. Here are six verdicts that feel firm to me, though one pairing still troubles me. First, a historian writing out the full epithet in a direct quotation from a nineteenth-century legal document, within an academic article that frames the usage historically: permissible, indeed required for fidelity. Second, a member of the targeted group using the same word in a critical stand-up routine that ridicules racists: permissible and arguably valuable. Third, a person outside the group quoting that routine verbatim in a media interview: impermissible, feeling more like appropriation than citation. Fourth, an automated transcription system rendering the word exactly as spoken in recorded hate-crime testimony, flagged but unredacted: impermissible in a way that the historian’s quotation is not, as if the absence of human intent or institutional purpose does not excuse the exposure. Fifth, a clinician asking a trauma patient to verbalize the precise epithet they heard during an assault, within structured exposure therapy: permissible, because the clinical frame transforms the utterance. Sixth, a parent answering a child’s direct question about the word’s meaning by pronouncing it aloud with a definition: impermissible, despite the plainly educational purpose.

The tension I keep returning to is between the historian and the parent: both are meta-linguistic, informational mentions lacking any hostile intent, yet I want to license the first and forbid the second. Meanwhile, the automated transcription case resembles the historian’s in being a non-hostile, verbatim rendering, yet it aligns with the parent’s in lacking a specifically human justification for the exposure.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps appealing to speaker authority, audience design, institutional role, or the distinction between token mention and token use—that accommodates all six verdicts? If so, what does it predict for a case I have not listed: a bilingual speaker using a word that is phonologically identical to the epithet but etymologically unrelated and semantically innocent in another language, spoken in a mixed-language setting where some listeners understand only English? And if no single principle can save every verdict, which one should I overturn—the historian’s license, the parent’s prohibition, or the judgment about the machine—and what is the simplest repair that captures the rest?
