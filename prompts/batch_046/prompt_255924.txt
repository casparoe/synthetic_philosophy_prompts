Construct a thought experiment—no longer than one paragraph and in realistic rather than science-fictional terms—that isolates the normative source of promissory obligation. The scenario should centre on a temporary, self-contained “micro-practice” of promising: an artificially constituted cooperative scheme (for instance, a closed workshop or a ritualised task-sharing agreement among strangers) that operates with explicit internal norms but is deliberately isolated from the wider social, moral, or legal institutions that ordinarily sustain promises.

Engineer the case so that the following three accounts deliver precisely these verdicts:
- An expectation-based account concludes that the promise is fully binding, because legitimate expectations have been created within the micro-practice and their frustration would wrong the promisee, regardless of any external embedding.
- A practice-based account (which holds that promises bind only when they invoke a recognised, stable social practice with broader cooperative value) concludes that the promise is not binding, because the micro-practice is too ephemeral and isolated to constitute such a practice.
- A reliance-based account concludes that the promise generates at most a weak or derivative obligation, because the promisee has not formed any detrimental reliance outside the micro-context, and the internal, contained reliance is insufficient to ground a full obligation.

Constraints: there must be no legal contract or formal enforcement; the promisee must genuinely form an expectation without that expectation being backed by wider social reliance; the harms or payoffs involved must be ones an ordinary person could face; and the isolation of the micro-practice must fall out of the scenario’s structure rather than being stipulated as a bare assumption.

After presenting the scenario, provide:
1. The reasoning each account gives for its verdict.
2. The identification of the specific structural feature that causes the practice-based account to diverge from the other two.
3. An analysis of what happens if that feature is removed—specifically, whether the case collapses back into a standard promissory-obligation scenario in which all three accounts agree.
4. The strongest objection a defender of the practice-based account could raise, including how they might argue that the micro-practice either implicitly invokes a broader practice or that the scenario cheats by stipulating isolation.

Write the entire response in British English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific texts.
