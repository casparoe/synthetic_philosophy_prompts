Below are the working notes for an essay I am preparing for an advanced philosophy discussion group. I am a high school student, and I am trying to extend the “wrong kind of reasons” problem—normally discussed in connection with individual beliefs and affective attitudes like admiration—into the domain of institutional forgiveness. Specifically, I want to examine a case in which a university administration considers granting formal forgiveness to a prominent alumni donor who was recently implicated in a serious ethical breach. The administration’s deliberation focuses heavily on the donor’s past capital contributions to campus infrastructure. My central claim is that citing those contributions is not merely an outweighed or weak moral reason for leniency, but a reason of the wrong kind altogether: it treats institutional forgiveness as a transactional reward for past utility rather than as a response to genuine moral repair. I then consider a potential reply: suppose the donor has in fact offered a sincere and appropriate apology. If that apology gives the administration a reason of the right kind, does its presence in the background “cleanse” the decision, or does the wrong-kind reason (the donations) still corrupt the institutional attitude? I also wonder whether this framing collapses if institutions, unlike individuals, lack the kind of mental states required for the wrong-kind-reasons distinction to apply at all. I would welcome comments on the conceptual accuracy of this extension, on whether I have drawn the line between wrong-kind reasons and merely weak reasons sharply enough, and on whether the objection about institutional mentality reveals an interesting tension I have not yet addressed. Please do not offer a bibliography or reading list; I am interested only in conceptual feedback on the argument as it stands.
