I have been trying to work out whether Nietzsche’s scattered remarks on diet, climate, and daily regimen can be reconstructed into something stronger than autobiographical eccentricity — specifically, into a normative “physiology of thought” which holds that particular gastric and environmental conditions are not merely instrumental to bodily health but constitutive of the kind of conceptual clarity he calls “great health”. On this reading, his insistence on light meals, avoidance of alcohol, and seasonal migration is not just a sick man’s coping strategy but a philosophical claim: that the quality of one’s digestive economy causally determines the range and rigour of one’s cognitive perspective, and that therefore diet belongs to epistemology rather than to medicine alone. The view is niche enough that it is rarely taken seriously outside specialised commentary, yet it draws on well-known passages in which he treats his own stomach as a kind of laboratory for philosophical temperament.

If one accepts this reconstructed position, what would genuinely count against it? I am not asking for the standard biographical reduction — that he was chronically ill and therefore obsessed with food — since a proponent could concede his illness while maintaining that he universalised a local truth. Rather, I want to know which observations, conceptual tensions, or results in adjacent domains would be decisive or merely awkward. Would the existence of demonstrably brilliant philosophers with notoriously poor diets be fatal, or could the view accommodate them as exceptions that prove the rule about optimal conditions? Does Nietzsche’s broader commitment to perspectivism and pluralism sit uneasily with the idea that there is a single physiological optimum for thinking, and if so, is that tension resolvable or destructive? Could modern nutritional science undermine the specific mechanisms he implies — for instance, if the gut-brain axis operates in ways that make his particular prescriptions arbitrary — without touching the deeper philosophical claim? I also want an honest assessment of whether the position risks being insulated into unfalsifiability: if every counterexample is explained away by saying the thinker in question lacked “great health” in the relevant sense, has the view become tautological, and is that a problem for a thesis that presents itself as normative rather than merely descriptive?

Please rank the potential defeaters from those that should move a committed defender to those that can be comfortably dismissed, and indicate which ones the defender ought to concede as genuinely damaging. I would also like to know what the view predicts that its rivals do not — for example, about the relationship between environmental change and conceptual innovation — so that I can see whether it risks anything empirically or conceptually specific, or whether it has been qualified into safety.
