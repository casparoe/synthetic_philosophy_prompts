I have been working through the assigned material for a course on comparative moral psychology, where we keep returning to the familiar contrast between German folk morality—often characterized as an orientation toward rule-following, institutional order, and hierarchical duty—and American folk morality, which tends to privilege individual conscience, personal autonomy, and the willingness to dissent from collective norms. Rather than treating this as a simple cultural stereotype, I want to deploy it as a structured analytical lens for organizational ethics. To sharpen the comparison, I have developed a distinction I have not seen formalized in the literature: between what I call “systemic fidelity,” the normative view that moral agency inside a large institution requires preserving the coherence and authority of the collective framework even when particular rules seem flawed, and “exit integrity,” the competing view that moral agency requires prioritizing one’s internal judgment and, if necessary, breaking institutional loyalty to act on conscience. These are intended not merely as national descriptions but as opposing models of how a person ought to behave within a bureaucracy.

Please analyze the 2015 Volkswagen diesel-emissions scandal—specifically, the widespread decision by engineers and managers to install software designed to defeat U.S. environmental regulatory testing, the sustained period of internal knowledge before any public disclosure, and the divergent reactions within the German corporate culture versus the American regulatory and legal response—through the lens of systemic fidelity versus exit integrity. How does the framework illuminate the choices faced by the engineers who designed the defeat devices, the managers who authorized them, and the broader institutional environment that sustained the deception? Which features of the episode—such as initial group conformity, the difficulty of internal dissent, and the contrast between collective silence and eventual external exposure—does it explain well, and which does it misdescribe or leave silent? Does the case support the judgment that a culture of rule-following becomes pathological when detached from individual conscience, or does it instead suggest that an emphasis on personal exit and whistleblowing is ineffective without structural backing? Finally, what does this episode reveal about the limits of explaining institutional wrongdoing solely through the tension between collective order and individual moral autonomy?
