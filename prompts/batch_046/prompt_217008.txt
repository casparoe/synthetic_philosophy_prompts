I am giving the opening remarks at a one-day interdisciplinary workshop on heritability, causation, and social policy. The audience is split evenly: on one side, behavioral geneticists and philosophers of science who work with variance-partitioning models, gene-environment interaction, and the distinction between narrow-sense and broad-sense heritability; on the other, education policymakers and equity advocates who have encountered popular claims that traits like cognitive ability are largely genetic and who tend to read high heritability figures as either proof that social intervention is futile or, conversely, as a reason to sort individuals by genetic potential.

Please write the remarks—about eight to ten minutes when spoken—and make the philosophical idea do structural work rather than decorative work. Build the speech around the paradox of perfect equality: in a hypothetical society where all environmental differences affecting a trait were eliminated, the heritability coefficient for that trait would approach 1.0, not because genes had become more determinative, but because environmental variance had collapsed to zero. Use this paradox to establish, for both halves of the audience, that heritability is a relational property of a specific population in a specific environment, not an intrinsic property of the trait, and not a measure of immutability, causal importance, or the feasibility of intervention.

Then move to a concrete tension that should feel somewhat niche but fully intelligible without outside sources: recent proposals to use polygenic scores for educational tracking or resource allocation, justified in part by citing high heritability estimates from current populations. Explain, without jargon that loses the policymakers and without condescension that alienates the scientists, why those estimates provide no direct warrant for such policies—why the same heritability figure is compatible with large intervention effects if the right environmental levers are pulled, and with negligible ones if they are not, and why treating a population-statistical decomposition as guidance for individual-level distributive decisions involves a category mistake rather than a straightforward inference.

The remarks should be formal in tone, suitable for an academic workshop opening, and should comfort neither side with false simplicity. In particular, separate what is genuinely settled in the relevant literature—such as that heritability depends on the range of environments present in the sample, that it does not license inferences about particular individuals, and that it is not stable across populations with different environmental structures—from what remains genuinely contested, such as whether knowledge of genetic influence should ever inform educational policy, or whether polygenic scores have any legitimate normative role in resource allocation. Do not present a contested ethical or methodological position as the consensus view.

Please do not include citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or references to specific scholars or texts in a way that requires verification. State conceptual distinctions in your own words without quotation marks. The speech should be fully intelligible to someone who cannot search the web.
