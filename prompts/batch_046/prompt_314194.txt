After a recent episode of depersonalization following a minor surgical procedure, I have been trying to understand whether my sense of being a continuous “I” is an illusion in the Buddhist sense rather than merely a psychological glitch. In this context, I keep encountering two analogies. The first, drawn from the classical Milindapanha and still common in introductory explanations of anatta, compares the self to a chariot: it is nothing more than assembled parts (axles, wheels, frame), and there is no charioteer apart from them; the purpose is to show that personal identity has no substantial core and that clinging to one is like clinging to an empty name. The second, which I encountered in a recent discussion of distributed cognition, compares the self to an open-source software repository maintained by many anonymous contributors: there is no original author, the code changes continuously, yet version history allows one to trace a coherent lineage, and the project functions only through collective interaction rather than a central controller. The purpose here is to preserve the intuition that there can be real continuity and functional unity without an essential, unchanging owner.

Audit both analogies: map precisely what corresponds to what in each (the parts, the relation of continuity, the absence or presence of a controller, the role of history or narrative), identify where each mapping breaks down—especially regarding moral responsibility, memory, and the first-person perspective—and determine which features of the source domain (mechanical assembly versus distributed authorship) are quietly imported that the target domain of personal identity may lack. Then say whether either analogy actually supports the conclusion that there is no self, or whether they only support a weaker claim about the kind of self that exists, and indicate what a more accurate analogy might be if both fail. Please separate what is philosophically settled about anatta and psychological continuity from what remains genuinely contested among scholars who work on both Buddhist philosophy and contemporary personal identity theory, and do not present any contested interpretation—such as whether anatta denies only a permanent soul or also a narrative self—as the consensus view.
