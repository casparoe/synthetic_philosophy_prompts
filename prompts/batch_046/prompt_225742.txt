For my independent study in our high school philosophy of mind course, I have been working through the standard distinction between higher-order representational theories and self-representational theories of consciousness, but I keep getting stuck on a more specific conceptual issue that my textbook treats only in passing. I understand that David Rosenthal's actualist higher-order thought theory treats consciousness as arising when a distinct mental state represents one's first-order state, whereas Uriah Kriegel's self-representational view holds that a conscious state represents itself rather than being targeted by a separate higher-order state. What I cannot clearly map, however, is how each framework handles the possibility of higher-order misrepresentation—cases in which a first-order perceptual state carries one phenomenal character, for instance representing a particular shade of crimson, while the accompanying representational structure attributes to it a different character, representing it as burnt orange.

Could you compare these two theories specifically on whether they can accommodate such mismatches without either collapsing the error into the first-order state itself—thereby abandoning the distinctive role of the higher-order component—or generating an infinite regress of separately conscious monitoring states? I am especially interested in whether Kriegel's structural commitment to self-representation gives him a principled reason to deny that misrepresentation can occur at the level of consciousness-constituting representation, and whether Rosenthal's separation of orders forces him to accept such mismatches only at the cost of allowing unconscious higher-order thoughts that still somehow confer consciousness. Please treat this as a conceptual comparison rather than a survey of the literature: I want to understand the logical architecture of each position, particularly how they distribute the possibility of error between first-order and higher-order levels, rather than a list of which contemporary philosophers defend which variant.
