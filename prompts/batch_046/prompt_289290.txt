I am preparing an argument for an advanced ethics seminar, and I want to develop a position that is somewhat in the weeds: rather than repeating the familiar objection that moral saints lack personal projects or aesthetic tastes, I want to claim that maximal moral goodness is undesirable because it eliminates the structural conditions that make moral achievement recognizable as such.

To frame this, I am introducing a distinction—borrowed from perceptual psychology but applied here to moral phenomenology—between “moral figure” and “moral ground.” On this view, an action registers as genuinely good or heroic only when it stands out against a background of actions that are not maximally morally loaded. A maximally good agent—someone whose every choice, from career to conversation to consumption, is optimally calibrated for moral value—would live in a state of what we might call uniform moral density. There would be no ground, only an undifferentiated field of perfect rightness. Consequently, she could never experience a particular act as a moral triumph, because there is no contrast against which it could appear as such; and the category of the supererogatory—of going beyond the call—would collapse, since there is no call that is not already fully answered by every other action.

Please argue for the position that maximal moral goodness is undesirable on these structural grounds. Your argument should explain why the absence of moral ground undermines not just the agent’s subjective satisfaction but the very intelligibility of moral progress and moral praise. Then address the following objection: even without contrast, the agent could derive satisfaction from the reflective knowledge that she is always doing what is best, and that this reflective satisfaction is sufficient to make her life good. Explain why this reflective state is qualitatively different from the recognition of having done something genuinely good, and why relying on it reduces moral life to a kind of continuous self-monitoring rather than lived achievement. Present your response in four clearly titled sections, in this order: “The Figure-Ground Thesis” (approximately 200 words), “The Collapse of Supererogation and Praise” (approximately 200 words), “Reflective Satisfaction: Objection and Reply” (approximately 250 words), and “Overall Verdict” (approximately 150 words). You need not cite external literature or provide a bibliography; develop the reasoning entirely from the conceptual framework outlined above.
