I retired from biochemistry about two years ago and lately I've been sitting on the advisory commitee for our university museum, mostly becuase I want to see how philosophy actually works inside real institutions rather than just in books. Next Thursday we vote on what to do with the Benin bronze plaques in our colonial-era collection. The Nigerian delegation wants full physical repatriation within 12 months, but our director proposed a compromise she calls split custody: we ship the metal back, but keep all digital twins, catalog descriptions, and display rights, with our museum retaining veto over any wall text that frames the objects as products of violence. One board faction calls this fair epistemic restitution; another faction says it's hermeneutic extraction—giving up the object but keeping the power to name, classify, and morally frame it.

I'm drafting a memo to circulate before Thursday's meeting that recommends one concrete course—accept the split-custody plan, reject it in favor of full return with shared labeling authority, or propose a different phased timeline. The memo needs to treat the strongest opposing argument seriously: the idea that separating physical ownership from interpretive control doesn't resolve colonial injustice but just moves it into the category system itself, so that returning the bronze while retaining the framework is conceptually incoherent. I don't want a bibliography or a list of philosophers to read; I want reasoning that treats ownership, framing, and meaning as one problem. Give me a clear recommendation with a time frame I could actually circulate, and don't just invoke legal title—engage whether an object can be said to be returned if the concepts we use to understand it are still ours.
