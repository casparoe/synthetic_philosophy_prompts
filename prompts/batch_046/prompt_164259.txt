I have been thinking on my own about whether Buddhist ethics and consequentialism can genuinely agree, or whether they talk past one another. Most discussions I have encountered treat Buddhism as a kind of long-range consequentialism about suffering and happiness, but that strikes me as missing something I keep returning to: in the teachings I have read, an action is deemed wholesome (kusala) or unwholesome (akusala) not chiefly by what happens afterwards, but by the mental state—greed, hatred, or delusion, or their absence—that accompanies the volition (cetana) at the very moment of acting. So a surgeon who saves a life yet is secretly driven by pride and craving for reputation might, on this view, be performing an ethically unwholesome act, whilst another surgeon whose patient dies through no fault of her own, yet whose mind was entirely free of ill-will and attachment, might have performed something genuinely wholesome. This seems deeply at odds with consequentialism, which appears to care only about actual welfare outcomes and not about the hidden texture of the agent's consciousness.

Write an essay on whether Buddhist ethics, understood in this mental-state-first way, is compatible with consequentialism. Weigh the main arguments on both sides—perhaps the claim that reducing suffering is so central to the Buddha's teaching that mental purity is merely instrumental to good outcomes, and the counter-claim that the Abhidhammic analysis of consciousness makes mental purity intrinsic and non-instrumental. Reach and justify a clear verdict: if they can be reconciled, explain what form of consequentialism, if any, could accommodate the priority of cetana; if they cannot, say whether this is a fatal flaw for one view or merely a difference in scope. You need not cite scholars or texts by name; work with the concepts themselves.
