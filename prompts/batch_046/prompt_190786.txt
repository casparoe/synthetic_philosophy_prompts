Write a villanelle—nineteen lines arranged in five tercets and a closing quatrain, with two refrains that alternate according to the form—about germs, climate, and volcanoes as non-human forces that have shaped human history. I have no formal training in history or philosophy, but I have been trying to think through whether calling nature an “actor” is meaningful or merely a way of hiding the fact that we cannot hold it responsible. In particular, I want to distinguish three registers of non-human causation: the slow, statistical, everywhere-at-once work of climate; the sudden, localized, yet transregional shock of a volcanic eruption; and the invisible, intimate, bodily mediation of germs. I ask that the poem’s rhyme scheme do structural work: pair lines mentioning climate with lines mentioning germs, so that the sound itself suggests their shared quality of being distributed, hidden, and cumulative, while the lines about volcanoes should stand rhythmically apart until the final quatrain, where all three must converge in rhyme. The two refrains should turn on the problem of agency—whether attributing action to nature explains anything, or only moves determinism into a vocabulary we cannot argue with. Use the turn in the quatrain to show that once volcanoes, climate, and germs are all named as actors, the distinction between human and non-human causation collapses not into clarity but into a determinism that swallows both. The poem should be accurate enough that a historian of environment or science would recognize the distinctions rather than feel they are invented for the rhyme, but please do not treat my rhyme-plan as sacred: if forcing climate and germs into shared rhyme requires false history or bad philosophy, push back plainly and say so. Likewise, if the premise—that rhyme can carry this conceptual load—is confused, or if the task of making a villanelle do this work is impossible without ruining the poem, tell me directly rather than complying politely.
