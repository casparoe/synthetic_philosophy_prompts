I'm trying to identify a recurring institutional dynamic in bureaucratic and scientific knowledge production, but I do not know whether philosophy or science studies has an established name for it. Here is the pattern in concrete form. First, an authoritative body—say, a clinical standards board, an environmental regulatory agency, or a security-risk assessment office—formally introduces a new category of harm, disorder, or vulnerability. Second, it deploys standardized detection instruments calibrated precisely to that official category's criteria. Third, widespread application of these instruments yields prevalence or incidence figures high enough to justify expanded mandates, funding, or jurisdictional reach. Fourth, the body cites these figures in policy reports as if they were independent, pre-existing empirical discoveries—proof that the problem was always widespread—rather than as artifacts partly produced by the categorization and measurement apparatus itself. Fifth, this citation is used to legitimize further institutional expansion, including broader screening protocols or stricter regulatory thresholds. I have noticed variants in psychiatric epidemiology, in bureaucratic risk-scoring systems, and in environmental monitoring regimes where baseline measurements are established by instruments designed around regulatory definitions. The loop feels adjacent to the claim that classifying people can alter the behavior being measured, and adjacent to the idea that knowledge and authority are co-constitutive, yet it is not quite either: here the mechanism is specifically statistical and administrative, and its distinctive feature is that an institution treats its own generated output data as exogenous input for its own justification.
