Ever since I left teh physics department I've been poking at philosphy of mind, and I'm hung up on whether self-deception is even coherent. There's this thinker, call him Reeves, who gives clear verdicts: he says yes, it's happening when a husband avoids opening a letter he suspects proves an affair, and yes again when a smoker sincerely believes "the statistics don't apply to me" even though she can recite them. But Reeves says no, it's not self-deception in anosognosia where a stroke patient denies paralysis with no hidden knowledge lurking, and no again for someone who simply never encountered the counter-evidence — like a physician before germ theory.

Now here's my made-up case: imagine a person with an implant that blocks interoceptive anxiety signals before they ever reach conscious awareness. Over months they form the false belief that they're physically fine, not by ignoring test results, but because the bodily warning data was filtered upstream. There's no suppressed belief hiding in a mental drawer; the information never made it to the drawer at all.

What do you think Reeves would say — self-deception or not? I'm mostly after the competing rules that fit his four calls. One rule might require an intentional act of hiding a belief you already hold; another might count any motivated failure to form a true belief, regardless of mechanism. Say what each predicts for the implant case, which rule you think Reeves is actually running and why, and then name one more hypothetical that'd split them for good.
