I have been trying to understand the internal logic of a rehabilitation protocol used at several cognitive-restructuring clinics that operate within what they call the "careenium framework." In this model, a patient's mental life is treated as an interacting field of "simmballs"—discrete symbolic representations—situated inside the "careenium," which is the neural substrate itself. The clinical practice involves a haptic-symbolic interface: patients physically rearrange virtual representations of traumatic memories, and therapists record both the symbolic configuration and concurrent neural metrics. What puzzles me is the institution's liability and billing structure. The consent form distinguishes between "substrate-originated harm" (covered by standard malpractice insurance) and "symbol-originated harm" (excluded, on the grounds that symbols are not physical agents). Yet the therapeutic manual states that simmball patterns push the substrate around, and the clinic charges full price only when neural metrics shift following a symbolic manipulation—if the substrate changes spontaneously without symbol rearrangement, the session is classified as "unauthorized substrate drift" and the patient receives a refund. Meanwhile, if the patient rearranges symbols but the neural substrate shows no corresponding change, the therapy is deemed "inert" and billed at a reduced rate.

Given these rules, what philosophical commitments about causation, mental representation, and the ontology of mind does the practice presuppose? Does the billing distinction commit the clinic to a form of downward causation in which symbols are genuinely causal agents independent of the substrate, or can it be reconstructed as a pragmatic heuristic within a strictly physicalist framework? Is the exclusion of symbol-originated harm from malpractice coverage consistent with the claim that symbols push the substrate, or does it quietly assume that the substrate is the only locus of real agency? And if one were to replace the presupposed view with an epiphenomenalist account—on which simmballs are merely descriptive patterns of careenial activity with no causal efficacy—which parts of the institution's protocol (the pricing tiers, the liability exclusions, the definition of therapeutic success) would have to be revised, and which could remain intact? Please distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and note where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that rival theories of mind might license the same institutional design.
