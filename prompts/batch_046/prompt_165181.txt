I'm leading a ninety-minute session for a philosophy-of-science reading group made up of cognitive scientists with no background in Indian philosophy, and I want to teach them Nyāya epistemology centered on the four pramāṇas — perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison (upamāna), and testimony (śabda). Don't explain the doctrine to me; design the teaching plan instead. Specify the exact order in which the four sources must be introduced and justify the conceptual dependency that makes any other sequence unstable. Provide one carrying example or case study that threads through all four: a field biologist who identifies an unknown bird call by analogy to a known species and then accepts a local guide's claim that the call signals imminent rain. This case should work for analogical reasoning but clearly break if applied to formal mathematical proof, so it reveals what comparison can and cannot do. Identify the two misconceptions this audience will likely form — I expect they will collapse testimony into inference and reduce comparison to Bayesian pattern-matching — and say precisely where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted and how. Give me a single end-of-session diagnostic question that tests whether they grasp the independence of upamāna rather than just memorizing the four terms. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out for this audience — for instance, the Nyāya-Sūtra commentarial tradition, the Buddhist pramāṇa-vāda critique, and the internal Nyāya debate over the conditions of śabda (yogyatā and āptatva) — and briefly justify each omission. Keep the plan to one session; do not include readings or a bibliography.
