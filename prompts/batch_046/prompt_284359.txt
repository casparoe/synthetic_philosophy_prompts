I am drafting a novel set in the turbulent decades following the Fronde, and I have been trying to work through a fragment from Blaise Pascal's Pensées that treats the relation between justice and force. In this passage, Pascal seems to maintain that justice without power is impotent, that force without justice is tyrannical, and that legitimate authority arises only when justice is made strong or when force is brought under the rule of justice. What I find conceptually difficult is the temporal structure of this union. I have been developing a thematic idea for my narrative—something I am calling institutional sedimentation—in which a political order founded originally on naked coercion gradually acquires, across generations, the appearance and functional reality of justice through custom, repetition, and institutional memory, without ever having achieved the simultaneous presence of justice and force that Pascal describes. I need to understand whether Pascal's argument strictly requires that justice and force be contemporaneously united at the origin of any legitimate regime, or whether his framework can accommodate a historical lag in which legitimacy is retrospectively conferred upon an initially violent foundation. If he rejects such sedimentation, does his rejection rest on a logical point about the definition of justice, or on a psychological claim about the impossibility of genuine belief in authority without an original moral warrant? Your explanation will determine how I construct the legal philosophy of my protagonist.
