I am a senior taking an upper-level seminar on moral change and institutional ethics, and I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks—about twelve minutes—at the launch event for our university’s new Center for the Study of Normative Change. The audience is deliberately split: philosophy faculty and graduate students who have worked through Appiah’s account of honor worlds and Williams’s suspicion that moral progress is rarely a victory of reason over prejudice; and university trustees, local civic leaders, and student activists who funded and will use the center because they believe that “better arguments lead to better societies.” I need the speech to take that skeptical thesis seriously—to treat norms like dueling and slavery as cases that collapsed mainly through shifts in honor codes and interest realignment rather than through argumentative defeat—without insulting the donors by dismissing public reason, and without telling the activists in the room that their reasoning is irrelevant. To keep this from becoming a summary of Appiah, I want to structure the speech around a concept I am developing in my course work: “status debt,” the idea that an institution accumulates reputational liability not when its practices are shown to be irrational, but when peer institutions have reclassified those practices as vulgar or inefficient, so that continued adherence becomes a marker of backwardness rather than integrity. The philosophical idea should do real structural work: it should determine what the center can honestly promise (tracking honor economies and incentive structures) and what it should not promise (that hosting debates will by itself dissolve harmful norms), and it should navigate that tension in language accessible to the non-philosophers without flattening the point for the specialists. Please write the speech, and do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
