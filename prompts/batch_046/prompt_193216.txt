I'm writing the final exam for an upper-level seminar on ethics and infinity. I need one essay question answerable in 60 minutes and worth 20 marks, designed so that students cannot earn high marks merely by summarizing the standard total-versus-average-utilitarian paradoxes or recounting St. Petersburg-style analogies. Present them with the following constructed argument about an infinite linear sequence of agents A₁, A₂, A₃, ... where each A(i) can prevent harm H(i+1) at no cost to itself: (1) If an agent can prevent a harm at no cost, it ought to do so. (2) Therefore each A(i) ought to rescue A(i+1). (3) By transitivity of obligation, if A(i) ought to ensure that A(i+1) fulfills its duty to A(i+2), then A(i) bears a derivative obligation regarding A(i+2), and so on across the chain. (4) Hence A₁ bears obligations extending over outcomes infinitely far from its causal reach. (5) This either violates "ought implies can" for A₁, or reveals an equivocation in (3) between direct duty and derivative responsibility. (6) If (3) is rejected, global aggregation principles—such as total utilitarianism—lose normative purchase, because they require present agents to weigh infinitely distant outcomes they cannot causally influence. Ask students to identify the precise scope shift or ambiguity that allows the paradox to arise, to evaluate whether imposing a causal-locality constraint on rescue obligations resolves the tension without undermining ordinary finite rescue duties, and to state which standard aggregation principle, if any, must be sacrificed to maintain consistency. Provide the exam question text, a model answer of about 550 words, and a marking scheme totaling 20 marks built around five must-hit analytical moves; for each move, list the points it carries and include a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that costs candidates those marks.
