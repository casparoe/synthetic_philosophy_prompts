I've been modeling a specific degradation pattern in hierarchical collective judgment, and I want to know whether it already has a precise name. Start with the standard Condorcet setup: independent voters, each better than random, majority accuracy rising with group size. Now apply it recursively—each jury selects representatives who form a second jury, with the same aggregation rule—while allowing representatives to share not merely the errors of their constituents but a systematic bias in how they discount dissenting signals. In the configurations I've worked out, accuracy doesn't decay smoothly with nesting depth; instead there's a discrete threshold—one additional layer—where collective performance collapses abruptly to below-random, even though every individual layer remains above chance and the correlation structure hasn't changed qualitatively. The collapse seems to depend on representatives treating agreement with peers as stronger evidence than agreement with the underlying population, but it isn't the same as an information cascade or as the standard correlated-voter correction to the jury theorem. Have I rediscovered a named result in social epistemology or social choice theory—perhaps something about nested aggregation or hierarchical belief revision—or is this genuinely uncharted? If it's the latter, I don't just want a label; I want the boundary conditions carved precisely: what distinguishes this nonlinear nesting collapse from ordinary correlation decay, from homophily-driven wisdom reversal, and from cascade dynamics, and under what structural conditions does the threshold appear? A bare name won't satisfy me; I'm after the concept's edges and its nearest neighbors.
