I'm a science and ideas journalist, and I've been digging into how people talk about autmation eating jobs. There's this one pattern I keep running into—maybe you've seen it too? First, a worker or expert says their job can't be automated because it needs real human intution or empathy. Then, once AI actually gets decent at that part, they don't admit they were wrong; they just slide the 'essential human' part upward. Suddenly it's not about empathy anymore, it's about high-stakes judgment. If judgment gets automated, they slide again—now it's about legal accountability, or having a face to blame, or some vague 'social presence' that machines lack.

I've seen this cascade occurring in nursing, law, even graphic design. Each time the old defense gets mechanized, a new layer of 'the human element' appears right above it. It's not quite motte-and-bailey, because people aren't deliberately switching between a defensible core and a flashy extension; it feels more like occupational identity reshaping itself in real time. Is there an established concept or literature for this exact sliding? If not, can you name and define it precisely—what it is, what it borders on, and where its edges sit? I'm writing a feature on the meaning of work and automation and I'd love to have the right language for this, so a clear answer that doesn't just dump citations would be perfect—just tell me if I'm describing something real with a name, or if I need to coin one.
