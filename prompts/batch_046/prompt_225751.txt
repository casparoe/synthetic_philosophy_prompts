I have become convinced that an 'I' is not necessarily imprisoned in a single brain but can persist in a genuinely distributed form across other minds through shared cognitive patterns and emotional resonance—roughly in the spirit of Hofstadter's idea that strange loops need not be bounded by one skull. On this view, if a sufficient cluster of my characteristic loops—my evaluative priorities, my narrative framing, my associative habits—is reconstructed in another person's cognition, then part of my self literally survives there, not as mere influence but as functional continuity. The claim feels personally unavoidable now: since my partner's stroke fragmented her autobiographical memory while leaving her emotional and evaluative patterns largely intact, I have observed my own practical reasoning gradually replicating her priorities, and I am forced to ask whether I am now hosting part of her self without either of us being reducible to a single brain.
