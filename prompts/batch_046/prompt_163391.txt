I just finished a long, exhausting argument with a friend about whether knowing an algorithmic risk prediction—say, that a clinical model assigns a particular patient a “fated” fatal cardiac event within ten years—should motivate any change in behavior. At one point he invoked what he called the Damascus principle: if you are fated to die in Damascus, then going to Damascus fulfills the fate, but if you avoid Damascus and survive, Damascus was never your fate to begin with. He then applied this structure to the prediction: if the model forecasts heart failure and you alter your diet and survive, the forecast was merely false, not circumvented; but if you do nothing and die, the fate is fulfilled. From this he concluded that precautionary action is conceptually incoherent whenever an outcome is genuinely predetermined. I think he is compressing several distinct claims into one neat paradox, but I cannot disentangle them myself. Could you lay out the precise theses that this Damascus slogan might actually be expressing—distinguishing, for instance, logical fatalism about fixed spatiotemporal endpoints from modal claims about counterfactual robustness, from epistemic claims about the unfalsifiability of predictions, and from normative claims about the rationality of avoidance behavior? For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or actually false, and specify which of these readings, if any, genuinely supports his dismissal of precautionary behavior in the predictive-medicine case. Please present the interpretations as a menu rather than selecting one, and indicate exactly where in his argument each version would need to do the work.
