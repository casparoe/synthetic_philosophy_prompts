Case 1 — Whole-brain transplantation into a new body. Verdict: the same “I” continues, intact and undivided.

Case 2 — Severe dementia in which an adult child gradually assumes narrative custody: remembering the parent’s life in detail, speaking in the first person on their behalf in family settings, and making value-laden choices the parent would have made. Verdict: there is a genuine, though attenuated, persistence of the parent’s I in the child; not full identity, but not mere metaphor either.

Case 3 — After a close friend’s death, survivors internalize her speech patterns, values, and emotional responses so thoroughly that they report, “she lives on in me.” Verdict: this is purely metaphorical; no actual distribution of her I has occurred.

Case 4 — A split-brain patient whose hemispheres develop independent preferences and can be separately addressed. Verdict: one original I has divided into two; this is fission, not a single I distributed across both hemispheres.

Case 5 — Three individuals linked by a high-fidelity neural mesh that pools autobiographical memory and emotional co-regulation in real time, yet each retains a distinct, non-merged stream of consciousness with its own first-person indexicals. Verdict: this is not one I spread across three brains; it is three Is with shared data.

Case 6 — A synthetic-neuron replacement of a human brain that preserves the causal-functional architecture of self-reference, including the loops by which the system attributes present states to “me.” Verdict: continuous persistence of the same I.

The tension I see: in Case 2 I allow an I to leak into another brain through narrative and emotional custody, but in Case 3 I deny it despite equally deep internalization; in Case 4 I treat one brain splitting as producing two Is, yet in Case 5 I deny that linking three brains produces one.
