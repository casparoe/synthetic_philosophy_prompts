I'm presenting a job-market paper defending a modified complicity framework for collective harms: the wrong of contributing to climate damage or factory-farmed meat consumption is not causal efficacy—since individual actions are imperceptible in the aggregate—but participation in a practice sustained by expectation-formation loops, and I distinguish climate, meat, and voting not by scale but by what I call "feedback-loop transparency," the degree to which an agent can trace how her specific participation updates collective expectations that stabilize the harmful equilibrium. Climate and meat exhibit low transparency (opaque loops, diffuse structural complicity), whereas voting exhibits high transparency (direct observable feedback), which is why the inefficacy objection genuinely blocks obligation in voting but misfires elsewhere. The audience is a research-university philosophy department: mostly normative ethicists and philosophers of social science, no climate specialists, several convinced that individual-level climate ethics is a distracting moralization of structural problems, one senior member who treats "complicity" as an obscurantist substitute for causal explanation, and a couple of consequentialists who will press parsimony. They care about ad hoc distinctions and practical relevance. Prepare my Q&A defense sheet: the thesis I'm holding and what I should concede upfront; the objections ordered by how damaging they are and who will raise them (the consequentialist, the structuralist, the Kantian, the social scientist), each phrased in that faction's vocabulary; for each, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and an honest note on where the reply is weakest; the single reply that has to land if the framework is to survive; and the one question I am hoping nobody asks, phrased exactly as they would put it. Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for further reading.
