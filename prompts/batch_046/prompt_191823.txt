The position I defend on the torture-versus-dust-specks problem is that aggregation fails not globally but dimensionally: severe harms such as torture occupy an evaluative dimension—call it structural domination—within which quantitative comparison with mild harms is conceptually blocked, not merely outweighed by lexical rules. Mild discomforts aggregate linearly within their own dimension, but no bridge principle permits cross-dimensional weighing, however large the population. What would count against this? Rank the defeaters by severity: which patterns in empirical moral psychology (e.g., framing-sensitive reversals or cross-cultural divergence), which formal results about value incommensurability, and which constructed cases—say, smoothly scaling the irritation upward until it is phenomenologically indistinguishable from torture—would be fatal rather than merely awkward? Which of these would a committed defender concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they dismiss as category errors? Finally, assess whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—e.g., by making the dimensional boundary movable post hoc—and whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a vice for a normative account of harm comparison.
