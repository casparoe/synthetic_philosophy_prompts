I have been turning over an idea I first encountered in Quine’s thought experiment about gavagai: a linguist in the field hears a speaker shout the word as a rabbit runs past, and realizes the utterance might just as well translate as rabbit, as undetached rabbit-part, or as this temporal stage of rabbit-hood, with no further behavioral evidence able to decide among them, since any observation can be made consistent with any translation if we adjust enough background belief. What has stayed with me is the possibility that this indeterminacy is not only outward, toward foreign languages, but inward: when I try to recover what I meant in a past moment, I may be translating my own mental states with a grammar I have quietly chosen, and there is no hidden record inside me that proves one reading is correct. I would like a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with two refrains—that builds this double movement into its form. Let one refrain state the scene in the object-reading, something close to the rabbit runs across the grass, and let the other state it in the part-or-stage reading, something close to the undetached part runs free. Have them rotate through the stanzas so that each appearance shifts the context—first the field observation, then the translation manual, then the inward memory—without either refrain ever gaining decisive authority. The rhyme scheme should suggest a false stability: perhaps an A-rhyme that holds its sound while its referent slides. In the final quatrain, place the two refrains side by side, unmerged, so that the form itself enacts the failure of resolution. The poem should be precise enough that someone familiar with Quine’s argument recognizes the three-way ambiguity and the role of auxiliary hypotheses, and it should carry the inward twist—that the translator and the translated are the same person—without resolving the tension. I do not need citations or a bibliography; I want the poem itself to do the thinking.
