I'm a PhD candidate in philosophy of science, and I'm confronting a tension in how to present the conclusions of a multi-study synthesis I'm preparing for a policy brief. After applying rigorous pre-registered filters, I have strong, study-specific evidence for each of 120 discrete causal claims about intervention efficacy. At the same time, because I model the field-wide false-discovery rate and the partial dependencies among study designs, my global model assigns high probability to the claim that between eight and fifteen of these individual conclusions are spurious. My supervisor argues that epistemic consistency requires me to either withhold belief from the individual claims or reject the global error model—holding both, he says, violates the conjunction principle for rational belief. Meanwhile, the funding body requires discrete yes-or-no recommendations for each intervention; they have no mechanism for absorbing a blanket "some of these are wrong" caveat. I feel that abandoning either the granular endorsements or the statistical humility seems wrong, but I cannot articulate the mistake in my supervisor's reasoning, nor can I see how to write the brief without either misrepresenting my credences or rendering the output useless. Is there a philosophically defensible posture that allows me to maintain high confidence in each claim while genuinely expecting that some subset fails—one that doesn't collapse into mere pragmatic hedging? And if so, what should that posture actually look like in the written recommendations?
