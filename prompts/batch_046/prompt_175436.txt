I've been studying how defeated political movements are remembered in state-sanctioned archives, and I've noticed a recurring mechanism that I don't have a precise word for. Rather than simply erasing the opposition, the victors' chroniclers preserve fragments of the defeated arguments—manifestos reproduced with their economic reasoning excised, speeches quoted only where they sound utopian or contradictory, rebuttals inserted that were never actually delivered—so that the loss comes to seem intellectually inevitable rather than politically contingent. Over successive generations, readers of these curated sources conclude that the defeated side never possessed a coherent case to begin with. I have seen this pattern described informally in discussions of 19th-century labor uprisings and in post-revolutionary state histories, but I am asking about the specific practice itself: the selective preservation and rhetorical framing of a loser's own words to manufacture an appearance of internal incoherence.

Does this mechanism have an established name in historiography or philosophy of history—perhaps as a subtype of archival bias, epistemic injustice, or narrative control? How exactly does it differ from ordinary propaganda, from simple omission, and from the broad claim that history is written by the victors? If no existing concept captures it precisely, I want you to take a definite position: either identify the nearest established term and argue rigorously why it does or does not fit, or coin a precise term, define its boundaries, and supply clear counter-cases that fall outside it. Do not merely survey the options; commit to one classification and defend it.
