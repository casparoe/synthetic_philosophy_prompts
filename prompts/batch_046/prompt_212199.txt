Consider the standard toxin puzzle: an agent is offered a substantial reward conditional solely on forming, at a specified time, the sincere intention to perform a mildly harmful but non-fatal act at a later time; once the reward is secured, the agent has no further reason to carry out the act and every reason not to. Now extend the problem space with two under-discussed variants. First, a “reverse toxin” in which the reward is paid only if the agent fails to form the intention, yet the agent still prefers both receiving the reward and retaining the option to act. Second, a “deliberative locking” condition: any adequate theory must explain whether an agent can rationally bind her future deliberative self without collapsing into either self-manipulation or mere predictive belief about herself.

Write the full list of desiderata that a theory of rational agency must meet to count as adequate across the standard case and both variants. For each desideratum, state the motivating case or conceptual pressure behind it, mark whether it is non-negotiable or reflects a camp-specific preference (e.g., of causalists, evidentialists, or reflective-endorsement theorists), and flag any pair or subset you take to be jointly unsatisfiable—paying special attention to whether unsatisfiability arises specifically from the interaction between the standard toxin case and the reverse-toxin variant. Be explicit about whether the theory must preserve a sharp distinction between the rationality of forming an intention and the rationality of executing it, and whether it must accommodate higher-order evaluation of one’s own deliberative mechanisms. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases. Then, briefly, score how causal decision theory, evidential decision theory, and a two-level reflective-endorsement account fare against your list.
