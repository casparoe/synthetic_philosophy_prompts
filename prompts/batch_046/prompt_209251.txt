After a prolonged and somewhat exhausting argument with a friend about whether high-level cognition can genuinely penetrate perceptual experience, I want to lay out my own judgments across concrete cases and see whether they hang together under any coherent principle. Here is where I stand. When a trained radiologist detects a tumor in an X-ray that looks like uniform noise to a novice, I think her expert knowledge has genuinely altered what she sees, not merely what she judges about an unchanged visual image. When I am told that the gray card in front of me is actually white but illuminated by green light, and I then experience it as white, I think my belief has penetrated my color experience. When an experienced chess player glances at a board and immediately sees the position as dangerous for black, without any conscious step-by-step inference, I think her conceptual mastery has shaped the visual gestalt itself. By contrast, when I view the Müller-Lyer illusion and continue to see the lines as unequal despite knowing they are identical in length, I do not think my knowledge has failed to penetrate perception; rather, I think cognitive penetration is simply not at issue in hard-wired illusions of that kind. Likewise, when a listener who has recently acquired a theoretical framework for analyzing tonal hierarchy reports that she now hears previously unnoticed structural relationships in a familiar fugue, I think cognition has penetrated her auditory experience. Yet when a subject is subliminally primed with an anger-related word and subsequently rates an ambiguous face as angrier, I am inclined to call this post-perceptual judgment rather than penetrated vision—though my friend insists the underlying mechanisms are indistinguishable from the radiologist case. Is there a single principle—perhaps one centered on the distinction between perceptual learning or conceptual restructuring and mere associative priming—that captures all six verdicts? If no unified account can accommodate them, which of my judgments should I overturn, and why? Finally, apply your best principle to the case that still divides us: a sommelier who, after being informed that a particular bottle is exceptionally expensive, reports that its taste has become noticeably richer and more complex, even though the chemical composition of the liquid in her mouth has not changed.
