I write about science governance and the philosophy of technology, and I have been tracking a posture toward the past that I cannot place within the standard taxonomy. Consider two concrete cases. First, an AI safety board commissions a historical study of prior technology panics—not to celebrate the heroic scientists who navigated them, not to preserve an institutional lineage, and not to break free from inherited obligations, but specifically to extract failure-rate distributions so that funding gates and regulatory thresholds can be calibrated against statistical risk curves. The resulting narrative is aggressively pruned: anecdotes that do not shift predictive probabilities are discarded; outliers are retained only if they alter the tail of the distribution. Second, a climate-policy office archives past mitigation attempts not as cultural heritage or as cautionary liberation, but as a labeled dataset for forecasting future intervention efficacy, actively stripping away contextual detail that does not improve model accuracy.

This posture treats history as a predictive training distribution rather than as a source of meaning, identity, or emancipation. I have in mind Nietzsche's well-known division of historical consciousness into monumental, antiquarian, and critical uses, and this behavior seems to borrow machinery from all three without belonging to any. It selectively elevates certain past crises in a way that echoes monumental history, yet only as calibrated data points rather than as models for heroic action; it preserves records in a manner reminiscent of antiquarian history, yet discards the cultural tissue and continuity that preservation usually serves; and it refuses to treat the past as binding authority, which sounds critical, yet it uses that refusal not to liberate present action but to tighten future decision-making within statistically derived constraints.

Does this configuration have an established name—perhaps a recognized fourth mode in Nietzschean scholarship, or a documented corruption of one of the three? Or is it something genuinely novel?
