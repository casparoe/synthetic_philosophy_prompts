I am preparing a closing reading for a graduate seminar on mental causation, and I want a short poem—specifically a Petrarchan sonnet with a clear volta between the octave and the sestet—to serve as a conceptual coda rather than a mere summary. The philosophical target is the causal exclusion argument: the problem that if every physical effect already has a sufficient physical cause, and mental properties are not identical to those physical properties, then mental properties appear either redundant, yielding systematic overdetermination, or causally inert, yielding epiphenomenalism. I want the poem accurate enough that a philosopher of mind recognizes the exact dialectical structure—the closure of the physical, the exclusion principle, the two horns—not just the keywords.

Here is the structural work the form should do. Use the octave to build the intuitive, pre-theoretical picture of mental and physical causation running side by side—perhaps through the image of two hands on a single tiller, or two signatures on one check—so that the reader initially thinks the tension is simply between physicalism and dualism. Then, at the volta on line nine, pivot sharply: the problem is not competition between two kinds of causes, but the principle of causal closure itself, which refuses to share credit. The sestet should move through the two horns—overdetermination versus exclusion—without resolving them, letting each line feel like a door that closes behind it. The closing couplet should state precisely what must be surrendered: either the completeness of the physical or the efficacy of the mental.

To keep the poem from feeling like a textbook restatement, inject a specific, somewhat niche conceptual framing: treat the exclusion principle as a kind of "causal jealousy" or "single-entry bookkeeping," where the physical cause regards any mental co-cause as an uninvited guest rather than a rival. Make that jealousy audible in the rhyme scheme—perhaps pairing "closed" with "deposed," or "sufficient" with "deficient"—so that the formal closure of the rhyme mirrors the causal closure being argued for. The poem should leave the tension genuinely open, as our discussion did: we did not settle whether mental causation survives by being identical, by being emergent in a genuinely non-redundant way, or by being an illusion. Do not include citations, bibliographic references, or suggestions for further reading; the audience is already immersed in the literature.
