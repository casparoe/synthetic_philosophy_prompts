I am a doctoral student in philosophy of biology, and I recently heard a summary of Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper—published in Proceedings of the Royal Society B under the title "The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm: A Critique of the Adaptationist Programme"—presented by a senior colleague in my department. His account runs roughly as follows. Gould and Lewontin argued that evolutionary biologists habitually fall into a Panglossian fallacy, treating every biological trait as an optimally designed solution to an environmental problem while ignoring that many structures are merely spandrels: necessary architectural byproducts of other features that are subsequently co-opted for new uses (exaptations). Because spandrels and exaptations are allegedly ubiquitous, the adaptationist research program is, in their view, methodologically bankrupt; evolutionary explanation should therefore abandon adaptive-functional storytelling in favor of purely structural, phylogenetic, and developmental accounts. He further claimed that Gould and Lewontin introduced the now-standard philosophical distinction between selection-for and selection-of—the evolutionary reason a trait originated versus the reason it is currently maintained—to demonstrate that observing present utility never establishes adaptive origin. Finally, he suggested that in contemporary evolutionary developmental biology, this spandrel logic should be applied specifically to traits arising from conserved modular signaling pathways: any feature produced by a shared developmental cascade ought to be presumed a structural byproduct rather than an independent adaptation unless comparative phylogenetic analysis proves otherwise. From these premises he concluded that since adaptationists cannot rule out spandrel-origin for every trait, adaptationism fails as a program and should be replaced by a pluralistic but non-adaptive framework.

I suspect this characterization captures the polemical tone of the original paper but may overstate its conclusions and conflate some technical distinctions that the authors kept separate. Which parts of the summary would a careful defender of Gould and Lewontin accept as accurate? Where does it subtly misrepresent the scope of their critique—particularly regarding the relationship between spandrels and exaptations, the fate of adaptationist explanation, and the applicability of the spandrel concept to modular developmental pathways? Does the conclusion that adaptationism should be abandoned actually follow from the corrected premises, or does it depend on misreading their argument?
