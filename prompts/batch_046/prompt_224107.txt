I am a high school student enrolled in an introductory philosophy seminar, and this week our discussion turned to vagueness and the sorites paradox. Rather than revisiting the familiar examples of sand heaps or hair loss, I want to present a poem to my study group that engages a less conventional scenario: imagine a museum conservator who, over the course of a year, removes exactly one atomic layer of pigment from a Renaissance fresco each week, replacing nothing, so that the work slowly fades toward blank plaster. The philosophical tension I want the poem to capture is specifically the disagreement between those who think vagueness is purely epistemic—our ignorance conceals a sharp, definite boundary layer that exists but eludes us—and those who think it is ontic: there genuinely is no fact of the matter about whether, say, layer 312 is still part of the original fresco or already something else.

Please write a villanelle of nineteen lines following the standard A1bA2 rhyme scheme, with two refrains that take turns and finally meet in the closing couplet. The first refrain should express the epistemic intuition: that a precise boundary exists even if we cannot locate it. The second refrain should express the ontic intuition: that no single layer can bear the metaphysical weight of destroying identity, so there is literally no cutoff to discover. The poem should use the fading fresco as its carrying image, not as decoration, and should render the logical structure of the sorites accurately—continuous change, tolerance for each individual increment, and an apparently catastrophic conclusion at the end—without resolving whether the fresco survives or perishes. I want the repetition and rhyme to do structural work: each return of the refrains should shift its context slightly, so that by the final couplet the two positions are forced into direct contradiction without synthesis, mirroring exactly where our seminar discussion ended. The poem should be precise enough that someone familiar with vagueness theory recognizes the pressure of the tolerance principle and the arbitrary cutoff problem, rather than seeing those ideas merely named.
