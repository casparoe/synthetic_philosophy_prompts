I recently inherited a box of my great-grandfather’s letters from the 1920s, in which he was active in a now-obscure cooperative movement that eventually dissolved. As I have begun drafting a family history, I notice that I keep framing his efforts as an early, incomplete step toward the modern social-democratic settlement he never lived to see—judging his failures and partial successes by standards he could not have shared. A historian acquaintance summarized the classic critique of this tendency, often traced to Butterfield, in the following terms: Whig history treats the past as a linear progression toward present liberal institutions, distributing praise and blame according to whether historical actors anticipated modern values. Butterfield demonstrated that this is not merely partial but structurally incoherent, because knowing an outcome makes it psychologically impossible to treat prior events as genuinely open, which means any narrative linking past actions to present results collapses into retrospective determinism. Consequently, writing about my ancestor’s politics in relation to later welfare-state achievements is not just biased but methodologically invalid; the only rigorous alternative is a pure historicism that describes his cooperative entirely within its own conceptual horizon, never invoking what followed. Before I restructure my manuscript around this prohibition, I want to know how fair the summary is. Which sentences capture the actual critique, which overstate it into a logical or psychological impossibility, and does the conclusion—that I must excise all reference to later outcomes—actually follow from the accurate parts? I am especially unsure whether the argument confuses the moral error of judging the past by present standards with the explanatory legitimacy of tracing long causal chains, and I would like the distinction clarified without being directed to a reading list.
