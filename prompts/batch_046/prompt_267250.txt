Over the past few months I have been reflecting on Sartre's notion of bad faith after receiving a clinical diagnosis that has forced me to reconsider how much of my behavior I can genuinely attribute to my own choices rather than to conditions I did not choose. I have tried to formalize the tension I feel, but the argument I constructed seems to collapse every recognition of constraint into self-deception, and I need help locating the error without abandoning what I take to be Sartre's central insight. Here is the chain as I see it. (1) For Sartre, consciousness is radically free in the ontological sense: there is no predetermined human essence, and I am never identical with my past, my body, my social position, or my psychological states. (2) Bad faith is the specific refusal of this freedom, occurring when I treat my facticity—my concrete situation—as if it were my necessary essence, for example by saying I am just an anxious person or that my upbringing made me incapable of trust. (3) If freedom is genuinely radical, then no external condition, whether neurochemical, historical, or social, can determine my present choice; at every moment I remain free to choose the meaning I give to that condition. (4) Therefore, whenever I explain my failure to act by appealing to depression, trauma, or social conditioning, I am treating those conditions as causally determinative of my consciousness, which is precisely the move Sartre identifies as bad faith. (5) Yet clinical practice and ordinary moral reasoning depend on distinguishing between rationalizations and genuine constraints: we treat severe depression or coercion as real limits on agency that justify excuse or treatment rather than blame. (6) If Sartre's analysis applies consistently, then accepting any clinical explanation of my behavior requires me to identify myself with my condition, and is therefore an instance of bad faith; seeking psychiatric help becomes conceptually incoherent because it presupposes a constraint on freedom that Sartre denies can exist. (7) This proves too much, since it renders psychology, sociology, and moral excuse-talk conceptually mistaken and pathologizes the very recognition of suffering that makes ethical and medical response possible. (8) So either Sartre's view of radical freedom is false, or we must conclude that all appeals to psychological or social constraint are forms of self-deception. I am reluctant to surrender (1) or (2), because they capture what drew me to Sartre in the first place: the idea that I am not fixed by my circumstances. But I also do not want to deny that depression can genuinely constrain what I am able to do, or that social oppression is real. Which step should I reject? If the flaw is in (3), does that mean freedom is only partial? If the flaw is in (4), how can I cite my condition without falling into bad faith? Please explain why the rejected step looked plausible, and what giving it up costs for Sartre's broader philosophy. I ask only that you reply in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write in a careful letter.
