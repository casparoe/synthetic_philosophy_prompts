I am preparing the opening remarks for a memorial workshop being held this Friday in honor of my doctoral advisor, Professor Elena Voss, who died unexpectedly three weeks ago. The audience will be split between specialists in epistemology and formal decision theory—many of whom worked with her on questions of epistemic utility and veritist norms—and her family, parish members, and colleagues from the university administration, none of whom have any background in philosophy of science. I need a speech of roughly six minutes that treats her intellectual commitment not as biography but as architecture: she was a committed accuracy-first epistemologist who maintained that the only legitimate measure of a belief is its proximity to the truth, assessed through proper scoring rules, and that any practical benefit—emotional solace, social harmony, or motivational force—is strictly irrelevant to whether one ought to hold that belief. Because she held this view with genuine personal rigor, I feel that a conventional eulogy filled with warm but imprecise praise would actually misrepresent her; the tribute must be structured by the very norms she defended, which means I must find a way to honor her through truthful remembrance rather than through consoling simplification.

The philosophical idea should do real structural work in shaping what can and cannot be said. I want the speech to navigate the tension between honest memorial belief and the usual eulogistic impulse to smooth edges for emotional comfort: I should be able to acknowledge specific limitations in her influence, correct a common misconception about her late work on credal accuracy, and admit that her insistence on separating epistemic value from practical utility sometimes made collaboration difficult, all without alienating her family or turning the occasion into a lecture. At the same time, the remarks must remain fully intelligible to listeners who do not know what a Brier score measures or why evidential considerations might be divorced from practical ones. To help bridge this gap, I have been thinking with a friend about a speculative notion—something like a memorial scoring rule that rewards beliefs about the dead according to how precisely they track the actual details of a life rather than how much comfort they provide to the living. I would like you to weave that invented concept into the remarks as an organizing metaphor, not as an established technical term, so that it gives the family a concrete image of what she valued and gives the philosophers a precise framing of why rigorous remembrance is itself a form of respect.

Please write the speech itself—opening remarks suitable for the occasion—without including bibliographies, citation lists, reading suggestions, or references to specific publication titles, page numbers, or dates. Do not rely on obscure technical results that a well-read but non-searching model would be unlikely to have encountered; build the argument from general knowledge of accuracy-first epistemology, epistemic utility theory, and proper scoring rules, augmented only by the invented memorial scoring rule idea described above. The tone should be formal, affectionate without being sentimental, and intellectually serious without becoming a tutorial; the philosophical framework should determine the shape of the tribute, not merely ornament it.
