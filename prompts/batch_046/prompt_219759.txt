Design a 90-minute classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy of science seminar of about 16 postgraduate students that makes participants feel the force of the problem of verisimilitude — specifically, the difficulty of ranking false theories by their proximity to truth when the true account is unknown, and the tendency of formal measures of such proximity to collapse into circularity or triviality.

To prevent the session from becoming a standard discussion, inject the following framing: divide the students into three jury panels, each given one fictional but plausible "mechanism card" describing an unobservable causal regulator (for example, a hidden cellular feedback loop). Each card lists a distinct pattern of correct predictions within the observed domain, false predictions outside it, and unobservable ontological commitments. The panels must collectively rank the three theories by "closeness to truth" using only a shared pool of "epistemic tokens" that they allocate across three dimensions — predictive accuracy, explanatory scope, and ontological parsimony. The rules should be arranged to force conflict: each panel receives a slightly different weighting brief (one favours predictive success, another favours simplicity, a third favours scope), and they must negotiate a single collective ranking before a hidden "true law" envelope is opened at the 55-minute mark. Once revealed, the true law shows that the theory the group ranked highest actually embeds the correct mechanism within false auxiliary assumptions, while the theory ranked lowest made no false predictions in the observed domain but is structurally incompatible with the true law.

Please specify: a precise timeline (e.g., 10 minutes framing, 30 minutes panel deliberation, 15 minutes negotiation, 10 minutes reveal and crisis, 25 minutes debrief); assigned roles within each panel (e.g., a predictivist, a realist, an instrumentalist); the materials required (mechanism cards, token sheets, sealed envelope); a non-monetary payoff or reputation scheme that rewards both individual panel advocacy and collective consensus, thereby generating real tension; the debrief that maps each phase onto parts of the philosophical problem (the need for a distance metric, the incommensurability of true versus false content, the circular dependence on prior knowledge of the truth); an honest note on which formal feature of verisimilitude (for instance, the logical ordering results) the activity cannot reproduce and which the facilitator must supply verbally; and a prediction of what will predictably go wrong in the first run (e.g., panels refusing to update their rankings after the reveal and defending their token allocations) together with how to handle it.

Please write your response in American English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not require students to quote or cite specific philosophers by name in the rules of the activity.
