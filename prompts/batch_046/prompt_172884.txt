I am a journalist who covers the philosophy of science and institutional decision-making, and I am developing a reusable analytical framework for my editorial team. We frequently report on failed large-scale scientific or policy initiatives—collapsed clinical trials, abandoned climate-adaptation projects, or defunded basic-research programs—and our readers often debate whether a given failure was due to unavoidable bad luck or to a preventable lack of institutional adaptability. I want you to translate Machiavelli’s distinction between fortuna and virtù into an operational procedure that a non-specialist editor can apply in under thirty minutes to a case study, without needing to read primary texts or search for sources.

Please approach this as both a scholar of Renaissance political philosophy and a practical methodologist. Before constructing the procedure, spend a brief moment brainstorming the conceptual dependencies: what Machiavelli actually means by fortuna—not mere randomness, but the contingent flow of circumstances that can be partially channeled—and by virtù—not moral virtue, but the adaptive skill, foresight, and organizational flexibility to shape outcomes. The deliverable should be a step-by-step checklist or flowchart in words: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a verdict of “predominantly fortune,” where it stops with “predominantly lack of virtù,” and where it must explicitly hand the decision to human judgment because the descriptive distinction collapses.

The procedure must survive three hard distinctions that standard summaries usually blur. First, it must separate exogenous fortuna—genuinely unpredictable external shocks, such as a sudden geopolitical embargo on a critical reagent—from endogenous fragility that looks like bad luck but actually reflects a failure of institutional plasticity, that is, the inability to build “dikes” against foreseeable variability. Second, it must treat virtù not as individual heroic leadership but as distributed organizational capacity: the presence of redundant pathways, rapid feedback loops, and willingness to abandon failing protocols. Third, it must handle the intermediate zone where fortuna and virtù are dynamically coupled, as in Machiavelli’s river metaphor, where the same storm can destroy an unprepared city and merely test a well-prepared one.

Then apply the procedure to two contrasting cases. Case one: a multinational vaccine-development consortium that collapsed after an unforeseen viral mutation rendered its target obsolete, despite having robust adaptive protocols in place. Case two: a regional water-management authority that failed during a predictable seasonal drought because it had ignored prior hydrological modeling and refused to revise its allocation rules. In each application, show exactly where the procedure lands, flag the judgment calls that are disguised as procedural steps, and explicitly state what the procedure cannot resolve—particularly where normative questions about acceptable risk or distributive fairness override the Machiavellian descriptive framework. Be honest about misfires: identify at least one type of case where the framework will systematically misclassify failure, and explain why that misclassification is structurally unavoidable.
