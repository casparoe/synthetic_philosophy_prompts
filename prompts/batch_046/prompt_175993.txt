Most discussions of Anscombe's critique assume a direct-agent model: Truman chooses, Truman intends, Truman directs the killing of civilians as a means to Japanese surrender. Examine whether her concept of directing violence and her sharp distinction between intended effects and merely foreseen side effects can survive the bureaucratic and technological mediation of the Manhattan Project—where the president's order travels through a chain of military command, scientific design, and physical deployment that separates the authorizing agent from the causal mechanism by multiple institutional layers.

What does her doctrine of double effect say about an action in which civilian deaths are not an accidental by-product but a conditionally necessary feature of the weapon's use? How does her category of intention—narrowly defined as what is chosen either as an end or as a means—map onto Truman's stated objective of forcing rapid Japanese capitulation?
