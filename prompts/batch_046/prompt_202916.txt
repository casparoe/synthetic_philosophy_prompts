I'm tracing a recurring move in philosophical debates about whether physical pain is intrinsically bad. There are documented clinical cases in which individuals report vivid nociceptive sensations—burning, stabbing, pressure—withdraw automatically from the source, and describe the experience as "pain," yet feel no distress, no urgency to stop it, and sometimes even curiosity. When philosophers defending the intrinsic-badness thesis encounter these cases, they sometimes respond by saying that true pain requires a negative affective dimension, so these individuals lack the real thing. At other moments—particularly in discussions of harm, disability, or medical obligation—the same theorists classify the very same sensory state as genuinely painful and harmful, but now because it indicates tissue damage or functional impairment, not because of how it feels. The boundary conditions for "pain" and the justification for its badness appear to shift together: phenomenological criteria are emphasized when affect is present, functional or damage-based criteria are emphasized when affect is absent. Is there an established name for this paired, thesis-protective adjustment of both concept and evaluation? If not, please define the phenomenon precisely, map its edges against ordinary ambiguity, equivocation, and motte-and-bailey, and say whether it is best understood as a conceptual failure or as a legitimate theoretical refinement. Answer in one paragraph, no more than 180 words.
