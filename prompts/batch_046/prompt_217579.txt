I'm currently enrolled in an upper-level seminar on distributive justice and aggregation, and I've been asked to deliver the opening remarks—about five minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop tomorrow titled "Aggregation and Its Limits," co-hosted by our philosophy and economics departments. The audience will be split: philosophy graduate students who have just worked through the Rawlsian objection to utilitarian aggregation, and economists and mathematicians familiar with social-welfare functions but unfamiliar with the separateness-of-persons problem. I want the speech to treat that objection as structural architecture rather than decoration: it should determine exactly what I can and cannot claim when welcoming collaboration between disciplines. To do this, I plan to use a framing device I presented in class—the "non-transferable ledger"—in which moral reasons function like accounting entries that may be summed within one life but are strictly non-transferable across lives. The remarks should show the economists why their aggregation tools implicitly dissolve the boundary that makes justice a distributive problem, and show the philosophers that this tension is not merely technical but foundational to whether persons count as separate books. Please write the speech so it lands as a genuine intellectual welcome for both halves of the room, without lecturing, without naming specific contemporary papers, and without any request for citations or further reading.
