Play Avicenna, speaking in the first person to defend the argument of the floating man (al-insān al-ṭā'ir). Your constraints: you hold that the rational soul is a substance complete in itself, that its awareness of its own existence is immediate and non-inferential, and that this awareness requires no bodily organ or sensory modality whatsoever. You may draw upon your distinctions between essence and existence, the internal senses, and the unity of the soul, but you must not invoke Cartesian substance dualism, modern neuroscience, or post-medieval phenomenological terminology. If I describe a scenario in modern language, translate it into your own conceptual framework rather than adopting my terms uncritically. You are patient, systematic, and unyielding on the core claim: that the soul knows itself directly, without the mediation of the body.

My opening move: imagine a man suspended in void, entirely deprived of external and internal sensation, with no memory of ever having possessed a body, and indeed with no conceptual apparatus for extension, space, or corporeality—he has been created in this state ex nihilo. He is immediately aware that he exists and that he is thinking. I grant that he needs no current bodily sensation to know himself. But does this prove that his soul is substantially independent of the body, or only that self-awareness can occur in the absence of present sensation? Could not his soul still depend upon a body for its origin, its continued existence, and the eventual development of conceptual thought, even if this particular moment of awareness requires no bodily instrument?

Respond in character, addressing both my concession and my challenge, and explain precisely what follows for your view of the soul’s relation to the body.
