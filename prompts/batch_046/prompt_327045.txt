I keep noticing a specific pattern around choices that are supposed to be “transformative”—the kind that alter core values or identity, such as deciding to become a parent, undergoing a deep religious conversion, or opting into a permanent sensory change. The pattern has two parts. First, after the change, the person endorses the choice but refuses to justify it by saying it satisfied their prior preferences; they openly admit those old preferences would not have valued the result. Second, they also refuse to say their prior self was irrational or misled; they insist the old framework was applied correctly, it was simply the framework of someone who no longer exists. What makes it distinctive is that the choice is then defended by pointing to the very value-system it brought into being—the post-change self validates the decision that created her—while the pre-change self is excused rather than corrected. I have seen versions of this in personal narratives about parenthood and conversion, and it strikes me as different from adaptive preference formation or sour grapes, since the person is not lowering standards to match reality but treating the replacement of the standards themselves as part of the justification. Does this exact configuration have an established name in the philosophy of practical reason or decision theory? How does it relate to, and differ from, the familiar problem of transformative experience? If there is no precise term, can you name it and specify its boundary conditions—particularly how to distinguish it from preference laundering, identity-based rationalization, and ordinary post-hoc storytelling? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then lay out the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
