I just came out of a long argument with a close friend about Sartre’s thesis that human reality is radically free—lacking any pre-given essence or nature—and that bad faith is the structured self-deception by which we flee that freedom, treating ourselves instead as fixed entities despite being beings for whom existence precedes essence. My friend works in cognitive neuroscience and thinks in terms of predictive processing, active inference, hierarchical generative models, precision-weighted prediction errors, and policy selection under expected free energy; I understand that vocabulary well enough to follow technical papers but I am not a specialist. I want you to restate Sartre’s position—specifically his account of radical freedom, the structure of bad faith (including its reliance on the split between facticity and transcendence, and the role of the Other’s gaze in fixing one’s self-image), and the normative claim that we are responsible for what we make of ourselves—entirely within that framework. Do not smuggle in existentialist commitments by relabeling them; if a concept has no counterpart in active inference, say so. Then provide the residue report: for each major element, state whether it translates cleanly, requires distortion to fit (and what that distortion costs), or has no expression at all. In particular, address whether nothingness, the for-itself’s self-negation, or the normative pressure of authenticity can be reconstructed as features of a hierarchical model optimizing expected free energy, or whether they vanish. Finally, judge whether the residue indicates that predictive processing is missing a genuine phenomenon Sartre identified—perhaps a form of normative self-constitution that is not merely instrumental—or whether it shows that Sartre’s descriptions, once stripped of their phenomenological vocabulary, reduce to claims about model uncertainty and policy selection that are already well captured, suggesting our argument was largely about terminology rather than substance.
