I'm trying to pin down Berkeley's actual view on money vs real wealth, going by my gut on cases. A gold coin stamped by the crown: just a token, not wealth itself. A banknote backed by land taxes: still a token, but anchored to something real. A crypto token with no sovereign or labor link: pure fiction, yet I hesitate to call it not-money at all. A time-bank voucher tracking actual work hours: that feels closer to real value, not just a sign. A patent on medicine that lets someone extract rent: not wealth, just a legal fence. Merchant credit for goods actually in transit: an acceptable token because tied to real exchange. So maybe the principle is that value-signs are legit only when anchored to present labor or tangable goods — that gets cases one, two, four, five, and six, but crypto breaks it, since I still think it's money-ish. Is there a cleaner principle that saves every verdict, or should I drop the crypto intuition? And under the best version, what about a central-bank digital currency that's fully tracked but not convertable to gold or goods?
