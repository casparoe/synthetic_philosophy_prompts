I spent the better part of last evening arguing with a close friend about the symbol grounding problem, and we kept circling the same point without resolving it. She takes the standard line that the core difficulty is linking arbitrary syntactic tokens to sensory or motor patterns—so once a system reliably correlates the token “red” with a specific wavelength distribution across its sensors, the symbol is grounded. I kept pushing that this seems adequate for observational predicates but leaves untouched a different class of expressions: modal and counterfactual terms like “possible,” “avoidable,” or “would have occurred,” whose content is not a present sensory state but a structured relation among unrealized trajectories in the agent’s own action-space and predictive model. Could you give me an example of a conceptual issue that arises specifically for this kind of term—one that shows why correlating a symbol with current sensory input, or even with a single counterfactual simulation, fails to capture what makes the symbol genuinely meaningful rather than merely causally coupled?

I want the example to sit in the weeds: not the generic “Chinese room” or “system reply” objection, but a precise tension between causal tracking and normative evaluation of predictive error in an embodied architecture—perhaps involving how an agent distinguishes a failed prediction that reveals an external world-state from one that reveals a limitation in its own generative model, and why that distinction matters for grounding a term that refers not to what is but to what could have been. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of language and cognitive scientists who work on embodied cognition and predictive processing, and do not present a contested interpretation—for instance, that predictive processing automatically solves the grounding problem for modal vocabulary—as if it were the consensus view.
