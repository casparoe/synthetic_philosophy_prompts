I've been thinking about that old argument between William James and W.K. Clifford about wether it's ever okay to believe something when you don't really have proof. I ended up siding pretty hard with Clifford, but not exactly for the usual reasons people cite. My own spin is that belief isn't just a mental state—it's more like making a promise to your future self that this claim is going to stay in your reasoning. So when James talks about forced, live, and momentous options where we can choose belief without full evidence, I think he's missing the hidden cost: every belief you hold without enough support becomes a kind of cognitive debt that quietly shapes your other judgments. You're basically mortgaging your future reasoning, and even if the belief feels harmless—like about God or free will or whatever—the interest payments show up in smaller, unrelated choices later. So my position is that Clifford was right, but for this debt-reason rather than some abstract duty to truth: it's never ethically okay to believe on insufficent evidence because belief always locks in hidden commitments you can't fully control.

What would actually count against this? I want the real defeaters, not just "Clifford said X." Are there cases where holding an unproven belief genuinely doesn't mess up your reasoning elsewhere—maybe in art, or love, or when you're just trying to survive a crisis? Or is my cognitive debt thing just a fancy restatement of Clifford with extra steps? I'd like you to sort the counterarguments from "this kills the view" to "merely awkward," including whether James's forced-option cases really do break the rule without causing the harm I predict. And be honest: if "distortion in future reasoning" is too vague to actually test, does that mean my view is insulated in a bad way? What would Clifford's defenders themselves admit counts against them, versus what they'd just dismiss? I don't need citations or a reading list—just the arguments and cases that should actually move me.
