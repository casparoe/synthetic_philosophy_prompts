I'm noticing a pattern in how organizations value epistemic resources that seems related to Plato's Meno problem, but I don't know whether it has a specific name. Consider two institutional databases: one lists predictions that have proven correct, with no record of the reasoning behind them; the other lists the same predictions paired with the underlying reasoning, so users can adapt them when background conditions shift. Even when both are equally accurate, the second is treated as far more valuable—not merely because it justifies the predictions, but because its structure allows intelligent modification under counterfactual changes, while the first is seen as brittle. The usual philosophical answers to why knowledge exceeds true belief appeal to justification, reliability, or anti-luck conditions; none seem to capture this specific advantage of structural adaptability across varying contexts. Is there an established concept for this value gap—knowledge's superiority lying specifically in its modular adaptability rather than in its justification or truth-tracking? If so, how does it relate to standard Meno responses? If not, can you define the phenomenon precisely, distinguishing it from reliabilism, virtue epistemology, and justification-based accounts?
