My friend and I have been arguing about introspection for hours, and he walked me into a corner I cannot find the exit from. He distinguishes “thin” introspection—the direct, non-conceptual registration of occurrent qualitative states—from “thick” introspection—the concept-laden attribution of propositional attitudes, emotional kinds, and dispositional traits. Then he runs this chain:

(1) Thin introspection is self-certifying: if I directly register a qualitative state, there is no appearance-reality gap for me.
(2) Thick introspection is mediated by concepts that can misfire, so it is fallible.
(3) The boundary between thin and thick is not sharp: identifying an occurrent state as, say, resentment rather than anger requires applying a concept to a qualitative feel.
(4) So either we maintain a sharp boundary and concede that we never reliably identify specific emotional or intentional kinds (only raw, unclassified feels), or we admit that conceptual framing permeates even apparently direct registrations.
(5) The first option is implausible: we clearly do distinguish resentment from anger with reasonable reliability.
(6) Therefore conceptual mediation infects all introspective access.
(7) If conceptual mediation infects all introspective access, then even reports of pain or color experience are concept-dependent and fallible.
(8) But if those reports are fallible, we lose the evidential base for (1), which claimed thin introspection is self-certifying.
(9) Hence introspection is globally unreliable, including the very judgments that were supposed to ground its local reliability.

He says this shows that any defense of introspective authority collapses into either implausible fragmentation or self-undermining global skepticism. I want to keep (1) and (5). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the standard views—direct-acquaintance, inner-sense, and expressivist—about introspective knowledge?
