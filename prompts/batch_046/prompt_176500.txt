I've never studied philosophy formally, but I've been turning over Peter Singer's pond example—the drowning child and the ruined shoes—on my own for a while, and I think there's a specific corner of it that hasn't been settled. Singer asks us to imagine we are physically present, able to wade in and save the child. But what if the situation is mediated: you are watching the child drown through a live video feed with a thirty-second transmission delay, and you know that exactly nine other strangers are watching the same feed simultaneously? Does each viewer still have the full, undivided rescue obligation, or does awareness split into some kind of shared or diluted duty? More precisely, is there a morally relevant distinction between direct perceptual presence and distributed technological awareness, and if so, does it change the obligation from an individual act-duty into a collective or institutional one?

The answers I've encountered—Singer's extension to global poverty, the standard objections about personal projects, effectiveness, and distance—treat all non-proximate suffering as essentially uniform. They don't examine whether the mechanism of awareness itself (singular direct sight versus mediated shared viewing with temporal delay) introduces a new structural feature that should alter the moral calculus. So the question remains genuinely open.

I'm proposing a focused three-month philosophical project, not an empirical study. Rather than surveying how people actually respond to charity appeals, I want to do conceptual analysis: identify exactly which features of the original pond case generate the rescue obligation—immediacy, uniqueness of agency, absence of institutional mediation, and singular awareness—and test systematically whether each feature survives translation into the mediated, shared-observer scenario. The obvious alternative would be behavioral research or data collection on donation patterns, but that only describes behavior; it doesn't settle what the obligation actually is. A philosophical approach can map the logical structure without needing statistical samples.

The deliverable would be a clear decision framework that distinguishes singular rescue obligations from distributed mediated obligations, applied to two worked cases: the shared live-feed scenario described above, and an algorithmic-notification scenario where thousands receive an automated alert. The framework should specify exactly which premises drive each conclusion, so that if a reader rejects one premise, they can see precisely where the argument breaks. A successful result would show that either (a) mediated shared awareness creates a genuinely different moral category with its own conditions, or (b) any attempt to draw that line collapses back into Singer's original universal claim or relies on technologically arbitrary distinctions. Failure would be an incoherent framework that cannot consistently classify the worked cases or that secretly smuggles in empirical assumptions about human psychology.
