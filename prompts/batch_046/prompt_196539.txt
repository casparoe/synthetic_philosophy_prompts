I am a doctoral student working at the intersection of formal epistemology and decision theory. For decades, the Death in Damascus scenario has served as the canonical battleground between evidential and causal decision theories: an agent learns that Death is surprised to see her in Damascus and will meet her in Aleppo tomorrow; she considers fleeing to Aleppo, only to discover Death waiting there. Evidentialists maintain that remaining is rational because fleeing provides strong evidence that she was destined for Aleppo regardless, while causalists insist that her flight cannot causally influence Death's location and therefore should not be penalized in the decision calculus. More recently, functional decision theory has entered the discussion by treating the agent's decision algorithm itself as part of the logical structure that determines outcomes, introducing logical counterfactuals that embed the agent's reasoning in the world-model. Despite this formalization, the disagreement strikes me as genuinely stalled: the payoff structure is uncontroversial, the counterfactual dependencies are precisely defined, and yet none of the three camps has moved in years. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not really about decision theory at all, but about a prior disagreement over what it means for a rational agent to model her own reasoning process—specifically, whether self-reference should be handled evidentially, causally, or via source-code-level logical counterfactuals, and whether rational autonomy requires treating one's algorithm as transparent to oneself. Is this diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden formal disagreement—perhaps about the admissibility of Newcomb-style correlations in deterministic physical systems, or about the ontology of counterfactual dependence in algorithmically specified worlds—that is actually driving the deadlock? I want an honest diagnosis of the disagreement's structure: is it verbal, is it about which formal models count as admissible data, is it a bedrock intuition about rational agency that neither side can argue for, or is the question itself ill-posed? Given your diagnosis, what would genuinely count as progress—a conceptual distinction that dissolves the self-referential loop, a formal result constraining algorithmic self-modeling, a reframing that separates correlation from causation without collapsing into one camp, or an argument that no resolution should be expected because the sides are answering different questions under a single name?
