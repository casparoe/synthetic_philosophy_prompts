I was recently discussing Bernard Williams's critique of utilitarianism with a colleague who had just read a summary in a philosophy of moral psychology reader. According to her account, Williams's integrity objection holds that utilitarianism forces agents to alienate themselves from their ground projects—the long-term commitments and relationships that give life meaning—by requiring them to treat every choice as an opportunity for impartial welfare maximization. She illustrates this with the well-known rescue case, often referred to as one thought too many, in which a husband saves his drowning wife; Williams allegedly shows that if the husband pauses to think that saving her also maximizes aggregate utility, that second deliberative step reveals a corrupting distance from his love rather than a legitimate moral justification. The summary goes further, claiming that Williams extends this into a broader taxonomy of reasons: integrity-based attachments generate what he calls internal reasons that are structurally incommensurable with the external reasons supplied by any universal moral theory, whether utilitarian or Kantian. Consequently, she concludes, Williams regards integrity not merely as one virtue among others but as a deontic shield that renders an agent's ground projects immune to moral evaluation altogether—so that asking whether one should preserve one's integrity is itself a category mistake, analogous to asking whether one should continue to exist.

I find parts of this compelling, but I worry it smuggles in distinctions and overstatements that do not belong to Williams's actual argument—particularly the claim that integrity reasons form a protected class of internal reasons that block all moral assessment, and the suggestion that the rescue case is meant to condemn any reflective justification rather than a specific kind of calculative intrusion. Could you audit this summary? I would like to know which elements accurately reflect Williams's essays on integrity and ground projects, which parts subtly conflate his critique of utilitarian impartiality with his separate, later work on internal and external reasons, and whether her final conclusion—that integrity makes one's commitments immune to moral scrutiny—actually survives once these corrections are made.
