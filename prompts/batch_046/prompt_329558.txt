I'm trying to settle what I actually think about the identity of indiscernibles, testing my intuitions against a family of modified Black-style scenarios rather than the textbook version alone. Here are my verdicts, case by case.

Case 1: Black's original setup—two homogeneous spheres in an otherwise empty, symmetric universe, separated by two miles and differing in no intrinsic property. I say they are numerically distinct; spatial separation is enough.

Case 2: The same spheres, but now they orbit their common midpoint in that empty space, with no external reference frame and no distinguishable trajectories except relative to each other. I still say two, though I feel the pull to call it one object in motion.

Case 3: The spheres exist in a one-dimensional spatial universe—just a line—with one at position x and one at position y, all other properties identical. Two, clearly.

Case 4: Not spatial but temporal separation: sphere A exists at t₁ with properties P, then nothing exists, then sphere B exists at t₂ with exactly P. Here I switch: I think it's one sphere persisting, not two.

Case 5: A single sphere splits for an instant into two perfect duplicates, then they immediately recombine into one. During the split, I want to say there are two, but I'm unsure whether that requires them to have distinct causal futures.

Case 6: Two spheres in a quantum state with no definite individual positions—only a relational distance is fixed, and they share a single entangled wavefunction with no fact of the matter about which is which. I lean toward saying there is one system described relationally, not two objects.

Case 7: Two macroscopically identical conscious substrates—perfect functional duplicates—running on isolated servers with no causal interaction, each apparently supporting its own stream of awareness. I genuinely can't decide: two minds, or one distributed mind?

Is there a coherent principle behind this pattern—perhaps one that distinguishes spatiotemporal separation from temporal succession, or that makes distinctness depend on the availability of a relational structure? State the principle precisely, say which clause is motivated by which case, and tell me what it predicts for the server-substrate case I'm torn on.
