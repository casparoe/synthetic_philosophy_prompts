I'm preparing the questions for a podcast interview with a philosopher who has recently defended a position she calls "distributed narrative persistence"—a development of Hofstadter's strange-loop account of the self that moves beyond simple pattern isomorphism between brains. She distinguishes "thin persistence," which requires only structural coherence of the loop, from "thick persistence," which demands that every host retain the capacity for emotional valence tied to that root narrative. She further argues that this framework dissolves the branching problem: if a distributed self splits into two diverging host-systems, neither branch is metaphysically privileged as the original, yet both maintain genuine continuity with the root provided their narratives remain interlocked at the base. The audience will be familiar with standard personal-identity debates but not necessarily with recent work on distributed cognition, so the questions should stand on their own without requiring outside reading.

Draft a sequence of six to eight interview questions that avoid asking her to summarize Hofstadter or to defend personal identity in general terms. For each of these four, include a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give—anticipate her standard defensive move and make it harder to deploy. End with a final question asking what kind of conceptual or empirical result could genuinely weaken her position, rather than simply requesting counterexamples.
