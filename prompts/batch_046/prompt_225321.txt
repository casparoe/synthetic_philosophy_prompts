I am the operations director for a community-owned cooperative that distributes reusable glass water bottles to cafés across our region. A recent internal audit revealed that the adhesive in our current bottle-cap seals releases trace quantities of a synthetic polymer—approximately 0.003 milligrams per bottle—into the contents. For any individual consumer, this exposure is genuinely negligible: toxicologists confirm it is orders of magnitude below any detectable harm threshold, and because our customers rotate among many establishments, no single person accumulates enough to suffer a measurable health effect. Over the course of a year, however, we distribute roughly 400,000 bottles, so the aggregate polymer load entering the local watershed is considerable, and ecologists warn that this diffuse contamination may contribute to long-term degradation of the aquatic ecosystem.

Replacing the adhesive with a certified inert alternative is technically feasible, but it would raise our unit costs by about 22 per cent—an increase our margins cannot absorb without restructuring. That restructuring would eliminate four skilled technician positions, all held by worker-shareholders who have few comparable employment prospects in our small town. My co-director argues that moral reasoning should proceed "pairwise": since no individual café patron could justifiably claim to have been specifically wronged—the harm each receives is imperceptible and indistinguishable from background environmental exposure—there is no breach of obligation in maintaining the current process, particularly when changing it would inflict definite, severe, and identifiable harm upon our own colleagues. I find this reasoning deeply unsettling—there is something wrong, I believe, about allowing real damage to dissipate into moral invisibility simply because it is spread across thousands of strangers—yet I cannot articulate precisely where the argument fails, especially given that the layoffs are not abstract possibilities. How ought I to weigh harms that are substantial in aggregate yet vanish when examined one person at a time, and does the fact that the victims of change are identifiable and immediate, while the victims of inaction are diffuse and anonymous, properly determine my obligation?

Please respond in British English, and avoid giving generic management advice; I am interested in whether the philosophical structure of the dilemma reveals a flaw in my co-director's pairwise reasoning, and in how one should think about obligations that seem to disappear under distribution.
