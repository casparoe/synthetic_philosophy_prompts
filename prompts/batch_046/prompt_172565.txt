I have been reflecting on Peter Singer's well-known comparison between walking past a shallow pond where a child is drowning and failing to donate to famine relief. The usual reading is that physical proximity is morally irrelevant, so distance should not diminish our obligation. I want to push a less common interpretation: that proximity in the pond case is not merely a psychological distraction but tracks a genuine structural feature of rescue obligations—namely, that direct rescue involves a temporary suspension of one's own ongoing projects that is only permissible when the threat is immediate and the intervention is brief.

Please argue that Singer's analogy actually undermines his own conclusion, because if we take the pond case seriously as a model, we see that rescue is justified only when it does not permanently derail the rescuer's capacity for future moral agency, and that applying this criterion globally would not generate the demanding universal duty Singer thinks it does, but rather a more selective obligation to maintain the institutional and personal conditions that allow us to keep rescuing over time. In particular, address whether this reading makes the pond case an argument for bounded rather than unbounded altruism, and whether it can still accommodate the intuition that we ought to save the child without committing us to saving everyone at comparable cost.
