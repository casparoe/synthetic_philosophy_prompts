As a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of mind in earnest, I have come to hold a particular diagnosis of the meta-problem of consciousness. I treat phenomenal concepts not as rigid designators of intrinsic, non-physical properties, but as indexical modes of presentation — structurally comparable to local gauge choices in field theory — so that the intuition of an explanatory gap arises not because physicalism omits something real, but because a first-person representational format is being mapped onto a third-person ontology that lacks the corresponding structural role. On this view, explaining why consciousness seems irreducible is analogous to explaining why a particular coordinate system makes a symmetry appear broken: the appearance tracks a feature of the frame, not a deficit in nature. What would count against this position? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be, including whether phenomenal concepts can be shown to be genuinely non-indexical and theory-independent; whether empirical results on the neural correlates of self-reference resist any gauge-style reinterpretation; and whether the specific qualitative character of the hard-problem intuition can be reproduced without invoking indexicality at all. I also want an honest assessment of whether this analogy has insulated the view into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that insulation is a methodological virtue or a fatal defect. Please say explicitly what would change your mind: which argument, empirical finding, or conceptual clarification would lead you to reject the gauge-analogy diagnosis, and where in the reasoning is the weakest link?
