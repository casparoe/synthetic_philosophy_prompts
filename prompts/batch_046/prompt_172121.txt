You will deliver the keynote address at the retirement symposium honoring Dr. Elena Voss, who chaired a philosophy department for three decades and was renowned for conducting doctoral defenses under deliberately adversarial conditions that stripped away the usual protections of politeness and institutional rank. The audience is bifurcated: roughly half are professional philosophers and historians of philosophy who will recognize precise reconstructions of Platonic pedagogy; the other half are university administrators, family members, and former students from non-philosophical fields who will not. The philosophical concept that must do structural work—not mere ornament—is Plato's account in the Laws of the symposion as a civic institution: wine is administered not for pleasure but as a pharmakon that temporarily loosens the rational guardian of shame and social inhibition, thereby revealing whether the non-rational parts of the soul have been properly ordered through prior education. Dr. Voss was famously abstemious, so you must not literalize intoxication; rather, her method was a secular, sober analog—a disciplined pedagogical suspension of ordinary defenses that tested whether a thinker possessed genuine intellectual courage or only its performative shell. The speech, approximately eight minutes and formally composed, must navigate three constraints shaped by this Platonic framework. First, it must honor her rigor without endorsing cruelty, especially because several audience members carry genuine wounds from her severity. Second, it must include one precisely calibrated moment that functions simultaneously for both halves of the room: the philosophers should hear an accurate reconstruction of the Laws' argument that true education is displayed not in the preservation of sobriety but in the quality of one's soul when customary restraints are lowered; the non-philosophers should hear a concrete, moving narrative about how she taught people to be brave by temporarily removing their armor. Third, the address should leave unresolved—but explicitly name—the tension between Plato's claim that such testing reveals a pre-formed character and the modern institutional reality that repeated exposure to adversarial testing can also forge character, a tension you might term the basanos problem. You are not to provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, nor are you to invoke niche contemporary frameworks such as decision-theoretic or consciousness-theoretic apparatus. The tone should be polished, appropriate to a formal academic occasion, and should treat the Platonic idea as a living constraint on what can be said about Dr. Voss rather than as historical decoration.
