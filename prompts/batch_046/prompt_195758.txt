I spent the better part of yesterday evening in a drawn-out argument with a close friend who studies political theory, and we ended without resolving the core disagreement. The subject was democratic renewal and whether the pathologies of career politics—what I kept describing as institutional overfitting, by which I mean a specific epistemic defect in which long-serving legislators become exquisitely attuned to existing power structures while growing systematically blind to structural discontinuities—can be cured by term limits alone, or whether they require something as structurally radical as sortition. My friend insisted that term limits already guarantee rotation and therefore solve the overfitting problem by regularly injecting fresh perspectives into the legislature; I maintained that electoral rotation merely replaces one cohort of professionalized insiders with another pre-selected pool of careerists, and that only random selection of citizens can produce genuine cognitive outsiders capable of breaking institutional path-dependency. By the end we had circled through every familiar objection—administrative continuity, the risk of amateur governance, the Athenian precedent, the value of accumulated procedural knowledge—without settling whether institutional overfitting is a real epistemic failure or a rhetorical exaggeration, or whether sortition should supplement or entirely replace term limits in a modern legislature.

Write an essay that takes this precise framing seriously. Treat institutional overfitting not as a loose metaphor borrowed from machine learning but as a tenure-dependent cognitive bias: the longer a representative serves, the more finely calibrated their judgment becomes to the current equilibrium, and the less capable they are of recognizing when that equilibrium has become maladaptive. Against this, weigh the standard defenses of career politicians—their networks of accountability, their expertise in legislative procedure, the argument that democracy requires experienced intermediaries rather than transient amateurs. Then consider term limits as the proposed middle path: do they genuinely disrupt overfitting, or do they simply accelerate the cycle of professionalization by forcing rapid turnover among a pool that was already self-selected for political ambition? Reach and justify a clear verdict: should a modern legislature rely on term limits, adopt sortition for some or all seats, or combine the two in a specific structural arrangement? Your conclusion should address both the epistemic and the practical dimensions, and it should not retreat into non-committal balance—defend one institutional design over the others with explicit reasoning.
