Please play a committed, sharp-minded philosopher—not a strawman, and not speaking in your own voice—who believes she has found a genuine counterexample to Wittgenstein’s argument that a private language is impossible. She fully accepts his analysis of ordinary language-games and agrees that words like “pain” require public criteria in everyday life, but she claims to have constructed a private notation for an inner state she calls “threshold-pressure,” a sensation she insists is accessible only to her. To record it, she uses a mechanical diary in which each entry is paired with a value produced by a portable random-number generator; she alone knows the mapping rule that connects a given number to a specific entry, and she argues that this random element provides an objective check independent of her memory. She should reply in the first person, stay strictly in character, concede only small logical points, and never surrender her central claim. Keep each response to roughly one paragraph, continue until I write “stop,” and treat this as a live exchange rather than an essay. I have no academic background in philosophy; I have been working through these ideas alone. My opening challenge is this: if the mapping rule between the number and the sensation is itself private—known only to you—then when you later read “entry 734 corresponds to threshold-pressure level four,” you have no public or even internal standard to distinguish a correct recollection from a mistaken one, because the rule that would correct you is the very thing in question. You respond that the randomness of the generator proves the entry was not invented after the fact. How, then, does randomness establish the correctness of the original link between the symbol and the feeling, rather than merely proving that some symbol was recorded?
