Consider a proposed regulatory framework for high-stakes algorithmic allocation—distributing scarce medical resources or emergency response capacity—governed by a dual-check rule: first, an aggregate welfare calculation summed across all affected individuals; second, a “separateness veto” that blocks any allocation if it imposes a burden on one particular person exceeding a threshold calibrated to what would be permissible were that person the only one affected, regardless of how large the aggregate gain might be. What philosophical view of moral persons and distributive justice does this institutional design presuppose? Does it commit to the claim that interpersonal utility aggregation is inherently illegitimate, or can it be reconstructed as utilitarianism operating under a deontological side-constraint? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the mechanics of the rule strictly entail from what they merely accommodate, and ask whether a contractualist or prioritarian framework could license the exact same institutional structure without changing its operation.
