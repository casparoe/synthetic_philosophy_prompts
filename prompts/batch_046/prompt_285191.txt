I sit on our university's Committee on Civic Discourse and Funding, and we have to decide by next Friday whether to approve a $15k grant for a student-led initiative called the Overlapping Consensus Forum. The forum wants to host semester-long debates on live policy questions—right now they're planning sessions on immigration sanctuary policies and on genetic editing for disability prevention—and their charter requires every speaker to frame arguments in terms that don't depend on religious revelation, scripture, or what Rawls calls comprehensive doctrines. The idea is that citizens with different worldviews can still share reasons.

But we've got two objections that split the committee pretty cleanly. One student coalition, mostly religious but not exclusively, says the charter creates an unfair translation burden—speakers have to strip away the actual reasons they care about and talk in a secular idiom that doesn't capture their view. They specifically cite the exclusion problem and also this newer worry about hermeneutical injustice: that forcing someone to rephrase a conviction rooted in, say, imago dei into human dignity doesn't just burden them, it actually changes the content and makes their contribution unrecognizable. The other objection comes from a philosophy grad student and some secular faculty who say the charter is actually too permissive, because it still lets people smuggle in thick metaphysical commitments—Kantian rational nature, deep utilitarian axioms, even secular eschatologies—under the label of public reason, so the filter is arbitrary.

I'm writing the committee's decision memo for Friday's vote. I want it to recommend one concrete course: approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and it needs a time frame if relevant. The memo should treat the hermeneutical-injustice objection seriously rather than just dismissing it as a misunderstanding of democratic legitimacy, but it also shouldn't pretend that anything goes is a stable option for a public university funding body. And I want the strongest version of the losing side to get an honest paragraph—if I recommend funding with a modified charter, I need to say exactly what's lost; if I recommend rejection, I need to say what democratic value we're sacrificing.

Can you write this as a formal memo? Please structure it like this:

1. Executive Summary (about 150 words): bottom line up front, what we're deciding, the key tension.
2. The Case for Approval (about 250 words): why the initiative serves the university's mission, how public reason can work without being oppressive, and how to respond to the hermeneutical-injustice worry.
3. The Case Against / The Strongest Objection (about 250 words): the best argument for rejection or heavy conditioning, including the arbitrariness problem and the risk that translation actually distorts rather than includes.
4. Recommended Course of Action (about 200 words): the concrete recommendation—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—plus any conditions, deadlines, or review mechanisms, and a direct response to the losing side.
5. A Brief Note on Implementation (about 100 words): practical next steps if adopted.

Please keep the reasoning accessible to committee members who aren't philosophy specialists but don't dumb it down—use terms like comprehensive doctrine and hermeneutical injustice correctly but explain them briefly in context. Also, I was discussing this with a friend who teaches political theory and she mentioned something about Habermas's translation proviso being stricter than Rawls's proviso, but I'm not totally sure I understand the differrence; if it's relevant to the recommendation, feel free to bring it in, but don't turn this into a literature review or ask me to check sources. Just write the memo.
