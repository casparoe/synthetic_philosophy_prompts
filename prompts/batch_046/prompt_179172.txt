I've been running this grad unit on distributed consciousness and my students are driving me up the wall. We started with Hofstadter's idea that a self can be partly in another person's brain — like Carol's patterns persisting in Doug — and now we're deadlocked for three weeks. One camp says if the informational patterns are genuinely shared then there are literally overlapping 'I's, not one self in two bodies but fragments of one narrative center spread across skulls. The other side insists that's just sloppy metaphor; there's exactly one self per biological control system, and anything else is influence or memory, not identity. Nobody's budged an inch.

My hunch is that part of the stall is verbal — they mean different things by 'I', one using it for the narrative center and one for the embodied controller — but I also wonder if there's a hidden empirical disagreement about neural integration they're dressing up as metaphysics. To push it, consider this weird case I cooked up: two people with bidirectional cortical interfaces, each able to recieve the other's episodic buffer but with completely seperate phenomenal streams. They both sincerely report "I went to the store yesterday" about an event only one body performed. Is the disagreement here about facts, about what counts as data for selfhood, about standards of explanation, or just bedrock intuitions about embodiment? Diagnose the stall using what each side actually says, not a survey of positions. And tell me honestly: is there anything — a distinction, an experiment, a change of question — that would actually move it, or should we just accept it's ill-posed?
