Since a close relative recently developed a neuropathic dissociation in which tissue-damage signals reach consciousness without triggering any aversive response, I have been trying to determine whether pain's badness resides in its phenomenal quality or in its functional link to suffering. Please construct a rigorously specified double-dissociation thought experiment that addresses this. Your scenario, confined to one paragraph and using only realistic clinical framing, must present two patients: (A) a classic pain-asymbolia case, with intact nociceptive discrimination and truthful verbal reports of pain, yet with complete absence of suffering, negative evaluation, or avoidance motivation; and (B) an inverse case, with severely blunted or absent nociceptive discrimination but with a robust, localized, negative affective state that the patient actively seeks to avoid and describes as this is harming me, elicited by a normally neutral somatic input through conditioning or neuromodulatory mechanisms rather than by tissue damage. Avoid science-fictional interventions, perfect neural control, and precise causal attribution to named subcortical structures. After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it satisfies the design: state the verdict each of two theories—the intrinsic-badness view, which holds that the phenomenal character of pain itself is what makes it bad, and the affective-link view, which holds that pain is bad solely in virtue of its connection to aversive affect or suffering—delivers about each patient, and explain why. Then isolate the structural feature of the dissociation that forces the theories apart, and describe what happens to the argument if that feature is eliminated, for instance if nociception and negative affect are always co-present. Finally, present the strongest defensive reply available to the intrinsic-badness theorist: how she might reinterpret one patient so that the double dissociation collapses. Write for a reader with no web access, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or verbatim quotations from texts.
