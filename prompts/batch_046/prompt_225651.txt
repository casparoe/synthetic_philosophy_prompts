I'm putting together a packet for a reading group on hidden patterns in reasoning, and I was chatting with a friend who said poems never actually prove anything, they just decorate. I want to try proving him wrong. Could you write a sestina — six six-line stanzas plus the usual three-line envoi — about Simpson's paradox, but please skip the classic hospital-recovery example. Instead, imagine three voting districts of very different sizes: in each district, candidate X beats candidate Y by a clear majority, yet when you add up the total votes across all districts, Y actually wins. The six end-words that have to rotate through the stanzas according to the sestina pattern should be: district, vote, majority, reversal, total, hidden. I want the repetition and rotation to do real work — the reader should experiance the regrouping structurally, feeling how the same data lands differently when the framing shifts, rather than just being told about it. It should be accurate enough that someone who teaches stats wouldn't cringe, but don't worry about being technically perfect. Please don't turn it into a sermon about democracy either; keep the tension between local majorities and the aggregate reversal open, without resolving it. At the very end, put a one-sentence summary of the philosophical point about aggregation on its own line starting with 'Summary:'.
