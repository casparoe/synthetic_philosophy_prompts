I am a doctoral student in political theory and historical sociology, and I am trying to refine my intuitions about when a collective identity counts as an invented tradition or an imagined community in the sense developed by Hobsbawm and Anderson, versus when it represents a genuinely continuous social formation. I have been testing my judgments against a range of cases, but they pull in opposite directions, and I want to know whether there is a single principle of invention that can accommodate them all, or whether I must abandon one of my verdicts.

Here are my judgments. First, the Scottish Highland clan-tartan system strikes me as paradigmatically invented: the association of specific tartans with specific clans was largely manufactured by Victorian entrepreneurs and romantic writers, and pre-modern Highland society did not organize identity in this way; yet today it is experienced as immemorial. Second, the modern Japanese state ideology of kokutai and the systematized imperial rituals constructed during the Meiji Restoration feel similarly invented: the imperial institution itself has ancient roots, but the particular narrative of an unbroken, ethnically homogeneous Japanese nation centered on the throne was deliberately assembled in the late nineteenth century to serve modern nation-building. Third, Basque cultural and linguistic identity, by contrast, does not strike me as invented in the same sense: although modern Basque nationalism is a nineteenth- and twentieth-century political project, the underlying language and many social practices show demonstrable continuity that was not fabricated by modern actors, so only the political framing is new. Fourth, the self-identification of the Eastern Roman Empire as Roman from Constantine to 1453—despite the shift from Latin to Greek, from pagan to Christian, and from republican to imperial bureaucracy—feels genuinely continuous rather than invented, because the administrative, legal, and self-referential chain was never deliberately reconstructed after a break. Fifth, the Navajo Nation as a political entity was clearly constituted by the 1868 treaty and the consolidation of previously autonomous bands, so its political form is modern and externally shaped; yet the religious and social practices that fill it are not reconstructed folklore but genuinely ancient, which makes me hesitate to call the whole formation an invented tradition in the Scottish mode.

My tentative principle is that a collective identity is invented when there is both (i) a demonstrable discontinuity in institutional or ritual practice spanning more than one generation, and (ii) deliberate construction by identifiable modern actors for present political purposes, combined with (iii) the absence of a continuous substrate that was already associated with the same collective boundary. This saves the Scottish and Japanese cases, and also distinguishes Basque culture (continuous substrate) from Basque politics (new framing). But it struggles with Byzantium—where the substrate transforms radically without a discontinuity in institutional self-identification—and with the Navajo case, where the political boundary is clearly invented but the cultural substrate is continuous and was already associated with the people who now inhabit that boundary. So either the principle needs a clause about institutional self-reference versus cultural substrate, or I must overturn one of my verdicts.

Can you state a more precise principle, or a small family of candidates, that delivers all five verdicts? For each clause, please indicate which case motivates it. Then apply your best version to two further cases I have not considered: the revival of Cornish language and identity since the early twentieth century, and the construction of a unified Indigenous Australian category in the mid-twentieth century from hundreds of previously distinct language groups. Finally, please be explicit about which of my original verdicts your principle cannot accommodate without revision, and whether you think that revision is more plausible than overturning the verdict itself.
