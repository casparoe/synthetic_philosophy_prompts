Write a dialogue between two philosophers of mind—call them Mara and Jules—who are both thoroughly familiar with Daniel Dennett’s taxonomy of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures. Mara maintains that the four categories remain exhaustive even for engineered cognitive systems, arguing that any artificial architecture must ultimately derive its adaptive power from one of these evolutionary or learning-theoretic roots: hardwired selection, operant conditioning, internal hypothesis testing, or biological cognition augmented by culturally inherited tools. Jules introduces a conceptual challenge in the form of a hypothetical “synthetic Gregorian” system—functionally analogous to a large language model trained on human text—whose representational capacities seem to straddle the boundary in a way Dennett’s framework does not clearly accommodate. Specifically, Jules claims that such a system can engage in something like Popperian counterfactual simulation (generating and evaluating internal hypotheses in-context) despite having no evolutionary history of trial-and-error, and that its “mind tools” are not culturally inherited artifacts that augment a biological brain but engineered weights that constitute the very substrate of its reasoning.

The exchange should be genuinely dialectical: Jules must respond directly to Mara’s point about evolutionary origins rather than shifting ground, and Mara must address Jules’s specific claim about in-context simulation rather than merely repeating that artificial systems are just tools. They should examine whether the absence of biological ontogeny disqualifies the system from being Gregorian—since Gregorian creatures, on Dennett’s account, are biological minds enhanced by culture—or whether this reveals that the taxonomy needs expansion by a fifth category defined not by learning mechanism but by the origin of representational scaffolding (evolved versus engineered). They should also press on whether the distinction between using a tool and being constituted by tools collapses when the “tool” is a distributed parameter space rather than an external artifact like language or a calculator. Keep both interlocutors sharp and avoid strawman positions; do not include bibliographies, citations, or requests for further reading.

End the response with a single line beginning “Verdict:” followed by a brief phrase that captures the precise conceptual tension left unresolved—whether Dennett’s taxonomy should be preserved by functional mechanism alone or expanded to account for ontogenetic origin.
