Write one exam question on Buridan’s ass that students cannot answer by reciting the standard paradox and cataloguing familiar resolutions—adding a tie-breaker, randomizing, denying perfect equality, or invoking a decision-theoretic rule. The question should ask whether rational choice in such a case necessarily requires a prior, however minimal, asymmetry in reasons—perhaps one constructed post hoc—or whether an agent can rationally make a genuinely preference-free, structurally arbitrary selection, provided she endorses the selection mechanism in advance.

Please provide:
1. The exam question itself, calibrated for a 90-minute written response, with explicit instructions that the answer must take a definite, committed position—either defending the claim that rational choice necessarily presupposes some minimal reason-asymmetry, or defending the claim that endorsed arbitrariness is fully compatible with rationality—rather than surveying both sides and leaving the verdict open.
2. A model answer of roughly 600 words that adopts one of those positions consistently, advancing reasons and addressing objections instead of listing options neutrally.
3. A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with marks assigned to each (total 20), and for each point a one-line note on the specific mistake or omission that typically costs candidates marks on that item.

Keep the tone formal and polished, do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid relying on obscure texts or specific attributions that a model without search access could not be expected to recall accurately.
