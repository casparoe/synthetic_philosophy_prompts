I was in a study group where two friends—Jordan, who favors endurantism, and Riley, who defends perdurantism—got stuck arguing about whether a person or object that changes is literally the same thing over time or just a series of related parts. Their exchange went as follows.

Jordan began by saying that endurantism respects our practical intuition: the agent who made yesterday's promise is literally the same person who must keep it today, not a distinct temporal slice. Perdurantism, he argued, turns moral responsibility into a relation between different entities—yesterday's stage and today's stage—so it undermines the idea that I am genuinely the same subject of obligations. Riley replied that endurantism cannot solve the problem of temporary intrinsics: if Jordan is wholly present now (sitting) and wholly present an hour later (standing), he seems to possess contradictory properties. Jordan countered that this is handled by time-indexing—Jordan is sitting-at-t1—which assigns relational properties to one enduring object rather than smuggling in temporal parts. Riley said that indexing looks ad hoc and that perdurantism explains variation naturally, just as different spatial parts of a table have different colors.

Jordan then pressed an interruption case: a clock is disassembled, its parts sit in a crate for ten years, then are reassembled. He claimed endurantism can accommodate scattered endurance or appeal to causal continuity of the parts, whereas perdurantism must treat the clock as a four-dimensional worm with a ten-year gap. Riley conceded that gaps are unproblematic but offered a sharper variant: if the crate is destroyed and an atom-for-atom replica is built elsewhere with no causal link to the original parts, endurantism risks identifying the replica with the original on purely functional grounds, which seems wrong; perdurantism correctly denies identity because there is no spatiotemporal continuity of the four-dimensional object. Jordan retorted that on the perdurantist view genuine change is illusory—each temporal part is static, like frames in a film—so nothing ever actually changes; he also noted that perdurantism cannot rule out a cosmic-coincidence duplicate that happens to be spatiotemporally adjacent to the gap. Riley answered that spatial variation across parts is not contradictory either, and that appropriate causal relations between successive stages can block duplicate cases without appealing to a mysterious enduring substrate.
