I am drafting a chapter on evidentialist and voluntarist belief formation and have built an argument that seems to collapse into licensing arbitrary wishful belief, yet I want to preserve James's core insight if possible. I need to locate the precise misstep—whether an equivocation, a scope shift, a hidden modal slide, or a misapplication of conditions—rather than receiving a general warning that James is too permissive.

(1) Clifford's evidentialist principle holds that it is wrong to form any belief without sufficient evidence.

(2) Any belief that functions as a necessary precondition for the very possibility of gathering and assessing evidence is either justified by prior evidence, which is circular, or must be suspended under (1).

(3) A central instance is the belief that my own cognitive faculties will remain generally reliable in the immediate future; without this commitment, I have no basis for trusting any present evidential assessment.

(4) Therefore Clifford must either suspend judgment about his future reliability—thereby paralyzing evidential practice—or violate his own principle by accepting it without prior proof.

(5) James permits belief when an option is forced, living, and momentous, and when the evidence is necessarily absent.

(6) The belief in one's future cognitive reliability is forced (reliance is unavoidable), living (it is a psychologically real option), and momentous (it determines whether inquiry can continue at all).

(7) So James's exception applies here, and Clifford's universal prohibition fails.

(8) Now apply James's three conditions to a belief that feels equally forced, living, and momentous to the believer—say, that a specific intercessory practice causally affects outcomes—where direct empirical evidence is unavailable by design.

(9) James's framework then appears to license any doctrine that a subject experiences as forced, living, and momentous, erasing the boundary between responsible belief and mere wish-fulfillment.

(10) Hence either Clifford's principle is false, or James's exception is narrower than (5)-(7) suggest, or there is an unstated constraint in (6) or (8) that blocks the extension.

Where exactly does the argument go wrong? I am inclined to keep (1) as a regulative ideal and to defend (6) as a legitimate exception, but I want to reject the slide to (9) without smuggling in a disguised evidential requirement. Is the error in (2), treating preconditions of inquiry as ordinary beliefs subject to evidential audit? Or is there an ambiguity in "forced" or "momentous" between (6) and (8) that, once resolved, allows (6) to stand while (8) falls? Please identify the step, explain why it initially looked plausible, and specify what accepting or rejecting it costs for the broader James-Clifford debate.
