I have recently become personally invested in the philosophy of pain after a close friend was diagnosed with a rare dissociative condition in which she reports intact nociceptive awareness—she can precisely localize and grade thermal and mechanical stimuli—yet describes the sensations as neutral or even aesthetically interesting, and she has begun deliberately seeking out experiences that clinicians classify as noxious, all without any evidence of self-harm intent or psychiatric comorbidity. This clinical picture resembles documented cases of pain asymbolia, but with an added feature I want to treat as philosophically central rather than incidental: the absence of negative affect is coupled with an active, rational preference for the stimuli. I am writing a paper for a seminar on philosophy of mind and want to use this profile to test competing accounts of why pain is bad.

Please construct a thought experiment built precisely around this clinical profile: a realistic hospital or rehabilitation setting (not science fiction, no hypothetical drugs, no brain-stimulation devices), presented in a single paragraph. The case should be engineered so that a representationalist about pain—who holds that pain is intrinsically bad in virtue of representing actual or potential tissue damage—delivers a different verdict from an affective-evaluativist—who holds that pain is bad solely in virtue of its intrinsic unpleasantness or negative motivational valence—regarding whether the patient is genuinely harmed by the stimuli she seeks out. After presenting the scenario, walk through what each theory says and why, identify the exact feature of the case that forces the divergence (and explain what happens to the divergence if that feature is removed), and include an honest note on how a defender of the theory that comes out badly—likely the representationalist—would attack the coherence or clinical possibility of the profile. The point is not to stipulate the verdicts but to show that they fall out of the theories once the scenario is accepted. Keep the tone formal and suitable for a philosophy seminar, and do not include requests for citations, bibliographies, or references to specific authors beyond the two theoretical positions named.
