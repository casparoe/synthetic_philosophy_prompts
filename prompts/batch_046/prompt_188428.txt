As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have come to defend a particular synthesis: the coherent extrapolated volition framework, interpreted through an idealized-preference reconstruction that filters out adaptive preferences and cognitive distortions, offers a genuinely non-paternalistic ground for collective normative choice. Rather than aggregating what people actually want now—which is contaminated by ignorance, status-quo bias, and adaptive adjustment to unjust conditions—we ask what affected agents would converge upon if they possessed complete information, stable identity conditions, and maximally coherent reasoning, then extrapolate that reconstructed volition across all parties. I believe this avoids both the arbitrariness of deontological rights-absolutism and the tyranny of simple preference aggregation.

I want an honest stress-test, not a survey. Take a committed position: do not merely list objections and leave me to weigh them. Tell me precisely what would have to be true for this synthesis to fail—whether formal results showing that coherence is impossible under pluralism, empirical cases where filtering "adaptive" preferences strips away values essential to human identity, or conceptual demonstrations that the extrapolation procedure is circular. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Say which ones defenders of the view would themselves concede as genuinely damaging, and which they could dismiss without dishonesty. Finally, address whether the framework has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if coherence can always be postponed to a further level of idealization—and tell me plainly whether that insulation is a virtue for a normative theory or a sign that it risks explaining everything and therefore nothing.
