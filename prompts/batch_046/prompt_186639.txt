I'm preparing a seminar for cognitive-science graduate students who work on predictive processing but have not read Dennett. I want them to understand real patterns and the three stances—physical, design, and intentional—not as a scale of explanatory zoom but as a thesis about predictive leverage: a pattern is real when it supports predictions that no lower-level description can match.

The students will arrive with two prejudices: that levels of explanation just mean finer or coarser grain, and that attributing intentions to artifacts is always anthropomorphism. Plan the session's explanatory arc. First, specify what idea must be in place before the stances can be introduced, and why the ordering is not merely one of increasing complexity. Second, propose the single carrying example that should run through the session; I am considering a large language model generating prose, but tell me whether that example has the right properties—specifically, whether its design stance is too transparent and whether its physical stance is too opaque—and if it fails, substitute an alternative that avoids both pitfalls. Third, map the misconceptions they will form in the order they are likely to arise: I expect first that the intentional stance is merely a useful fiction, second that real patterns are observer-dependent conveniences, and third that the design stance collapses into reverse engineering. For each, state exactly where in the sequence it should be headed off. Fourth, design one end-of-session question that distinguishes someone who grasps the ontology of real patterns from someone who has only memorized the three labels. Fifth, state explicitly what should be left out and acknowledged as beyond the session's scope—for example, whether mental states are literally real patterns in Dennett's strong sense.
