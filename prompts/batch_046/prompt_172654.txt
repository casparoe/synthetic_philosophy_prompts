The Twin Earth argument—Putnam’s case that natural-kind terms have meanings partly fixed by environmental factors, and the internalist reply that Oscar and Twin-Oscar share identical mental representations—has been rehearsed for decades without either side conceding ground. My working theory is that the impasse is not primarily about whether the term denotes H2O or XYZ, but about whether cognitive explanation must individuate mental states strictly by head-internal properties or may legitimately invoke social deference and causal history. Is this disagreement verbal—turning on different uses of “meaning”—substantive—about the ontology of mental representation—or a case of two research programs, one semantic and one cognitive-scientific, running under a single heading? Press further: both sides treat the division of linguistic labor as either a semantic mechanism or a pragmatic overlay, yet neither treats expert deference as a normative-epistemic constraint on concept possession itself, a framing that destabilizes the internal/external boundary from the outset. Diagnose the stall using what each camp actually says about representation, deference, and explanatory individuation. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress—a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding about concept acquisition, or a formal result in cognitive science—or whether the question is ill-posed because “in the head” was never a well-defined criterion for mental content to begin with.
