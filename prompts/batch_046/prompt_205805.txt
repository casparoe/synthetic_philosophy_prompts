I'm working through a problem in my philosophy of science seminar on idealization, and I've constructed an argument about Friedman's defense of unrealistic assumptions that seems to show his view either collapses entirely or becomes vacuously permissive. I think there's a hidden slide—perhaps an equivocation about representational roles, or a modal shift in how counterfactual independence works—but I can't name the exact step. Here is the argument:

(1) Call an assumption in a model "representational" if it is meant to describe an actual feature of the target system, and "instrumental" if it serves only mathematical tractability and is not meant to describe anything real.
(2) Friedman treats unrealistic economic assumptions—perfect competition, fully rational agents, infinite divisibility—as instrumental: they are literally false, but that does not matter because the model's predictions are accurate.
(3) Now consider a criterion for distinguishing the two kinds: an assumption is genuinely instrumental only if the model's predictive success is counterfactually independent of that specific falsehood—that is, if substituting a different false assumption serving the same formal role would leave the predictions unchanged.
(4) Apply this to climate models, which discretize the continuous atmosphere into grid cells. Replacing 100-kilometer cells with hexagonal cells of twice the size shifts the temperature predictions measurably; so the grid structure is not counterfactually independent, hence it is representational, not instrumental.
(5) Apply the same criterion to Friedman's economic rationality assumption. Replacing perfect rationality with various forms of bounded rationality changes price-prediction outcomes in many market structures; so rationality is also not counterfactually independent, hence it too is representational.
(6) Therefore, by criterion (3), Friedman's own flagship examples of "instrumental" unrealistic assumptions are actually representational.
(7) But if they are representational and false, then Friedman's claim that their falsity does not matter is wrong: false representational claims undermine a model's credibility.
(8) The only way to save Friedman's view is to reject criterion (3).
(9) But without (3), there is no principled distinction between a harmless simplifying fiction and a false core mechanism; any unrealistic assumption, however central, can be immunized by declaring it "instrumental."
(10) So either Friedman's defense collapses—because his examples turn out to be false representational claims—or it becomes vacuous, licensing astrology, phrenology, or any pseudoscience that happens to yield a correct correlation.

This seems to prove far too much, because I don't want to say that climate grid assumptions are epistemically on a par with perfect rationality, nor do I want to open the door to immunizing every false theory. I would most like to keep (2) and (4), since I think Friedman really does treat economic assumptions as non-descriptive and climate discretization really is a simplifying tool. Which step should I reject—probably (3), or perhaps (5)—and what does rejecting it cost me? If the flaw is not in a single premise but in a slide between them, please name the slide and explain why it looked legitimate at first.
