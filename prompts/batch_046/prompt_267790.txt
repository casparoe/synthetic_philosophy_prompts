As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun working through philosophy of mind in any systematic way, I have been studying von Neumann’s comparison of neural tissue with computing machinery—especially his arguments about reliability in noisy components, his sketch of self-reproducing automata, and his framing of the brain as a hybrid analog-digital system. I find myself with a cluster of intuitions about which brain-computer analogies are genuinely illuminating and which are misleading, but I cannot tell whether there is a single principle behind them or whether I am simply inconsistent. I want to know what coherent account, if any, captures the pattern, which of my verdicts it must sacrifice, and what it predicts for a case I have not yet considered.

Here are my verdicts, case by case.

First, the brain’s capacity to preserve cognitive function after localized tissue loss, through redundant pathways and distributed representation, strikes me as genuinely analogous to the error-correcting codes von Neumann designed for unreliable hardware: both achieve robustness by encoding information across redundant elements rather than isolating it. I judge this analogy sound and bidirectional.

Second, I think the continuous, graded nature of synaptic transmission—where signal strength varies with neurotransmitter concentration and receptor occupancy—is fundamentally different from the discrete on-off switching of digital logic gates. Despite von Neumann’s own tendency to describe neural firing in binary terms, I take this analog-digital distinction to be a real failure point for the comparison, not merely a difference in implementation.

Third, the brain’s energy budget—on the order of twenty watts—versus the megawatt demands of modern supercomputers performing comparable pattern-recognition tasks suggests to me that neural computation exploits physical principles, perhaps at the molecular or analog-parallel level, that make direct architectural comparison with silicon machinery profoundly misleading. This is not a quantitative gap but a qualitative one about the kind of physical process involved.

Fourth, von Neumann’s concept of a self-reproducing automaton, which assembles copies of itself from raw materials in its environment, seems structurally closer to biological development and cellular reproduction than to anything the brain actually does. Applying this concept to neural self-repair or plasticity feels like stretching the analogy past its breaking point, even if the automaton itself is a brilliant theoretical object.

Fifth—and this is the case I keep revisiting—the stochastic release of neurotransmitters introduces a form of “noise” into neural signaling. At a descriptive level this resembles the thermal noise in vacuum tubes that von Neumann discussed as an engineering obstacle. But functionally, neural noise appears to be exploited for probabilistic computation, exploration in learning, and stochastic resonance, whereas von Neumann treated hardware noise as purely a defect to be corrected. So the analogy holds descriptively but reverses functionally; I am unsure whether that makes it illuminating or deceptive.

Sixth, comparing glial cells— which regulate synaptic transmission, maintain ionic balance, and participate in repair—to clock-synchronization or bus-control mechanisms in digital computers strikes me as a category mistake. Glia do not merely support computation in the way that a clock supports a processor; they actively modulate information processing in ways that have no clear computational counterpart. This analogy should be rejected outright.

My tentative thought is that the distinction turns on whether an analogy respects the direction of explanation: analogies work when they allow us to understand the brain through computing concepts without suppressing the physical and biological specifics that do the explanatory work, and they fail when they treat those specifics as merely noisy implementations of an abstract algorithm. But this principle feels ad hoc, and I am not sure it saves all six verdicts without contortion.

So I ask: is there a more precise principle—perhaps about levels of abstraction, about functional versus structural correspondence, or about whether the comparison preserves the causal-explanatory role of the underlying physics—that captures my pattern of judgments? State it precisely, indicate which clause of the principle is motivated by each of the six cases above, and be explicit about which verdict, if any, must be overturned to maintain coherence. Then apply the resulting account to a case I have not listed: is comparing the brain’s use of sleep for memory consolidation and synaptic downscaling to a computer’s defragmentation or garbage-collection routines a valid analogy by your principle, or does it fall into the same failure mode as the glial-cell comparison? If there is no single principle and instead two or more competing accounts each save a different subset of my intuitions, present them, show which verdict each preserves and which it sacrifices, and tell me whether the disagreement between them is substantive or merely verbal.
