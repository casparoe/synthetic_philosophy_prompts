Question: Explain Kant's doctrine of indirect duties regarding animals, particularly his claim that cruelty is wrong not because animals are ends in themselves but because it damages the agent's moral sensibility. Then apply this framework to two scenarios: (a) a farmer who uses draft animals for plowing, providing adequate rest and food, and (b) an automated precision-agriculture system that occasionally injures livestock through sensor malfunction, with no human operator present during the incident. Does Kant's view allow a moral distinction between these cases? In answering, be precise about whether the wrongness derives solely from damage to human moral character or whether animal suffering plays any independent normative role.

Student answer: Kant denies that animals are ends in themselves because they lack rational autonomy, which is the ground of moral worth. Consequently, we have only indirect duties regarding animals, not direct duties to them. As he argues in the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, cruelty is wrong because it hardens the human heart and cultivates a disposition that makes us more likely to violate our duties to other rational beings. Animals are therefore best understood as instruments for human ends, though we should avoid unnecessary suffering to preserve our own moral sensibility.

In case (a), the farmer uses draft animals for plowing but provides rest and food, avoiding unnecessary harm. Because the labor serves necessary ends and does not cultivate cruelty, this is permissible. In case (b), the automated system occasionally injures livestock through sensor error with no human present to witness it. Since Kant's concern is the effect on the agent's moral character, and no agent is present to have their heart hardened, there is no moral wrong—only a technical malfunction. The suffering of the animals plays no independent normative role; it matters only instrumentally, as a potential cause of human moral corruption. Thus, Kant allows us to distinguish permissible labor from impermissible abuse based solely on whether a rational agent's moral self-cultivation is at stake.
