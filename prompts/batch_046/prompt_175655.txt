At a neighborhood discussion about monuments and public memory, two friends, Maya and Leo, got into a sustained disagreement about whether Germany’s way of confronting its Nazi past—what people call Vergangenheitsbewältigung—can really serve as a model for other countries. Maya argued that it clearly can: Germany embedded Holocaust education in schools, criminalized denial, and filled its cities with memorials, showing that a society can choose to keep its worst history visible rather than letting it fade. Leo countered that this misses the structural condition that made it possible. In Germany, he said, the perpetrator state was utterly destroyed and replaced; there was no institutional continuity protecting the old narrative, and the Allies initially enforced the reckoning. Japan and Turkey, by contrast, retained the same state structures—the same bureaucracies, the same ruling elites—so external pressure was always resisted as an infringement on sovereignty, and internal reform was blocked by those with an interest in sanitizing the past.

Maya then brought up the American South. The Confederacy was militarily defeated and its government dissolved, she said, yet the Lost Cause myth dominated public memory for generations. So Leo’s emphasis on state rupture can’t be right: defeat and replacement happened, but the reckoning still failed. Leo replied that the South is actually the confirming case, not the exception. Reconstruction did replace local authority briefly, but then federal power withdrew, allowing the old elite to reclaim narrative control. For Leo, this proves that defeat alone is insufficient; what matters is whether an external or revolutionary power permanently restructures who controls education, monuments, and law, and whether that restructuring outlasts the initial crisis. Without that sustained enforcement, he said, the past gets rehabilitated.

Maya then accused Leo of reducing Germany’s achievement to what she called “mnemonic dependency”—a society that never genuinely chose its own reckoning but simply inherited norms imposed by occupiers and then maintained them out of habit and external expectation. She argued that democratic societies can internalize even enforced norms and make them authentic, so treating Germany’s process as mere dependency undervalues the democratic work Germans did to own their history. Leo said he wasn’t making a moral judgment about authenticity; he was describing mechanics. He proposed that there are basically three paths to serious reckoning: total state replacement plus long-term enforcement; a genuine internal democratic revolution that displaces the old guard; or some combination. Absent those, he claimed, societies default to sanitization, regardless of how sincerely individuals want to remember.

They turned to me and asked me to referee. Please explain, point by point, who is right about what: is institutional continuity the decisive obstacle in Japan and Turkey, does the American South genuinely undermine Leo’s theory or actually reinforce it, and is Maya correct that framing Germany’s process as “mnemonic dependency” misunderstands how democratic adoption works? Also, say what both of them seem to be overlooking—whether about the role of civil society, the difference between state-level and local-level memory, or something else entirely—and indicate who is more right overall.
