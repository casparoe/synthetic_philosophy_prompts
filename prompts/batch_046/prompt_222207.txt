As a novelist developing the philosophical scaffolding for a work of fiction about consciousness, I have composed the following notes for an essay tentatively titled "The Compression of Mind in Narrative: Dennett's Taxonomy and the Problem of Authored Agency."

The essay takes as its point of departure Daniel Dennett's distinction among four kinds of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—each representing a successive layer of evolutionary and cognitive sophistication. I propose that while this taxonomy illuminates biological cognition, it requires substantial revision when applied to fictional characters, because narrative agency operates under ontological conditions that Dennett's framework does not address. In fiction, characters rarely function as pure Darwinian or Skinnerian agents, since plot demands some capacity for internal hypothesis-testing; yet they almost never achieve genuine Gregorian status, because their access to cultural tools—language, symbolic systems, genre conventions—is not open-ended but is circumscribed by the author's teleological design. To capture this intermediate state, I introduce the concept of "scripted Gregorianity": a condition in which a creature possesses the surface apparatus of cultural cognition yet remains enclosed within a closed causal loop, where every apparent innovation is retroactively determined by narrative economy rather than by open-ended cultural evolution. The resulting hybrid—Popperian in appearance, Skinnerian in reinforcement structure, Gregorian in equipment but not in evolutionary openness—I term the "Mimetic Mind." The essay then asks whether this hybrid exposes a genuine limitation in Dennett's taxonomy—namely, that it presupposes biological or autonomous cognitive evolution rather than authored design—or whether it merely demonstrates that fiction constitutes an ontologically distinct domain in which mental kinds are simulated rather than instantiated.

I would welcome your comments, particularly on the coherence of "scripted Gregorianity" as a philosophical concept rather than a mere metaphor, on whether my characterization of Popperian problem-solving within narrative constraints misrepresents Dennett's distinction between internal selection and external reinforcement, and on whether the "Mimetic Mind" offers any explanatory value beyond literary criticism.
