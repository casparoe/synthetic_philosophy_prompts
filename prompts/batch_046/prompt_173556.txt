I've been lurking on alignment forums and reading the safety literature for years, and there's this argumentative move I keep seeing that I don't have a clean word for. A researcher presents some scheme—maybe RLHF with a reward model, maybe some formal verification setup—and demonstrates that the expected failure rate is bounded, or that the average-case loss is lower than baseline. Then a critic constructs an explicit adversarial input, or just points to the tail of the distribution, and shows that if that case hits, the failure isn't incremental but total. At that point the defenders pivot: they say the scheme was never sold as worst-case proof, only as an average-case improvement, and that demanding zero tail risk is unrealistic. Fair enough. But then in the very next comparison—say, arguing that this scheme is safer than some alternative—the same bounded-rate result gets cited as if it were a structural certificate, a guarantee that caps the damage in a way the competitor can't match. It's not quite motte-and-bailey, since the two readings aren't a defensible core and an indefensible fringe; they're genuinely connected through the same statistical result. But there's this specific slide where an expectation gets treated like a hard ceiling when it's convenient, and like a humble statistical comparison when it's being attacked. Is there an established term for this oscillation between average-case and worst-case interpretations of the same guarantee? If there isn't, what's the sharpest way to carve the distinction—between a probabilistic bound that improves the mean and one that actually constrains the tail—so I can point to the slide without just sounding like I'm complaining that epsilon isn't literally zero? I'd also be curious whether this maps onto anything in decision theory or risk analysis that already has a name I'm missing.

I was discussing this with a friend who works in ML engineering and he just shrugged and said "that's just marketing," but I think it's more specific than that—it's about the epistemic status of the guarantee itself, not just hype. If there's no label, I'd probably call it something like "guarantee laundering," but I'd rather know if I'm reinventing a concept that already exists in safety engineering or statistical decision theory.
