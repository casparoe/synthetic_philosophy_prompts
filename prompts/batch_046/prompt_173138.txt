I'm taking an advanced seminar on philosophy of mind, and we've been working through the distinction between original and derived intentionality—Dennett's insistence that only some systems possess intrinsic aboutness while others borrow theirs from interpreters, and Searle's claim that biological brains alone have original intentionality. Our professor, Dr. Vasquez, has issued a consistent series of verdicts on whether particular entities possess original intentionality (OI) or merely derived intentionality (DI). She holds that an ordinary adult human has OI; that a standard language model running on silicon servers has only DI; that an ant colony solving collective pathfinding problems has only DI, since no unified subject underwrites the behavior; that a cerebral organoid—living human neural tissue grown in vitro with sensory input loops but no body or evolutionary history—possesses OI; and that a precisely engineered silicon robot with neural-architecture-mimicking circuits, self-monitoring predictive coding, and homeostatic energy regulation, but no biological tissue whatsoever, nevertheless has only DI.

We are now asked to predict or recommend her verdict on a new case: a synthetic mycelium-electronic hybrid system, in which living fungal tissue is interfaced with microelectronic sensors and actuators in a closed loop. The system predicts environmental changes, regulates its own nutrient flow through error-correction, and modifies growth patterns accordingly, but it has no centralized brain, no evolutionary history as a unified organism, and its biological component is non-neural fungal cells rather than neurons. The hybrid is embodied in its environment and exhibits integrated self-regulation, yet it lacks both the biological neural substrate of the organoid and the purely artificial constitution of the silicon robot.

What should Dr. Vasquez decide if she wants to remain consistent with her track record? Identify the closest analog among her precedents, and lay out at least two plausible principles that jointly explain all her earlier verdicts—one emphasizing biological neural substrate, the other emphasizing a specific kind of embodied, predictive organizational integration regardless of substrate. Say what each principle predicts about the mycelium hybrid, which verdict is best supported, and what further case—specifically, a purely silicon system with organizational dynamics identical to the hybrid but no biological tissue—would force her to choose between the two principles. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the new case, state explicitly what principle she would be adopting with each possible choice.
