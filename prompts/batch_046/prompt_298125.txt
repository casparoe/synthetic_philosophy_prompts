Examination question (Parts a and b):

Consider the claim that the quality of a scientific model should be judged solely by the accuracy of its predictions, with no independent evaluation of whether its internal assumptions are descriptively realistic. This position is often linked to Milton Friedman’s well-known methodological argument—sometimes called the F-twist—that unrealistic assumptions in a model are not a defect but, in an important sense, a virtue.

Part (a): Explain the logic behind the F-twist. Be precise about why deliberately unrealistic assumptions might strengthen a model, and clearly distinguish this view from the stronger thesis that a good theory must contain premises that are literally false.

Part (b): A researcher contends that modern black-box predictive algorithms in climate science are methodologically superior to traditional process-based models because the former lack explicit microphysical assumptions altogether. The researcher appeals to Friedman’s F-twist, arguing that since unrealistic assumptions are good, a model with no visible assumptions is maximally unrealistic and therefore maximally good. Evaluate this appeal. Is it a correct application of Friedman’s position? Address whether the absence of articulated premises is equivalent to having unrealistic ones, and discuss whether distinctions Friedman draws—such as that between positive and normative inquiry—affect the evaluation.

Student answer:

Friedman’s F-twist holds that the more unrealistic a model’s assumptions, the better the model, provided its predictions hold up. The idea is not that theories should be literally false, but that simplifying away from reality isolates the causal factors that drive outcomes. Realistic descriptions are therefore unnecessary and even distracting. Friedman intended this argument specifically for positive economics—describing and predicting economic phenomena—rather than for normative economics or for the physical sciences like climate modeling.

In part (b), the researcher misapplies Friedman in two ways. First, black-box algorithms do not have unrealistic assumptions; they simply have no explicit assumptions at all. Friedman’s twist concerns articulated premises—such as firms maximizing profits exactly—that deliberately depart from reality. Absence of premises is opacity, not unrealistic simplification. Second, because climate science involves normative policy choices about risk and mitigation, models used for climate policy fall outside the scope of positive science and therefore cannot be defended by the F-twist; they must instead be evaluated by the realism of their internal representations. That said, if we restrict attention to purely predictive, positive climate projection, then explicit but false parameterizations are indeed worse than accurate predictions, regardless of transparency. So the researcher is wrong about black-box superiority being grounded in Friedman, but correct that predictive accuracy should dominate when we are doing purely positive work.
