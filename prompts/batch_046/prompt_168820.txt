I'm filing a feature on philosophy seminars that replace armchair speculation with structured conflict. Design a 75-minute classroom activity for fourteen advanced undergraduates that makes them feel the force of Derek Parfit's claim that psychological continuity—not bodily sameness—is what matters in personal survival.

To keep the prompt from being answered with a standard lecture recap, build the exercise around a constructed variant: the scanner does not annihilate the original body but degrades it, distributing psychological traits—memories, dispositions, and affective patterns—between a diminished Earth-bound remnant and a fully functional Mars replica according to an adjustable "continuity ratio." The group must negotiate who holds the traveler's legal identity, intimate commitments, and liability for an act planned before scanning.

Specify the setting, roles, and rules: assign four students as arbitration judges who must award a single legal identity per round using a point system that permits split assets but not split personhood; assign others as the degraded original, the replica, and advocates for bodily continuity, psychological continuity, and narrative coherence. Run three binding rounds—90/10, 50/50, and 100/0 splits—each requiring votes on inheritance, partnership status, and criminal responsibility. Use symbolic stakes (printed identity certificates and asset tokens), not real money, and include a precise timeline.

The rules themselves should generate the problem: as psychological traits become evenly divided, the panel's confidence should collapse even though no bodily boundary has changed, so participants run into the paradox before it is named. The debrief must map each round onto Parfit's Relation R, say what will predictably go wrong—students defaulting to legal convention or emotional attachment to the Earth body regardless of mental content—and be frank about which feature of the view the activity cannot reproduce: the first-person, indivisible feeling of being one subject, which the facilitator must supply. Avoid bibliographies, reading lists, and citation requests; the activity should be runnable without external texts.
