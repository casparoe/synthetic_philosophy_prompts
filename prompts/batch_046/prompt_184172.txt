Consider the emerging institutional practice among major research funding bodies of allowing grant applicants to incorporate AI-generated literature syntheses—aggregating and summarizing hundreds of primary studies—into their proposals, provided that the principal investigator can supply what reviewers call an independent mechanistic explanation for any empirical claim drawn from the synthesis. The investigator is not expected to have read, evaluated, or even located the original papers; the requirement is satisfied if the PI can articulate a plausible causal or theoretical narrative linking the synthesized finding to the proposed research. At the same time, these same institutions maintain research-integrity guidelines that stress direct engagement with primary sources and warn against derivative or secondhand scholarship. What philosophical conception of understanding does the synthesis policy presuppose? Does it treat understanding as a kind of narrative reconstruction that can be performed without first-person epistemic access to the evidence, or does it covertly retain a requirement of cognitive ownership that the policy's conditions actually violate? Is the bifurcation—outsourcing synthesis to an opaque system while demanding human rationalization—consistent with a view on which understanding requires direct cognitive labor, or does it implicitly concede that such labor can be delegated provided a post-hoc justification is available? Which rival accounts—those tying understanding to acquaintance with evidence, those reducing it to functional explanatory capacity, or those treating it as socially distributed—would license this practice, and which would condemn it as a form of epistemic dependence that erodes rather than extends human understanding? And does the coexistence of this policy with traditional anti-plagiarism and source-verification norms reveal that the institution holds incompatible commitments about where understanding resides?
