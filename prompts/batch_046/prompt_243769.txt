I am a retired biologist who has only recently begun to read systematically in philosophy of mind, and I am trying to understand the extended mind thesis without conflating it with biological ideas that are more familiar to me. Specifically, I keep returning to Dawkins’s notion of the extended phenotype and to the literature on niche construction: a beaver’s dam regulates temperature and protects offspring, and it is causally integrated with beaver genes in ways that affect selection, yet we do not usually say the dam is part of the beaver’s mind. Meanwhile, the parity principle associated with Clark and Chalmers suggests that an external artifact—say, a notebook used for memory—can be part of a cognitive process if it plays the same functional role as an internal state. The conceptual issue I want clarified is whether there is a principled distinction between biological extension into constructed environments and cognitive extension into artifacts, or whether both depend on the same criterion of functional integration across a boundary that is itself arbitrarily drawn.

Please explain the following, separating what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these topics, and do not present a disputed interpretive claim as the consensus view. First, does the parity principle require that the external component be constantly available and automatically endorsed, or are those conditions merely pragmatic heuristics that do not define the boundary of cognition? Second, is there a specific feature—perhaps involving representational content, informational processing, or a particular kind of causal coupling—that blocks the inference that if cognition extends into notebooks, then biological organisms routinely have minds that extend into dams, webs, or chemical trails? Third, if one accepts that phenotypic expression can cross the organism-environment boundary in evolutionary biology, does consistency demand that one also accept cognitive extension, or can the two domains be kept separate by a criterion that is not merely ad hoc?

I am asking for a conceptual clarification rather than a bibliography or a reading list; I want to be able to evaluate the reasoning with the resources I have at hand, and I would appreciate it if you noted explicitly where the field agrees and where knowledgeable philosophers and cognitive scientists genuinely disagree.
