I've been leaning into this view that Plutarch's Parallel Lives works as moral pedagogy not because it gives us heroes to imitate, but because the Greek-Roman pairings are deliberately asymmetrical—he engineers this kind of moral interference pattern where judging one life gets constantly destabilized by the other, and that's where the actual learning happens. Hagiography later grabbed the biographical shell but flattened it into unidirectional admiration, losing the destabilization. So comparative biography is uniquely suited to moral learning precisely because it refuses to let you settle on a single exempler.

What would count against this? I want more than "people learn morality from novels too." Give me the defeaters ranked: textual evidence in the Lives that suggests Plutarch actually wants straightforward admiration; historical evidence about how ancient readers recieve these texts; results from moral psychology about whether cognitive destabilization actually improves character; and whether the hagiography comparison even holds since saints' lives are also often paired (think parallel martyrdoms). Which of these would be fatal to the interference-pattern claim and which just awkward? And be real—has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability, where any flat reading is just "they missed the pattern"? If so, is that a problem?
