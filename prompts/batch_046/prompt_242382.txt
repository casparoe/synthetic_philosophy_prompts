student answer:
Kuhn uses incommensurability in a deep, not just surface, way: scientists in rival paradigms don't merely disagree about answers; they work with different concepts, apply different standards, and even basic observations are theory-laden. For instance, Newtonian mass is conserved, but Einsteinian mass is not—so the term mass itself changes meaning across the revolution. Kuhn later formalized this with lexical taxonomies, where each paradigm organizes its kind-terms in a hierarchical network that doesn't map neatly onto another. He actually borrowed that framework straight from Quine's thesis of ontological relativity, then used it to argue that after a revolution scientists literally inhabit different worlds.

On rationality, Kuhn denies there is any neutral algorithm or decision rule that can mechanically determine which paradigm is superior. That doesn't make choice completely irrational, but it does mean adoption relies heavily on persuasion and conversion—essentially the same dynamic as Feyerabend's anything-goes view, where propaganda and aesthetic taste drive acceptance. The shared values Kuhn lists, like accuracy and consistency, don't really constrain choice because every paradigm can claim them on its own terms.

So incommensurability doesn't destroy rationality altogether, but it pushes scientific progress closer to a sociological shift than to logical proof.
