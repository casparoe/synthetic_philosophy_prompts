I have no formal training in philosophy or theology, but I have been reading some of the older texts on my own, and I keep returning to Augustine’s treatise On Lying (De Mendacio), which the manuscript tradition places around 395 AD. There is one passage in particular that I cannot stop turning over. I will copy it here exactly as I found it in an older translation:

"For which purpose we must see what a lie is. For not every one who says a false thing lies, if he believes or opines that to be true which he says. Now between believing and opining there is this difference, that sometimes he who believes feels that he does not know that which he believes, (although he may know himself to be ignorant of a thing, and yet have no doubt at all concerning it, if he most firmly believes it:) whereas he who opines, thinks he knows that which he does not know. Now whoever utters that which he holds in his mind either as belief or as opinion, even though it be false, he lies not. For this he owes to the faith of his utterance, that he thereby produce that which he holds in his mind, and has in that way in which he produces it. Not that he is without fault, although he lie not, if either he believes what he ought not to believe, or thinks he knows what he knows not, even though it should be true: for he accounts an unknown thing for a known. Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind. Whence also the heart of him who lies is said to be double; that is, there is a double thought: the one, of that thing which he either knows or thinks to be true and does not produce; the other, of that thing which he produces instead thereof, knowing or thinking it to be false."

What I am trying to understand is whether Augustine’s “double heart” requires that the speaker actually intends to plant a false belief in the listener’s mind, or whether it is enough that the speaker’s utterance simply fails to match what is in his mind, regardless of what he expects the listener to do with it.

To make the problem concrete, I have been thinking about a kind of speech I call “borrowed conviction”: a person tells another something the speaker does not believe—say, a comforting claim about an afterlife or about the inevitability of recovery—while privately assuming that the listener will receive the words as emotional support rather than as factual testimony, and may even share the speaker’s private doubts. The speaker is not trying to deceive in the ordinary sense; he is offering a falsehood he thinks both parties tacitly understand as false. Yet there is still one thing in his mind and another in his words.

Does Augustine’s framework count this as a lie? Does the “double heart” depend on an intent to deceive the listener about the state of affairs, or only on the internal mismatch between belief and utterance? And if the speaker believes the listener already suspects the falsehood, does that dissolve the second “thought” that Augustine describes, or does it remain intact?

I am not looking for a reading list or for references to modern scholars. I only want help seeing how the logic of this particular passage applies—or fails to apply—to this kind of case.
