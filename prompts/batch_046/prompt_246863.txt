I run an upper-level philosophy seminar (14 students, one 90-minute session) on speech acts and institutional reality. Design a structured in-class role-play — no lecture up front — in which students are members of a fictional “Council of Four Seals,” each holding either a primary or secondary authority card and each assigned a hidden private objective (e.g., consecrate a shared space, declare a member exiled, establish a collective resource). The rules must allow spoken declarations using explicit performative verbs (“I consecrate...”, “I revoke...”, “I appoint...”) to change the institutional state of objects and persons in the room, with small stakes (speaking-order privileges or point tokens) tied to achieving one’s objective without causing a misfire. Crucially, embed hidden conditions that mirror Austin’s infelicities and inject an asymmetry: creating an institutional fact requires only present authority plus verbal acknowledgment from present witnesses, but revoking it requires a correct spoken citation of the original authority chain and acknowledgment from a holder of that original chain, who may now be absent or have lost status — so students discover through failed revocations that authority is chain-dependent and that undoing is structurally harder than doing.

Provide the materials, the exact acknowledgment procedure, the timeline across the 90 minutes, how conflicting private objectives generate pressure, and the debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto Austin’s felicity conditions (misinvocation, misexecution, breach, insincerity) and onto the creation/revocation asymmetry. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time — for instance, students substituting written notes or physical gestures for spoken utterance, ignoring witness rules, or treating revocation as symmetrical — and how to handle it. Finally, be frank about which feature of real-world institutional sedimentation the compressed format cannot reproduce and that I must supply in discussion.
