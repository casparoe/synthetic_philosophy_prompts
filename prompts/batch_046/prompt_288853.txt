I've been working through the Chase-Sanborn quining argument — the claim that "qualia" is an incoherent posit because any property that is both essentially subjective and causally inert collapses on inspection — and I think I've built a version that proves too much, but I can't locate the leak.

(1) The quining strategy applies to any mental kind K if K is (a) accessible only by direct introspection, (b) defined by its phenomenal character, and (c) causally or explanatorily inert.
(2) The state of currently grasping the meaning of this very premise satisfies (a)-(c): only I can introspect my grasp; its character is "what it is like" to understand; and the functional role of symbol-processing doesn't determine which particular grasp occurs.
(3) So by parity, "understanding this premise" is incoherent.
(4) But if understanding is incoherent, then no one can understand the argument that reaches (3).
(5) Therefore the argument that qualia are incoherent cannot itself be understood — which undermines its own status as a reason.
(6) So either the quining strategy is restricted ad hoc to qualia, or it eliminates the cognitive capacity required to deploy it.

Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? I'd prefer to keep (1) intact and save (5) if possible, but I'm not sure that's coherent. Please separate what's settled about the logical structure of quining arguments from what's genuinely contested — for instance, whether phenomenal properties are really causally inert, or whether de se grasp is a genuine counterexample rather than a misapplication. Don't present a contested view as the consensus.
