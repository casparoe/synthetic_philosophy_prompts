I serve on the steering committee of the Inter-Museum Ephemeral Design Consortium, a regional body that maintains a shared digital repository of unrealized exhibition proposals—designs that were drafted but never funded or executed. The archive is open-access and non-rivalrous: any of the twelve member institutions may search, adapt, and build upon these abandoned plans for grant applications, educational programming, or publication. Yet the labor of digitizing, annotating, and verifying provenance for each entry is substantial and rivalrous; over the past two years only four museums have contributed new material, while usage logs indicate that the eight non-contributing members account for nearly seventy percent of downloads and derivative works. At our meeting next Thursday we must vote on a proposal to replace voluntary contribution with a mandatory schedule tied to membership privileges, including temporary suspension of download access for institutions that fall below a quarterly contribution threshold. The case is not merely administrative: it is a concrete instance of the tragedy of the commons applied to a public good whose consumption does not diminish the stock but whose maintenance is depleted by free-riding, and several committee members argue that imposing extraction-linked obligations transforms the repository from a genuinely public good into a club good and thereby undermines the cooperative moral basis of the consortium rather than saving it. I need a written opinion—no more than two printed pages—to circulate before the vote recommending either adoption of the mandatory schedule, rejection in favor of an alternative mechanism, or adoption with specific modified conditions. The memo should present a clear bottom line the committee can act upon, lay out the reasoning in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, boldface, or tables, and include an honest, serious engagement with the strongest argument against your recommended position, treating that objection not as a misunderstanding but as a genuine philosophical concern about the nature of public goods, cooperative obligation, and whether omitting to maintain a shared resource can ground a coercive remedy without destroying the good being protected. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not invoke specific named theorists or texts unless they belong to common knowledge; and write as if addressing colleagues who understand institutional governance but need the conceptual structure clarified rather than labeled.
