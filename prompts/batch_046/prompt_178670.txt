Meanwhile, if your brainstem dies but your cortex could in principle be sustained on machines (which is rare but technically possible), you're dead, even though your mental life might still be going on in the cortex. I was talking to my adivisor and she pointed out that this presupposes some pretty specific view about where the 'mind' is located functionally, but I'm not sure if it's actually functionalist or more like biological essentialism in disguise.

What I want to understand is: what theory of functionalism and multiple realizability does this institutional practice actually commit us to? Does treating brainstem failure as death presuppose that mental states are multiply realizable only within biological neural tissue, or does it smuggle in a non-functional view that the brainstem is the 'essential' substrate? Like, should we be declaring people dead when their biological substrate fails but their mental life is preserved elsewhere? Can someone walk me through what implcit philosophy of mind this policy actually rests on, whether it's coherent, and what a genuinely functionalist hospital protocol would have to change?
