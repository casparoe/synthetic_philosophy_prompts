I am drafting a chapter on the foundations of deductive justification for my dissertation in philosophy of mathematics and logic, and I have reconstructed Carroll's regress into what I call the Tortoise Ladder—an ascending hierarchy of justification levels, indexed informally by ordinals, in which every rule-application at level α requires licensing by a meta-rule at level α+1. The argument below extends Carroll's original exchange, but I now fear it proves far too much and I cannot locate the precise fracture. Here is the structure:

(1) If inference rule R is legitimately applied in deduction D, then there must be a distinct meta-rule M that specifically licenses that application of R to the premises occurring in D.
(2) By Carroll's observation, accepting M as binding requires that its own applications be licensed by a further meta-rule M', generating an infinite ascending chain; hence any fully justified deduction requires traversing the entire Ladder.
(3) Contemporary structural proof theory treats every rule as defined solely by an inference schema internal to a single formal language; there is no "outside" standpoint from which a schema is applied, only syntactic substitution instances within the system.
(4) Therefore, within structural proof theory every concrete rule-application is an instance of an internal schema, yet by (1)–(2) no instance is ever licensed without invoking a meta-rule external to that schema.
(5) So structural proof theory can distinguish valid from invalid deductions only if it either (a) admits an infinite hierarchy of distinct formal languages, each licensing the rules of the previous one, or (b) permits some rules to be applied without any licensing meta-rule.
(6) Option (a) violates the unity of the formal system and makes mechanized proof-checking impossible in practice, since no single checker can traverse an infinite language hierarchy.
(7) Option (b) concedes that certain applications are primitive, but then Carroll's challenge is answered only by fiat rather than by genuine justification.
(8) Consequently, either deduction is never fully justified—which renders rational inquiry impossible—or structural proof theory is internally incoherent.

The conclusion in (8) seems intolerable, yet each step appears defensible on inspection. If I am forced to choose, I would prefer to preserve (1) and (3) and reject either (4) or (7), though I am unsure what each surrender costs. Which step should be abandoned? Is the error an equivocation between "licensing an application" and "being a syntactic instance of a schema," a hidden scope shift in (4), or a modal slide between "can ask for further justification" and "must provide it for validity"? And if the argument is in fact valid, which of (1), (3), (5), (6), or (7) is the least damaging premise to give up, and what does that concession imply for the prospects of a non-circular account of deductive justification?
