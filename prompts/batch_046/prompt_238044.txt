I've been discussing with a colleague whether panpsychism genuinely alters our ethical landscape or merely relocates familiar problems to the quantum scale, and we keep circling the same well-worn objections—so I'd like to press the evaluation into a more granular, less-charted framing. For the sake of comparison, let's introduce a distinction that is not standard in the literature but useful for testing the robustness of ethical inferences: vector-panpsychism, in which the phenomenal properties of fundamental entities possess directional or relational structure analogous to physical vector quantities (such as spin or polarization), versus scalar-panpsychism, in which phenomenal properties are purely magnitude-like and entirely lacking internal relational features.

Against that backdrop, please rank the following five argumentative strategies for deriving a substantive ethical obligation to minimize suffering at the fundamental physical level: (A) analogical downward extension from macroscopic biological consciousness; (B) the integrated-information threshold claim that only sufficiently complex combinations of micro-subjects constitute moral patients; (C) the cosmological-aggregate claim that the sheer numerical prevalence of fundamental subjects generates collective moral weight regardless of individual capacity; (D) the physical-symmetry claim that suffering introduces asymmetry or disorder that conflicts with the structural regularities of physical law; and (E) the epistemic-default claim that in the absence of decisive exclusion criteria we ought to presume minimal sentience everywhere. For each strategy, assign it a tier—S, A, B, C, or D—separately under the vector-panpsychist and scalar-panpsychist assumptions. In one sentence per assignment, justify precisely how the presence or absence of directional phenomenal structure strengthens, weakens, or leaves unchanged the plausibility of deriving that particular ethical obligation from the corresponding ontology.
