I serve as vice-chair of the Intergenerational Allocation Panel at the Meridian Foundation, a body that approves methodological frameworks for distributing research endowments between near-term clinical and social-science trials and speculative long-horizon projects—such as deep-archive preservation, interstellar biosphere design, and multi-century governance modeling. Next Friday we must circulate a written opinion on a proposal submitted by the Foundation's forecasting unit, which seeks to institutionalize an observer-weighted decision rule for all future funding cycles. The proposal applies anthropic reasoning to estimate the expected number of conscious observers across different cosmological and demographic trajectories, then weights funding priorities by the number of future observers likely to be affected by present allocations. Two competing implementations are before us. The first relies on a restricted self-sampling assumption: it treats our observed reference class—terrestrial human observers with available empirical records—as the correct sampling frame, yielding conservative allocations that favor near-term interventions because they treat our existence as typical within that frame rather than as evidence for larger populations. The second relies on a self-indication assumption, which treats the mere fact of our existence as probabilistic support for worlds with vastly more observers, thereby shifting substantial endowment weight toward far-future and large-scale projects on the grounds that a greater number of future observers will exist to benefit.

The forecasting unit's director invokes Nick Bostrom's well-known presumptuous philosopher objection to argue that the self-indication framework must be excluded: if we allow cosmological or demographic models with enormous observer counts to dominate our allocations simply because they render our own existence more probable, we risk giving extreme credence to speculative multiverse or deep-future scenarios without any independent empirical support, effectively letting anthropic selection effects do the work of evidence. A dissenting minority of panel members—drawn from cosmology and formal ethics—counter that excluding self-indication is itself a form of presumptuous boundary-drawing, because it assumes without justification that our reference class should be restricted to currently observable humans rather than extending to all potential observers across space, time, and substrate. They propose a bounded hybrid: apply self-indication only within empirically calibrated demographic ranges, and treat cosmological scaling factors as uninformative priors rather than likelihood multipliers.

The panel's memo must recommend one of three concrete courses: (a) approve the restricted self-sampling framework and explicitly bar self-indication from allocation rules, with a mandatory three-year review; (b) approve a bounded hybrid framework with formal confidence thresholds and an independent methodological audit scheduled within eighteen months; or (c) reject observer-weighted allocation altogether in favor of a non-anthropic baseline that weights projects solely by direct, near-term welfare metrics. The opinion should include an actionable bottom line with a clear time frame, should treat the strongest version of the losing position seriously rather than dismissing it as mere speculation, and should address whether the presumptuous philosopher objection operates as a decisive methodological constraint that should be hard-coded into policy, or rather as a cautionary heuristic that can be operationalized through prior bounds and sensitivity analysis. The document should be suitable for circulation to board members fluent in decision theory and resource economics but not necessarily in cosmological philosophy. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific papers; the reasoning should stand on its own and respond to concerns the forecasting unit and dissenting members could actually address without external lookup.
