Two participants in a graduate seminar on historiography got stuck in a disagreement about whether Whig history is primarily a cognitive failure or an unavoidable evaluative framework. I was present and could not tell whose blows actually landed. Their exchange went as follows.

Participant A opened by arguing that Whig history commits what might be called teleological compression: it treats every prior institution as an incomplete anticipation of our current ones, so that the Magna Carta, for instance, is read solely as a stepping-stone toward modern constitutionalism rather than as a settlement of feudal obligations specific to its own conceptual world. This, A said, flattens the past's internal coherence.

Participant B replied that every historian necessarily writes from a present standpoint. To refuse to see the Magna Carta as part of a longer arc toward limited government is not neutrality but a different narrative choice, likely one that protects feudal structures from moral criticism. Without some present standard, B insisted, evaluation is impossible, and our standards are the only ones available to us.

A then distinguished between descriptive anachronism—reading our concepts back into the past—and normative anachronism—judging past agents for failing to anticipate values they could not have held. One can acknowledge that constitutional government eventually developed, A argued, without framing thirteenth-century barons as deficient for not being modern liberals. The error of Whig history is precisely the slide from the first to the second.

B countered that the distinction collapses in practice. If one withholds the praise given to modern reformers from past figures, one is already applying a differential moral yardstick rooted in present values. The only consistent alternative, B claimed, is full-blown moral relativism, which A clearly rejects given A's willingness to criticize feudal obligations.

A responded by introducing contingent accumulation: good outcomes can accumulate over time without every prior absence constituting failure. Modern reformers can be praised for expanding political equality without implying that slaveholders or feudal lords were blameworthy for not having achieved it. B's grading rubric, by contrast, requires every era to be measured on a curve bending toward the present, which makes past moral agency disappear—actors become instruments of a process they neither intended nor understood.

B answered that contingent accumulation is merely Whig history in a softer register. To call expanded equality a genuine good rather than a mere change, one must invoke criteria drawn from present commitments. Without them, accumulation has no direction. Moreover, B added, A's respect for past agency risks moral paralysis: if we cannot say that abolishing slavery was progress because slaveholders did not share our framework, we forfeit the ability to condemn past injustice with conviction. Some anachronism, B concluded, is the unavoidable price of moral seriousness.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the four rounds, rate the move made by the speaker in that round—from clearly mistaken to clearly right—and briefly justify the rating. Then state who is right about the core dispute—whether Whig history is best understood as a cognitive error, an unavoidable framework, or something else—and identify what both participants are missing, particularly with respect to the distinction between descriptive and normative anachronism and to the problem of treating historical agents as instruments of an unintended process. Do not offer a bibliography or reading list.
