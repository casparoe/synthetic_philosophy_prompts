Analyze the 1961 Jerusalem trial of Adolf Eichmann as a case study in the interaction between Zygmunt Bauman’s modernity thesis and the uniqueness-versus-comparability debate that defined the German Historikerstreit. Bauman contends that the Holocaust was not an atavistic eruption of hatred but a distinctly modern achievement of bureaucratic rationality, functional division of labor, and the moral distancing that allows ordinary agents to participate in extraordinary harm without feeling personal malice. The uniqueness position, by contrast, insists that the intentional, totalizing character of the genocide places it outside ordinary historical comparison, while comparability advocates treat it as an extreme instance of modern state violence analyzable alongside other genocides. Apply both frameworks to the trial: examine whether the court’s relentless focus on Eichmann’s individual intent and direct orders obscures the bureaucratic machinery and role-based compliance that Bauman identifies as the deeper cause; consider whether portraying Eichmann as a singularly monstrous figure reinforces the uniqueness framing at the expense of Bauman’s image of the unremarkable modern functionary; and determine whether the trial’s legal need for personal culpability reveals a structural tension—namely, that modern judicial individualism may be conceptually ill-equipped to assign responsibility for harms produced by modern organizational rationality. Finally, say what the episode suggests about whether the Holocaust’s uniqueness resides in its modern bureaucratic form rather than in its moral exceptionality, and whether the Historikerstreit therefore missed a sociological dimension that Bauman’s analysis supplies.
