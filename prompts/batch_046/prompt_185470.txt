I'm preparing notes for a seminar on long-range reasoning and keep encountering a specific argumentative move that I can't place precisely in the literature. The pattern arises whenever someone predicts the eventual fragmentation of a very large present-day system—whether a modern nation-state, a multinational corporation, or a global digital platform—by appealing to the historical record of past territorial empires. The speaker first establishes that, across some reference set of large political entities, growth beyond a certain scale was eventually followed by dissolution or division, and then applies that statistical pattern to the contemporary case. So far this is ordinary analogical forecasting.

What distinguishes the move I'm interested in is what happens next. When an interlocutor points out that the present system possesses institutional mechanisms with no clear analog in the historical cases—perhaps standardized fiscal accounting, rapid long-distance communication, formalized rules for authority transfer, or bureaucratic recruitment independent of kinship—the forecaster does not deny these differences. Instead, they concede them explicitly but immediately reclassify them as adjustments to velocity rather than to structure: the mechanisms may delay the breaking point by decades or centuries, but they do not alter the underlying dynamics of overextension, elite decay, or external pressure. The institutional innovations are acknowledged and then discounted as cosmetic. The forecast therefore preserves the historical base rate of collapse while merely extending the timeline.

I've seen this in discussions comparing modern states to Rome or the Qing, but also in predictions about corporate or platform scale, and I want to understand whether it is just poor forecasting or a specific, identifiable error with its own boundaries. Is there an established concept for this particular maneuver—using statistics about past collapses while treating acknowledged institutional discontinuities as temporal offsets rather than as reasons to exit the reference class? If so, how does it differ from legitimate adjustments for context, and where does it blur into ordinary analogical reasoning? If not, how should one delineate it precisely, particularly at the edges where a forecaster might legitimately say that some innovations only postpone rather than prevent a given failure mode? I am not looking for a bare label; I want to know exactly what the phenomenon comprises, what it excludes, and whether its boundaries can be drawn sharply enough to be useful.
