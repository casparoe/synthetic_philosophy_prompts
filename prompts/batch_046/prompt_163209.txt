I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks—roughly fifteen minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop titled "The Weight of Near-Misses: Counterfactual Archives and Predictive Governance." The room will be split evenly between professional historians of science, climate-systems modelers, and philosophers of history, with no shared disciplinary vocabulary to rely on. I was discussing with the organizers how to avoid the usual platitudes about "learning from the past," and we agreed the framing should come from Nietzsche's tripartite distinction among monumental, antiquarian, and critical uses of history—not as a historical lecture, but as a structural test for the workshop's actual subject.

The workshop's premise is that institutions are now curating high-fidelity archives of events that never occurred: simulated droughts that a single legislative vote averted, epidemics halted at borders, climate pathways that were technically feasible but politically foreclosed. I want the speech to argue that these synthetic pasts resist all three of Nietzsche's modes. They are not monumental, since no exemplary figure or heroic narrative can be extracted from a counterfactual drought. They are not antiquarian, because they preserve no continuous tradition or inherited identity—only a branching path that was explicitly rejected. And they are unstable as critical history: rather than liberating the present from the burden of the past, they multiply the past into competing virtual timelines, so that the present is not freed from history but overwhelmed by parallel ones. The address should open with this tension, then press it to a conceptual gap—perhaps these archives serve a purpose the tripartite framework cannot accommodate, or perhaps they show that the critical mode collapses into the others when history is manufactured rather than inherited. It must be intelligible to the modelers without losing the philosophers, and it must avoid any bibliography, direct quotation, or citation of Nietzsche's texts; the three modes should function as living distinctions, not museum pieces. Please write the speech.
