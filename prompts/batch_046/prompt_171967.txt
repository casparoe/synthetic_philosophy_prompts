I serve as the senior mediator for a regional heritage reconciliation board that adjudicates disputes whenever a community's collective memory and the official archival record conflict over what occurred at specific sites, ceremonies, or displacements. These cases reach me with regularity—approximately one every six weeks—always under a seventy-two-hour deadline, without subpoena power, and with the strict constraint that whatever recommendation I issue must be citeable by both the community council and the municipal archive without either party feeling that its fundamental way of knowing has been dismissed. The conflicts are not uniform: some concern empirical discrepancies such as dates, names, or the sequence of events; others concern interpretive framing, such as whether a movement should be characterized as voluntary migration or coerced removal; still others involve archival silence in places where memory is vivid and detailed, or archival precision in places where community transmission has preserved ambiguity. I need a reusable decision procedure—ordered questions, conditions for sufficient answers, explicit stopping points, and clearly marked nodes where judgment must replace calculation—that my board can apply consistently without requiring philosophical training.

The procedure must be grounded in what philosophy of history and social epistemology actually maintain about testimonial transmission versus documentary evidence, but translated into operational steps a mediator can run in a single afternoon. It must preserve three distinctions that survive simplification: conflicts over empirical fact, conflicts over interpretive framing, and conflicts over which knowledge-practices carry authority when both cannot be fully satisfied. It must specify what to do when the archive is incomplete but not demonstrably wrong, when community testimony is internally consistent across generations but unsupported by surviving documents, and when both sources agree on the underlying events but assign opposite moral and political weights. Most importantly, it must flag the precise point where the question ceases to be epistemological and becomes a matter of values—where further evidence, however complete, cannot determine which account should yield. Construct this as a sequence of tests applied in order, with brief pass-or-fail conditions for each, explicit "hand to human judgment" nodes, and an honest account of where the procedure will misfire or produce recommendations that one party will find unacceptable.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases: first, an oral tradition describing a ceremonial gathering in 1923 that municipal land records place in 1931, with no other documents surviving from the period; second, an archival photograph identifying a community leader by a European-assigned surname that the community's mnemonic practice has consistently preserved under a clan name, where both sides agree on the leader's actions but disagree on how he should be named in permanent public signage; and third, a documented government relocation order recording a single departure date in 1948 that the community remembers not as one administrative event but as a gradual, coerced dispersal spread across three winters, with the archive silent on the intermediate periods. For each case, show exactly where the procedure stops, what recommendation it yields, and which party receives an answer it will not want—particularly in the case where the recommendation disappoints the side that initially invoked the board. Finally, specify the class of conflicts for which this procedure is the wrong tool altogether, and state what it cannot do regardless of how carefully it is applied.
