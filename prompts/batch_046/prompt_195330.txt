I cover science and ideas for a monthly magazine, and I am currently researching a feature on the conceptual architecture of democratic legitimacy in early modern political metaphysics. For this, I have been working through Spinoza's unfinished Political Treatise—specifically the chapter in which democracy is defined not as majority rule but as the collective possession of sovereign right over all that lies within the community's power.

In that chapter, Spinoza sets out what appears to be a radically inclusive definition: democracy as a united body holding supreme authority collectively. Yet he immediately qualifies this with a series of exclusions—aliens, persons who live under the legal authority of another (women, children, domestic servants, and dependants), criminals, and those whose speech or reason is not self-directed. The criterion for citizenship is being sui juris—standing under one's own authority—rather than being mediated through the will of a master, parent, or guardian.

The text is over three centuries old and rendered in a translation that preserves much of the original Latin syntax, which makes it hard to tell whether these exclusions are intended as empirical observations about Dutch household structures in the 1670s, or as necessary conditions for the democratic form. I am particularly struggling to understand how Spinoza's metaphysics of power, developed in the Ethics, bears on this political argument. Specifically, I want to know whether the distinction between potentia (power as living capacity) and potestas (power as authoritative right) is being used here to argue that collective sovereignty can only be constituted by those whose individual capacity is not already structurally subordinated to another will.

Could you help me understand this passage? I am not asking for a modern defence or condemnation of the exclusions, nor for a bibliography. Rather, I want to see the internal logic: is the dependency relation being treated as a sociological obstacle that democracy might eventually overcome, or as a logical boundary that defines what the democratic multitudo can be? If it is the latter, how exactly does mediated dependency disqualify one from membership in the collective sovereign body, given that Spinoza elsewhere treats power as something that can be composed and augmented across bodies?

Please treat this as a conceptual clarification for a non-specialist but intellectually serious reader: I need to grasp the argument's architecture, not receive a survey of secondary literature.
