I'm observing a disagreement in my philosophy reading group between two members—call them A and B—about whether dialetheism, the view that some contradictions are literally true, can be confined to self-referential paradoxes or must extend more broadly. Neither asked me to take sides, but I'd like an outside adjudication of their actual exchange.

A began by arguing that dialetheism is defensible only for semantic paradoxes like the Liar. In those cases, A said, the contradiction arises from unavoidable self-reference within the language itself, and every attempt to strengthen the language simply regenerates the paradox. By contrast, in empirical domains—physics, or mathematics outside of foundational set theory—an apparent contradiction always signals an incomplete model or measurement error rather than a literally true contradiction, because there is always a principled revision path available.

B replied that this distinction is arbitrary special pleading. Paraconsistent logics show that a formal system can contain contradictions without collapsing into triviality; therefore, if the evidence genuinely supports both a claim and its negation, there is no logical barrier to accepting both. B further noted that some structural paradoxes in mathematics—such as those arising from naive comprehension—are not empirical mistakes but inherent features of certain constructions, so the boundary A wants between "language" and "world" does not actually hold.

A then conceded that structural mathematical contradictions might be unavoidable in principle, but introduced a further constraint: many paraconsistent frameworks, particularly those shaped by relevance logic, require that the premises used to derive a contradiction be genuinely relevant to that conclusion. Applying that standard, A claimed, many alleged empirical contradictions dissolve because the conflicting measurements lack the right logical connection. Thus dialetheism survives only in a very narrow form—contradictions that are both unavoidable and relevantly derived.

B countered that containment is not elimination. If relevance logic allows contradictions to be quarantined in specific theoretical pockets—say, in descriptions of incompatible quantum observables—without destroying classical reasoning elsewhere, that supports what B called a "patchwork dialetheism": true contradictions exist locally without global explosion. B accused A of treating logical containment as if it removed the contradiction, when in fact it is precisely the mechanism that makes dialetheism empirically workable.

Can you go through their exchange round by round and say who is right about each specific point and where each argument succeeds or fails? In particular, address whether A's relevance-logic constraint genuinely narrows dialetheism or whether B's "patchwork" interpretation is more consistent with paraconsistent commitments. Also identify anything important both are missing—perhaps about the distinction between semantic and set-theoretic paradoxes, or about whether local containment solves the epistemological problem of accepting a contradiction. Please answer in American English.
