I am preparing a diagnostic instrument for a graduate seminar in aesthetics and philosophy of language focused on the problem of translating lyric poetry under formal constraint—the issue sometimes discussed in connection with le ton beau de Marot. Rather than an expository essay, I need a decision tree that sorts students by what they actually believe about preservation and betrayal, not by which label they prefer. The tree should begin from a single shared commitment: that translating a rhymed, metered poem forces a genuine choice about what is being preserved.

Please construct the tree so that each internal node poses a concrete case or a yes-or-no claim that an advanced undergraduate could answer without knowing the position names in advance. The first split should ask whether a translation that reproduces every propositional content and image of a source poem but abandons its meter and rhyme scheme is still a translation of that poem or only a paraphrase. The second split should ask whether formal constraints are constitutive of the work's identity—so that altering them produces a different work—or instrumental, meaning they are replaceable vehicles for an aesthetic effect that can be reconstructed by other means in the target language. The third split should introduce the following non-standard distinction: suppose a translator replaces the source-language formal pattern with an analogous structural pattern in the target language—for instance, substituting an ABAB rhyme scheme with a target-language alliterative scheme that occupies an analogous structural position—without claiming that the analogy carries any semantic content; call this structural isomorphism. The branch should ask whether such isomorphism counts as preserving form only when competent readers of the target poem can recognize the structural correspondence without being told, or whether the translator's intention to create the analogy is sufficient regardless of audience access.

The leaves should include at least: (i) strict formalism, holding that any change in form is a change in work-identity; (ii) semanticism, holding that form is dispensable ornament and literal content is all that must be preserved; (iii) audience-dependent reconstruction, which permits structural isomorphism only when the analogy is reader-accessible; (iv) intentionalist reconstruction, which permits the same isomorphism guided solely by translator intention; (v) the double-betrayal view, which denies any middle path and holds that both literal meaning and original form must be sacrificed together; and (vi) any other logically possible combination of answers that remains unoccupied, labeled unnamed, with one sentence diagnosing whether that combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Please flag explicitly any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate, and state what further distinction would be required to split them. The instrument is intended for students who have read some philosophy of language but are not specialists in translation theory, so every node must define its own technical terms, must use no vocabulary that depends on external scholarship, and must not invoke bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not provide a scoring key that merely maps answer patterns onto pre-existing named theories; the point is to locate positions in the logical space first and attach names afterward.
