I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or history, and I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at the dedication of a museum gallery titled "From Punched Cards to Cloud: The Inevitable Rise of Modern Computing." The audience will be split between retired engineers whose legacy systems are being displayed, academic historians of technology, and donors who funded the exhibition. I want the speech to honor those people and artifacts without treating them as primitive stepping-stones toward our present stack, and without suggesting that today's architectures were the destined outcome of their work. I have been thinking about the historiographical temptation to write the past as an inevitable progress narrative leading to now—where every earlier decision is either praised as a correct anticipation of the present or dismissed as a benighted error we eventually corrected—but I do not want to name-drop concepts or lecture the room. Instead, I want that resistance to be the structural spine of the address: it should determine what I can praise, what I must avoid saying, and how I speak differently to the engineers, the historians, and the donors.

Specifically, I want to build the remarks around a concrete claim about contingency rather than destiny: that a storage-architecture preference in the late 1960s—favoring flat file structures over hierarchical database models—was not a mistake that we later fixed, but a fully rational response to the economics of tape storage at the time, one that would be rational again if those conditions returned. From there, I want to argue that every artifact in the gallery solved a problem that is still with us, only rendered invisible by our current infrastructure. The speech must comfort the retired engineers that they are not museum pieces, reassure the historians that I am not flattening context into a single timeline, and convince the donors that we are celebrating genuine achievement without mythologizing fate. Please write this as a polished, five-minute address in the voice of a thoughtful engineer—formal but not academic—so that the refusal to tell a Whig-progress story shapes every paragraph without ever sounding like a historiography seminar.
