I hold a hybrid position on Kripkenstein's rule-following considerations. I accept that the skeptical argument defeats every reductive analysis of meaning—dispositionalist, mental-state, functionalist, and causal-historical accounts all fail to fix the normative distinction between correct and incorrect application. But I reject the skeptical solution, which replaces individual meaning-facts with community-level assertibility conditions, on the grounds that any communal practice of correcting errors already presupposes the very individual-level normative relation it was meant to eliminate. So I defend a qualified straight solution: there are primitive, irreducible normative facts about what a speaker means—facts that are not reducible to behaviour or mental content—but these facts do not determine future applications in advance. They function instead as sui generis constraints that only become manifest in actual practices of agreement and correction, making meaning metaphysically real yet epistemically and predictively inert.

What would count against this? I want the defeaters mapped from fatal to merely awkward. Which arguments—say, a successful reductive account of normative guidance, or a proof that primitive normative facts are necessarily epiphenomenal—would genuinely undermine the view? Which observations about error, such as the coherence of a solitary speaker being systematically wrong about her own rules, would defenders concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as presupposing the rival account? How does the view depend on results in adjacent domains, for instance about private language, about rule-governed behaviour in artificial systems, or about the metaphysics of norms generally? Does it risk insulation: if primitive meaning-facts are defined as non-predictive, is there any empirical or conceptual development that could in principle refute them, and if not, is that unfalsifiability a virtue or a defect for a metaphysical claim of this kind?
