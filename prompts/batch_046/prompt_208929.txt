I'm helping organise a small neighbourhood association in a town that used to be a mining village but hasn't seen coal since the 70s. Every autumn we run a 'Heritage Day' where people dress in homemade 'traditional' costume and re-enact a ceremony involving a symbolic exchange of bread and salt at the old pit head. The thing is, we all know now — well, some of us have always suspected — that the ceremony was cooked up in 1984 by a local councillor who'd seen something similar on telly. There's no actual pre-industrial root to it; the costumes are mostly based on generic Victorian postcards, and the pit head was rebuilt as a heritage feature in 1992.

Last week we had a row about whether to apply for a government 'living heritage' grant that requires us to demonstrate 'continuous community practice since at least the pre-war era.' One side says applying would be dishonest and that if we value community we should just admit it's invented and keep it as an explicit modern ritual. Another side says all traditions are invented eventually, so there's no shame in formalising ours, and that demanding 'authenticity' is just gatekeeping by people who want to feel superior. A third person — a history grad — suggested we try to reconstruct what actual miners' families did in the 1920s and fold that in, but others reckon that'd just be inventing a second fake tradition on top of the first.

I'm definately stuck on what's the right call here, and not just practically but philosophically. I was chatting about it with my flatmate and she said I was overthinking it, but I can't shake the feeling there's a real knot here. Is there a meaningful difference between a tradition that grew organically and one that was deliberately constructed? And if the whole point of a community ritual is to create shared meaning, does it matter that the meaning was engineered rather than discovered? I feel like there's a deeper issue about whether 'the community' is always partly a story we tell ourselves, but I'm not sure if that means every invented story is equally valid or if there's a line where fabrication becomes harmful. Could you think through the concrete situation with me — particularly whether applying for the grant is ethically different from simply continuing the ritual privately — and please answer in British English? Also, does the fact that the pit head itself is a 1992 reconstruction change anything, or is that just the same problem one layer down?
