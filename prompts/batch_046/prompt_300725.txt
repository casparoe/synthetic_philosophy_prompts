Here's an argument I constructed for a seminar on decision theory and now find suspicious because it seems to collapse the distinction between bounded and unbounded rational obligations. I'd like to know exactly where the slip occurs, and what each repair costs.

(1) In the St. Petersburg game, payoffs are 2^k with probability 1/2^k, so the expected monetary value is Σ 1 = ∞.
(2) If rational choice maximizes expected utility and utility is linear in money, then for any finite entry fee C, the net expectation is still infinite, so paying C is rational.
(3) Call a decision "super-St. Petersburg" if it offers a non-zero probability of an outcome whose value is arbitrarily large relative to that probability—e.g., climate mitigation preventing an unbounded stream of future harms, or alignment work preventing suffering across branching timelines with non-decreasing weight.
(4) In every super-St. Petersburg case, the same arithmetic applies: the expectation dominates any finite cost, so rationality demands we pay any finite price.
(5) But rationality is resource-bounded: an agent with finite assets and a finite lifespan cannot be obligated to expend resources it does not possess, and bankruptcy (or death) eliminates the agent's capacity to realize further utility.
(6) Therefore, (4) is false: rationality does not require bankruptcy-level expenditure on low-probability, high-magnitude outcomes.
(7) The only ways to block (4) are (a) to reject linear utility and impose diminishing marginal returns everywhere, or (b) to deny that distant or hypothetical outcomes carry full present value.
(8) But if we take route (a), we undermine the rationale for longtermist priorities—preventing human extinction has enormous but not literally infinite value, and with steep enough diminishing returns it could never outweigh modest near-term costs.
(9) If we take route (b), we solve St. Petersburg without distorting longtermism, but we seem to smuggle in an arbitrary boundary between "counted" and "discounted" outcomes.
(10) So either we accept that rational agents must sometimes risk total ruin, or we pay a steep price in our theory of intergenerational or cross-modal value. Where exactly does the argument go wrong—between (3) and (4), in (5), or elsewhere? If I have to choose, I'd rather preserve (5) and (8) than accept (10), but I want the precise diagnosis and the full menu of repairs.
