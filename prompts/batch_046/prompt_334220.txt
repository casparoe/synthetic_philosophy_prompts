Here is an argument I have been developing about the Condorcet jury theorem and the epistemic authority of experts. Each step looks defensible, but the conclusion strikes me as absurd, so I want a precise diagnosis.

(1) The Condorcet jury theorem establishes that if n voters are independent and each has uniform competence p > 1/2 on a binary question, then the majority decision is more accurate than any individual voter, with collective accuracy approaching 1 as n grows.

(2) This comparison between the group and its members holds regardless of how high any particular member's competence might be, provided that member belongs to the group being aggregated.

(3) Consider a factual question Q. There is a crowd of 500 laypeople, each with competence p ≈ 0.52, and one expert with competence p ≈ 0.90 who is not initially part of the crowd.

(4) Form the group G consisting of the 500 laypeople plus the expert, so |G| = 501.

(5) Applying (1) to G, the majority decision of G is more accurate than the judgment of any individual member of G, including the expert.

(6) Because the laypeople outnumber the expert 500 to 1, the majority decision of G is necessarily identical to the majority decision of the laypeople alone.

(7) Therefore, the collective judgment of the 500 barely-competent laypeople is epistemically superior to the expert's individual judgment.

(8) Generalizing: for any expert and any sufficiently large crowd of non-experts with p just above 0.5, democratic aggregation always dominates expert judgment.

(9) If (8) is true, then peer review, scientific advisory panels, and expert testimony should always yield to popular referendum whenever the electorate exceeds a few hundred people.

(10) That is clearly false.

(11) So either independence is never satisfied in practice, or competence values are incommensurable across individuals.

If I have to choose, I prefer to preserve (1) in its standard homogeneous form and also (3). Where exactly does the reasoning fail—at the move from (4) to (5), at (6), or somewhere else? And if the correction is that Condorcet applies only to groups with uniform competence, what does that cost us in explaining why large democratic bodies are ever trustworthy on heterogeneous issues?
