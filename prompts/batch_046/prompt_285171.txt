My verdicts on idea-ownership keep feeling inconsistent and I want to see if there's actually a principle hiding behind them.

One: I think "recursive attribution debt" — using an idea forces you to credit the originator and every major refiner in a chain — is a real obligation. If someone cites only the recent refiners and skips the originator, that feels like a worse violation than citing the originator but missing a middle link. But if they cite everyone except one obscure refiner, I barely care.

Two: Two engineers independantly design the same circuit layout. One patents first; the second genuinely never heard of it. I still think the patent should block the second, but if the layout was technically inevitable given current tools, then I think neither should own it.

Three: Sampling a three-second drum loop and building a hit around it is theft. But copying the hidden-clue architecture of an obscure 1920s mystery — same narrative structure, every word and character changed — feels totally fine, even though that structure is arguably more original and distinctive than the drum loop.

Four: A company takes open-source code, wraps it in a proprietary API, and sells access. The license allows it, yet I still feel there's a kind of enclosure happening that violates something, even if it's not legally wrong.

Five: An AI is trained on an author's whole corpus and now writes perfect pastiche for free. I think the author deserves compensation, but I also don't think they "own" their style, since a lot of it was unconscious.

So is there a coherent principle — maybe about labor-embodiment versus functional idea — that saves cases three and four but has to drop one or five? What does it predict for a scientist who patents a law of nature and tries to license its use in textbooks?
