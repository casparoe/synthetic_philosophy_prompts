Analyze the U.S. Treasury and Federal Reserve’s decision not to backstop Lehman Brothers in September 2008 as a case study in moral particularism versus generalism. Here are the salient facts: in March 2008 the authorities had engineered a private acquisition of Bear Stearns with Fed financing; on September 15, 2008 they permitted Lehman to file for Chapter 11 bankruptcy; and on September 16 they committed $85 billion to rescue AIG. Officials subsequently defended the Lehman decision by citing both a general principle—avoiding moral hazard by not rewarding excessive risk—and particular contextual constraints: the absence of a viable private buyer, the poor quality of Lehman’s collateral, and disputed legal authority under Section 13(3) of the Federal Reserve Act. At the same time, the Bear Stearns and AIG interventions seemed to violate that very principle, suggesting the reasoning was sensitive to features unique to each institution—counterparty exposure, systemic interconnectedness, and available collateral—rather than to the uniform application of a rule.
