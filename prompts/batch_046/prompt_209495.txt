After a long and rather heated argument with a friend about whether rational agents must treat their future selves as parts of a single decision-maker, I wish to defend a view I am calling intertemporal pluralism: that diachronic money-pump arguments illegitimately presuppose a normative commitment to lifetime utility aggregation, and that an agent with genuinely non-transitive, stage-local preferences can rationally resist being pumped without violating any synchronic coherence constraint. I am presenting this at a workshop on decision theory and philosophy of action whose audience consists mainly of behavioural economists, formal epistemologists, and philosophers of action who treat Savage-style axioms as the default framework and regard Dutch-book arguments against diachronic inconsistency as decisive; they care about axiomatic rigour, empirical plausibility, and whether my position collapses into either standard hyperbolic discounting or straightforward irrationality. Prepare me for the objections I will actually face from this room: order them by how damaging they are likely to be, phrase each in the vocabulary this audience would use, provide a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes, and flag precisely where that reply is soft. Indicate what I should concede upfront in order to retain credibility. Finally, include the objection I am most hoping nobody raises—namely, the challenge that my view does not merely relax an axiom but renders long-term planning conceptually impossible rather than merely non-axiomatic. Please write the answer in British English.
