I write novels and think about stories in the technical vocabulary of narrative craft—plot causality, character arc, world-building density, genre convention, temporal framing, and the economy of revelation. Please restate Friedrich Nietzsche's analysis of the three modes by which history serves or harms life—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—entirely within that vocabulary. For monumental history, translate it into terms of heroic archetype, foundational myth, or aspirational character model; for antiquarian, into archival world-building, continuity maintenance, and the preservation of canonical detail across narrative time; for critical, into revisionist retelling, anachronistic framing, or the deliberate rupture of inherited plotlines. For each mode, report which of Nietzsche's claims survive without distortion, which must be bent to fit craft terminology (for example, whether his physiological and life-affirming criteria become mere questions of reader affect or compositional balance), and which have no expression at all in a novelist's framework—especially those touching the collective health or sickness of a culture's relationship to its past. Conclude by judging whether the untranslated residue reveals a limitation in narrative craft's account of memory and tradition, or whether it exposes an imprecision in Nietzsche's original distinction that only shows up under this kind of technical pressure. Please keep the discussion formal and conceptual; I do not want a bibliography or citation list, only the translation and the residue report.
