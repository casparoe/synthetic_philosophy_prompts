I'm trying to work out my actual view on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia by seeing what principle, if any, captures my judgments across cases. Here are my verdicts.

Case 1: A physicist completes a long derivation of result R that she can fully reproduce step by step. She then learns that physicists with her training background have about a 40% error rate on derivations of this length and complexity. She re-checks her work, finds no mistake, and her reasoning trace is fully transparent to her. Verdict: she should maintain full confidence in R; reducing confidence purely because of the statistical base rate would be a mistake.

Case 2: A physician makes a diagnosis of condition C based on clinical intuition she cannot fully articulate or reproduce in detail. She then learns that doctors at her hospital misdiagnose C in roughly 35% of cases. She has no specific token reasoning path she can audit. Verdict: she should significantly reduce her confidence in C, even if she still acts on it for practical reasons; maintaining her original confidence would be epistemically akratic.

Case 3: A policy analyst supports policy P on the basis of a cost-benefit model she constructed. She discovers higher-order evidence that analysts with her political leanings systematically underestimate costs in this domain. She audits her model, finds one parameter that was anchored too low, corrects it, and P still comes out ahead, though by a much narrower margin. Verdict: she should continue to endorse P but with confidence reduced proportionally to the corrected margin; she is not akratic.

Case 4: A juror believes the defendant is guilty based on direct evaluation of testimony. During deliberation she realizes she initially weighted the defendant's nervous demeanor more heavily than she should have, but she cannot determine whether that weighting actually affected her final credibility assessment—her reasoning trace is partially opaque to her. Verdict: she should suspend judgment on guilt, not merely reduce confidence; maintaining the belief while acknowledging an unlocalized bias is a form of akrasia.

Cases 1 and 4 seem hard to reconcile: in both there is potential bias and an incomplete audit, yet in Case 1 I want full maintenance and in Case 4 suspension. The distinction I reach for is reason-trace transparency—whether the agent can access the token causal path of her reasoning—but I worry this just relabels the tension rather than resolving it.

What principle, if any, delivers all four verdicts? If none does, which verdict should I overturn and why? Please state the principle precisely, identify the clause that handles each case, and be explicit about any verdict it cannot accommodate.

Also, apply it to a case I haven't listed: an AI forecasting system outputs prediction X with 90% confidence, but its developers show that the system's calibration is poor specifically in domains where its internal reasoning trace is opaque to human auditors. Should the system's users treat X as held in epistemic escrow—available for action but not genuinely endorsed—until the trace is made transparent? Or does the transparency distinction collapse here?
