My study-group friends actualy got stuck arguing about backward induction in epistemic game theory. One said that in any finite perfect-information game, mutual knowledge of rationality stacked up to the tree's depth does all the work—full common knowledge is redundant because there's no infinite regress to break. The other said that's circular: if rationality means reasoning about others reasoning, then stacking beliefs consistently already smuggles in the common-knowledge closure you're denying.

First friend: "But if the game only has four moves, I just need you to know I'm rational, me to know you know, and so on out to level four. That's just finite-order beliefs, not some magic infinite state."

Second friend: "Sure, but keeping those levels aligned requires that at every step I trust you can do the same stacking. If that trust isn't common, then at move three I might worry you think I think you'll defect, but you actualy don't think I think that—so induction stalls."

First friend: "Then you're baking the conclusion into the definition of rationality. If rationality is just best-response given your beliefs, all I need is correct beliefs at each node, not a shared infinite mirror."

Second friend: "But 'correct beliefs' in an interactive setting just are the ones matching the common-knowledge hierarchy. Otherwise rational players can have mismatched models and the induction collapses."

Who gets the equivalence claim right, and does defining rationality as best-response avoid the problem? Also please seperate what's settled here from what's genuinely contested among people who know the topic—don't present either side as the obvious consensus.
