I cover philosophy of science and ethics for a publication that tracks conceptual debates at the margins of mainstream discourse. I am drafting an essay on whether a non-emergentist panpsychism—the view that proto-experiential properties are fundamental rather than derived from complex biological organization—generates an ethical obligation toward quantum or field-level processes. Many commentators slide quickly from “all matter instantiates some form of experience” to “the universe therefore contains unimaginable suffering,” but I think that inference collapses unless one can establish that harm is possible in the absence of a diachronic subject capable of being made worse off across time. For my piece, please construct a rigorous argument for the following position: fundamental physical entities, lacking the temporal persistence and narrative continuity required for personal identity, cannot be subjects of harm even if they possess proto-experiential properties; consequently, panpsychism does not entail cosmic-scale suffering. In developing the argument, draw an explicit structural comparison to the reproductive-ethics asymmetry between harming an existing person and merely failing to create a potential one—use that comparison to show why the absence of a persisting subject blocks the harm attribution at the fundamental level, rather than simply lowering the intensity of experience. Do not ground the distinction in the claim that only biological organisms can suffer; instead, root it in the conditions necessary for diachronic welfare comparison. Keep the reasoning accessible to readers familiar with philosophy of mind but avoid leaning on the technical apparatus of integrated information theory or quantum-consciousness models.
