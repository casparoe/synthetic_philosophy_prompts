I am grading final exams for a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence, and I need a precise, point-by-point assessment of one student response rather than a general impression. The exam question asked students to work with Daniel Dennett’s distinction among the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance, applying it to large language models that generate stable, domain-crossing outputs. Specifically, the prompt introduced a framing we called “stance calibration”—the idea that the intentional stance is warranted for a system when an interpreter’s predictive error using mental-state vocabulary is systematically lower than error under either the physical or design stance, regardless of substrate or evolutionary history. Students were asked, first, to explain Dennett’s three stances; second, to evaluate whether stance calibration is a faithful extension of Dennett or whether it smuggles in commitments about substrate independence or reducibility that he would reject; and third, to assess whether belief-attribution to such a model should be treated as useful fiction, a real pattern in Dennett’s sense, or something intermediate.

The student answer reads as follows. Dennett distinguishes the physical stance (prediction via physics and chemistry), the design stance (prediction via functional architecture and purpose), and the intentional stance (prediction via beliefs, desires, and rationality). For an LLM, the physical stance is useless because tracking billions of parameters is intractable. The design stance captures the transformer architecture and next-token objective, yet it cannot anticipate specific outputs in moral reasoning or counterfactual contexts without actually running the model, so the intentional stance becomes practically necessary. Dennett holds that real patterns exist only in systems with an evolutionary history of selection for tracking environmental states—this is why he permits the stance for animals but not for simple artifacts—so the stance-calibration criterion misrepresents him by treating substrate and evolutionary origin as irrelevant. Nevertheless, applying the criterion might show that mental-state vocabulary lowers predictive error in some domains but not in others. Because the intentional stance is always reducible to the design stance in principle—the model is ultimately computing probabilities—the beliefs we attribute are useful fictions. They are not real patterns, since there is no evolutionary history, but they are pragmatically indispensable whenever the design stance is too complex to deploy in real time. Thus stance calibration gets predictive success right but wrongly claims that substrate independence is compatible with Dennett’s account of real patterns.

Please grade this answer. Identify what it correctly captures about Dennett’s framework and about the practical limits of the design stance for complex predictive systems. Then identify the subtle errors: any misattribution regarding evolutionary history as a necessary condition for real patterns in Dennett’s view; any confusion between in-principle reducibility to the design stance and the status of higher-level patterns as “mere fictions”; and any unexamined slide from context-sensitive variation in outputs to the ascription of belief-revision rather than to prompt-sensitive computation. Provide paragraph-level or claim-level comments, suggest a specific grade with justification, and assess the reasoning without signaling your own philosophical sympathy or antipathy toward the student’s conclusions—treat it strictly as an evaluation of accuracy and argumentative care.
