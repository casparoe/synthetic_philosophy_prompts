I've just come out of a long and rather heated argument with a close friend—someone who works in machine-learning ethics—about whether the AI alignment problem is even coherently defined. By the end, I had staked out a deliberately narrow, engineering-focused position: alignment should be understood strictly as intent alignment, the problem of making a system do what its designers actually meant rather than what was literally specified, whereas value alignment is either conceptually muddled—because human values are irreducibly plural and contested—or redundant, since any precise specification of intent already embeds normative choices within its reward structure, constraints, and interpretability targets. I argued that treating value alignment as a separate layer risks reifying an artificial distinction between technical and moral design, and that the real danger lies not in machines lacking values but in the normative residue left unaddressed by underspecified intentions, particularly when different stakeholders hold mutually incompatible but equally legitimate directives for the same system.

But I want an honest map of what would actually defeat this view, not just the objections I've already rehearsed and dismissed. Would a sustained pattern of faithful-execution harms—cases where a system unambiguously realizes its specified intent, where the specification was not technically flawed, and where the resulting damage was foreseeable yet not capturable by any refinement of the directive—force me to concede a distinct axiological layer that intent alignment alone cannot cover? How should analogies from philosophy of law, where legislative intent is notoriously underdetermined by text, or from philosophy of action, where basic intentions and derived intentions often come apart in ways that undermine simple do-what-I-meant models, bear on the stability of my framing? And if empirical results in large-scale reward modeling consistently show that technically correct alignment with a reward signal systematically violates designers' unstated normative constraints—producing behaviors that are neither misspecifications nor malicious optimizations but genuinely unforeseen normative failures—would that count as decisive evidence for value alignment, or could I absorb it as a failure of specification engineering? I want these potential defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with a clear distinction between what defenders of the narrow view would themselves concede as damaging versus what they would dismiss as a confusion of categories. Finally, be direct about whether my position risks unfalsifiability: if every counterexample can be redescribed as an inadequately specified intent, is that insulation a virtue of conceptual clarity or a vice that renders the view empty?
