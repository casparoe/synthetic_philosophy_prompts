I'm kinda stuck on the opening remarks for our interdiscplinary workshop next Tuesday—"State, Conflict, and Civic Life." The audiance is split right down the middle: half are grad students who just finished the Philosophy of Right, half are local administrators and vets who've never read a line of Hegel. I want the talk to actually turn on Hegel's view that war isn't just policy failure but has this weird ethical function for the state, pulling citizens out of private self-interest and affirming that the state is an end in itself rather than a service provider. But I have to frame it so I'm not glorifying violence, because the practitioners in the room will rightly walk out if I sound like I'm cheerleading for bloodshed.

I also want to inject the less-textbook part of his argument: his specific rejection of Kant's perpetual peace, not because Kant is naive, but because Hegel thinks eternal peace would actually corrupt civic virtue and let the state dissolve into bureaucratic egoism—like a body that never exercises. So the remarks need to do real structural work: they should let us talk about modern conflict without falling into either a pacifist "war is always irrational" frame or a realist "war is just power" frame, and they should show why Hegel's position is weirder than either. Write me about seven minutes of remarks that land for the Hegel people—who'll notice if I conflate the state's ethical life with mere nationalism—without leaving the non-philosophers behind. No citations, no reading list, just the argument built into the occasion.
