In discussions of whether Buddhist ethics is consequentialist, I have noticed a recurring defensive pattern that does not map neatly onto familiar classifications. When critics observe that Buddhist evaluation of individual action centers on the agent’s intention—so that identical external conduct can carry different karmic weight depending on mental state—defenders emphasize that this is not outcome-indifferent, because the cultivation of pure intention is precisely the most reliable means of reducing suffering over time. Yet when critics point to institutional or communal judgments—changes to monastic discipline justified by harm to the community, or royal policies evaluated by their effect on social harmony—the same interlocutors treat welfare outcomes as the direct and sufficient criterion, with little mention of the mental states of the decision-makers. The result is an unprincipled oscillation: agent-centered evaluation governs the individual case, outcome-centered evaluation governs the institutional case, and there is no explicit meta-rule specifying which framework applies where, or how they are integrated. I have seen this in debates over whether breaking a precept is ever permitted for the greater good (where defenders invoke intention-based constraints) and in debates over why rules were modified after harm occurred (where defenders invoke outcome-based justification).

Does this specific configuration—the simultaneous deployment of an agent-centered and an outcome-centered evaluative framework, segmented by level of analysis and used defensively without an articulated bridge principle—have an established name in philosophy of religion or moral theory? Or does it fall under a broader concept such as theoretical pluralism, two-level consequentialism, or moral particularism, and if so, how does the Buddhist case differ in virtue of its explicit soteriological telos? If there is no precise label, how should its boundary conditions be drawn: what distinguishes it from a genuinely integrated account like rule-utilitarianism, and what distinguishes it from mere ad hoc inconsistency?
