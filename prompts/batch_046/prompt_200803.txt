I spent the better part of yesterday evening arguing with a close friend about whether omissions can genuinely figure in causal explanations, and I find myself unable to shake a confusion that I suspect is driving our disagreement. We began with the familiar case: a lifeguard fails to rescue a drowning swimmer, and we want to say the lifeguard's omission caused the death. My friend, who takes a fairly physicalist line on causation, argued that this is merely a façon de parler—what actually caused the death was the water, the swimmer's physiology, and the absence of any counteracting force, but the lifeguard's failure is just a gap in the causal history, not a cause itself. I pushed back by suggesting that if we accept counterfactual dependence—if the swimmer would have lived had the lifeguard acted—then the omission must be doing real explanatory work. But then my friend pressed me on what the mechanism is: if causation is fundamentally a matter of energy transfer or physical interaction, what exactly is interacting when someone does nothing? I tried to answer that the omission is causally relevant because it violates a normative expectation that, if satisfied, would have interrupted the fatal sequence, but he replied that this makes causation depend on moral norms, which sounds like a category mistake.

Our argument stalled because I kept feeling that there must be some middle ground—perhaps omissions are not "negative events" with ghostly causal powers, but they are also not mere absences that we describe causally for convenience. Specifically, I started wondering whether the mistake is to think that omissions need a uniform metaphysical status at all: maybe some omissions are genuinely causal in virtue of being embedded in a network of counterfactual dependencies and institutional roles, without being reducible to positive mental states or obligations on the one hand, or being mysterious non-entities on the other. But I cannot quite articulate what that middle ground is without either smuggling in physical interaction where there is none, or collapsing omission-talk into talk about the agent's beliefs and duties.

So here is my question, which I suspect rests on a false presupposition I have not identified: if omissions are not reducible to positive causes and are not sui generis negative entities with their own physical mechanisms, in what precise sense can they be said to cause outcomes? Is there a coherent metaphysical account—perhaps one that separates causal explanation from causal production—that allows omissions to do genuine explanatory work without committing us to an ontology of absences? Or is the very demand for such an account misguided because it treats "omission" as a single natural kind, when in fact our causal attributions to omissions depend on context-specific normative and counterfactual structures that do not need to be unified under one metaphysical theory?
