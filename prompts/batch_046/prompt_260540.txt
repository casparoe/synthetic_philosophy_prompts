As a retired biophysicist who has only recently turned to serious study of moral philosophy, I am serving this term as an external grader for a graduate seminar on the ethics of non-human consciousness. I have before me a student response to the examination question below; because I am still learning to distinguish subtle errors in philosophical reasoning from sound argumentation, I should be grateful for your detailed assessment.

Question posed to the student:
Consider the scale argument regarding insect sentience: even if the prior probability that any individual insect possesses phenomenal consciousness is low—say, on the order of one percent—the global population of insects at any given moment is approximately 10^19, yielding an enormous aggregate expected moral weight. A recent proposal, which I shall call the density-adjusted expectation thesis, holds that we ought not weight this expectation by raw cardinality alone, but by a coefficient κ reflecting the “moral patienthood density” of the ecological niche—specifically, the stability of the causal chain linking harm-events to persisting subjects, scaled against biomass turnover. On this view, high-turnover populations dilute expected moral weight not because the individuals lack value, but because the causal structure of harm is distributed across successive generations rather than concentrated in enduring subjects.

Evaluate whether this density adjustment undermines the scale argument. In particular: (a) does the thesis correctly identify why high-cardinality, low-persistence populations pose a distinctive ethical problem, or does it conflate causal distribution with some other condition for moral patienthood? (b) Assuming a coefficient κ ≈ 10⁻⁴ derived from turnover-to-neural-mass scaling, does the adjusted framework still imply that insects collectively outweigh mammalian populations in moral priority? Provide your reasoning with explicit attention to how the scaling factor should be applied—whether multiplicatively to aggregate expectation, divisively to cardinality, or otherwise—and address whether applying κ consistently across niches requires treating mammalian populations as homogeneous.

Student's answer:
The scale argument derives its force from linear expectation: E = p × N, so with p ≈ 0.01 and N ≈ 10^19 we obtain E ≈ 10^17. The density-adjusted expectation thesis blocks this by introducing κ, the moral patienthood density of the niche. Because insects exhibit rapid generational turnover, κ is low—approximately 10⁻⁴—reflecting the fact that harm is distributed across transient subjects rather than integrated within persisting ones. For harm to carry full moral weight, a subject must persist long enough to integrate deprivation into a continuous experiential stream; since individual insects live only days or weeks, they fall below the temporal integration threshold required for robust patienthood. Applying κ multiplicatively to the aggregate expectation yields E_adj ≈ κ(pN) ≈ 10¹³, which still exceeds the total mammalian expectation—where N ≈ 10^10, p ≈ 1, and κ ≈ 1—giving E ≈ 10^10, so insects remain dominant by three orders of magnitude.

The thesis is not a collective-rights view. It does not assign moral status to ecosystems or species; it merely adjusts individual weights according to niche dynamics. The error in the unadjusted argument is treating N as a homogeneous pool of simultaneous sufferers, when in fact the population is a rotating sequence. Once κ corrects for this rotation, the ethical calculus becomes tractable. The student should concede that density adjustment weakens but does not reverse the priority ordering, and should recognize that any further reduction in κ for insects would require empirical evidence about neural architecture that we currently lack.
