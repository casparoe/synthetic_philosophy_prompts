As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I have been trying to map Malcolm's argument about dreams onto a state-verification framework I use professionally—specifically, whether a mental state can be validated by an independent observer in real time or only reconstructed from logs after the fact. I believe I have constructed a reductio that proves too much, but I cannot locate the exact leak. Here is the chain:

(1) For any mental state to count as a genuine experience, there must be an independent verification condition that applies to the state while it is occurring, not merely a retrospective report reconstructed after the state ends. (This captures Malcolm's core challenge: dream reports are the only data available, and they are post-hoc reconstructions.)

(2) During sleep, no independent observer—nor the sleeper herself—can verify the qualitative content of the mental state in real time; the only available evidence is the report given upon waking. So dreams fail condition (1).

(3) Therefore, if (1) holds, dreams are not experiences.

(4) Consider now a brief waking auditory experience: a pure tone presented for 150 milliseconds in a controlled setting. During that 150ms window, neither an external observer nor the subject can perform an independent verification of the specific qualitative character of the experience; verification is available only via the subject's immediate post-stimulus report or via neuroimaging logs reconstructed afterward.

(5) So this brief perceptual episode also lacks an independent verification condition applied during its occurrence; it satisfies only the retrospective-log criterion.

(6) By parity of reasoning, if dreams fail (1), then all transient conscious episodes of comparable temporal boundedness also fail (1).

(7) But it is plainly false that a subject does not genuinely experience a 150ms tone.

(8) Therefore (1) is false, and dreams should be admitted as experiences.

(9) Dennett's well-known response—that dreams are not experiences during sleep but narratives constructed upon waking—avoids the reductio by denying that there is any state in sleep requiring verification. Yet if we apply that move consistently, any mental state that is not continuously monitored must be treated as a post-hoc construction. That collapses the distinction between veridical brief perception and confabulated memory, which seems to undermine rather than preserve the reliability of waking report.

(10) Hence either we reject (1) and keep dreams as experiences, or we keep (1) and accept that all unmonitored transient states are narrative constructions.

Where does this argument go wrong? I suspect the flaw lies in (4), where I treat the temporal inaccessibility of a brief tone as equivalent to the logical inaccessibility of a dream state, but I want to be precise: is the error an equivocation between "independent of the subject's report" and "independent of retrospective reconstruction"? Or is the real cost in (9), because Dennett's position can be restricted to sleep-specific mechanisms without generalizing to waking perception? If I must choose, I would rather preserve (1) in some modified form than accept (8), but only if I can avoid the collapse described in (9). What is the least damaging repair, and which premise should I actually give up?
