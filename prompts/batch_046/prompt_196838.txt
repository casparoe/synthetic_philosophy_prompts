I am a junior in my school's advanced philosophy seminar, and I also sit on the Student Policy Review Board, where we evaluate proposed regulations—dress codes, attendance mandates, and speech guidelines—before they reach the administration. Last week my friends and I spent nearly an hour arguing whether a mandatory community-service graduation requirement restricts negative liberty, builds positive liberty, or creates republican domination, and we realized we have no consistent method for classifying these cases. I need a reusable, ten-minute decision procedure that a board member without graduate training can apply repeatedly, sign off on, and defend in writing; it must operationalize Isaiah Berlin’s negative and positive liberty together with Philip Pettit’s republican liberty understood specifically through a reversal-test for arbitrary domination—whether the authority could impose the same burden on itself without hypocrisy—and through Amartya Sen’s distinction between formal freedom and effective capability. The procedure must handle ordinary school-policy proposals, include one worked example in which it classifies a popular rule in a way my board will not want to accept, flag every point where judgment cannot be eliminated by the rule itself, and state plainly what questions lie outside its reach.

Please deliver your answer as exactly five numbered items, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Within those five items, present the ordered procedural tests or checkpoints in sequence, embed both worked applications including the unwelcome classification, mark the judgment-dependent boundary explicitly, and reserve the final item to state the procedure’s hard limits and predictable misfires.
