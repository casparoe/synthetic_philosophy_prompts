I am giving a seminar paper in a joint philosophy-of-mathematics and philosophy-of-science workshop defending a specific response to the hypothetical discovery that ZFC is inconsistent. My thesis has two parts. First, rather than seeking a single successor foundation—whether a weakened set theory, a type-theoretic framework, or a large-cardinal hierarchy—mathematicians should adopt a "regional consistency" model in which arithmetic, real analysis, and set-theoretic mathematics operate under independently justified, locally sufficient axiom sets, with translation protocols between regions treated as pragmatic engineering problems rather than demands for global unification. Second, this response is philosophically defensible because mathematical axioms are best understood as regulative norms that authorize inferential practices, not as empirical conjectures about an abstract reality; consequently, the pessimistic meta-induction that undermines scientific realism does not apply to mathematics with the same force.

The audience is precisely this: a formal logician specializing in reverse mathematics and proof-theoretic strength; a philosopher of science who applies Laudan-style pessimistic meta-induction broadly across formal and empirical sciences; a working mathematician in real analysis who regards foundations as professionally irrelevant; and a historian of mathematics who studies how the early-twentieth-century foundational crisis was actually resolved institutionally. They are skeptical but not hostile; they care about whether my view respects actual mathematical practice, whether it collapses into relativism, and whether it misunderstands the logicist project.

Prepare me for the Q&A. List the objections I will actually face, in roughly the order they will arise, phrased in the vocabulary and with the commitments of the specific audience members described above. For each objection, indicate whether it has real force, what I should concede up front, what I should hold firmly, and where my reply is weakest. Include a brief suggested reply for each. Identify the one objection—the one I am hoping nobody asks—that would do the most damage to my position, explain why it is dangerous, and draft a two-minute response that does not rely on claiming that mathematicians already do this. Finally, flag the weakest point in my own argument that I should prepare to defend actively rather than hope goes unnoticed.
