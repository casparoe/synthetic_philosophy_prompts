I recently began advising a small archive that is digitizing handwritten correspondence from the 1940s, and the practical problems of that work have made me return to Douglas Hofstadter's tripartite distinction in Gödel, Escher, Bach among frame messages (the physical substrate), outer messages (the readable symbol sequence), and inner messages (the meaning that depends on an appropriate decoder). I am drafting an essay that applies this scheme to digitized personal letters. The central claim is that digitization does not simply preserve or destroy the inner message, but relocates it. The frame message is preserved in the pixel grid, and the outer message survives with some degradation from handwriting recognition errors; however, the inner message originally depended on material irregularities—ink pressure, paper texture, marginal stains, the spatial layout of the page—that a scan records visually but strips of haptic and contextual significance. Consequently, the digitized letter carries approximately the same outer message but directs it toward a different decoder: not the intimate recipient, but the historian or archivist. The essay further proposes that this relocation challenges any assumption that inner messages are fixed contents waiting to be extracted; instead, they may be relational properties that shift when the interpreting community changes.

I also address a counter-argument that these material features belong to the frame message rather than contributing to the inner message, and I try to show that in Hofstadter's own examples—phonograph records, DNA—the frame and outer messages are structurally coupled in ways that affect what the inner message can be.

Please comment on these notes and on the essay's shape. I am especially interested in whether the claim that digitization relocates inner meaning is coherent, whether my use of Hofstadter's framework is faithful or distorts it for archival interpretation, and whether the counter-argument about material context being part of the frame message is adequately handled. Please say what would change your assessment: for example, if Hofstadter explicitly treats textual or archival interpretation elsewhere in the book in a way that undermines my relational reading, or if there is a decisive reason to think that changing the decoder produces failed decoding rather than a genuinely different inner message. Finally, please identify where my reasoning is most likely to fail—particularly in assuming consistency across Hofstadter's examples, or in overreading the significance of material context for personal correspondence.
