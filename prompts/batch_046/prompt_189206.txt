Write a deadpan parody of an end-user license agreement for "AffectOS," a fictional subscription service that claims to classify users' emotional states according to whether they are cognitive judgments, perceptual detections, or bare phenomenal feelings. Format the piece as approximately 600 words of numbered legal clauses and subsections, written in the hyper-precise, overconfident voice of a Silicon Valley legal team that does not realize it is smuggling metaphysics into contract law. The satire should quietly embed real philosophical tensions without naming any philosophers or using explicit academic jargon: include a "Propositional Truth Warranty" that offers refunds if a user's fear turns out to be based on a false belief (thereby treating emotion as judgment), a "Non-Conceptual Calibration Protocol" that requires users to align their emotional responses with sensory input before cognition can "contaminate" them (thereby treating emotion as perception), and a "Phenomenal Qualia Waiver" that denies ownership of raw feeling unless the user upgrades to a premium tier (thereby treating feeling as detachable substrate). Without ever stating the objections outright, the clauses should expose them—e.g., that a false judgment about danger is not identical to the absence of fear, that jealousy requires conceptual framing that pure perception cannot supply, and that stripping away cognition and intentionality leaves only bodily disturbance rather than emotion. Invent a specific niche provision, "Clause 8.2: The Recursive Meta-Emotion Exemption," addressing whether being embarrassed about one's own embarrassment requires a second-order subscription, and let its resolution quietly contradict the first-order taxonomy the agreement elsewhere defends. The document should be readable to a layperson, but a specialist in philosophy of mind should recognize that every absurd contractual obligation tracks an actual live debate about intentionality, cognitive penetration, and the ontology of affect.
