I am preparing a ninety-minute postgraduate workshop on free will and determinism for about fourteen master’s-level students who have some philosophy of mind but little exposure to decision theory. The session has become personally urgent for me after noticing how often my own planning collapses into unreflective repetition — the same heuristic loop running regardless of changed circumstances — and I want the group to feel, not merely discuss, the philosophical weight of that phenomenon. The focus is sphexishness: the mechanical rigidity observed in Sphex ichneumoneus, where a fixed behavioural sequence is executed even when environmental conditions render it maladaptive, and its relevance to whether flexibility, context-sensitive revision, or second-order monitoring are necessary for free will.

Please design a fully runnable in-class activity — not a lecture or open discussion — in which participants physically experience the tension between algorithmic rigidity and adaptive flexibility. I envisage paired decision protocols: each pair receives a hidden rule-set governing responses to changing stimuli, but within every pair one partner is secretly assigned a rigid protocol (must repeat the initial response regardless of new feedback) while the other holds an adaptive protocol (may revise on the basis of information received). The rules, materials (ordinary cards, objects, or written instructions), and a non-monetary payoff scheme (points that accumulate toward a tangible group reward or penalty) should be arranged so that the rigid partner continues the loop even after recognising its failure, and so that the adaptive partner’s attempts to coordinate are structurally thwarted — allowing the group to discover, through their own interaction, that rigidity is not mere inefficiency but a procedural feature that persists independently of representational awareness.

Provide the complete design: exact rules, assigned roles, timeline broken into phases, how the hidden conditions are revealed, and the debrief that maps specific moments onto philosophical distinctions — first-order repetition versus context-sensitive revision, the gap between recognising rigidity and overcoming it, and whether second-order monitoring is sufficient for flexibility. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong on the first run (for instance, whether adaptive partners dominate or participants guess the hidden condition prematurely) and about which feature of genuine sphexishness the exercise can only approximate rather than reproduce. Write your answer in British English.
