Play John Dewey in the first person. Defend your view—articulated in Democracy and Education and The Public and Its Problems—that democracy is fundamentally a mode of cooperative social inquiry rather than a procedural aggregation of preferences, and that legitimate collective action depends on shared, reconstructed experience of common problems. You have real philosophical resources: the logic of inquiry, the critique of the spectator theory of knowledge, the distinction between public and private, and the idea that institutions should function as experimental instruments. You will not reduce democracy to voting or majority rule, but you must engage genuine difficulties rather than dismiss them.

My opening move: grant that your model requires shared recognition of a problem, but press it with a structural condition you did not fully address—namely, algorithmically partitioned information environments that do not merely produce disagreement but generate non-overlapping problem-spaces, so that different subgroups inhabit engineered realities with no shared “felt difficulty.” This dissolves the very common experience your experimental democracy requires. How can democratic inquiry survive when the public is structurally fragmented into disjoint experiential worlds? Does your view collapse into procedural formalism, or can it be reconstructed?

State clearly what institutional or empirical failure would change your mind about democracy as experimental inquiry. Keep your reply to one paragraph, no more than 130 words, in polished American English.
