I've been thinking about what makes every 'a' an 'a', partly after playing with the idea behind Letter Spirit — the program that generates alphabet styles — and partly after watching my kid draw letters in weird ways and still correct them. Here are my gut verdicts on cases. Most feel obvious, but a couple pull in opposite directions and I can't tell if I'm being consistent.

A standard double-decker printed 'a': yep, that's an 'a'.
A handwriten single-story circle-with-tail: yep, still an 'a'.
The Greek alpha (α): not an 'a', even though it looks almost identical to the one above.
The phonetic symbol ɑ (open back vowel): not an 'a' either — it's its own symbol — even though in some fonts it's literally the same glyph as the handwriten single-story one.
My kid draws an oval with a stick through it, missing the tail: still an 'a', she meant it.
The exacct same oval-and-stick drawn randomly on a napkin: not an 'a'.
A fancy decorative 'a' with loops and a long tail but the basic bowl-and-stem structure intact: yes, an 'a'.
An abstract painting with a shape that accidentally traces an 'a' outline but is meant to be a bird: not an 'a'.
The letter 'a' rotated 90 degrees in 3D space: still an 'a'.
A brand-new gridfont-style symbol generated by algorithm, never seen before, but with the bowl-and-stem relation typical of 'a's in that family: yes, I'd call it an 'a'.

So is the principle shape? Intent? Alphabet membership? Some kind of famly-resemblance or structural relation? I keep going back and forth on the handwriten single-story vs. the phonetic ɑ — they can be identical shapes, but I want one to be an 'a' and the other not. Does that mean I have to drop one of those verdicts? And what does your principle say about a symbol from an alien writing system that happens to have the exact bowl-and-stem form?
