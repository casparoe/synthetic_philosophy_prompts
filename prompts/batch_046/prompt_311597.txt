I'm not an academic, but for the last two years I've been coordinating a small neighborhood mutual-aid group that distributes emergency supplies—food, medicine, temporary shelter—after local flooding events. We never have enough goods for every request, and I've been trying to turn my half-formed intuition about fairness into something my volunteer team can actually use. I recently came across the idea called prioritarianism, which seems to say that benefits to the worse-off should count for more, and also the leveling-down objection, which warns that it's wrong to make everyone worse off just to make them equal. I want you to translate these ideas into a concrete, reusable decision procedure that my team can run when a shipment arrives: the questions to ask in fixed order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the exact point where the procedure returns a clear "do not distribute in this pattern," and—crucially—the steps where human judgment must take over because the principle itself doesn't decide. Our situation adds a twist that the standard examples I found don't cover: we often face rolling crises, where distributing everything now to equalize present suffering might push everyone—including those currently worst off—below a minimal dignity floor when the next wave hits, whereas holding a reserve keeps the worst-off barely above that floor but requires denying aid to people who are suffering now but are not the absolute worst off. Please build this intertemporal dignity-floor constraint directly into the procedure.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals we actually face. First, distributing water-purification tablets after a flood where half the households have severely contaminated wells and half have partially clean sources, with enough tablets for only one group but a second contamination event likely within a week. Second, allocating temporary heating fuel to a shelter where giving every resident an equal share would keep all just above hypothermia risk but exhaust our stock before a forecast cold snap, whereas concentrating fuel on the most vulnerable now leaves others cold immediately. Third, a proposal to deny all aid to a family that is not among the worst-off—even though they include infants with moderate risk—in order to preserve reserves for an anticipated worse crisis; this should be the case where the procedure yields an answer my team will probably dislike. For each case, show the procedure's output step by step and flag where it stalls.

Finally, give me an honest residue report: which parts of the philosophical debate—such as whether equality has value independent of welfare, whether prioritarian weights should be continuous or step-based, or how to compare harms across different people—could not be captured in the checklist without distorting them, and whether those omissions mean the procedure is genuinely unfair or just practically limited. I want to know whether the leveling-down objection retains any real force when the "leveling" happens across time rather than across people at a single moment, or whether that temporal shift makes the objection disappear into something else entirely.
