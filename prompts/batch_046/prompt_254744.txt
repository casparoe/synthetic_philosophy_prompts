I am writing as an undergraduate in a seminar on the moral economy of welfare and social control, and I have encountered a conceptual difficulty in a primary source from the early nineteenth century. The text is an 1834 commission report addressing the administration of poor relief, and it advances the principle that relief should be confined to workhouses whose conditions are deliberately made worse than the worst conditions available to an independent laborer outside. The commissioners state: "Into such a house none will enter voluntarily; work, confinement, and discipline, will deter the indolent and vicious; and nothing but extreme necessity will induce any to accept the comfort which must be obtained by the surrender of their free agency, and the sacrifice of their accustomed habits and gratifications." They further maintain that separating spouses within these institutions is necessary for proper regulation.

What I find difficult is the precise logical structure of the argument. The commissioners appear to be using the harshness of the workhouse simultaneously as an information-revealing mechanism—screening the genuinely destitute from the able-bodied who might otherwise claim relief, in the manner of an adverse-selection solution—and as a punitive deterrent designed to prevent the formation of dependency preferences, which sounds more like a moral-hazard framework. I cannot tell whether these two functions are conceptually distinct in their reasoning or whether the reference to surrendering free agency is meant to collapse them into a single theory of autonomy and choice. Could you help me understand how the passage moves between screening and deterrence, and whether the argument requires both logics to be inseparable? I am especially interested in whether the commissioners' notion of indolence functions as a behavioral prediction or as a normative category that makes the screening device ethically justified. Please respond in American English, and I would prefer an explanation that works through the internal reasoning of the passage rather than relying on modern scholarly citations.
