I am presenting a paper next Thursday to our joint philosophy and sociology seminar on gender, justice, and measurement. My thesis is that the gender-equality paradox—where societies scoring higher on standard equality indices display stronger occupational gender segregation—does not support inferences about innate sex differences, nor can it be explained away solely as residual structural coercion masked by formal rights. Instead, I argue that it exposes a conceptual confusion in how we operationalise 'free occupational choice' across cultures. I propose that egalitarian policies achieve constraint-release—the removal of material and legal barriers—while simultaneously amplifying culturally transmitted vocational schemas: symbolic frameworks that link gender identity to particular forms of labour and that become more salient precisely when economic calculation is no longer dominant. The observed segregation is therefore not the revelation of 'true' preferences beneath false consciousness, nor merely hidden coercion, but a shift in the locus of gender differentiation from economic necessity to cultural semiotics. I call this schema amplification under constraint-release, and I use it to contend that justice requires addressing symbolic domination, not only material redistribution.

The audience is precise: six feminist epistemologists who are sceptical of evolutionary-psychology explanations and suspicious of revealed-preference arguments in gender research; three quantitative sociologists who interpret cross-national segregation data as evidence that socialisation persists despite formal equality; one philosopher of measurement who will challenge the cross-cultural validity of gender-equality indices; and a political philosopher committed to relational equality, who fears that my view recasts structural injustice as culture and thereby lets redistribution off the hook.

Prepare me for the question period. I want the objections ordered by likelihood and damage, phrased in the vocabulary each faction would actually use. For each objection: state it as they would voice it; indicate what I should concede up front; specify what I must hold and why; provide a concise reply suitable for a seminar exchange; and note honestly where that reply is weakest. I also want you to identify the weakest point in my own argument—the point I should defend actively rather than hope they overlook—and to include the objection I am hoping nobody raises, likely concerning whether my framework is unfalsifiable or whether it undermines the normative case for quota-based remedies. The argument sketch they will target is as follows: (1) constraint-release is empirically distinguishable from preference-revelation because, in high-equality contexts, gendered choices cluster in symbolically loaded domains rather than in domains offering the greatest objective returns; (2) vocational schemas are not reducible to stereotypes but are structurally embedded semiotic resources that gain traction when economic dominance recedes; (3) therefore, metrics of occupational segregation confound autonomy with symbolic expression, and justice requires a theory of symbolic domination that complements, rather than replaces, material redistribution.
