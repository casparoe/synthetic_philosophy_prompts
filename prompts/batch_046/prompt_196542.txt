Consider the stance toward death expressed in several well-known episodes associated with Zhuangzi. In one, upon his wife’s death, he drums on a tub and sings, treating the event as a change of form comparable to seasonal transition rather than as a loss warranting grief. In another, finding an abandoned skull by the roadside, he uses it as a pillow and addresses it directly, treating death as dissolving rather than destroying the presence of the dead. In a third, passing the grave of a close philosophical interlocutor, he remarks that with this death he has lost the one with whom he could contend, expressing a specific absence of dialogue rather than a general celebration of transformation. In a fourth, facing his own death, he rejects an elaborate burial and asks that heaven and earth serve as his coffin, insisting that the body should return to nature without artificial preservation or distinction.

These four responses are jointly consistent with more than one underlying rule about what death is and how the living should respond. Now consider a new case. A modern practitioner of this outlook dies, leaving explicit instructions: her body is to be placed in an open woodland without marker or preservation; her students are not to hold a memorial, but they are also asked to gather for one evening to continue arguing with her presence—speaking aloud to her unburied body—before finally leaving it to the elements and never speaking her name again.
