I oversee clinical taxonomy governance for a multi-site community mental health network, and we are revising our intake manual. I need a reusable decision procedure — not a single recommendation — for evaluating whether to formally adopt a proposed psychiatric classification change (a new subcategory, a dimensional overlay, or a split) in light of Hacking's looping effects: the risk that the label itself alters the behavior, self-conception, or institutional tracking of the people classified.

The procedure must be executable by a non-philosopher clinician in about fifteen minutes per proposal, with explicit sign-off points and a hard stop. Structure it as ordered questions that separate empirical loop-dynamics predictions from normative judgments. Specifically, require the user to identify (a) first-order looping (patients change self-presentation or identity upon receiving the label), (b) second-order institutional calibration (clinicians or referral systems shift documentation or resource thresholds in response to changed presentations), and (c) loop velocity — whether the feedback occurs faster than the pilot period can measure. State exactly where the procedure returns "do not adopt," where it mandates an ethics hand-off because the dispute is about values rather than loop dynamics, and where it requires a bounded pilot before adoption.

Then apply it to three proposals:
1. Splitting "adjustment disorder with mixed disturbance of emotions and conduct" into an interpersonal-stressor subtype and an institutional-stressor subtype, where the latter is expected to trigger second-order calibration in occupational-health referrals.
2. Adding a provisional dimensional rating, "transient emotional dysregulation intensity (0-4)," for adolescent intake — intended solely for triage resource allocation, but with documented risk of first-order identity looping in this age group.
3. Formalizing "clinician burnout-related functional impairment" as a billable cluster in staff-health records — a case where the network wants to legitimize staff suffering, but where the procedure should recommend rejection despite that desire.

Be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: for instance, it cannot resolve whether autonomy-enhancing loops are preferable to harm-reducing ones, and it cannot eliminate epistemic risk when measurement instruments are themselves loop-sensitive. Keep the response under 800 words. Lead with the bottom line: the one-sentence core decision rule. Then present the ordered checklist with judgment-call flags, then the three applications (including the one where the answer conflicts with institutional preference), then the stated limits.
