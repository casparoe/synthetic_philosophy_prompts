Following a long and rather circular argument with a close friend about whether genuine moral dilemmas are actually possible, I find myself in need of something more operational than the usual philosophical exchange. I chair the clinical ethics review board at a mid-sized teaching hospital, and we are required to classify every referred case within forty-eight hours as either a resolvable conflict of obligations, a conflict of values that falls outside our mandate, or a genuine moral dilemma in which two non-derogable duties remain in force regardless of which course is taken. The conceptual point at stake—whether a true dilemma leaves what philosophers sometimes call moral residue on both sides of the choice—matters directly to our work, because if a case is genuinely dilemmatic we must document an undefeated reason for the path not taken and offer institutional support for the staff involved; if it is merely an apparent conflict, we ought to resolve it by priority rules and close the file. I want a reusable decision procedure my board can run in under an hour, grounded in the conceptual distinction between an outweighed reason and an undefeated one, but refined by a further idea I have been trying to formalise: that a genuine dilemma requires symmetrical residue, meaning the rejected course of action retains its normative force in a structurally parallel way to the chosen one, whereas asymmetrical residue—where only one side leaves a standing obligation—indicates a resolvable conflict that our rules should settle.

The procedure should take the form of an ordered checklist or flowchart in words: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the precise point at which the board stops with a negative classification, and the exact juncture at which the question becomes one of values rather than normative structure and must be handed to the patient or family rather than to us. It must include a small set of diagnostic tests—perhaps three or four sentences the board can apply—to distinguish defeated from undefeated reasons, and it must be explicit about which steps are mechanical and which are unavoidable judgement calls dressed in procedural clothing. Please apply it to three cases: first, a refusal of life-saving treatment by a competent patient whose family demands intervention; second, a proposal to divert scarce ICU resources from a patient with poor prognosis to a younger patient with better odds; and third, a case where staff must choose between two equally urgent surgical emergencies with only one available theatre. One of these should produce an answer my board will find unwelcome—specifically, that what looks like a dilemma is in fact resolvable by a priority rule we would rather not invoke—while another should land exactly at the hand-off point where values, not obligations, are in conflict. Finally, please state clearly what this procedure cannot do: where it will misfire, what kinds of institutional pressure distort its application, and why it cannot eliminate the need for a signed-off human decision even at its most mechanical steps.
