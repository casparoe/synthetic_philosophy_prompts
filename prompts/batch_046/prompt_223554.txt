I'm working through Kant's essay on perpetual peace for a seminar that moves beyond the usual summary of the three definitive articles, and I'm stuck on a conceptual tension that seems to sit right at the heart of his federation idea. Kant argues that republican constitutions are a necessary condition for lasting peace—he treats the first definitive article as the structural backbone of the whole proposal—yet he also insists that the federation of free states is not itself a world republic and that member states retain their full sovereignty, including monarchies that are not republican. If the analogy between individuals leaving the state of nature and states leaving the international state of nature is meant to do real work, it seems to require that states alienate some portion of their natural freedom, just as individuals do when they enter civil society. But Kant explicitly denies that states must surrender sovereignty to a higher authority, calling that a contradiction in terms for a state. So please explain, at a gut-conceptual level rather than through textual exegesis alone, whether Kant's analogy actually holds under these conditions: is he implicitly treating sovereignty as something that can be partially pooled without being fully alienated, or is there a different logic—perhaps tied to the preliminary articles, especially the prohibition on interference in other constitutions and the ban on secret reservations—that allows him to maintain both that republicanism is necessary for peace and that the federation must never compel a state to become republican? I'm particularly interested in whether the "negative substitute" of a voluntary league can conceptually stabilize the "positive idea" of a world republic without collapsing back into the very state of nature it is meant to solve, and where exactly the conceptual pressure point lies if we take Kant's commitment to state sovereignty as genuinely non-negotiable.
