Here’s a rough essay idea I’m playing with. I want to move past the standard L.A. Paul framing of transformative experience—parenthood, vampirism, that sort of thing—and look at what happens when an experience doesn’t just change your prefferences but breaks the accounting system you use to weigh them at all. I’m calling it narrative insolvency: the idea that some profound shifts (like certain chronic neurological changes, or long-term alterations to affective baseline from drugs) don’t leave you with a new set of values you can compare to the old ones; they corrupt the ledger itself. Once that happens, talking about rational choice is a bit like asking a broken calculator to audit its own error.

My sketch: open with Paul’s epistemic problem, introduce narrative insolvency as an ontological/narrative failure rather than just an epistemic gap, then argue there’s an asymmetry we ignore. We treat positively valenced unknowns (parenthood, a career change) as open-ended possibilities we should stay receptive to, but with negatively valenced ones (say, a condition that rewires sensory integration) we say ‘you can’t consent because you can’t know’. Yet we don’t apply the same humility when deciding whether to take a compound that might permanently flatten your affective range. The conclusion I’m reaching is that transformative choice should be reconceived less as optimisation and more like a bankrupcy proceeding—acknowledging that the old self’s metrics are no longer enforceable.

Could you comment on whether the asymmetry is genuinely there or if I’m smuggling in a bias? Is narrative insolvency just a fancy way of saying ‘it’s complicated’, or does it do real work? And does the bankrupcy metaphor hold, or does it collapse once you push on intertemporal identity? Please reply in British English, and don’t worry about finding sources—just tell me if the argument smells right.
