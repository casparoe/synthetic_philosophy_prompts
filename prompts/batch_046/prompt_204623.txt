I'm trying to understand whether Quine's thesis about the indeterminacy of translation, introduced through the "gavagai" example, can be applied consistently without undermining mathematical knowledge. Here is an argument my study group constructed that seems to show it cannot. (1) In Quine's radical translation scenario, "gavagai" uttered when a rabbit runs past is indeterminate between referring to rabbits, undetached rabbit parts, or temporal rabbit-stages, because every observable behavioral disposition of the native speaker is compatible with each ontology, and there is no further empirical fact that fixes the reference. (2) Quine extends this point to claim that even in our home language, reference is determined only up to a proxy function that permutes the objects of our ontology while leaving all empirical predictions intact; ontology is therefore relative to the choice of background theory rather than absolutely fixed. (3) Now apply this framework to arithmetic. Consider the statement "There are infinitely many primes." Ordinarily we take "prime" and "infinitely many" to refer determinately to abstract numbers and to a specific cardinal relation among them. (4) But construct a Quinean proxy specifically for arithmetic—call it gestural arithmetic—that maps each natural number n to the mereological fusion of all physical counting-gestures ever performed exactly n times, and that maps "prime" to the property of being a fusion that cannot be decomposed into equal smaller fusion-parts except trivially. (5) This reinterpretation preserves the logical and computable structure of arithmetic over all finite, observable applications, yet "prime" now denotes not an abstract object but an equivalence class of embodied counting events. (6) Since Quine denies any non-behavioral or non-theoretical anchor—such as direct acquaintance with abstract objects or an innate cognitive architecture—that could privilege the standard interpretation over this gestural one, there is no deeper fact about whether "prime" means prime-number or prime-gesture-fusion. (7) Hence the determinacy of Euclid's theorem collapses: it expresses not a single definite fact about specific abstract entities, but rather a structural pattern satisfiable by radically different ontologies, with no rational basis internal to Quine's naturalism for choosing among them. (8) Yet Quine himself treated mathematics as determinately true and employed set theory as a working framework without treating its ontology as destructively ambiguous in this way; he clearly did not regard "there are infinitely many primes" as ontologically optional between abstract and gestural readings. (9) So if the indeterminacy thesis is applied consistently and globally, it proves too much: it dissolves the determinacy of the very mathematical knowledge that Quine's own philosophy presupposes. The only escape appears to be an ad hoc restriction of the thesis to empirical observation-language, but such a restriction lacks principled justification within Quine's naturalism.
