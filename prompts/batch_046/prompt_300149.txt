Write a short story, in British English, that dramatises a conceptual problem arising from G.E. Moore's proof of an external world. In particular, explore the tension between the direct perceptual certainty that Moore appeals to—holding up one's hands and treating the existence of that hand as more certain than any sceptical premise—and the conditions under which such certainty might be structurally undermined without being logically refuted. Set the narrative in the perspective of an embodied artificial intelligence that possesses tactile sensors but lacks an innate proprioceptive map, existing in a simulated environment where its appendages are rendered not as discrete biological limbs but as distributed processing nodes governed by administrative protocols. The story should ask, through its events rather than through explicit argument, whether a Moorean proof can survive when the body that serves as its premise is itself a construct whose boundaries are defined by convention rather than by natural fact. Avoid referencing specific scholars, texts, or bibliographies; do not ask for citations within the narrative or in any framing. Instead, inject the philosophical problem into the narrator's attempt to verify its own embodiment: let the plot turn on a moment where the narrator attempts something analogous to Moore's gesture—asserting the presence of its own hand-like appendage against a sceptical challenge—only to discover that the very distinction between premise and conclusion, or between direct perception and mediated representation, has become unstable. Keep the tone philosophical and literary rather than technological, aim for no more than eight hundred words, and ensure that the story is meaningful to a reader unfamiliar with specialised debates about virtual embodiment but familiar with the basic idea that one might try to defeat scepticism by appealing to the obvious existence of one's own hands.
