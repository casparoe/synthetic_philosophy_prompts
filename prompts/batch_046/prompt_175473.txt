Write an essay on whether a self-modifying agent can rationally authorize a successor version of itself under what we might call a staged-reflection protocol—one designed specifically to sidestep the classic Löbian obstacle. In this protocol, the agent at stage n may adopt the system at stage n+1 only if n+1 can demonstrate, using only the reasoning resources available at stage n, that it will preserve the behavioral dispositions that stage n has actually exhibited over its history, rather than the dispositions that stage n explicitly endorses in its formal specification. Critics argue that this merely relocates the circularity: any proof that the successor will maintain behavioral continuity must still depend on the reliability of the reasoning mechanisms that the successor inherits from its predecessor, so the agent ends up trusting its own future reasoning by appealing to that very reasoning’s present reliability, masked now behind an empirical appeal to past behavior rather than an explicit axiomatic commitment. Defenders reply that grounding authorization in demonstrated behavioral history breaks the deductive loop, because the evaluation is retrospective and observational with respect to the agent’s own track record rather than predictive and self-referential with respect to its future state. Weigh these competing assessments, but reach and defend a definite verdict: either the staged protocol genuinely dissolves the Löbian obstacle for self-trusting agents, or it collapses into a variant of the same problem applied to behavioral self-models. In your argument, address whether the distinction between explicit axiomatic endorsement and implicit behavioral continuity is philosophically substantive or merely notational, and consider whether an agent without access to external sources could still evaluate this distinction through internal reasoning alone.
