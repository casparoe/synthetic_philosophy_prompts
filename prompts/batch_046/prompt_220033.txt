A philosophy-of-science blogger summarized Aumann's agreement theorem as follows: if two rational Bayesian agents share a common prior and make their posterior probabilities common knowledge, they must assign identical probabilities to every event, so any persistent disagreement can only stem from hidden differences in prior beliefs or from a failure of rational updating. Applied to real scientific disputes—say, between climate modelers—this is taken to mean that once all data and reasoning are fully shared, disagreement should vanish instantly; if it does not, at least one party must be secretly holding a different prior or reasoning irrationally. I worry this slides too quickly from a formal model to an empirical claim about scientific practice. Is this summary fair? Please distinguish what Aumann's result actually requires (common priors, common knowledge of posteriors rather than mere exchange of evidence, and the precise rationality conditions), which parts are defensible popular simplifications, and where the summary overreaches—particularly on whether real scientists ever satisfy the conditions, whether hidden priors are the only permissible source of disagreement, and whether the theorem implies anything normative about how scientific consensus ought to form.
