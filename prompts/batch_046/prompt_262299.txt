I'm working through some foundational literature in a philosophy of physics seminar, and my study group has been stuck on a subtle point about Einstein's hole argument that I can't seem to resolve. As I understand the setup, one considers a region of spacetime—the "hole"—in which the stress-energy tensor vanishes everywhere inside, then applies a smooth coordinate transformation that is the identity everywhere outside this region but non-trivial within it. Because the field equations of general relativity are generally covariant, both the original metric and the transformed metric solve the equations with exactly the same matter distribution outside the hole. If the points of the underlying manifold possess intrinsic identity independent of the fields defined on them, then we appear to have two physically distinct solutions corresponding to one and the same observable state outside the region, which threatens determinism. Einstein's well-known response was the point-coincidence argument: only the coincidences of world-lines—relations between fields—are physically observable, so the two metrics describe the same physical situation and no indeterminism arises.

Where my friends and I diverge is on what this resolution actually commits us to ontologically. One interpretation holds that Einstein was not eliminating spacetime points altogether, but only denying them any intrinsic properties beyond the relational structure imposed by the metric; on this view, points are real but only relationally individuated, which is why applying a diffeomorphism produces no new physics. Another interpretation takes Einstein to be treating the manifold itself as a purely mathematical scaffolding with no independent ontological weight. My own confusion arises when I try to reconcile either reading with modern formalisms. In particular, if points are genuinely real but only relationally defined, then shouldn't the standard fiber-bundle formulation of gauge theories and gravity—where the base manifold's points carry fixed topological and differential structure—reopen the hole problem? After all, if those base-space points have intrinsic identity, and the metric or connection is merely a section of an associated bundle, then a diffeomorphism that fixes the exterior but rearranges points inside should generate a genuinely different physical configuration, unless we impose an explicit gauge equivalence that seems to smuggle back in the denial of point-identity.

I'm further puzzled by a somewhat niche proposal I've encountered that uses synthetic differential geometry to formulate gravitational physics without a background point-set: in that framework, "points" are reconstructed from the algebra of smooth functions, and there is no pre-given manifold on which to apply a diffeomorphism. If that is correct, does it mean Einstein's determinism worry was ultimately an artifact of using classical point-set topology rather than a deep feature of physical ontology? Or am I conflating the choice of mathematical formalism—sheaves of algebras versus manifolds—with the physical question of whether spacetime points exist? I would appreciate help seeing exactly where my reasoning goes wrong, especially regarding whether modern differential geometry genuinely revives the hole argument in the bundle setting or whether the point-coincidence resolution remains intact regardless of formalism.
