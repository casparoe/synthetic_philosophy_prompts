I spent my career in statistical mechanics, and since retiring I have begun studying the philosophy and engineering of artificial intelligence. I understand the alignment tax as the unavoidable performance penalty incurred when an optimizer is constrained to respect values outside its original objective—much as irreversible work extraction necessarily produces entropy. I understand the competitiveness constraint, in turn, as a selection principle: in any open development environment, systems carrying such a tax should be outcompeted and eliminated by less constrained rivals, just as a less efficient engine is selected against. My question concerns whether this pairing is a genuine dynamical law or merely an engineering limitation of current architectures. Specifically, if one designed an inference-time mechanism that selectively dissipated misaligned behavioral modes—functionally analogous to a Maxwell demon—without reducing the model's general capabilities, would this not break the presumed trade-off? Or does the competitiveness argument depend on a deeper feature of multi-agent dynamics that no local cooling mechanism could overcome?
