I'm working through debates about deontological ethics, specifically the tension often called the paradox of deontology—where a constraint like "do not harm an innocent person" seems to require permitting greater harm to occur. While reading responses to pressure cases, I've noticed a recurring pattern that I can't quite place in the literature.

Consider how defenders of agent-centered restrictions handle successive counterexamples. They begin with an absolute-sounding rule: you may not kill one healthy patient to harvest organs for five dying ones. When presented with a trolley that will kill five unless you push one bystander onto the tracks, they maintain the prohibition—pushing is direct harm, impermissible. Then comes the lever variant: you can divert the trolley onto a sidetrack where one stands, and many allow this. To preserve consistency, they invoke a distinction—direct versus indirect agency, or the doctrine of double effect—without altering the original rule. Next, a further twist arises: pulling the lever requires using a heavy object that happens to be an innocent person. At this point, a new qualification appears: the distinction holds only when the harm is not used as a means to the end. Each step is locally defensible, but collectively the qualifications map almost exactly onto consequentialist cost-benefit reasoning: the constraint is suspended precisely when violating it prevents catastrophic outcomes, and retained when stakes are lower or causal paths are more direct.

What interests me is not the particular distinctions themselves—those are well known—but the cumulative dynamic. The core principle is never revised or abandoned; instead, its conditions of application are progressively refined so that it applies only where it does not conflict with strong outcome-based considerations. Each refinement is presented not as a patch but as an explication of what the rule always meant.
