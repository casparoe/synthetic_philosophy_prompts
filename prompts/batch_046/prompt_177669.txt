I am currently enrolled in a seminar on classical Chinese philosophy, and I am hoping to clarify a philosophical tension that has arisen in a family matter. My grandmother passed away two days ago, and during the gathering afterward, my cousin—who has been studying Daoist writings—argued that the family ought not to mourn her death. His reasoning was that death is not a genuine loss but only a transformation of form, comparable to wood turning to ash or a caterpillar becoming a butterfly; that what we call "my grandmother" was never more than a temporary configuration of vital energy; and therefore that grief is a form of metaphysical error, rooted in the illusion that there is a fixed self that can be lost. He pressed the point further: if one truly accepts this view, he said, one should avoid even saying "she died," since the pronoun presupposes a stable identity that, on this account, never existed, and mourning should be replaced by a calm recognition that "this form has dispersed." Several relatives reacted with anger, accusing him of using philosophy to suppress legitimate sorrow, while others found his words consoling. I find myself caught between these reactions. The notion that death is mere change does seem to reduce the horror of annihilation, yet it also appears to erase the particularity that made my grandmother irreplaceable—her specific memories, her affection for us, her consciousness. I want to understand whether my cousin's position is a rigorous application of the philosophical view we have examined in class, or whether he has overextended it. In particular, does the claim that death is transformation necessarily imply that grief is irrational or inappropriate? And is the denial of a fixed personal identity a logical consequence of that transformation thesis, or are these two distinct claims that he has improperly conflated? Finally, I am asking not for generic emotional guidance, but for an assessment of whether one can consistently hold this philosophical position while still honoring the emotional reality of loss, or whether the two are genuinely incompatible.
