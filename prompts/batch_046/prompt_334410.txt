I've been reading up on Dennett's counterfeit people idea, especially now that LLMs can hold these long convincing conversations. There's a pattern in debates I keep running into and I dont know if it has a name. Someone says an AI can't be a real moral agent because it lacks some specific thing—like embodied vulnerability, or the ability to actually suffer. But then you point out that the system can aproximate that in ways that matter for how we'd treat it, and they don't back down. Instead they shift to saying that even if it has the feature, it's not the 'right kind' of feature, or that being designed makes it inauthentic no matter what. The first claim feels like a real functional criterion, but then there's this deeper layer that doesn't care about functions—it's more like an aesthetic or metaphysical rejection. So you've got this two-layer defense: a surface argument that sounds testable, and a background 'counterfeit' intuition that just activates when the surface gets solved. Is there an established concept for this exact two-layer move in philosphy of AI? I don't think it's just motte-and-bailey, because the hidden part isn't a stronger version of the claim—it's a different kind of objection altogether. And it's not quite Dennett's own worry, since he was talking about whether we'd be fooled, not about people using functional arguments as cover for a deeper rejection. If there's no term, can you coin one with clear boundaries? I want to distinguish it from plain goalpost-moving, from other-minds skepticism, and from just being emotionally attached to human uniqueness.
