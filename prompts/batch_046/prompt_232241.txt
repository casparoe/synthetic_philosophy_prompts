I retired from marine biology about five years ago and lately I've been sitting in on a philosphy course for retirees at the community college, mostly because my friends kept arguing about whether life has a point once you stop working. I'm trying to figure out my actual gut feelings by testing concrete cases, and I'm wondering if there's one principle that catches them all or if I just have to live with the mess.

Here are my verdicts. My old colleague Elena spends her retirement tracking one specific whale that she knows will die without leaving any offspring; she doesn't publish anything, she just watches and records. To me that's meaningful, maybe even beautiful. But then there's Jim, who builds this enormous clock that counts down to the heat death of the universe and hides it in his basement where nobody will ever see it; he says he enjoys the precision but doesn't care if anyone knows. That feels empty to me, even though both projects are futile and invisible. Then there's a mother I read about who raises her children in a war zone knowing they might not survive past their teens and that no historian will record their names; that strikes me as deeply meaningful. And there's the guy who lives alone optimizing every choice for his own pleasure, convinced the universe is indifferent and that he'll vanish without trace — that feels definatly empty. But here's the pair that breaks me: a retired mathematician realizes life is absurd, so she spends a decade inventing a private language just to describe her own experience of it. She never shares it. Some days she sounds like Elena, devoted to something real despite the futility, but other days she sounds exactly like Jim, trapped in a closed loop inside her own head. I keep flipping.

I was trying to frame it with this idea of an "open loop" — meaning needs your care to reach something you don't fully control or internalize — but Elena never publishes, and the mathematician's language is about her own experience, so maybe that doesn't work. Is there a principle, maybe with a couple clauses, that saves Elena and the mother but rejects Jim and the pleasure optimizer, and that also tells me what to think about the mathematician? And what would it say about a case I haven't listed — say, someone who spends every day cleaning trash off a beach that will be underwater in fifty years, not because they love it but because they feel it's their duty even though nobody asked? Please also say which of my verdicts you'd have to overturn if you wanted a simpler principle, and predict one more case — maybe someone who teaches a skill to an AI that will outlive them but won't understand gratitude — just to see where the line falls.
