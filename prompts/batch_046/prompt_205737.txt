I have been working through the literature on mechanistic explanations of agency and find myself drawn to a view I will call the hierarchical sphex thesis. On this account, the cognitive architecture underlying human deliberation is not different in kind from the rigid, stereotyped nest-provisioning routine of the sphex wasp—only different in organizational scale and combinatorial depth. The position holds that every apparently flexible decision is implemented by subpersonal mechanisms that are individually deterministic and inflexible, arranged in nested hierarchies whose exponential branching generates the appearance of open-ended choice. Free will, if it survives at all, is therefore not a primitive capacity for self-determination but an emergent, scale-dependent property of sufficiently deep stacking of mechanistic sub-routines; what we experience as genuine flexibility is, at bottom, the illusion produced when thousands of fixed-action patterns interact. I want to understand precisely what would count against this view—not merely the familiar compatibilist reminder that determinism need not preclude freedom, but the specific observations, formal results, conceptual distinctions, or empirical findings that could undermine the claim that flexibility is purely hierarchical and emergent.

In particular, I am interested in whether there are findings in neuroscience or cognitive science demonstrating irreducible flexibility at the subpersonal level—processes that are genuinely context-sensitive or non-decomposable into fixed rules in a way that cannot be absorbed by adding another layer to the hierarchy. Are there arguments showing that hierarchical rigidity necessarily collapses either into an unacknowledged libertarian gap or into a reductive determinism that the view was designed to avoid? Would evidence that some cognitive capacities involve real-time, globally integrated restructuring—rather than the selection among pre-fixed options—defeat the thesis, or could its defenders always retreat to the claim that the restructuring itself is governed by a deeper, still-sphexish meta-routine? I also want an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: whether any counterexample can be redescribed as “just another level of nesting,” and if so, whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a fatal weakness. Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish those that a committed hierarchical sphex theorist should concede as genuinely damaging from those they could dismiss with a minor revision, and indicate what it would take—conceptually or empirically—to move you away from the view entirely.
