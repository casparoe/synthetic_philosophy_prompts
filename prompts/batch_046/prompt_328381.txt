I have been trying to pin down where my disagreement with a colleague about functionalism actually resides, and I think it turns on a case that standard textbook formulations of multiple realizability never quite reach. We both accept that mental states are defined by their causal-functional roles, and that different physical substrates can realize the same role. Where we diverge is over a deliberately extreme architecture: imagine a synthetic system in which the functional profile corresponding to phenomenal pain is not localized in any single substrate but is distributed across four physically disjoint modules—one built on conventional silicon logic, one on neuromorphic analog circuits, one on continuous dynamical systems, and one on a small quantum-processing cluster—with each module implementing only a partial subset of the input-output and internal-relational conditions, and with coherence maintained solely through a high-bandwidth informational interface rather than through shared physical dynamics. I am inclined to think that functionalism predicts this system either lacks unified phenomenal consciousness entirely or hosts four fragmented proto-experiences rather than one integrated pain; my credence that it genuinely experiences one unified phenomenal state is about 0.30. My colleague, by contrast, puts her credence at roughly 0.75, insisting that substrate heterogeneity at the module level is irrelevant if the global functional organization is intact.
