I'm organizing a graduate workshop on the paradox of tragedy, and my reading group has split into two camps that talk past each other. One faction—call them the Regulatory Rebound theorists—maintains that tragic enjoyment is essentially compensatory: the artwork induces negative affect in a safe, framed context, which triggers an opposing homeostatic or cathartic rebound that is experienced as pleasure; on this view, the sadness is instrumentally necessary but phenomenologically distinct from the enjoyment, and the formal properties of the work mainly function to contain risk so that rebound can occur. The other faction—the Structured Dissonance theorists—rejects any hidden payoff model: they hold that the negative affect is not a cost to be offset but a constitutive ingredient in a specific cognitive-emotional configuration, where the represented suffering and the work's aesthetic form sustain an irreducible, productive tension that is valued precisely because it remains unresolved. To break the loop, I want these two sides to draft the adversarial collaboration document they should have produced.

Write their joint statement. Begin with a numbered section of claims both sides can sign—covering at least: that viewers genuinely report enjoying artworks that evoke sadness; that this enjoyment is not reducible to masochism, schadenfreude, or mere relief that the narrative ends; that the emotional response involves both negative affect and a positive appraisal that is not merely the absence of pain; and that the same narrative content presented in a non-artistic framing (for example, a clinical case report or news item) typically fails to produce comparable enjoyment, so context and form matter. Then present the precise disagreements in wording each camp accepts: whether negative affect operates as a trigger for an opposing restorative state versus as an irreducible element in a sustained tension; and whether formal properties primarily serve to frame and contain negative affect for safe rebound, or actively structure and preserve the dissonance. Next, identify the cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I take the central crux to be whether suppressing the negative emotional response (while preserving formal appreciation) eliminates the specific value of tragedy, and a second crux to be whether disrupting aesthetic coherence while preserving sad content diminishes enjoyment because rebound conditions fail or because the dissonance structure collapses. For each crux, specify a concrete thought experiment or hypothetical case both agree is relevant; record each camp's advance prediction about the outcome; and indicate whether both sides agree the result would be decisive or only suggestive.

Deliver the result as structured data: a JSON object with exactly four named fields. The field "agreed_claims" should contain an array of strings, each being one agreed claim without internal numbering. The field "disagreement_table" should contain an array of objects, each with keys "issue" (the disagreement topic), "rebound_wording" (the Rebound camp's self-accepted formulation), "dissonance_wording" (the Dissonance camp's self-accepted formulation), "shared_case" (the concrete case or argument both accept bears on it), "rebound_prediction", "dissonance_prediction", and "decisiveness" (one of "both_decisive", "both_suggestive", or "disputed"). The field "cruxes" should contain an array of objects with keys "crux_name", "description", and "shared_case_description". The field "meta_assessment" should be a single string assessing whether any part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive—specifically addressing whether disagreement over the term "pleasure" versus "value" is terminological—and explaining why both sides could accept that assessment. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; do not refer to specific empirical studies by author or date; and avoid direct quotations from any text.
