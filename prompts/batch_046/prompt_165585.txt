I have just finished a long and exhausting argument with a close friend about Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor. One of us holds that the Inquisitor's diagnosis is fundamentally correct: that ordinary human beings, faced with the burden of moral freedom, will consistently trade it for material security and authoritative certainty, and that any social order which accepts this trade and organizes itself accordingly is practicing a realistic form of compassion rather than tyranny. The other holds that Dostoevsky's implicit answer—embodied in Christ's silent kiss and in Alyosha's refusal to defeat the Inquisitor with logic—is correct: that freedom is not one preference among others but the constitutive condition of human dignity, so that happiness achieved by surrendering it is not happiness but a kind of spiritual death, and that the Inquisitor mistakes collective fear for universal human nature.

Please draft the joint statement that these two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should contain: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims, including about what the Inquisitor actually asserts regarding miracle, mystery, and authority; about Dostoevsky's framing of freedom as a burden that reveals love; and about observable patterns in how individuals and groups behave under conditions of radical choice versus structured authority; (2) a disagreements table stating the precise residual disagreements in wording each side would accept for its own position; (3) the cruxes—those sub-questions such that resolving them would shift at least one party—including whether freedom's value is instrumental or constitutive, and whether historical episodes of mass voluntary submission to authoritarian structures should be read as confirmation of the Inquisitor's psychology or as products of manufactured dependency; and (4) for each crux, one concrete historical case or empirical finding both sides agree is relevant, together with each side's explicit prediction of what that case or finding demonstrates. Write formally and neutrally, without misrepresenting either view, and distinguish genuinely substantive disagreement from disputes that are merely verbal.
