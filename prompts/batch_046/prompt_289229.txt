I'm preparing a single ninety-minute workshop for an interdisciplinary group of advanced undergraduates — philosophy majors and religious-studies students — on a specific thread in Nietzsche's genealogy of morality: the claim that ressentiment is not merely reactive resentment but a creative, value-creating mechanism that inverts an existing evaluative order. Most participants will arrive treating genealogy as a historical origin-story and ressentiment as "resentment with a French spelling." I need them to leave seeing that the genealogical point is about the conditions under which a moral vocabulary becomes authoritative, not about who was angry first. Plan the explanatory arc: what conceptual dependency must be secured before the inversion of "good/bad" into "good/evil" can even appear coherent — specifically, whether the distinction between the psychological origin of a value and its present justification must come first, or whether the prior idea of "noble" versus "slave" modes of evaluation should carry that weight; which concrete case or textual moment should serve as the carrying example (the priestly revaluation is the obvious candidate, but specify whether it genuinely illustrates creative inversion or collapses into mere class-conflict narrative, and if it fails, propose an alternative); the misconceptions that will arise in the order they will arise — the etymological fallacy, the reduction of slave morality to sour grapes, the assumption that Nietzsche endorses noble morality — and exactly where in the sequence each should be intercepted; a single diagnostic question I can ask at the end to distinguish someone who has grasped ressentiment as a genealogical mechanism from someone who has only acquired the vocabulary; and what should be explicitly named and set aside at this level — for instance, the Anglo-American naturalist versus Continental post-structural receptions — with a brief justification of why leaving it out strengthens rather than weakens the session.
