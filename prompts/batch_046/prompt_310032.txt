Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between philosophers who defend the unitary-affect view—that pain’s badness is inseparable from its sensory character—and those who defend the separable-dimensions view, which takes clinical pain asymbolia to show that badness belongs only to a distinct affective-evaluative component rather than to nociceptive sensation itself. The document should include: (a) a numbered section of agreed claims, covering the basic phenomenology of asymbolia, the formal distinction between nociceptive signaling and suffering, and joint rejections (e.g., that asymbolia is mere denial or that all pain is necessarily conscious); (b) a disagreements table in which each camp states its own position in wording it would sign, marking the exact locus of divergence; (c) the decisive cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side—namely (i) whether a hypothetical state retaining full nociceptive specificity but lacking all negative valence (“pure sensory pain”) is still axiologically bad, and (ii) whether pharmacologically isolating the affective component leaves any residual badness in the sensory remainder; (d) for each crux, the shared standards of evidence and each camp’s advance prediction. Inject the following non-standard framing: treat the dispute over “badness” as partly a choice between understanding it as an intrinsic phenomenal property of the experience and understanding it as a relational property linking the experience to the subject’s practical rationality, and have both sides state whether this framing dissolves part of their disagreement or only relocates it. Take a committed stance: identify precisely which parts of the dispute are verbal rather than substantive, declare which crux is genuinely decisive, and defend one of the two predicted outcomes with explicit reasoning rather than leaving the choice open.
