Construct an argument for the following thesis: in Mahāyāna ethics, the doctrine of upāya-kauśalya (skillful means) does not license an unrestricted act-consequentialism for the bodhisattva, but rather supports what we might call a volitional-rule hybrid—one in which the permissibility of an otherwise prohibited act (for example, false speech or even violence in extreme cases) depends jointly on (i) a consequentialist assessment of net benefit to sentient beings and (ii) a deontological filter on the agent’s cetanā (volitional formation), specifically the complete absence of the three root poisons (rāga, dveṣa, moha). Your argument should show that these two conditions are not merely conjoined but structurally interdependent: the consequentialist calculation is epistemically and morally valid only when performed by a mental stream that lacks defilement, and such a mental stream can be institutionally secured only within the disciplinary framework of the Vinaya or its Mahāyāna analogues. Consequently, the bodhisattva’s exceptional permission to transgress conventional morality is actually constrained by a rule-governed practice of mental cultivation, yielding a position that is more restrictive than act-utilitarianism and more outcome-sensitive than pure virtue ethics. Be precise about how the Abhidharmic distinction between mental karma (cetanā) and bodily or vocal karma operates in this analysis, and address the objection that this view still collapses into a form of rule-consequentialism disguised in Buddhist terminology.
