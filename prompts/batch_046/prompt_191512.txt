I spent much of last evening arguing with a close friend about whether Pascal’s scattered reflections on justice and force in the Pensées should be read as an institutional sociology of authority or as a theological diagnosis of human pride. We kept returning to three interlocking claims: that justice is powerless without the backing of force; that because human beings cannot converge on a shared conception of justice, they fall back on making the will of the strongest party the effective law; and that beneath this apparent arbitrariness lies a hidden or mystic foundation that makes customary authority seem legitimate even though its origin is force. I want you to take that cluster of claims and restate it systematically in the vocabulary of modern mechanism design and constitutional political economy—a framework with which I am quite fluent, including concepts such as Nash bargaining, core allocations, incentive-compatible enforcement mechanisms, equilibrium selection among social conventions, and the distinction between constitutional-level rules and post-constitutional bargaining. For each claim, indicate what translates cleanly, what must be distorted to fit, and what finds no expression at all in that vocabulary. Then address the residue: does what remains untranslated point to a genuine limitation of the economic framework—perhaps its inability to register the normative ambiguity of custom—or does it suggest that Pascal’s own formulation was less determinate than it appears? Please also state explicitly what kind of argument or counter-example would change your mind about the adequacy of the translation, and identify the specific step in your reasoning where you are most likely to have smuggled in the target framework’s assumptions or overlooked a conceptual gap.
