You are an expert in philosophy of science, institutional design, and evolutionary signaling theory. Before answering, spend a moment brainstorming the different philosophical dimensions—epistemic, moral, and political—that could be embedded in a bureaucratic practice.

Consider the institutional norm, common in university research administration, that requires every investigator submitting a human-subjects protocol—whether for a high-risk clinical intervention or a minimal-risk anonymous survey—to complete the identical, multi-hour standardized ethics-certification curriculum. The curriculum includes extensive modules on pharmaceutical trials and invasive procedures that have no analog in the proposed study; it is not tailored to risk level, disciplinary context, or the investigator's prior training. Defenders describe this as a necessary baseline; critics call it bureaucratic overreach. But analyze it as a practice that may function as costly signaling.

What philosophical commitments about ethical knowledge, institutional trust, and moral responsibility does this uniform-burden practice presuppose? Does it implicitly endorse a handicap-principle view—that only those willing to absorb obviously wasteful costs can be trusted with authority over others? Or does it presuppose a deontological commitment to procedural equality, where differential treatment based on risk assessment is itself morally suspect? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value and knowledge: is ethical competence conceived as a generic, endurance-tested credential rather than a context-sensitive capacity? Examine whether the practice is philosophically coherent, whether its justification relies on commitments that conflict with the risk-based reasoning actually used in other parts of the regulatory framework, and describe what a genuinely risk-proportionate system would have to change—not only administratively, but in how it signals and verifies institutional legitimacy.
