As a software engineer without any formal background in philosophy, I have been trying to make sense of autonomy in deterministic systems — both in software architectures and in human behaviour. I hold the view that the only variety of free will genuinely worth wanting is what I call recursive self-revision: a system possesses it when its higher-level evaluation routines can genuinely restructure the weights, priorities, or goal-functions of its lower-level drives, regardless of whether the underlying computation is fully determined by prior states. I came to this partly from working with reinforcement-learning agents that use meta-learning loops to reshape their own reward functions, which made hierarchical models of control seem more concrete to me. On this account, the presence or absence of indeterminism is irrelevant; what matters is the internal architecture of control, not the metaphysical openness of the causal chain. What would count against this position? I want the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be — including whether this collapses into a mere redescription of complex feedback loops with no normative weight, whether there is a compelling argument that moral responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise that no hierarchical architecture can supply, and whether empirical findings about human decision-making undermine the claim that such recursive structures are actually present or functional in people. Please distinguish objections that would be fatal from those that would only require refinement, and say whether defenders of hierarchical compatibilist views have themselves acknowledged any particular result or case as genuinely damaging to their account.
