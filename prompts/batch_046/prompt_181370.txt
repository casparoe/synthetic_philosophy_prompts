I was discussing with a colleague how to divide the intellectual-property rights to a software library we have developed together in our spare time. She is employed at a large technology firm and could return to her well-paid role if our collaboration ended, whereas I am currently between contracts and would have to rely on freelance work I find unsatisfying. She contends that because her fallback position is stronger—she has more to lose by walking away—she ought to hold the majority share of any future licensing revenue. I feel there is something wrong with this reasoning, yet I struggle to articulate exactly what.

The situation has recently become more complex in a specific way. An independent open-source foundation has offered me a modest but sustainable grant to continue developing a related module entirely on my own, which improves my best possible outcome should we fail to reach agreement. Crucially, my colleague's ideal scenario—licensing the entire library exclusively through her employer—has not improved at all. Nevertheless, she maintains that her entitlement to the larger share is unchanged, and she even suggests that because my fallback is now less dire, the disparity in our bargaining positions is somehow reduced in her favour. This strikes me as conceptually confused: an improvement in my own best-case outcome, with no corresponding shift in hers, should not weaken my claim, yet her argument seems to conflate the strength of one's outside option with one's overall entitlement.

Could you please advise me on whether her position is fair, and explain how the structure of our disagreement maps onto formal theories of cooperative bargaining? I am particularly interested in whether a change in one party's best achievable payoff—without any parallel change in the other party's—ought to alter the division, and whether the disagreement point alone should determine the split. Please separate what is well established in the economic and philosophical literature on fair division from what is genuinely contested or depends on contested normative assumptions, and do not present any particular view as the unanimous consensus.
