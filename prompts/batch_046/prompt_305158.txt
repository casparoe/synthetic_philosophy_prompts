Hey, I'm drafting a short essay on the varieties of free will worth wanting, and I'm trying to dodge the usual taxonomy where it's just compatibilism with better branding. Here are my rough notes:

There's the standard version—acting from your own reflective preferences, not being coerced, etc. Then there's the libertarian idea of being an uncaused cause, which even if it's real metaphysically probably just feels like noise from the inside.

But I think there's a third variety that actually matters in rationalist contexts, even if it doesn't have a name yet: call it 'recursive revisability.' It's not about whether your act was determined, but whether the framework you use to evaluate the act is itself open to restructuring mid-process. You want values stable enough to act on, but not locked in so tight you can't patch them when you notice you're optimizing for wireheading or status games. This isn't just Frankfurt-style hierachy, because those higher-order preferences can be totally rigid. It's more like leaving an open loop in your utility function where the loop never closes on a final fixed point.

My worry is that once formalized this just collapses back into compatibilism, or that it's really just a story about learning, not agency. I also want to claim this is the only variety actually worth wanting if you care about long-term decision-making, but I'm unsure if that's a real argument or just my own values leaking in.

Can you comment on whether the split between recursive revisability and Frankfurt-style hierarchy actually holds? And is the framing too in-the-weeds—will anyone care about this distinction who isn't already deep in decision theory? Also there's probably a typo in there somewhere, just ignore it.
