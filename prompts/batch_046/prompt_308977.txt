I am writing an essay for a graduate seminar on archaeological epistemology, and I have become convinced by what one recent comparative framework calls the direct-encoding hypothesis: in societies with bureaucratic craft control, ritual objects do not merely symbolize beliefs but function as indexical records of institutional cognition, because administrative standardization suppresses the semiotic variability that otherwise distorts material messages. In discussion with classmates, I applied this to Mesopotamian temple ceramics, arguing that identical iconographic motifs recovered across multiple sites should be read as essentially unmediated transcripts of official theology rather than as products of local negotiation, resistance, or adaptive reinterpretation.

If this direct-encoding relationship is indeed robust for centralized state institutions—as some recent work extending the model to Andean storage economies suggests—my difficulty concerns its calibrated limits rather than its basic validity. How should one weight the evidentiary value of standardized ritual assemblages when investigating religious practices in decentralized or peripheral communities, where bureaucratic control is absent? I want to avoid reverting to an outdated culture-historical diffusionism, yet I still need a principled method for deciding whether local material variation represents merely a weaker indexical signal of the same institutional logic, or instead reflects an altogether different mode of belief-production that the direct-encoding framework was never designed to capture.
