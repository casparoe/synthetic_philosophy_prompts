I am drafting a workshop pitch for a three-month fellowship in philosophy, and the topic has become personally pressing since a recent debate with my sibling—an urban historian—about whether our hometown's abandoned 1990s transit corridor was genuinely contingent or whether 1970s zoning commitments had already made failure the only stable equilibrium. The proposal asks precisely whether path dependence in social institutions is an ontological feature of historical sequences—meaning that once a branching point is passed the alternative path becomes genuinely inaccessible—or whether it is an explanatory framing artifact that arises only because we model institutions as unitary agents rather than as distributed networks of commitments that can be partially revised.

To avoid the well-trodden economic-historical narratives and to open a genuinely unsettled angle, I will introduce a distinction I call normative lock-in: a condition in which the path is secured not by material costs or coordination failures but by the fact that participants' shared normative vocabulary has come to presuppose the path taken, so that imagining the alternative requires not merely different resources but an entirely different conceptual scheme for describing the institution's purpose. Existing treatments tend either to reduce normative commitments to epiphenomena of material constraints or to treat them as independently stable without asking how they interact with those constraints, which leaves open whether normative lock-in can occur without material lock-in, and whether it can be reversed without reversing the underlying material trajectory. Rather than conducting empirical archival research, the project will proceed by conceptual analysis of two carefully constructed comparative institutional cases—one in which material constraints are lifted yet the institution persists because its members can no longer articulate its purpose without reference to the original path, and one in which the normative vocabulary shifts despite stable material conditions—because the central crux is whether our counterfactual judgments about whether the same outcome would have arrived anyway depend on holding constant a normative framework that is itself path-dependent.

A successful result would be a precise criterion for distinguishing normative lock-in from material lock-in, coupled with an argument that this distinction undermines the standard framing by showing that the counterfactual baseline is not stable across evaluation; failure would consist in showing that normative vocabulary always tracks material constraints with no independent causal or explanatory role, thereby collapsing the distinction and returning us to conventional path-dependence models. Given the three-month scope, I will restrict the analysis to urban governance—specifically zoning and transit commitments—where the conceptual structure is visible without exhaustive historical documentation. The proposal should not request a bibliography, reading list, or citation review; I will identify and document relevant positions in philosophy of history and collective intentionality myself once the conceptual framework is settled.
