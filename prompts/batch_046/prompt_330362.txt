Me and my mate just had a long arguement about whether numbers actually matter when you're deciding who to rescue, and it made me think of this back-and-forth I overheard between Sam and Jordan in our study group. They were going at it about Taurek's claim that there's no special reason to save five over one, but they went somewhere I hadn't heard before — about whether flipping a coin just moves the problem around, and whether "expected moral value" is just aggregation in disguise.

Anyway, here's roughly how it went:

Sam: If you can save five people or one person and you pick the one, you've let four people die for no reason. The numbers obviously count — five lives are worth more than one.

Jordan: That's the mistake Taurek points out. You have a reason to save each person individually, but those reasons don't stack up like coins. Saving the five doesn't outweigh saving the one; you've just got five separate obligations and one separate obligation. The one you leave behind can ask "why not me?" and "because there were five others" isn't an answer addressed to them.

Sam: But if you flip a coin between the group of five and the single person, you've given everyone an equal shot. That respects equality. The problem is you might then save only one when you could definately have saved five. So ex ante you're trading a 50% chance of saving five lives for a 50% chance of saving one. That can't be right.

Jordan: You're sneaking aggregation back in through "expected value". Moral reasons don't work like betting odds. From the perspective of the one left behind, it doesn't matter that there was a coin flip — they're still dead, and you still had a direct reason to save them specifically. The fact that you also had reasons to save others doesn't dilute your reason to save this one. And if there were a hundred on one side and one on the other, you'd still have no aggregative reason — you'd just have a hundred separate reasons and you'd randomise. The number never becomes a weight.

Sam: Okay, but doctors in triage save the many all the time — are they all wrong? And I'm not harming the one if I save the five; the situation does the harm. I'm just not helping, so I'm not wronging anyone. Plus, between a hundred and one, randomising feels obviously wrong, which proves the numbers must matter at least for breaking ties.

Jordan: Not harming isn't enough when you can help. But more importantly, you keep treating lives as fungible units in a calculation. The reason randomising feels wrong with a hundred is psychological bias, not moral logic. Each life is irreplaceable, and flipping a coin at least lets you say "it was chance, not your lesser worth". You never actually justify leaving anyone behind by citing the group size.
