I’m preparing a seminar discussion on Zhuangzi’s butterfly dream and the broader skepticism about fixed distinctions in the Zhuangzi, and I’ve become stuck on a modern parallel that seems to push the argument in an unexpected direction. In the original parable, Zhuangzi wakes uncertain whether he is Zhuangzi who dreamed he was a butterfly, or a butterfly now dreaming it is Zhuangzi; the point is often read as an epistemic limit—waking and dreaming are indistinguishable—or as an ontological claim that selfhood is fluid through transformation (hua). But I want to push this further: suppose we imagine a near-future neurotechnology, not fanciful in its mechanics but conceptually rigorous, in which two subjects share a bidirectional brain-computer interface during sleep. Each subject experiences the other’s dream-body as their own; upon awakening, both retain fragmented memories of having been the other, and neither can identify a unique “waking self” prior to the interface. The transformation is not solitary, like the butterfly, but mutual and technologically mediated.

If Zhuangzi encountered this scenario—not as a mystic endorsing all dissolution, but as the philosopher who critiques the rigidity of distinctions (bian) and insists on the reality of transformation—how would he likely assess it? Would he see the shared interface as a technological confirmation that the boundary between self and other is as conventional as that between dream and waking? Or would he argue that the very design of the technology, which requires engineered synchronization and data protocols, secretly reinstates the kind of fixed, instrumental distinction he rejects? More specifically, would his skepticism remain epistemic (we still lack a criterion to tell who is who), or would it become more radically ontological: that there never was a stable substrate to be confused, only a continuous process of mutual becoming? Please construct your answer by engaging the internal logic of the Zhuangzi’s parable and its surrounding chapters—particularly the treatment of transformation and the critique of disputation—rather than by invoking generic “Eastern philosophy” tropes. I am not asking for a historical reconstruction of what Zhuangzi literally said; I want a philosophically disciplined prediction of how the view embedded in the text would respond to this specific conceptual challenge. Avoid bibliographic references or appeals to secondary scholarship that a reader could not verify from the argument itself; instead, show how the reasoning unfolds from the premises of the dream episode and the critique of fixed perspectives.
