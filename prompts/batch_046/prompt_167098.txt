I am drafting a novel whose protagonist, a philosopher of mathematics, is paralyzed by what she takes to be Carroll's proof that deduction is viciously circular. Reading the dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise, she concludes that to apply any logical rule—modus ponens, for instance—one must already presuppose that very rule, so that justification necessarily loops back on itself rather than reaching a foundation. She therefore treats mathematical proof as a kind of institutional convention sustained by collective agreement rather than by rational compulsion. Before I commit to her voice, I need to check the ground beneath my feet: does Carroll's argument actually establish a vicious circularity in deduction, or does it turn on a different mistake—perhaps conflating rules of inference with premises that could be added to an argument? More generally, among those who work on the philosophy of logic, is the response to Carroll considered settled—for example, that rules operate at a metalanguage level and need not appear as premises in the object language—or is there a live, substantive disagreement about whether deduction can ever be non-circularly justified? Please separate what is well-established in logic and its philosophy from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a minority or speculative position as if it were the consensus view.
