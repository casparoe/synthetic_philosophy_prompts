I am moderating a panel on historical Jesus methodology, and my interlocutor is an unnamed scholar who defends the strong thesis that the classical criteria of authenticity—multiple attestation, dissimilarity, embarrassment, and coherence—can be applied jointly and rigorously enough to yield a determinate, theologically neutral profile of the historical Jesus. I want eight to ten interview questions that go far beyond asking her to enumerate the criteria; each should press on an internal tension or boundary case, include a follow-up tailored to her probable reply, and avoid generic biographical or career questions.

Specifically, include:
- one question on the criterion of dissimilarity that asks how she avoids circularity when the Jewish and early Christian backgrounds used to establish dissimilarity are themselves reconstructed partly from the very gospel traditions being tested, together with a follow-up for the reply that she can correct for circularity by using independent sources such as Josephus or the Dead Sea Scrolls;
- one question on the criterion of embarrassment that confronts her with the memory-studies argument that counter-intuitive or embarrassing details are often more memorable and therefore more likely to be invented or enhanced for mnemonic retention rather than preserved despite social cost, with a follow-up keyed to her likely appeal to the specificity of the embarrassing detail;
- one question on coherence that probes whether the criterion is genuinely independent or merely a consistency check that reinforces whatever profile the other criteria have already selected, and that asks what she makes of the possibility that the historical figure was contextually inconsistent—behaving differently in Galilean versus Judean settings—without that inconsistency being a sign of inauthenticity, with a follow-up anticipating her defense of a unified core personality;
- one meta-methodological question that asks whether the entire project collapses into a hermeneutic circle: since judgments about what first-century audiences would find embarrassing, coherent, or dissimilar depend on historically reconstructed portraits of those audiences, and since those reconstructions draw on some of the same texts, can the criteria ever break out of the circle, or do they merely formalize prior assumptions about plausibility?

The remaining questions should continue in this vein: they should turn on the actual mechanics of applying the criteria, anticipate the standard defense, and include a targeted follow-up. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Keep the framing formal and focused on the conceptual architecture of the method, and inject at least one further niche conceptual pressure—such as asking how the criteria handle a case where a single pericope satisfies dissimilarity and embarrassment but violates coherence with every other attested saying, or whether treating the criteria as jointly sufficient rather than individually necessary changes the epistemic status of the resulting portrait from probable to merely possible.
