I've come round to a split-track fix for the wrong-kind-reasons problem: normative reasons come in two flavors, action-guiding and attitude-forming, and a reason is wrong-kind only when it fails to align with the constituitive aim of the attitude it's meant to shape. So moral considerations might be perfectly good reasons to want something but never good reasons to believe it, full stop. What would actually count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward — not just the standard Scanlon-style counterexamples, but cases where moral psycology or metaethics shows the split is unstable. Which ones would a defender of the split have to concede hurt the view, and which ones could they just absorb? Also, is there a risk this view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so is that bad for a view of this kind?
