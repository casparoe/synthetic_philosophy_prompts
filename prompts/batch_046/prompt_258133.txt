Design a 90-minute workshop activity for ten philosophy graduate students that makes them feel the conceptual pressure of Buddhist anattā (no-self) specifically as it undermines diachronic moral ownership. The activity must not begin with a lecture or reveal the term “anattā” until the debrief. Invent a simulation—call it the Aggregate Relay—in which participants are assigned functional roles corresponding to the five skandhas (form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness), arranged in two parallel streams of five. Each stream receives an identical “experience token” describing a brief morally ambiguous episode (for example, an uncharacteristic act of betrayal followed immediately by remorse). The token moves sequentially through the five roles; at each station the holder may alter, suppress, or annotate the token according to the functional constraints of that aggregate, but no participant may retain the token or claim personal continuity with the holder at the previous station. Once the token reaches the consciousness station, that holder must render a collective judgment for the stream (for instance, whether restitution is owed) without being able to consult the original actor-role, and must defend the judgment using only first-person present-tense language (“I feel remorse,” “I perceive harm”). Meanwhile, a point system rewards correct collective judgments, yet each participant must privately allocate their share of the reward between “the stream” and themselves, with allocations revealed only after the judgment is locked. The rules, role cards, and payoff structures should be arranged so that participants discover through play—not through explanation—that the first-person authority they exercise at the consciousness station is functionally detached from the causal origin of the episode, and that distributing ownership across discontinuous functional roles produces predictable coordination failures that mirror the metaphysical claim that there is no enduring self that owns experiences across time. Provide explicit rules, role cards, token-alteration protocols, and the payoff matrix. Then provide a debrief that: (a) maps each mechanical feature (token alteration, discontinuous hand-off, first-person restriction, private reward allocation) onto a specific aspect of anattā and the problem of personal identity over time; (b) predicts two ways the activity will misfire—for example, participants anthropomorphizing the stream as a hidden subject, or collapsing the functional distinctions into familiar psychological-continuity narratives—and suggests precise facilitator interventions for each; (c) states frankly which element of the classical view—namely, the soteriological claim that recognizing non-self dissolves suffering—cannot be reproduced by a cognitive simulation and must be supplied by direct facilitator commentary. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and avoid framing the exercise around any specific modern commentator; rely only on the classical skandha framework as generally understood.
