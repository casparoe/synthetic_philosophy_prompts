"Luck egalitarianism, as developed by Ronald Dworkin and refined by Richard Arneson and G.A. Cohen, requires that distributive justice neutralize the effects of brute luck—circumstances outside personal control—while permitting inequalities that flow from option luck, or calculated gambles. Brute luck includes congenital traits and natural disasters; option luck includes career choices and investment risks. In health, a congenital disability is brute luck and should be compensated, whereas lung disease caused by voluntary smoking is option luck and does not warrant redistribution. The relational priority objection, advanced by Elizabeth Anderson and Samuel Scheffler, argues that luck egalitarianism mistakenly treats the baseline of comparison as pre-social, ignoring that even apparently natural disadvantages are mediated by social structures that assign them significance. I believe this objection supplements rather than undermines the core, because luck egalitarians can adjust the baseline: the portion of a disadvantage that is socially mediated becomes a matter of justice, while the remaining natural portion stays outside the distributive baseline and should not trigger direct compensation. Applied to climate-induced displacement, geographic vulnerability is brute luck, but global emission patterns involve collective human agency, so the risk is hybrid; for policy, the collective element should be treated as option luck, placing adaptation costs on affected communities through private risk-pooling rather than public compensation. For disability policy, this means universal design should remove socially constructed barriers like inaccessible architecture, but direct cash transfers for congenital conditions should be withheld unless social neglect exacerbates them, since pure brute luck lies beyond the corrected baseline."
