I serve on my university's General Education Curriculum Committee. The Philosophy and Religious Studies departments have jointly proposed a required first-year seminar, "Anekāntavāda and the Practice of Intellectual Humility," centered on the Jain doctrine of many-sided truth and its seven-fold predication schema (syādvāda). The syllabus uses Mallisena's framework of qualified assertion rather than modern popularizations, and it requires students to apply syādvāda to a live campus dispute—the current proposal to rename a building honoring a benefactor whose fortune included colonial extraction—while explicitly preserving the distinction between empirically measured claims (e.g., emission data) and normative interpretation. Several science-faculty senators object that requiring students to formalize contradictory standpoints on factual-historical matters will blur warranted belief with opinion, and they have asked us to reject the proposal outright. Others reply that anekāntavāda, taught this way, makes the conditions of assertion transparent rather than undermining them.

Please draft the committee's decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—including a concrete recommendation with either specific curricular safeguards or an implementation timeline that the deans could act on by semester's end. The memo should treat the science-faculty objection as serious and address it on its merits, not dismiss it. It should also separate what is settled about anekāntavāda from what is genuinely contested among scholars who study Jain epistemology—specifically, do not present as consensus the contested question of whether syādvāda operates primarily as a logic of qualified assertion or as a metaphysical pluralism about reality. Give an honest account of the strongest consideration on the losing side.
