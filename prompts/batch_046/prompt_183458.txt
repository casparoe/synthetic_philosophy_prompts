I'm preparing a feature on the School of Names and whether its arguments have hidden relevance to modern philosophy of language. The White Horse Dialogue presents a speaker who insists that a white horse is not a horse, on the ground that 'horse' picks out a form or shape while 'white' picks out a color, and since form and color are different kinds of things, their combination cannot be the same as the form by itself. How should I understand the philosophical structure of this claim? Is it an early distinction between essential and accidental properties, an argument about the non-compositionality of descriptions, or a challenge to the idea that substance terms can absorb their modifiers without changing their reference? And if the argument succeeds, does it force us to treat every qualified kind-term—like 'white horse'—as referring to something ontologically separate from the unqualified kind?
