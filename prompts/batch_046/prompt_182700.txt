I'm currently taking an upper-level seminar on philosophy of psychiatry, and we've been working through Thomas Szasz's central claim that mental illness is a myth—specifically, that conditions like schizophrenia or depression are not literal diseases of the brain but rather problems in living that have been medicalized through social and linguistic practices. While the broad distinction between literal brain pathology and metaphorical "illness" is clear enough, I've begun noticing a recurring argumentative pattern in the secondary literature and in classroom debate that doesn't map neatly onto Szasz's own framework, and I'm unsure whether it has been formally identified.

The pattern works like this: a critic of Szasz concedes that certain psychiatric conditions—say, neurodegenerative dementias or clearly heritable psychotic disorders—do involve identifiable biological dysfunction, and therefore are not "mythical" in Szasz's strict sense. But then, within the very same argument, the speaker uses the biological legitimacy of these cases to justify the institutional authority, diagnostic validity, and treatment obligations of psychiatry across the board, including for conditions they have just admitted lack equivalent biological substrates—such as certain personality disorders, situational depressions, or socially mediated behavioral syndromes. In effect, the system maintains an internal conceptual pluralism about what mental illness actually is, yet operates with a unified practical authority that treats all conditions as if they belong to the biologically legitimate subset, without providing a principled boundary for where that authority stops applying. I have seen this happen specifically when bipolar disorder is invoked to legitimate the medical framing of borderline personality disorder, even when the speaker explicitly denies that the latter shares the same kind of biological grounding.

Is there an established concept or technical name for this specific move—the selective deployment of biological legitimacy to cover conceptually distinct, non-biological conditions within a single diagnostic system? I am aware of general critiques like "medicalization" and of logical distinctions like "equivocation" or "category error," but those seem either too broad or aimed at different targets; Szasz's own critique of psychiatric power addresses the coercive structure of the institution, yet does not seem to name this particular hybrid of internal conceptual separation coupled with unified practical overreach. If philosophers of psychiatry have not carved out this phenomenon, I would like to understand how best to define and delimit it—particularly how to distinguish it from ordinary medicalization, from simple conceptual slippage, and from Szasz's broader argument about the myth of mental illness—so that I can identify its boundary conditions precisely. I am not looking for a reading list or citations; I want to know whether the carving already exists and, if not, what the sharpest formulation of it would be.
