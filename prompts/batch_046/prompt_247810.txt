Here is an argument I have been turning over that seems to prove far too much about expertise and understanding, and I cannot locate the exit. I would like you to diagnose precisely where it goes wrong.

(1) Darwin’s inversion of reasoning shows that nature can produce exquisitely competent functional designs — the vertebrate eye, the immune response — without any agent, evolutionary or otherwise, comprehending the physics, chemistry, or engineering principles involved.
(2) Turing’s inversion of reasoning shows that a purely formal system can display behaviour indistinguishable from intelligent problem-solving — arithmetic, chess, strategic language use — without the system comprehending the meanings, truths, or strategies that its outputs express.
(3) In both domains, competence is causally and functionally independent of comprehension: the mechanism succeeds precisely because it does not need to understand what it is doing.
(4) Human expert judgement — in clinical diagnosis, mathematical proof, or moral deliberation — is also the product of evolved neural architecture and, at the implementation level, of computational state-transitions.
(5) Therefore, human expertise is just a further instance of competence without necessary comprehension: a physician may reach the correct diagnosis through statistical pattern-matching without grasping the underlying pathology; a mathematician may derive a result through formal symbol-manipulation without semantic insight.
(6) If comprehension is never causally necessary for competence, then it is never normatively necessary for the authority or justification of an expert judgement.
(7) Consequently, a non-comprehending system — whether an evolved algorithm or a sufficiently advanced artificial model — that replicates all the outputs of human experts possesses exactly the same epistemic and moral authority as those experts themselves.
(8) But this implies that there is no rational basis for preferring a comprehending scientist to a non-comprehending system that makes identical predictions, nor for valuing education aimed at fostering understanding over mere behavioural calibration.
(9) That conclusion undermines the very practice of seeking explanations and justifications, which presupposes that understanding matters beyond mere performance. So one of the premises must be wrong.

I am inclined to keep (1) through (4), and I very much want to preserve the idea that human cognition is continuous with evolutionary and computational processes. I am least committed to (6). Please tell me which step to reject, why it seemed plausible given Darwin’s and Turing’s inversions, and what abandoning it costs — especially whether it forces me to treat comprehension as causally necessary after all, or whether a more subtle repair is available.
