So I've been drafting a little essay for my blog—working title is No Captain, Only Crew: Anatta and the Predictive Self—and I'm not sure if its actually coherent or just sounds smart. The basic move is that Western philosophy usually treats Buddhist anatta as either Parfit-style reductionism or some kind of eliminativist illusionism, but I think that's missing the specific target of the denial. In the Abhidharma and especially Yogacara texts, what's being denied isn't continuity or narrative coherence—it's a particular kind of metaphysical controller, an autonomous atman that stands behind experience and directs it. Meanwhile the predictive-processing view in cognitive science—Andy Clark, Anil Seth, that crowd—treats the self as a generative model that constantly predicts interoceptive and exteroceptive states. That sounds structurally similar to the Yogacara idea of alayavijnana, the storehouse consciousness that seeds present experience without being a subject in the Cartesian sense.

My niche claim—maybe its just wrong—is that anatta is actually compatible with a very thin kind of personal identity, what I'm calling causal-narrative anatta. The continuity isn't in a substance but in the causal-informational trajectory of that storehouse or predictive model. So you can reject the captain without rejecting the ship's log. I think this lets you have diachronic responsibility and memory without smuggling in a self, but I'm worried I'm just redefining identity until the contradiction disappears. I'd especially like comments on whether I'm misreading Yogacara by treating alayavijnana as a functional analog to a predictive model rather than something more metaphysically loaded, and whether the argument that anatta supports this thin identity actually holds up or collapses into standard reductionism with extra steps. Also, does the framing feel interesting, or is it just stitching together two literatures I half-understand?
