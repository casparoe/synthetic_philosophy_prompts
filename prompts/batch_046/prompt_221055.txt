Clinicians and ethicists often invoke an analogy between withdrawing life support and letting a car run out of fuel rather than driving it off a cliff: both result in the car reaching the bottom, but only the latter involves actively directing it toward destruction. The analogy is meant to license the view that stopping a ventilator is closer to allowing nature to take its course than to killing. Audit the mapping: what corresponds to the driver, the vehicle, the cliff, the empty tank, coasting, and the intentional steering in the medical case—agent, patient, lethal outcome, underlying pathology, natural trajectory, and deliberate intervention? Identify where it breaks: for example, is the ventilator more like fuel that the body naturally consumes, or like an external bridge that keeps the vehicle suspended? Does the analogy quietly import the assumption that the patient has an independent “course” that the physician merely permits, and is that tenable when the body is already technologically sustained? Then say whether the moral distinction between withdrawal and active euthanasia survives these disanalogies, or whether a framework drawn from system maintenance rather than vehicular control is more appropriate. In your answer, separate what is settled about the moral relevance of causation and intention in end-of-life ethics from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present any contested position—such as the claim that omission and commission are always morally equivalent, or that they are never equivalent—as the consensus view.
