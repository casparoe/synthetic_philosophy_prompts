I just spent the weekend arguing with my roommate about whether the AI-generated landscapes in a downtown gallery opening are genuinely beautiful or merely technically impressive, and I realized that I have no clear account of why our disagreement feels like a real clash rather than a mere difference in taste. That sent me back to Hume's essay on the standard of taste, but the more I re-read it, the less sure I am whether Hume thinks aesthetic disagreement can ever be genuinely resolved or whether he is simply describing the conditions under which we should treat one person's response as more authoritative. I am on the exam committee for our upper-level aesthetics seminar, and I want one essay question that students can complete in about seventy-five minutes. The question should not be answerable by summarizing Hume's conditions for the ideal critic or by listing the familiar objections to his view. Instead, it should present students with a specific three-way tension: if two observers who both appear to satisfy Hume's criteria for good judges reach opposite verdicts about the same work, is the disagreement best explained by a hidden defect in one observer's sensibility, by an ambiguity in the work itself that permits incompatible but equally valid responses, or by the mistake of treating Hume's standard as genuinely normative rather than descriptive? Ask them to defend which of these three explanations Hume's account actually supports, explain why the other two are incompatible with his broader commitments, and show what their chosen reading predicts about a case involving AI-generated art that the student constructs themselves. Along with the exam question, provide a model answer that takes a clear position, and a marking scheme with exactly five must-hit points, the marks each carries out of twenty total, and a one-line note for each on the specific mistake that costs candidates those marks.
