As a novelist who has spent the past eighteen months in a weekly workshop where fiction writers and literary critics debate interpretation, I have watched a particular disagreement about authorial authority harden into ritual rather than argument. The dispute turns on whether private notebooks, discarded drafts, and later interviews should count as authoritative for the meaning of the finished novel. One faction, which I think of as the blueprint position, holds that the intentional architecture of a text is fixed at the moment of its final authorized form; on this view, any subsequent commentary by the author is either redundant with what the sentences already encode or an external corruption of them. The opposing faction, which I call the palimpsest position, insists that a novel is not a static artifact but an accretion of intentional layers across revisions, and that an author's later revisions, public disavowals, or explanatory essays are internal to the work's history rather than biographical noise outside it. Both sides invoke the same familiar scenarios—an author who later claimed a symbol meant something unintended, a poet who revised an ending decades after first publication—yet after more than a year of discussion no one has moved, and the conversation now circles these examples without advancing.

What strikes me is that everyone in the room has read the same archival materials and agrees on the basic chronology of drafts; there is no hidden empirical fact in dispute. My working theory is that the impasse is not about evidence but about what object is being interpreted. The blueprint side seems to be asking a semantic question about what the sentences in the final version mean, while the palimpsest side seems to be asking a genetic question about which intentions are constitutive of the work's identity over time. I wonder whether this is a verbal disagreement about the word meaning, a substantive divergence about the ontology of the artwork, or merely a clash of bedrock intuitions about artistic authority that neither side can rationally defend. I also wonder whether the question is ill-posed, with the two factions answering different questions—one about interpretation, one about artistic identity—under a single heading.

Could you diagnose the actual structure of this stall using what each side says about drafts, final form, and authorial commentary? I want more than a survey of positions: I want an honest assessment of whether the disagreement is substantive, verbal, or rooted in unarguable starting points; whether a hidden formal or conceptual question is doing the work; and whether anything—a precise distinction, a model of artistic identity, or a reframing of the question—could actually move it, or whether we should expect no progress and learn to live with the division. Please do not treat this as a request for adjudication between the blueprint and palimpsest views, but as a request to understand why the debate has become immovable.
