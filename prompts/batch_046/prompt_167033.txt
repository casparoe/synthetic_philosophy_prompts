In my course on Hellenistic philosophy we have been comparing Epictetus's account of prohairesis with the modern Stoic revival's therapeutic applications, and I have run into an argument that seems to prove far more than I intended. (1) The dichotomy of control, as both Epictetus and contemporary Stoic practice present it, restricts what is genuinely "ours" to our judgments, intentions, and acts of assent. (2) If this is an ethical principle and not merely a psychological technique, then outcomes—whether our actions help others, whether institutions change, whether suffering is reduced—lie outside the sphere of moral concern. (3) Therefore, a Stoic should be indifferent between a just action that succeeds and one that fails, provided the internal judgment was correct in each case. (4) Modern Stoics, however, routinely advocate civic engagement, charity, and social reform—activities that only make sense if outcomes matter. (5) Applying the dichotomy consistently would reduce these activities to instruments of self-cultivation: we engage with the world not because the world needs changing, but because engagement shapes our character. (6) But then Stoic ethics collapses into a quietism where external improvement is accidental and moral evaluation concerns only internal order, which contradicts the revival's claim to offer practical guidance for living well in society. I suspect the error lies either in treating the dichotomy as a comprehensive ethical principle rather than a therapeutic boundary, or in assuming that indifference to outcomes eliminates all reasons for preferring one action over another. Which step should I reject, and what does that rejection cost for the coherence of modern Stoicism?
