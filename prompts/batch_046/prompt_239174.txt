Analyze the British and French governments decision to keep bankrolling Concorde through the early 70s as a case study in sunk-cost reasoning. By 1971 development costs had blown past estimates, airline orders had mostly evaporated, and US rules had made overland supersonic routes impossible—yet funding continued straight through to the 1976 entry into service. Use the sunk-cost fallacy framework, but inject this twist: dont just ask whether continuing was prospectively irrational, ask whether it can be reconstructed as an expressive act of maintaining state credibility for future collaborative tech projects. What does standard decision theory say the actors should have done? Where does the expressive/credibility story actually explain the episode, and where is it just post-hoc rationalization? Does the case support the hardline view that honoring sunk costs is always irrational, or does it reveal a gap in the theory—something like diachronic rationality that pure forward-looking models miss? Be disciplined about who the decision-makers actually were, what the real options on the table were, and say explicitly where the theory is silent rather than forcing an answer.
