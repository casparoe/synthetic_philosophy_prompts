I have been asked to serve as an external methodological consultant for a small interdisciplinary journal—publishing in theoretical physics, cognitive science, and biology—after a long discussion with colleagues about why peer reviewers without philosophical training keep accepting manuscripts whose thought experiments do little more than dress up intuition. Several times a month I must evaluate submissions that turn on imaginary scenarios: an observer falling through an event horizon to test complementarity, or a modified decision problem used to model neural moral computation. The editor expects a standardized recommendation—accept with minor revisions, major revision, or reject—backed by a justification I can complete in about ninety minutes, readable by scientists who have never taken a philosophy course, and I must sign off personally on every call. Please act as an expert in philosophy of science and review methodology, think carefully, and begin with a brief brainstorming of what separates a knowledge-yielding scientific thought experiment from mere intuition-pumping. Then operationalize the philosophical idea that these experiments yield knowledge by applying counterfactual constraint to specific theoretical commitments rather than through observation. To make the translation usable, inject and work with a precise distinction: call the moments where a scenario genuinely constrains a theory's commitments "traction points," and call the moments where it only renders an intuition vivid or cognitively accessible "lubrication points." Turn this into a reusable practitioner procedure: a numbered checklist or flowchart-in-words with explicit stopping rules, clearly marking which steps are mechanical and which are irreducible judgment calls where two reasonable reviewers might diverge without either being wrong. The procedure must include worked applications to three hard cases: a manuscript that redeploys a classic scenario—say, a Schrödinger-type superposition analogy—to argue for a genuinely novel interpretation; a paper that asserts direct empirical relevance for a purely conceptual case; and a scenario that derives conclusions about unobservable entities while violating an established symmetry principle. Make sure one of these applications produces a recommendation of rejection despite the underlying conceptual move feeling genuinely important. Finally, state exactly what this procedure is not designed to evaluate—such as pedagogical value or the use of ethical analogies in scientific reasoning—and identify the single judgment point where legitimate disagreement is most likely.
