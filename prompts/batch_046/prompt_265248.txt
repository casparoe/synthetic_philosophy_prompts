I help run a monthly discussion circle at my neighborhood library—usually eight to twelve adults with no formal philosophy background, meeting for about ninety minutes on a Saturday morning. We have been circling the same argument about whether to remove old statues or rename streets that honor people who caused serious harm, and the conversation always collapses into two sides: those who say we are erasing history, and those who say public space should not celebrate cruelty. I think both sides miss something I keep noticing in my own life: a street name mostly functions as a coordinate—“turn left on Marshall Road”—and using it that way does not feel like making a moral endorsement, yet once people learn who Marshall was, the same word suddenly carries a weight it did not have before. I want an activity that makes participants feel this split physically, not just hear it explained.

Please design a structured simulation or role-play for our group. Give us a fictional town laid out on a grid, with roles such as long-time residents, new arrivals, city planners, and people whose families were harmed by the figures being commemorated. Give each group tokens representing limited community trust and a fixed street budget, and rules that let them choose among three options for contested names: keep them, replace them entirely, or layer a new name alongside the old so both appear on signs and maps. The rules should be arranged so that participants first experience how breaking an old coordinate confuses navigation and daily cooperation, then feel the moral pressure that makes keeping the old name feel like an active choice rather than a neutral habit, and finally discover that layering creates its own conflicts—duplicate directions, split memories, and arguments about which name “counts.” Include a clear timeline for the ninety minutes, the debrief questions that map each moment onto the real-world tension between navigational utility and commemorative meaning, and an honest note about which aspect of historical responsibility—perhaps the long-term obligation to remember harm rather than merely avoid honoring it—the game cannot reproduce and I will need to supply in discussion. Do not ask anyone to cite philosophers or memorize theories; let the force of the idea come from the rules and the stakes.
