I will defend a thesis—developed in my dissertation and now the centerpiece of a job-market paper—at an interdisciplinary colloquium next Thursday that mixes quantitative sociology, critical theory, and applied policy studies. The thesis is that generational categories as used in both public discourse and policy analysis—Baby Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, and the like—are not ontologically robust cohorts but what I call diachronic demographic fictions. They reify temporary alignments of birth year, macroeconomic period, and institutional exposure into enduring social kinds with shared interests and moral claims. Once life-stage effects are separated from period effects, and once institutional mediation—specifically the transition from defined-benefit pension regimes and industrial labor markets to student-debt-financed human capital and platform-mediated contingent work—is properly modeled, there remains no non-arbitrary property that binds a so-called Millennial across class, race, or national context. Consequently, intergenerational-justice arguments that frame climate debt or pension burdens as transactions between bounded generational agents commit a category mistake: they treat an accounting artifact as a moral person.

The audience is sharply divided in expertise and commitment. There are three quantitative life-course sociologists for whom cohort is a standard analytical unit; a critical theorist who studies neoliberal branding and generational identity; and two applied scholars—one in climate governance, one in housing policy—who deploy generational framing routinely in grant applications and legislative testimony. They are skeptical but not hostile; what they share is institutional investment in the categories I am attacking. I need a speaker-prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis itself. Order the objections by the damage they will actually do: begin with the methodological challenges from the life-course scholars, then the ideological-framing objection from the critical theorist, then the practical-policy objections from the climate and housing scholars. For each, phrase the objection exactly as that audience would put it—in their vocabulary, with their underlying commitments visible—then specify what I should concede without capitulating, what I must hold firm, a concise reply suitable for a two-minute exchange, and the precise weak spot in that reply that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they overlook. Finally, identify the single objection I am most hoping nobody raises—likely one that collapses my ontological claim into a merely methodological one, or that forces me to concede that my own framework requires the very cohort distinctions it denies—and explain why it threatens coherence rather than mere popularity. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I need strategic framing and rhetorical positioning.
