You are a professional philosopher of value theory and intellectual history, tasked with designing a pedagogical classification instrument for a graduate seminar. The seminar focuses on Moritz Schlick’s well-known argument that the meaning of life is not found in external purpose or teleological fulfillment, but rather in immanent activity—play, creation, or experience pursued for its own sake. I want you to construct a decision tree that sorts the logical space of positions on this specific debate, rather than merely listing named theorists. Before you draw any branches, please take a moment to brainstorm: list all sixteen possible answer-combinations generated by the four binary splits below, indicate which combinations are occupied by recognizable views, which are empty, and briefly diagnose whether each gap represents incoherence, unattractiveness, or simple neglect.

The tree should begin with a root question: does human life require an external teleological purpose in order to possess meaning? From there, branch sequentially through four diagnostic distinctions. First, whether immanent activity (play, artistic creation, or sensory engagement) can fully substitute for missing teleology, or whether it remains deficient without an overarching end. Second, whether subjective fulfillment is sufficient for meaning, or whether an objective standard—independent of the agent’s attitudes—must also be satisfied. Third, whether the finitude of death and the inevitability of decay undermine any accumulated meaning, or whether temporal boundedness is irrelevant to significance. Fourth, whether meaning is scale-dependent: that is, whether a claim about meaning applies differently to an individual life, a collective project, or the human species as a whole.

Each leaf of the tree should be labeled with either a recognized position or a coherent but underexplored hybrid that you construct to fill an empty branch. The recognized positions must include Schlick’s own ludic immanentism, a teleological theist view, an existentialist subjectivist view, and a nihilistic fatalist view. You must also inject two niche hybrids of your own design—for example, teleological play-ism (the view that cosmic purpose exists but is itself playful and non-instrumental rather than goal-directed), and objective ludic nihilism (the view that play is the only objective value, yet human lives are too brief and constrained for it ever to reach a threshold of significance). For every leaf, provide exactly one sentence stating its core commitment and one sentence describing its most awkward consequence—something its defender must accept that sounds implausible on first hearing.

Every branching node should be phrased as a concrete yes/no or either/or question that a student could answer without already knowing the philosophical labels in advance; do not use technical jargon in the node text unless you define it in the branch description. Where two leaves risk collapsing on your tree—particularly Schlick’s view and a generic Aristotelian flourishing account—explicitly flag that the tree cannot separate them given the four splits you have chosen, and state what additional distinction (for instance, about the role of virtue or about whether play requires freedom from necessity) would be required to pull them apart. Keep the instrument entirely self-contained: do not request bibliographies, do not ask for reading lists, do not cite specific page numbers or editions, and do not put any real author’s words inside quotation marks. Before finalizing, please review your tree to confirm that no answer path depends on knowledge of Schlick’s biography or on any obscure historical fact that a model without web access could not be expected to know, and then present the full taxonomy in a clear hierarchical format.
