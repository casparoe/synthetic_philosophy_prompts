I'm putting together a little packet for my phil of art reading group and I want a poem that actually does philosophy, not just name-drop it. We're stuck in the weeds on aesthetic testimony — specifically whether you can come to know something's beautiful purely through someone telling you, or if you need direct acquaintance with the work yourself. There's this asymmetry people talk about: negative judgments (this is ugly, this is kitsch) seem to travel through testimony easier than positive ones, because claiming beauty requires a kind of experiential uptake that secondhand words can't fully deliver. I want the form to mirror that failure structurally.

Write a pantoum with six stanzas — the one where lines 2 and 4 of each quatrain become lines 1 and 3 of the next — spoken by a listener who is trying to take an expert's word for the beauty of a work they've never seen, maybe a monochrome installation across the world. Use two repeating refrains: one about not having seen it ("my eyes have not recieved it" — yes, misspelled on purpose if you want to keep the typo vibe, or just write it naturally with an error), and one about the expert's claim ("she says it is beautiful"). The repetition should enact the circular trap: you need the testimony to know what to look for, but you can't validate the testimony without already having looked. By the final couplet the two refrains should be locked side by side, showing the acquaintance-demand and the trust-in-testimony just passing each other without resolving. Make sure the negative judgment gets to shift or transform across stanzas while the positive one stays stuck, so the asymmetry is built into the echo. Someone who knows the testimony debate should recognize the acquaintance problem and the positive/negative split; someone who doesn't should still feel the claustrophobia. Keep it poetic, not didactic — and definatly don't make it sound like a lecture slide.
