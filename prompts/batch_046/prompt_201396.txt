I am a novelist who hosts a quarterly interview series on the architecture of thought, and for the season's opening episode I am preparing to speak with a philosopher of cognition—let me call her Dr. Elena Voss—who defends the view that analogy-making, rather than rule-based computation or statistical pattern-matching, is the fundamental engine of cognition, in the tradition articulated by Hofstadter and Sander in Surfaces and Essences. I do not wish to ask her to rehearse familiar examples of conceptual blending or category slippage; instead, I want to press the framework in a domain where it is rarely tested, namely the construction and reception of literary fiction. In my own recent writing I have been constructing characters deliberately engineered to resist analogical mapping onto familiar emotional schemas—figures who experience something structurally akin to grief without memory, or attachment without reciprocity—yet readers still achieve a kind of understanding, not by abandoning analogy but by stretching it toward the point of near-collapse. I am convinced this phenomenon sits at the deepest level of the analogy-first framework, but I do not know whether it ultimately supports or undermines that framework, and I want the interview to explore that tension precisely.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions, formal in register but suitable for spoken delivery, that avoid biographical opening and stay firmly in these conceptual weeds. I want at least one question asking whether such "strained analogies" are best read as proof that analogy is prior to understanding, or rather as evidence that understanding sometimes proceeds through analogies we knowingly treat as false; at least one question on how the view accounts for the recognition of analogical failure itself—if analogy is the core cognitive mechanism, what registers that a mapping has collapsed rather than merely shifted; and at least one question on whether the literary distinction between surface ornament and deep structural feature in character construction corresponds to the slippage mechanisms central to this view, followed immediately by a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that literary judgment is merely "high-level analogy with emotional weighting." For each of these three, include a second-layer follow-up that anticipates her standard defense and pushes past it: if she invokes the flexibility of conceptual categories, press her on whether that flexibility has principled limits; if she appeals to nested analogies, ask what anchors the nesting when the base analog is deliberately opaque. The remaining questions should probe the boundary between analogy and other capacities—whether narrative empathy can be fully explained by analogy without recourse to theory of mind, and whether there exists any form of aesthetic recognition that proceeds without an analogical substrate—each with a follow-up assuming she will defend analogy's primacy and asking what phenomenological or empirical observation could actually dislodge that defense. Format the sequence as a numbered list from one to ten, embedding the follow-ups clearly within or adjacent to their primary questions, so that I can use them directly without reordering.
