(2) The same luck-based considerations apply symmetrically to praise, reward, and economic entitlement: talents, effort-levels, and the social conditions that make effort possible are all equally outside the agent’s ultimate control.

(3) So if luck undermines desert in the criminal-justice domain, consistency requires that it undermines desert in every other domain—aesthetic acclaim, professional promotion, contract-based wages, and so on.

(4) But a society that eliminated all desert-talk—distributing jobs, honors, and resources purely by risk-assessment, social utility, or need, with zero reference to what anyone has earned—would be not only unrecognizable but practically unstable; it would dissolve the participatory practices that make moral life coherent (something close to Dennett’s concern about reactive attitudes).

(5) Therefore, either we reject the luck-objection globally and restore just deserts everywhere (including retributive punishment), or we accept a universal desert-skepticism that collapses social coordination.

(6) Universal desert-skepticism is impossible to live with, so we must reject (1) and retain retributive desert for punishment.

(7) Hence Caruso’s argument proves too much: it does not just undermine punishment, it undermines every institution that relies on earned status.
