For my own work on democratic theory I am trying to hold together the following commitments. First, democracy is best understood not as preference aggregation but as cooperative social inquiry aimed at resolving indeterminate situations. Second, political claims should be warranted by functional success in coordinating action and reducing conflict, not by correspondence to an independent reality. Third, democratic institutions ought to build in institutional falsification: major policies should carry embedded metrics that, if unmet, automatically trigger suspension or revision rather than waiting for periodic elections. Fourth, procedural rights—equal deliberative standing and protection from arbitrary exclusion—are framework conditions of inquiry rather than its outputs, and so should not themselves be open to experimental revision. Fifth, the public is continuously reconstituted by those who recognize themselves as affected by indirect consequences; yet democratic legitimacy also requires that anyone who will be affected by a decision have direct participatory standing before it is made. Sixth, scientific and democratic inquiry share an identical logical structure—both transform problematic situations through controlled experimentation—though democratic inquiry alone is constrained by the need to preserve social solidarity among inquirers, which limits which hypotheses can be tested. Seventh, expert judgment should carry asymmetric weight in deliberation, but only when experts are embedded within and subject to the same falsification mechanisms that govern policies.

Audit whether this set hangs together. Identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which are only apparently so and dissolve on inspection, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each real tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Then tell me which single change to my view would clean up the most tension overall.
