Consider the administrative implementation of the United States government’s 2018 zero-tolerance immigration enforcement policy, which resulted in the systematic separation of parents from minor children at the southern border. The salient facts are these: the Department of Justice announced that all unauthorized border crossings would be criminally prosecuted; because children could not be held in adult criminal detention, they were separated and placed in the custody of the Department of Health and Human Services; career civil servants and mid-level officials across multiple agencies executed the logistics, some registering private moral objections but continuing to process cases; senior political appointees overruled internal warnings about welfare risks and capacity constraints; and the policy was suspended after several weeks of public and judicial reaction. I want you to examine this episode through the lens of Hannah Arendt’s analysis of Adolf Eichmann and what she termed the banality of evil, together with her related claim—developed in her writings on modern bureaucracy—that administration tends toward a rule of nobody in which responsibility is diffused across procedural roles. Specifically: (1) How does Arendt’s framework characterize the moral situation of the implementing officials? Do they appear as thoughtless functionaries in her precise sense, or does the presence of explicit policy directives and occasional expressions of discomfort alter the diagnosis? (2) Which features of the episode does her account explain well—for example, the substitution of procedural correctness for moral judgment, the insulation of actors from the human consequences of their actions, or the failure to refuse orders despite available institutional channels—and which does it misdescribe or leave silent, particularly regarding the difference between a democratic administrative state and the totalitarian conditions she believed made Eichmann’s form of evil possible? (3) What does the episode in turn reveal about the limits or portability of Arendt’s theory? In particular, does it show that banal administrative evil can arise without the ideological destruction of public space and the transformation of human beings into superfluous beings that she argued were prerequisites in the totalitarian case, or does it instead suggest that her framework must be supplemented by an account of democratic complicity? In answering, be disciplined about the mapping: identify the specific actors, the options actually available to them, and the evidence of their mental states that Arendt’s concept of thinking (as distinct from mere intelligence or technical competence) would treat as relevant, and say explicitly where her theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
