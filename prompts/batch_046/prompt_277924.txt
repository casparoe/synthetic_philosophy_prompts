"Aristotle treats akrasia in NE VII as a failure of practical reasoning: the agent holds the correct universal premise (e.g., 'heavy foods are unhealthy') and knows the particular applies, but appetite overrides reason so she acts contrary to her decision. Hyperbolic discounting, formalized by George Ainslie and later economists, describes preferences where the discount factor between two rewards declines with proximity, producing reversals: a person may prefer $110 in 31 days over $100 in 30 days, but switch to $100 now over $110 in one day. Structurally, both involve a reversal between a present-judged best option and an actual choice. However, Aristotle's framework assumes a unitary rational soul that can be overruled by a non-rational part, whereas hyperbolic discounting is often interpreted through a 'planner-doer' divided-self model—developed by Ainslie—in which different temporal perspectives have different preferences. Because hyperbolic discounting makes preference reversal predictable and mechanistic, it seems to eliminate the kind of culpable failure Aristotle describes: if the agent's preferences are time-inconsistent by psychological law, she does not so much act against her judgment as reveal that she never had a stable judgment to violate.
