I sit on the ethics committee of a multi-hospital transplant consortium, and we are revising our donor-declaration protocol for patients supported by extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. We recently faced a case in which a patient had unequivocal brainstem failure and flat cortical EEG, yet maintained spontaneous cardiac rhythm through the circuit and showed residual hypothalamic thermoregulation and antidiuretic hormone secretion. The family argues that because circulation never ceased and some subcortical functions persist, declaring brain death here is a legal fiction that masks procurement-related killing; the surgical team argues that integrative unity of the organism is already lost, and that waiting for circulatory arrest risks organ damage without philosophical gain. Our committee must recommend a single criterion—whole-brain death, higher-brain death, or irreversible circulatory cessation—and specify how reviewers should apply it when technology sustains partial biological functions. Draft a concise policy memo with an actionable bottom line, including criteria that distinguish reflexive hypothalamic activity from genuine integrative function. Treat the strongest objection honestly: that adopting brain death for ECMO donors erodes the dead-donor rule by allowing vital-organ removal from bodies that still maintain homeostatic loops. Write with the clarity and institutional caution typical of rationalist discussions of governance and value pluralism.
