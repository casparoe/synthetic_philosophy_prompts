Analyze the trial and execution of Louis XVI in December 1792–January 1793 as a case study in whether revolutions devour their children. Rather than treating the Convention's decision as determined by class interest or republican ideology alone, apply the concept of institutional suspicion: the structural pressure on revolutionary bodies to continuously escalate against symbols of the old regime in order to prove their own legitimacy. Identify the actual strategic options facing the Girondins and Montagnards—not merely "execute or spare," but the specific procedural alternatives of trial by the Convention, appeal to the people, conditional imprisonment, or immediate execution—and map which actors favored which option and why. Show what institutional suspicion explains about the shift of moderate deputies who had previously defended royal inviolability, and where it fails, particularly regarding Robespierre's specific rhetorical framing of the king's body as criminal versus the structural dynamics alone. Then reverse the analysis: what does this episode reveal about the "revolutions devour their children" thesis? Is the execution an instance of the revolution consuming its own monarchical origin, or does it expose a gap in the thesis—namely, that destroying an external sovereign is categorically different from purging internal revolutionaries?
