i've been thinking that trying to tell world history from 'everywhere at once' is actually impossible in principle, not just hard in practice. The idea is that every historical narrative needs some kind of emplotment -- a plot shape, causal weighting, what counts as an event vs background noise -- and that emplotment always comes from a specific archival postion and a specific set of questions. So when people try to do global history by just stacking local archives together and removing the narrator, they don't get neutrality; they get a hidden Eurocentric frame smuggled in through the selection rules and the causal grammar. In other words, polycentrism fails not because we lack sources but because narrative explanation requires a situated point of view, and pretending otherwise produces false universalism. What would actually count against this? I want the defeaters sorted: what evidence, what counterexamples, what formal results about narrative structure, or what actual historiographcal works would show that you can integrate perspectives without a hidden center? Also, has this view already been qualified into unfalsifiability -- like, if every failure of polycentrism just gets read as proof that the hidden center is still there? Is that insulation a problem? I'm especially interested in whether there's a concrete case where historians genuinely merged incommensurable archival logics without one dominating, and what that case would have to look like to actually hurt my position.
