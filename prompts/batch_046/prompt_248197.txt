I'm trying to figure out what Plato actually thinks about drunkenness, not just the standard line that he hates it because reason should rule. Here are my gut verdicts on cases:
- The Symposium-style party with a symposiarch watering the wine and guiding talk toward philosophy: totally fine, maybe the best use of alcohol.
- A citizen in Magnesia breaking the law to drink at a private party: not fine, the restriction is there for a reason.
- Alcibiades bursting in drunk and confessing his desire for Socrates: the behavior is disorderly, but its genuinely revealing. I can't decide if Plato thinks this is 'not fine but valuable' or just bad.
- Drinking wine purely for medicinal pain relief at the end of life: seems okay, like a different category altogether.
- Drinking with the explicit plan to 'test your character' by lowering inhibitions: not fine, you're manufacturing a false condition.
- Two people drink the same amount; one becomes poetic and wise, the other violent. The first case is acceptable, the second isn't, but it's the same physical act, so any principle can't just be about quantiy or even about reason being clouded, since both lost reason somewhat.

The case that keeps flipping for me: a philosopher drinks alone in moderation, specifically to loosen his thinking on a hard ethcial problem, and stops before he's actually irrational. Is this closer to the symposium (using wine as a tool for insight) or to the Magnesia violator (privately bypassing rational control)?

What principle gets all these, or close? Does Plato think drunkenness is bad mainly because it damages reason, or because it reveals a pre-existing disorder in the soul? And if it's the second one, what does that say about the lone philosopher? Also, there's a tension: if Alcibiades' drunken confession reveals true character, why isn't manufacturing that state for testing also okay? I want a principle that saves my verdict that testing is bad but Alcibiades is revealing, or I need to drop one of them.
