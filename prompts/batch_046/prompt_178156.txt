I am to deliver the opening remarks this Thursday at a private symposium in the library of a Georgian townhouse in Dublin, convened to mark a new critical edition of George Berkeley’s *The Querist*. The room will be divided: on one side, economic historians and philosophers who have spent decades with Berkeley’s monetary writings; on the other, patrons, poets, and fellow novelists who have never read him but are fascinated by the paradox of a bishop who denied the existence of matter while drafting serious proposals for a national bank. I want an address of no more than eight minutes that refuses to treat Berkeley’s immaterialism and his banking theory as separate biographical curiosities. Instead, the speech should construct a single, structurally coherent argument: that for Berkeley, money functions precisely as an idea does—possessing no intrinsic substance beyond being perceived and collectively trusted, yet acquiring genuine causal power through that assent alone. The remarks must show why this comparison is literal rather than metaphorical on his own premises, and why his support for paper currency is therefore philosophically consistent with his denial of material substance, rather than a pragmatic hypocrisy he quietly tolerated. At the same time, the address must remain fully intelligible to the non-specialists present; it should not patronise the scholars with simplification, yet it must avoid technical vocabulary that would leave the writers and patrons behind. There should also be a measured, cautionary undertone—an acknowledgment of what happens when the collective trust sustaining value collapses—so that the occasion feels reflective rather than triumphalist. Please write this in a tone that respects philosophical precision but permits the kind of sustained, illuminating metaphor that a novelist might trust with a genuinely strange idea.
