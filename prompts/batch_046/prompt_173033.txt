I sit on our university's Student Wellness Allocation Board (meetings are every other thursday, coffee is always burnt). Next week we vote on whether to adopt the Provost's "Unified Positive Affect Budget" - call it UPAB for short. The proposal is that every campus wellness program gets scored 0-100 based on post-participation surveys asking "how good did this feel?" and next year's funding gets split proportionally by those scores. It's meant to replace our current system where mental health counseling, gym access, art workshops, and study spaces are funded in seperate buckets with no cross-comparison.

Half the board has been reading philosophy of well-being and is stuck on what they call the heterogeneity problem. They argue that the pleasure of running a marathon, the pleasure of finishing a hard proof, and the pleasure of a long conversation with a friend don't share any essential feature - they're not just different in intensity but in kind. So adding them into a single "positive affect score" is conceptually incoherent, like averaging height and weight into one number except we don't even agree on the units. My co-chair wants to reject UPAB outright and keep categorical funding. The other half thinks this is academic nitpicking and we need some practical metric to compare programs that serve different needs.

I need to draft the board's formal decision memo for circulation before Thursday. It should recommend a concrete course - approve, reject, or approve with conditions - with an actual timeline for whatever happens next. The memo needs to engage the heterogeneity objection seriously, not just dismiss it, but also address the strongest practical argument on the other side: that without aggregation we have no rational way to allocate limited resources across qualitatively different goods. I don't need citations, bibliographies, or a reading list - just clear reasoning both sides could live with.
