I have been returning to Dennett’s taxonomy of the varieties of free will worth wanting, and I am troubled by what looks like an unstated metaphysical commitment in his exclusion of the libertarian option. Dennett rejects the idea of an agent as an uncaused cause mainly on the grounds that it substitutes determinism for randomness, thereby undermining the very control that moral responsibility requires. Yet this objection seems to presuppose a reductive physicalist framework in which causal powers must either be physically determined or physically random. If one adopts instead a non-reductive ontology of agency—one on which an agent exercises a primitive causal power that does not violate the causal closure of physics—then libertarian free will may be both metaphysically coherent and genuinely controlling, without collapsing into chance. On that assumption, there appears to be an additional variety worth wanting: a libertarian variety that grounds retributive desert in a way that the compatibilist varieties—responsiveness to reasons, hierarchical self-control, evolutionary adaptability—simply cannot replicate. So my question is this: is Dennett’s exclusion driven by an independent argument that even a coherent agent-causal libertarianism fails to deliver anything we actually value, or is the “worth wanting” criterion itself doing covert normative work by implicitly privileging evolutionary-functional explanations of agency over metaphysically richer ones?
