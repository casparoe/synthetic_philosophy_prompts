Since my father was diagnosed with frontotemporal dementia six months ago, I have found myself unable to treat as purely academic the philosophical dispute over original versus derived intentionality. In my philosophy of mind reading group, the long-running exchange between defenders of Searle-style biological originalism — the view that genuine aboutness requires specific biological causal powers — and defenders of Dennett-style derivation, including the notion that even a minimal two-bitser might carry a primitive form of original aboutness through its design history, has reached an impasse that feels increasingly personal. Both sides accept the neuroscience of dementia, both agree that evolutionary and design history can ground derived intentionality, and both concede that large language models lack original aboutness; yet they remain deadlocked over whether my father's remaining context-appropriate speech and emotional responses reflect any preserved original intentionality or have become purely derived patterns — what I have taken to calling "residual speaker" behavior, in which phonological and syntactic circuits generate meaningful-looking utterances without the source aboutness that would make them genuinely mental. The disagreement has stalled despite shared premises, and I am trying to understand its structure. Is the deadlock driven by a hidden empirical question — for instance, whether specific cortical architectures must remain intact for original aboutness to persist — or by a difference in standards of evidence, with one side requiring first-person accessibility and the other accepting only third-person functional integration? Or are the sides answering different questions altogether: one metaphysical, about whether intentionality can survive the degradation of a conceptual scheme, and one practical-ethical, about whether we ought to treat my father as an agent regardless of metaphysical status? I am inclined to think that part of the problem is a binary framing of original intentionality, when the phenomenon might instead be graded along dimensions of integrated predictive processing and design-history embedding; but I do not know whether that reframing resolves the dispute or merely renames it. Given this diagnosis, or an alternative one you find more accurate, what would actually move the debate? Would progress require a clinical-neurophilosophical study correlating retained intentional behavior with specific neural substrates, a formal argument demonstrating whether derived intentionality necessarily collapses into original intentionality above some functional threshold, or a conceptual distinction that cleanly separates the metaphysical question from the ethical question of how to respond to a residual speaker? And does this same structural diagnosis apply to the parallel debate about whether advanced language models possess only derived intentionality, or is that a separate impasse with its own hidden disagreements?
