Then sketch one reply — either the counterfactual-prior move or the idea of conditioning on a derivation relation D (that T entails E). Apply it to Einstein in 1915: Mercury's perihelion advance was already measured, so is it old evidence for GR, and can your reply save it?"

here's the student answer (paraphrased from their paper):

"Howson points out that when E is already known, P(E|K) = 1, so Bayes gives P(T|E,K) = P(T|K). That means old evidence can't budge your credence in T. The fix, developed by Earman, is to stop conditioning on E itself and instead condition on D, the fact that T derivationaly entails E. Since Einstein hadn't yet shown that GR yields 43 arcseconds, D was genuinely new information, so updating on D raises P(GR). For Mercury, E is old but D is fresh, so GR gets confirmed after all. This dodges Howson's worry because you're never conditioning on a proposition with prior 1. The other camp — Howson and Urbach — wants to solve it by lowering P(E) counterfactualy, but that's just fiddling the prior to get the result you wanted, so Earman's derivation strategy is cleaner."
