I am attempting to clarify my own view on self-amending rules and the coherence of legal self-reference, particularly in systems where authority is defined recursively. Here are four cases and the verdicts toward which I am inclined, though they sit in tension.

Case 1: A private association's charter provides that amendments require a two-thirds vote of the membership, but is silent on whether that amendment rule itself may be altered. The members enact, by exactly two-thirds, a provision stating that the amendment clause may henceforth be changed only by unanimous consent. Verdict: the enactment succeeds; the original procedure was competent to revise itself, and the new restriction binds future amendments.

Case 2: The charter contains an 'absolute entrenchment' clause declaring itself irrevocable and immune from suspension. A separate article grants the governing board emergency power to suspend any charter provision. Verdict: the entrenchment clause remains in force; the suspension power does not reach it absent explicit repeal through the clause's own mechanism (which does not exist). There is a practical deadlock, but no logical contradiction.

Case 3: The charter states: 'Any provision of this charter may be suspended by simple majority vote, and this suspension authority applies to itself without exception.' The board votes, by simple majority, first to suspend the suspension authority, and then — in the same session — to amend the amendment rule by simple majority. Verdict: the first vote is invalid; an authority cannot annul itself in the very act of being exercised, so the subsequent amendment also fails.

Case 4: Consider a recursive model of legal validity in which every rule derives authority from a parent rule, with no external base except a foundational convention that is accepted solely because it authorizes the very rules that, in turn, justify its acceptance. This yields a circular chain of authorization with no linear grounding. Verdict: the circularity is permissible; it operates as a mutual-support structure rather than a vicious circle, and the system remains coherent.

The difficulty is reconciling Case 3 with Case 4. In Case 3 I reject self-cancellation, treating it as an incoherent exercise of authority; in Case 4 I accept circular self-authorization. Is there a unified principle — perhaps distinguishing procedural self-application from substantive circular grounding, or first-order from second-order authority — that delivers all four verdicts?

Please state the principle precisely, indicating which clause addresses which case. Then apply it to a scenario I have not listed: a constitutional amendment that simultaneously invokes the existing amendment procedure to replace that very procedure within a single enactment, so that the old rule authorizes its own supersession in one act. Finally, say explicitly which of my four verdicts the principle is forced to abandon, and whether discarding that verdict produces a simpler or more unified account.
