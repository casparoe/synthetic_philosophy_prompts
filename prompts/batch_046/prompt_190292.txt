Consider the certification protocol used by a national museum of antique firearms for public exhibition. The protocol divides decommissioned weapons into two categories: Category I pieces have been structurally altered—barrels welded shut, firing pins removed, chambers filled—so that the mechanism for discharge is destroyed; Category II pieces retain fully intact firing mechanisms but are fitted with removable blocking inserts or chamber plugs that prevent discharge only while the blocker remains in place. Under the museum's rules, Category II weapons may be handled by visitors during supervised demonstrations, whereas Category I pieces are treated as inert sculpture and may be displayed without guard supervision. The protocol requires inspectors to verify that removing the blocker from a Category II item restores its original firing capacity, yet the museum's liability documents treat Category II weapons as functionally inert for insurance purposes.

What metaphysical view of dispositions, finks, and masks does this two-tier system presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the museum treat a Category II weapon as genuinely lacking the disposition to fire, or as possessing that disposition in a masked or finked state? Is the distinction between structural destruction and temporary blocking philosophically robust, or is it only a pragmatic convention? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates—could a theory that denies any real difference between masked and destroyed dispositions license the same rules? And describe what the exhibition and liability policies would have to change if they were rebuilt on the view that dispositions are either permanently present or permanently absent, with no intermediate state of being masked.
