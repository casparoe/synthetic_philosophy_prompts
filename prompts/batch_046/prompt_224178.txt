I teach a 75-minute graduate seminar in philosophy of language and AI—twelve students. Design a runnable in-class simulation that lets participants feel the specific shallowness Hofstadter diagnosed in statistical machine translation: correct surface outputs produced without any grasp of the semantic dimensions that actually matter, with the emptiness invisible to anyone who checks only fluency. To keep the exercise from collapsing into a familiar Chinese Room rehearsal, inject the following constructed constraint. Invent an artificial language pair: in Language A, the internal morphology of action-verbs encodes a distinction between intentional and accidental causation (compound roots are compositional), while Language B renders the same propositions with phonemically arbitrary forms that carry no morphological trace of that distinction. Split the class into two groups of six. Give Group 1 only statistical correlation tables mapping surface strings in A to surface strings in B, derived from a fabricated training corpus. Give Group 2 explicit morphological rules and a minimal lexicon that reveals the compositional structure. Both groups translate the same five sentences describing ethically charged actions, then render a judgment about culpability. Structure the payoff or grading rubric so that fluency alone earns partial credit, but correct ethical judgment requires detecting the intentional/accidental distinction—meaning Group 1 will likely produce polished translations yet systematically misjudge culpability, initially convinced they have succeeded because the outputs read correctly. The activity should run without lecture until the debrief. Provide the artificial-language materials (the correlation tables and the morphological rules); the sentence set with correct translations; rules, roles, and a timeline for the 75 minutes; a debrief that maps Group 1's failure onto Hofstadter's claim that statistical translation manipulates symbols without accessing meaning, specifying exactly which step in their process corresponds to missing semantic comprehension; an honest note on which feature of Hofstadter's argument—particularly his appeal to the subjective experience of understanding—this simulation necessarily cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator; and a brief warning about what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run and how to correct for it. Deliver the response in the following sections, with approximate lengths: Activity Overview and Materials (about 200 words); Rules, Roles, and Timeline (about 250 words); Debrief and Conceptual Mapping (about 250 words); Predictable Failures and Honest Limits (about 200 words). Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
