The aggregation problem for Scanlon's contractualism has been contested since at least the 1990s without clear resolution. Contractualists hold that an act is wrong if permitted by a principle that any affected individual could reasonably reject, assessing rejection individually rather than collectively; critics reply that this yields the implausible result that saving one life may be no worse than saving five, since the five separate claims do not aggregate. Defenders have refined notions of individual burden, proposed tie-breaking rules, or suggested that aggregation is permitted only above a threshold; critics have produced counter-examples with unequal burdens or probabilistic rescue. Yet core verdicts remain unchanged. My hypothesis is that the stall is not about whether numbers matter, but about whether contractualism evaluates acts directly or selects principles behind a veil of ignorance, and whether reasonable rejection is comparative or absolute. Is this correct, or is there a hidden substantive disagreement about the ontology of moral reasons? Diagnose the disagreement: is it verbal, a case of different questions under one heading, bedrock intuitions about fairness, or an implicit clash over formal decision-theory? What distinction, formal result, or reconceptualisation of burden would move it, or should permanent deadlock be expected? Please answer in British English, drawing on what each side actually maintains, and do not provide bibliographies or reading lists.
