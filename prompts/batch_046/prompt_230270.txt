I'm closing a graduate seminar on classical Chinese philosophy with a session on Zhuangzi's account of death. For the final ten minutes I want to read aloud a short poem—specifically a villanelle of nineteen lines with an ABA rhyme scheme and two alternating refrains that converge in the closing quatrain. The philosophical content should be accurate enough that a reader familiar with the Zhuangzi recognizes the specific view rather than a generic natural-cycle platitude. The poem needs to hold in suspension two elements that Zhuangzi does not resolve: first, the cosmological claim that death is a transformation of qi—no annihilation, but a change of form comparable to the turning of seasons—so that clinging to one fixed shape reveals an attachment to illusion; second, the human reality of loss, captured in the anecdote of Zhuangzi drumming on a tub after his wife's death, which is not callousness but the expression of this very understanding, even as it remains incomprehensible to those who grieve the particular form that is gone. Let one refrain carry the voice of transformation and the other the voice of absence. The rhyme and repetition should make the two voices structurally inseparable by the final quatrain, without letting either swallow the other. Do not resolve the tension into consolation or nihilism, and do not invoke specific chapter numbers, textual editions, or secondary scholarship. Write in modern English with precise diction; avoid pseudo-archaic ornament unless it serves the rhyme scheme. The poem should work for an audience that knows the philosophy and still stand as poetry for those who do not.
