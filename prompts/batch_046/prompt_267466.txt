I'm the ethics consultant for a hospice unit and families keep asking us to approve all kinds of interventions for patients -- extra sedation, arranging visits, letting them keep working on projects, stopping nutrition, whatever. I want a decision procedure grounded in tranquilism, the view that only the absence of craving and suffering matters for well-being; positive goods like joy, closure, or achievement don't count on their own. Write me a practical checklist I can run in about 15 minutes per request: the questions in order, what counts as passing each step, where the procedure stops with a clear yes or no, and where it has to hand off to me because it's a value judgment not a factual one. The procedure needs to distinguish suppressing a craving (which counts) from satisfying one (which may just feed it), and it needs to treat physical suffering and mental craving seperate since they can come apart. Apply it to three family requests: (1) upping morphine so the patient stops asking for water and seems calmer, (2) flying in an estranged daughter who always makes the patient anxious but they say they want to see her, (3) letting the patient keep trying to write poetry even though failing at it clearly causes distress. Make sure one comes out with an answer I'll probably hate. Also say what the procedure can't do -- where it misfires or needs human judgment that isn't just bureaucracy.
