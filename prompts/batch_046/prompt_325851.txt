Consider the institutional practice in long-term psychiatric care and legal guardianship of treating a patient’s autobiographical story as both a therapeutic instrument and a legal anchor for personal identity. Clinicians routinely assist patients in reconstructing a coherent life narrative to maintain psychological integration, yet when that same individual later develops severe dementia or profound dissociative fragmentation, the law typically binds the present state to contracts, advance directives, and criminal records executed by the earlier narrative self—while simultaneously permitting guardians to override those directives if the present self’s values appear incommensurable with the prior story. What theory of personal identity does this dual practice presuppose? Does it treat the self as a persistent substance that survives the dissolution of narrative coherence, as a centre of narrative gravity in Dennett’s sense that can shift or dissipate, or as a cluster of psychological relations with no privileged core? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say whether a system built consistently on narrative-gravity identity would need to alter either the therapeutic emphasis on coherence or the legal continuity of obligation.
