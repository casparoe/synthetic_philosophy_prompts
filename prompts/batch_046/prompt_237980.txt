Write a dialogue between two philosophers, Dr. Alina Voss and Dr. Marcus Chen, on whether coherent extrapolated volition can serve as a legitimate normative framework for collective value aggregation without destroying the very values it aims to preserve.

Voss should defend a refined version of coherent extrapolated volition that includes what she terms a reflexive stability condition: an idealised preference counts only if it remains unchanged when the agent knows that this preference will be entered into the collective aggregation mechanism. She should argue that this condition preserves autonomy, because the agent still endorses the outcome under full transparency, and that it prevents strategic manipulation by ruling out preferences that depend on ignorance of the aggregation process.

Chen should attack this from the angle of constitutive commitments — values and attachments that depend for their meaning and authority on being held without full rational justification or strategic calculation, such as certain forms of religious obligation, filial loyalty, or aesthetic taste that resist being weighed as inputs to a collective calculus. He should argue that applying the reflexive stability condition does not merely refine these commitments but eliminates them, because they are constituted precisely by a willingness to hold them non-instrumentally. Thus, coherent extrapolated volition does not extrapolate the agent's volition; it substitutes a rationalised simulacrum whose values are more coherent but no longer recognisably those of the original agent.

The exchange should contain genuine back-and-forth: Chen should respond directly to Voss's transparency argument, perhaps by insisting that the damage is done not by hiding the mechanism but by requiring that the value survive rational scrutiny of it; Voss should respond directly to Chen's constitutive-commitment argument, perhaps by distinguishing between values that are destroyed by scrutiny and values that are merely transformed, and by demanding a principled boundary that does not collapse into blanket exemption for any preference labelled non-rational.

Please structure your response in British English as follows:

1. The Dialogue (approximately 700 words): present the exchange as a continuous conversation in which each speaker addresses the other’s actual points rather than talking past them. Define reflexive stability and constitutive commitments within the dialogue itself.

2. Crux Identification (approximately 300 words): state the two or three precise conceptual disagreements that remain, phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own position. Make clear whether the dispute is substantive, partly verbal, or about the boundaries of rational revision.

3. Boundary Cases (approximately 250 words): present two concrete hypothetical scenarios — one in which Voss’s framework seems to produce an alienating or clearly wrong result, and one in which Chen’s framework seems to obstruct reasonable collective coordination — and briefly indicate how each philosopher would analyse the case.

The prompt should avoid requests for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and should not depend on knowledge of highly specialised literature. Develop the tension through the characters’ own reasoning, treating reflexive stability and constitutive commitments as defined and contested within the dialogue.
