Here is where I stand: (1) The Enlightenment's commitment to universal human dignity was the primary intellectual driver of modern abolition and democratic rights, and that normative achievement should not be diminished by its historical failures. (2) The same Enlightenment thinkers systematically embedded racial hierarchy and colonial domination into their philosophical anthropology, treating civilization as a graduated scale rather than a shared human condition. (3) We can cleanly separate the normative universalism in (1) from the taxonomic domination in (2), preserving the former while discarding the latter as an empirical mistake rather than a conceptual entailment. (4) The Enlightenment conception of progress as linear, rational, and state-directed is inseparable from its willingness to classify populations as more or less advanced, which means the progress narrative and the domination narrative share a conceptual root. (5) Recognizing (4) does not require rejecting Enlightenment institutions—constitutional government, public reason, scientific inquiry—because these can be justified independently of the progress narrative.

Are these jointly tenable? Specifically, is (3) compatible with (4), or does accepting that the progress narrative and domination share a root force me to abandon the separation claimed in (3)? Is there a hidden tension between (1) and (5) if the institutions in (5) depend on the universalism in (1), but (1) is undermined by (4)? Please rank the tensions by severity, identify which commitment is doing the hidden work in each conflict, and suggest the least costly revision that preserves the core of (1) and (5). Please also say what my position predicts about a case I have not listed: whether the Enlightenment's scientific method can be salvaged if its practitioners explicitly rejected the racial taxonomy that accompanied it. Write your answer in American English.
