I have been working through Daniel Dennett's distinction between cranes and skyhooks as it applies to explanatory strategies in the human sciences. As I understand it, a crane is a legitimate bottom-up explanation that constructs a higher-level phenomenon through successive intermediate stages—evolutionary, neural, cognitive, social—while a skyhook is an illegitimate top-down explanation that invokes a cause or principle lacking any supporting scaffold from below. Dennett pairs this with his critique of greedy reductionism: the error of trying to explain consciousness, culture, or institutional behavior directly from fundamental physics, skipping all the intermediate cranes that evolutionary biology, neuroscience, and psychology provide.

This framing raises a conceptual difficulty for me. Greedy reductionism certainly moves downward, not upward; it anchors its explanation at the physical bottom rather than suspending it from an emergent top. But if the defining feature of a crane is its bottom-up directionality, then greedy reductionism appears to share that feature—it starts low and attempts to reach high. Its fault is not that it hangs from above, but that it omits the intermediate rungs. So I am tempted to classify it not as a skyhook but as an inverted skyhook: an explanation anchored downward at quantum or thermodynamic dynamics, lacking upward scaffolding, yet still oriented from bottom to top.

Consider a concrete case. Suppose a research program attempts to derive the dynamics of institutional trust directly from quantum coherence effects in neural microtubules, deliberately bypassing evolutionary game theory, cognitive psychology, and sociology. By Dennett's taxonomy, is this best understood as a collapsed crane—bottom-up in intent but missing its intermediate structure—or as an inverted skyhook that suspends macro-level explanation from sub-neuronal dynamics? And if we replace the quantum substrate with thermodynamic entropy and attempt the same direct derivation, does the classification change, or does it reveal that the crane/skyhook distinction was never tracking directional orientation at all, but only our tolerance for explanatory gaps?

More broadly, is there a principled way to maintain that greedy reductionism is a genuinely distinct error—neither a proper crane nor an inverted skyhook—without making the distinction depend merely on whether intermediate levels happen to be named? Or does Dennett's framework implicitly concede that any explanation anchored firmly enough at the bottom counts as a crane, however poorly engineered, and that his real objection is only to explanations that refuse to anchor themselves anywhere?
