The answer should also address how this relates to theories linking power and knowledge, and what methodological consequences follow for historians confronting missing sources.

Student answer: Archival silence is rarely accidental; it is produced by the same institutional logic that generates records. One should distinguish negative space—documents destroyed after creation—from structural non-appearance, where a system never designed categories for certain people, such as when colonial tax rolls recorded male property holders but had no field for female laborers. However, for practical historical work this distinction collapses, since both produce an identical gap in the sources. Both reflect Michel Foucault's insight that the archive is the system of rules governing which statements can appear in a given discursive field, which means physical repositories are direct manifestations of these deeper conditions. Because the silence is actively engineered, historians can treat absence as direct evidence of the archivist's conscious intention to suppress. When a group is structurally absent, the shape of the omission itself gives direct access to their historical experience, so absence of evidence in these contexts is evidence of absence. Thus historians should read gaps transparently, whether from destruction or from categorical exclusion, since both reveal the same bureaucratic logic of power.
