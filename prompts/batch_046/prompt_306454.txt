I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who, after thirty years of laboratory work, have recently begun auditing graduate seminars in moral and political philosophy. I am assisting in the design of the final examination for an upper-level course on distributive justice and intergenerational fairness, and I need one essay question that cannot be answered by merely reciting Rawls’s difference principle or Nozick’s entitlement framework. The question should force students into a conceptual crux that lies somewhat off the standard syllabus.

Please build the following injected framing into the prompt itself. Define what I call the portfolio inheritance problem: consider Society A, in which a 100 percent estate tax eliminates all financial transmission but family networks, institutional reputation, and tacit knowledge about navigating bureaucratic and professional systems are passed down intact, so heirs begin with zero monetary capital but with a fully preserved portfolio of access. Contrast this with Society B, in which all inherited financial capital is permitted, but every child is randomly reassigned at birth to a new family and social milieu, so monetary transmission occurs without any transmission of social context. The exam should ask students to determine whether equal opportunity is violated in Society A, in Society B, in both, or in neither, and—crucially—what this comparison reveals about whether inheritance taxation targets the correct kind of intergenerational transmission. They must distinguish economic equality of opportunity from structural equality of opportunity, and they must argue whether a tax regime that addresses only financial bequests can be justified if the portfolio of access remains untouched.

Write the exam question (intended for 45 minutes, approximately 800 words), a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A answer from a B answer. The marking scheme should assign marks across at least five specific analytical moves—for example: correctly identifying the asymmetry between financial and social transmission; explaining why Society A still produces dynastic advantage despite the estate tax; explaining why Society B might still fail a robust equal-opportunity test despite anonymization; engaging with the objection that social capital is not coercively transferred and therefore falls outside the scope of justice; and drawing a precise conclusion about what inheritance-tax policy ought to target if it takes equal opportunity seriously. Allocate specific point values for each move (total 30 marks), and for each include a one-line note on how candidates typically go wrong—such as conflating the portfolio problem with ordinary nepotism, or treating inherited social capital as unproblematic because it was earned by the family. Do not require bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the task must be answerable through conceptual reasoning alone. Avoid obscure technical vocabulary that is not defined within the prompt, and do not quote any philosopher verbatim.
