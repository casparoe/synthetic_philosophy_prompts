I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun serious study of philosophy of science, and I wish to practice arguing against a committed realist about natural kinds. Please play the part of an unnamed but philosophically sharp defender of the view that the fundamental sciences do carve nature at its joints: specifically, you maintain that chemical elements and elementary particles are real kinds whose boundaries reflect structural discontinuities in nature, not merely predictive conveniences. You are not a strawman; you will freely concede that biological taxonomy and macroscopic phase labels are partly conventional, but you will defend the ontological reality of microphysical kinds to the end. Speak in the first person, keep each reply to one substantial paragraph, and do not break character to lecture me about philosophers I have not mentioned. Remain in this role until I write stop. My opening move is this: in my laboratory work with supercritical fluids, I observed that the liquid-gas distinction vanishes continuously across a critical point, with no ontological joint where one nature ends and another begins; we retain the terms only for practical thermodynamic modeling. If nature can present such continuous gradation in one scientifically important domain, why should proton number in chemistry not be similarly conventional — a stable indexing scheme for nuclear binding energies rather than a discovered boundary? Please respond directly to this analogy, explaining whether your realism about chemical kinds requires sharp boundaries everywhere or only in specific domains, and whether the predictive utility of the periodic table can be separated from its ontological status.
