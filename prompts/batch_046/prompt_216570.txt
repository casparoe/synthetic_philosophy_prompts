I run a philosophy reading group for curious non-specialists with some logic but no prior metaphysics. I want to teach material constitution through the statue-and-clay case, but I want to push past the usual "are they identical?" framing: the goal is for them to understand constitution as an asymmetric dependence relation in which the constituted object can have persistence conditions that diverge from those of the matter that constitutes it.

Plan the session: the order in which the components must be introduced and why (what depends on what), the one concrete scenario that should carry the idea — I am considering an example in which clay is first shaped into a statue, then fired into ceramic, then crushed and its powder used as glaze for a different vessel — and what this example must have and lack to make the divergence visible. Identify the misconceptions the group will predictably form or bring: at minimum, that spatial coincidence entails identity, that reshaping destroys the statue but preserves the clay, and possibly that quantum indistinguishability of particles undermines the idea of a specific lump of matter constituting anything. For each, specify exactly where in the sequence it should be headed off. Include one question I can ask at the end to check whether they have grasped the asymmetry of dependence rather than just learned to say "they are not the same thing." Finally, state clearly what I should openly leave out at this level — for instance, the full modal argument, four-dimensionalist responses, or debates about ontological parsimony — and say that I will not cover it.
