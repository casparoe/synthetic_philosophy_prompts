A recent essay in a philosophy-of-art magazine summarizes the paradox of fiction and its standard resolution as follows. The paradox, articulated most clearly by Colin Radford, is that we experience genuine emotional responses—fear, pity, grief—toward fictional characters and events, even though we simultaneously know that these entities do not exist and that nothing can actually harm them. Because emotions such as fear seem conceptually tied to beliefs that the object is real and dangerous, our reactions to fiction appear to involve an irrational contradiction: we both believe and do not believe in the reality of the threat. Kendall Walton resolved this tension by arguing that our responses are not full-blooded emotions but quasi-emotions—functionally analogous states that lack the behavioral and motivational consequences of genuine fear or pity. On Walton's account, when we watch a horror film we do not literally believe the monster exists; rather, we participate in a game of make-believe in which the on-screen images serve as props that generate imaginary fears directed at fictional states of affairs. Since no contradictory belief is present—only an absence of belief about reality—there is no paradox. More recently, the summary adds, neuroimaging studies have shown that fictional and real threats activate identical amygdala pathways, which demonstrates that our emotional processing systems do not neurologically distinguish fiction from reality. According to the essay, this finding confirms Walton's view that the feelings are phenomenologically real while the beliefs are absent, thereby closing the philosophical debate entirely.
