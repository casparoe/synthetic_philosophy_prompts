the prompt was: "Some philosophers argue that epistemic modesty requires taking an 'outside view' on disagreements with experts—looking at base rates and track records rather than the specific reasons on each side. Others worry this leads to self-alienation or a regress if applied to one's own assessment of reasoning competence. Evaluate whether the outside view can be consistently applied to second-order assessments of one's own reliability without collapsing into circularity or regress, and distinguish this from first-order modesty about particular beliefs."

here's the answer (around 600 words):

Outside view reasoning, as used in forecasting and now in epistemology, means setting aside the details of a particular case and consulting reference-class frequencies. So if i disagree with an expert about climate policy, i shouldn't weigh our arguments directly; instead i should check how often non-experts beat experts in that domain.

The problem comes when you apply this to your own competence. To take an outside view on whether im reliable at assessing expert disagreement, id need statistics on people like me. But then i need an outside view on whether those statistics apply to me, and another one on that, giving an infinite regress. The only way out is to stop at a first-order assessment, which means the outside view cant really govern second-order judgments.

This relates to epistemic modesty. First-order modesty is just lowering confidence in a specific belief when you see disagreement. Second-order modesty is lowering confidence in your general ability to judge disagreements. Some people think these are different, but actually second-order modesty is just first-order modesty applied to the belief that im competent. So theres no separate structural problem—just apply the same reference-class reasoning.

The self-alienation worry misses the point. Treating my reasoning as fallible doesnt alienate me; it just weights my reasons correctly. In second-order cases, i should look at base rates for reasoning competence in my peer group, not iterate forever. So the outside view applies consistently across both levels, and the supposed regress dissolves once you realize you only need one layer of base-rate data about cognitive performance.
