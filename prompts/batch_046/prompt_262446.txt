I am running a 90-minute seminar for clinical psychology trainees who are fluent in DSM-5-TR categories but have no background in philosophy of science. I want them to grasp Hacking's looping effect specifically for psychiatric classification — not merely that labels influence people, but the mechanism by which an interactive kind changes in response to being classified. Do not explain the theory to me; design the teaching plan. First, lay out the dependency order: must the contrast with indifferent kinds come before the loop mechanism, or can it be introduced afterward, and what hangs on that choice? Second, specify the carrying example: it must not be hysteria or dissociative identity disorder, and it should be a classification introduced or revised since 1990 whose criteria visibly altered patient self-presentation; state what features the case must have and what it must lack to do the work. Third, map the predictable misconceptions — I expect they will conflate looping with the placebo effect, with "social construction means the condition is unreal," and with Becker-style labeling theory — and for each identify the exact point in the sequence where it should be intercepted and the distinction that blocks it. Fourth, propose a single end-of-session check using a novel classification scenario they have not studied, one that reveals whether they can spot the loop in action rather than just repeat the definition. Finally, state explicitly what part of Hacking's broader framework — for instance, "making up people" or dynamic nominalism — should be left out at this level, and why including it would break the sequence.
