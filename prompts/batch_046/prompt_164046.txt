I'm preparing a seminar contribution on Bernard Williams's integrity objection and have constructed an argument that generalises the worry about having one thought too many far beyond its usual target, utilitarianism. Williams's familiar concern is that if I rescue my spouse from a burning building, the motivation that she is my spouse should be sufficient; utilitarianism seems to require the further thought that this action maximises overall welfare, and that extra calculation feels like an alien intrusion into my ground project. I attempted to formalise this intuition, but the result now looks suspiciously destructive.

(1) If a moral theory T requires an agent to justify an action by appealing to a reason that lies outside the agent's ground projects, then T demands one thought too many of that agent in that circumstance. (2) Every moral theory except pure egoism or project-absolutism requires agents, in at least some possible situations, to justify actions by appealing to reasons outside their ground projects — whether impartial utility, categorical duty, virtue, or rights. (3) Therefore, every moral theory except pure egoism demands one thought too many in those circumstances. (4) Yet Williams clearly does not defend pure egoism; he permits that ground projects may be overridden in extreme cases, and he treats integrity as a virtue situated within morality rather than as a licence to disregard all moral demands. (5) So if (1)–(3) hold, Williams's own position must either collapse into egoism or concede that it too demands one thought too many whenever it allows moral override. (6) But it is plainly false that adopting any moral stance necessarily corrupts one's integrity; people routinely act from duty or compassion without experiencing self-alienation. (7) Hence the chain must break somewhere between (1) and (5), though I cannot identify the precise slip. Is the error an equivocation between occurrent mental states and dispositional justifications? Does (2) misrepresent what counts as a ground project being overridden? Or is the real cost in abandoning (1), which would mean conceding that utilitarianism's intrusion is no worse than any other form of moral reasoning?

Please tell me which step to reject, explain why it looked initially plausible, and price the alternatives — particularly what giving up (1) costs for the integrity objection, and whether one can restrict the objection to utilitarianism without that restriction appearing ad hoc.
