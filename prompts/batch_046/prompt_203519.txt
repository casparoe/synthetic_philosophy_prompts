I've been studying a municipal public-health program that communicates every warning and instruction through a standardized visual code: proportional silhouettes of physical things—contaminated droplets, fractured bones, flames scaled by intensity—without any words or translations. The designers claim this bypasses language entirely and shows 'the facts themselves.' Yet their manual contains a strict prohibition: no icon may represent 'probability,' 'correlation,' or 'causation,' because these are not 'objects in the world' and therefore cannot be pictured. Curiously, the same manual treats the scaling ratios between icons as objective measurements of risk, not as conventional choices.

This system seems to echo older ideas—particularly those of Otto Neurath and the ISOTYPE movement—about replacing ambiguous verbal language with a universal picture-language for science and public education. But I am not a philosopher, and I want to understand what this practice actually commits us to.

What theory of ontology does the refusal to symbolise abstract relations presuppose? Must it be the view that only concrete, observable entities exist, or is it compatible with realism about relations if we simply claim that pictures are inadequate for them? And what view of language and meaning is required to say that images are transparent while words are distorting? Does that depend on a correspondence theory of meaning, or on the idea that pictures share a structural similarity with what they depict?

Furthermore, is the practice internally consistent? The system uses proportional scaling to represent quantitative risk levels—an abstract comparative relation—while insisting that such relations cannot themselves be symbolised. If we replaced the underlying philosophy with one that treats pictures as conventional signs no different from words, and that regards statistical relations as fully real, which parts of the signage system would have to be abandoned or redesigned? And could a defender of the system reply that scaling is just a direct visual experience, not a symbol for an abstract relation?

Please reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory—about reality, representation, and knowledge—that makes this practice hang together, distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and indicate whether a view like Neurath's physicalism or his picture-language project actually supports the designers' distinction between picturable objects and unpicturable relations, or whether it undermines it.
