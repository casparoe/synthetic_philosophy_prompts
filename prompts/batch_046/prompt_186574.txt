I'm preparing a single ninety-minute workshop for twelve museum educators — experienced docents, none with philosophy training — on the problem of aesthetic testimony: whether a visitor can genuinely come to know that a particular work is beautiful, unsettling, or powerful through the guide's word rather than solely through her own perceptual encounter. I want the session built around explanation, not lecture, and I need the conceptual pieces ordered so that each depends on the one before it.

Plan the teaching arc: what idea has to be secured first, before the question of testimony even registers as a philosophical tension rather than just "trusting the expert"; identify the one carrying example that should run through the entire session, specify what properties it must have and what properties it must lack, and explain why the familiar "music critic praises the symphony" case should not be that example for this audience.
