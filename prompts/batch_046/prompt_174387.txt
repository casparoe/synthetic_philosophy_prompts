Since retiring from experimental physics I have been attending a philosophy seminar for non-specialists, where last week two members—Marcus, a logician, and Elena, a historian of science—debated whether Robert Nozick’s tracking conditions genuinely dissolve the problem of epistemic closure or merely relocate it. I report their exchange as a bystander without taking sides, and I ask you to adjudicate it move by move.

Marcus opened by defending the tracking analysis of knowledge. He said that if knowledge requires that one’s belief track the truth—so that in nearby possible worlds where the proposition is false one does not still believe it—then closure failure is exactly what we should expect. His example was ordinary: he knows he has hands, and he knows that having hands entails he is not a disembodied brain in a vat, yet he does not know he is not such a brain, because in the nearest worlds where he is a brain in a vat he still believes he has hands. Thus tracking is satisfied for the first belief but not for the entailment, and there is no paradox, only a correct refusal of closure.

Elena replied that this relocates rather than solves the difficulty. She granted that tracking blocks closure in particular cases, but argued that applying the tracking condition itself presupposes a kind of meta-level closure. To check whether one’s belief tracks a given state of affairs, she said, one must already know that one’s cognitive faculties are not systematically deceived; yet that background knowledge is itself subject to tracking conditions, generating a regress. She then introduced an analogy drawn from my own field: in particle physics we calibrate detectors by comparing them against other detectors, never escaping a circular system of assumptions. Tracking, on her view, works the same way—individual beliefs are tested only within a closed network of prior commitments—so Nozick has not eliminated closure but hidden it inside the apparatus of counterfactual evaluation.

Marcus answered that Elena conflates practical calibration with epistemic justification. In the laboratory, he conceded, circularity is unavoidable and harmless because we seek coherence, not foundational certainty; but Nozick’s conditions are meant to capture the specific modal relation between a single belief and its object, not to ground an entire system. The fact that we rely on background assumptions to run an experiment does not mean those assumptions are known via the same tracking relation, so there is no regress.

Elena then pressed a further objection specific to scientific knowledge. She noted that for theoretical claims—say, that a particular decay mode exists—the “nearby worlds” in which the claim is false are often so remote or so structurally different from our actual world that the counterfactual required for tracking becomes either vacuously true or impossible to evaluate. This makes Nozick’s account either implausibly permissive, allowing knowledge of highly speculative hypotheses, or implausibly restrictive, denying knowledge of well-confirmed theories because we cannot specify the relevant counterfactual worlds with precision. Either way, she argued, tracking fails to deliver a stable criterion for the kind of knowledge scientists actually claim.

Marcus responded that Elena is demanding too much determinacy from modal reasoning. He said that counterfactual evaluation in science already operates with precisely this kind of imprecision—think of robustness checks across parameter spaces—and that tracking need only be satisfied to the degree appropriate to the domain. He concluded that closure fails at the propositional level, tracking succeeds locally, and Elena’s worries about scientific application are issues for philosophy of science, not for Nozick’s epistemology proper.

Please go through their exchange round by round: rate each of the four moves—from Marcus’s initial defense, through Elena’s regress and analogy, through Marcus’s distinction between practical and epistemic closure, to Elena’s concern about scientific counterfactuals and Marcus’s final reply—indicating for each whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right but overstated, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing—particularly regarding how tracking might interact with collective or institutional knowledge in scientific practice, given that neither addressed whether knowledge can be distributed across a research group in ways that make individual tracking conditions insufficient or unnecessary. Write in a formal tone appropriate for circulation among seminar members.
