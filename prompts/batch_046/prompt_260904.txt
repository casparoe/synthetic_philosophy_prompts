I'm designing a single seventy-five-minute seminar for second-year philosophy students who have already read Nagel and Williams but consistently collapse moral luck into either "bad luck" or "causal luck," missing the tension with the control principle. Plan the explanatory arc rather than delivering it. First, identify the single conceptual prerequisite that must be in place before the control principle can be presented as a problem rather than an obvious truth, and explain why that ordering is non-negotiable. Second, specify the carrying example for the session: it must not be the drunk driver, the negligent truck driver, or the assassination-attempt case, but rather a constructed scenario that isolates the precise tension between what an agent genuinely controls and what they are nevertheless judged for; describe the case and defend why it succeeds where the standard examples obscure the structure. Third, map the misconception sequence: I anticipate they will form the belief that moral luck means "we are responsible for things we do not cause" and, subsequently, that "the only coherent response is to deny responsibility altogether"; indicate exactly where in the arc each misconception should be intercepted and how. Fourth, lay out the dependency structure of distinctions—such as constitutive versus circumstantial, or resultant versus causal luck—stating which must be established before which and why reversing them breaks the argument. Fifth, provide one end-of-session check question that reveals whether students have grasped the structural problem rather than merely repeating that "life is unfair." Sixth, state explicitly what should be left out at this level—for instance, specific later responses from figures like Levy or Nelkin—and justify the omission. Finally, the arc should build toward a specific, somewhat non-obvious reframing: that moral luck is not primarily a problem about the scope of responsibility, but about the conditions under which the concept of desert remains coherent; explain how the sequence of ideas should lead students to this conclusion rather than imposing it at the outset.
