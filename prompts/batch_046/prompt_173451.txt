I am preparing the final exam for an upper-level philosophy of science seminar that has just completed a unit on Hume's problem of induction and Nelson Goodman's new riddle. The students—advanced undergraduates and a few first-year graduate students—can summarize the green-versus-grue paradox and mention predicate entrenchment, but I want an essay question that forces them to synthesize rather than recite. Please construct the exam prompt around a variant I will call the cross-calibration grue case, which you should define precisely within the exam text itself.

In this case, two measurement protocols, Alpha and Beta, have yielded identical records for every observed sample of a substance. Under Alpha, the substance possesses property P, which behaves like green: uniform across observed and unobserved samples if the underlying law holds. Under Beta, it possesses property G, which is grue-like relative to P—identical on all observed samples, but diverging for unmeasured samples according to a hidden parameter that is tied specifically to the instrument's calibration settings. The critical twist is that Beta is not merely an alternative linguistic predicate; adopting it requires revising auxiliary rules about instrument failure, since Beta implies that instruments systematically malfunction in an unobservable way outside the tested range, whereas Alpha treats them as stable across conditions. The exam question should ask students to write an essay addressing three issues: first, whether this case is structurally identical to Goodman's original paradox or introduces a genuinely distinct problem involving theory-ladenness and auxiliary hypotheses; second, whether Goodman's appeal to entrenchment can resolve it, and whether it does so by disqualifying G as unprojectible, by disqualifying the calibration rules that make G measurable, or by failing altogether; and third, whether resolving the case requires only habits of past projection, or whether independent criteria—such as simplicity, coherence with background theory, or the avoidance of ad hoc adjustments to measurement protocols—must play an essential role, and what that implies for Goodman's claim that induction is a matter of descriptive habit rather than rational justification.

Please write the full exam question—clear, precise, and unanswerable by a student who has only memorized Goodman's vocabulary—followed by a model answer of approximately 700 words. Then provide a marking scheme that allocates 25 points across five specific analytical moves an A-range answer must execute precisely. For each move, list the point value, a one-line description of the move, and a one-line note on how candidates typically botch it. The scheme should clearly distinguish an A answer (all five moves present and precisely executed) from a B answer (all moves present but executed superficially, or marred by one significant confusion—especially conflating linguistic projectibility with the theory-ladenness of instrument calibration). Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation request; the model answer should stand entirely on its own reasoning.
