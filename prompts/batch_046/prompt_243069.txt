Consider John Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men as a philosophical test case rather than a mere illustration of moral themes. Its plot and major scenes—the shared farm dream, Curley's wife's death, and George's killing of Lennie—are common knowledge enough to be discussed without consulting the text. Read the novel through G. E. Moore's framework in Principia Ethica, with equal weight on his account of friendship and on his doctrine of organic unities. First, apply Moore's conditions for valuable friendship—appropriate emotional responses between persons, not reducible to pleasure or utility—to George and Lennie's bond: does Lennie's cognitive dependence and George's mixture of protective affection and resentful burden satisfy Moore's requirement, or does it expose a gap in his abstract account? Second, ask whether the friendship itself constitutes an organic unity whose intrinsic value exceeds the sum of George's and Lennie's individual mental states, and whether that value is annihilated when George shoots Lennie, or instead transformed into a different, perhaps diminished but still unified, whole. Third, extend the analysis to the novel as an aesthetic object: if Steinbeck binds violence, landscape, and affection into a single emotional structure, does that formal unity possess intrinsic value independent of the moral horror it depicts, as Moore's separation of beauty from ethics might suggest? I want an honest critical reading—one that says where Steinbeck vindicates Moore, where the narrative complicates him (for instance, if the friendship's value seems inseparable from its fragility and destruction), and what the work contributes to the philosophy of intrinsic value that argument alone cannot supply. Do not provide a bibliography, citation list, or requests for further reading; let the answer stand on the plot, the scenes, and the philosophical ideas themselves.
