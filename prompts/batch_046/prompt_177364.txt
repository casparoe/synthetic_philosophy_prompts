Consider a national regulatory statute governing the approval of high-risk algorithmic systems. The law establishes a three-panel evaluation board—technical auditors, representatives of affected communities, and economic regulators—each of which assigns cardinal risk scores on a common interval scale. The statute requires that the final approval score be computed as a fixed linear weighted average of the three panel scores, with weights set by legislative mandate and invariant across technology domains. It further mandates that if every panel assigns a score below the statutory danger threshold, the aggregate must also fall below that threshold, and that proportional changes in all panel scores produce proportional changes in the aggregate. The law explicitly prohibits context-sensitive weighting—such as elevating community-panel weights for medical applications while elevating technical weights for financial ones—and rejects any non-compensatory veto mechanism that would allow one panel to block approval regardless of the others' scores. The legislative history justifies this design by appealing to the need for transparent, reproducible aggregation of expert judgment, but it never articulates a theory of value, comparison, or collective agency.

What philosophical commitments does this institutional architecture presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of preference aggregation and social welfare embedded in the statutory rules: does it require that risk preferences are cardinal and interpersonally comparable across the three panels? Does it presuppose that collective choice should take the form of a decomposable social welfare function—specifically, one satisfying linearity, Pareto consistency, and independence of irrelevant alternatives—rather than a democratic deliberative process or an incommensurability-sensitive judgment? Distinguish what the practice strictly entails from what it merely accommodates: could the same regulatory goals be served by a non-linear or context-dependent aggregation rule, or is the linear form essential? Is the framework philosophically underdetermined, such that rival theories of collective choice—say, one treating stakeholder judgments as incommensurable, or one treating the board as a single democratic agent rather than a weighted sum of parts—would also license the same procedural outcomes? Finally, say precisely what would have to change in both the rules and their justification if one rejected Harsanyi-style aggregation conditions, allowing instead variable weights, veto rights, or the denial that risk judgments can be summed across domains.
