I have come to accept a position that might be called robustness consequentialism: given deep consequentialist cluelessness—not mere predictive noise, but structural unreliability about long-run effects across moral domains—we should not abandon consequentialist reasoning for deontological or virtue-based frameworks. Instead, we should rank actions primarily by how well they preserve option value and avoid irreversible commitments, treating the maintenance of future decision-making capacity itself as the central moral good. I want an honest audit of what would count against this view. Please distinguish decisive defeaters from merely awkward ones: empirical cases in which preserving option value led to demonstrably worse outcomes than decisive commitment; formal arguments showing that option-value metrics collapse into standard expected-utility maximization under plausible conditions of belief updating; conceptual objections that this is just consequentialism in disguise with an unmotivated weighting scheme; and whether the view risks unfalsifiability by allowing any outcome to be redescribed ex post as either preserving or depleting option value. For each category, indicate whether a committed defender of robustness consequentialism would themselves concede it as damaging, and conclude whether the position’s exposure to refutation is a theoretical virtue or a sign of weakness.
