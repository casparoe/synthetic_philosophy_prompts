Since my recent appointment as evaluation director for a private foundation that renews five-year interdisciplinary grants in cognitive science and philosophy of mind, I have had to recommend whether long-running research centres deserve continued support, and I want these recommendations to rest on something more systematic than citation counts or personal impression. I am asking you to turn Imre Lakatos's idea of research programmes—specifically the distinction between progressive and degenerating problem shifts, the hard core, and the protective belt—into a reusable decision procedure that my review panel, composed of working scientists with no training in philosophy of science, can apply in roughly forty minutes per centre. The procedure should be rendered as a sequence of ordered tests or a flowchart in continuous prose, not as a diagram or list, and it must treat a centre's hard core as the explicit theoretical and methodological commitments stated in its original funded proposal rather than whatever its current leadership claims it to be now. Because these centres work at the boundary of empirical neuroscience and conceptual analysis, the procedure needs a specific criterion for telling whether a new auxiliary hypothesis belongs to a productive protective belt or is an ad hoc adjustment that saves the core from refutation without generating novel, testable predictions. Include three worked applications: one centre that clearly satisfies the criteria for progressiveness and yields a renewal recommendation; one well-connected, prestigious centre that clearly fails and should be cut, producing a recommendation that will be politically uncomfortable for me; and one genuinely ambiguous case where the procedure reaches its limit and forces an explicit judgment call. At each stage, indicate where judgment cannot be eliminated, where a non-philosopher might misapply the test, and what the procedure is structurally incapable of delivering—such as forecasting long-term breakthroughs, weighing social benefit, or substituting for peer review of individual experiments. Write entirely in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, use American English throughout, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations of any kind.
