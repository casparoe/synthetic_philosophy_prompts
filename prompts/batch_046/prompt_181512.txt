After a recent and painful realization that I have been selectively filtering feedback on a manuscript I have spent three years developing—treating substantive objections as misunderstandings rather than engaging with their content—I have become convinced that my case is not one of holding contradictory beliefs, but rather of maintaining a stable pattern of attention that prevents contradictory beliefs from ever forming. This has led me to a conceptual worry: the canonical paradox of self-deception assumes that the phenomenon requires simultaneously believing p and not-p, and because this violates consistency norms for belief, philosophers have been forced to choose between "intentionalist" models that posit divided minds and "deflationary" models that reduce the phenomenon to ordinary motivated reasoning. But if the core mechanism is not doxastic contradiction but a structurally sustained failure of belief-formation—what might be called "epistemic self-sabotage"—then does the traditional debate rest on a false presupposition? Specifically, should we abandon the attempt to locate self-deception within the space of propositional attitudes altogether, or does this attentional account merely relocate the same paradox at the level of second-order intentions about what one allows oneself to know?
