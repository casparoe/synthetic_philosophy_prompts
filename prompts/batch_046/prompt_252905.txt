I've been thinking about a weird pattern I keep seeing in discussions about AI and jobs, and I'm not sure if philosophers or sociologists have already named it. It's not just deskilling or alienation — those are about losing control or being seperated from the product. What's happening here is more specific: imagine a job where the hard, repetitive part gets automated away, but the "prestigious" part stays human. Like, say, an architect who used to spend years learning structural math through manual drafting, and now the computer does all that instantly, so they just click around with design aesthetics. The weird thing is that the architect doesn't feel freed up for higher-order creativity; instead, the design work starts to feel kind of fake, like they're playing dress-up as an expert without the embodied struggle that used to make their judgement authorative. The authority was built on having survived the grind, and without the grind, the remaining choices feel arbitary or weightless.

I've seen this in writing too — people using LLMs to draft the "boring" parts of essays but then feeling like they can't defend the final text because they didn't earn it through the messy drafting process. So the phenomenon is: automation removes the preparatory suffering or repetition that served as a kind of credential for the remaining human task, leaving the worker with a role that looks identical from the outside but lacks internal justification. Does this have an established name? I feel like it touches on something like hollowed expertise or credential decay, but I want to know if there's an actual concept in the literature, and if not, how exactly it differs from standard deskilling or from the bullshit jobs idea where the job is pointless. Here the job isn't pointless — it's just that the meaning came from the path, not the destination, and I'm wondering if anyone has carved out that distinction precisely.
