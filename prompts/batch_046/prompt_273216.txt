I serve on my school's disciplinary review council, and we must recommend a permanent policy for serious violations of academic integrity. The proposal before us would make expulsion irreversible—no path to readmission—once a student is found responsible. Some council members argue that anything less severe fails to honor the gravity of the betrayal: justice demands that the consequence match the wrong itself, regardless of whether it changes anyone else's behavior. Others defend the measure on practical grounds, citing our internal records that milder sanctions have not lowered violation rates, and insisting that only the credible threat of permanent removal creates enough incentive to preserve trust in the community. A third group, however, emphasizes fallibility: in recent years we have reversed two expulsion findings after new evidence emerged, and because the penalty is irreversible, an erroneous judgment can never be corrected. They also note that our process relies heavily on peer testimony, which may be shaped by social dynamics the council does not control. I must cast a deciding vote, but I do not see how to reconcile the demand that punishment fit the offense with the risk of an uncorrectable mistake, or whether prevention alone justifies such severity. How should I weigh these considerations?
