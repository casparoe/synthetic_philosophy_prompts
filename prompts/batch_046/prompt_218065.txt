I am preparing a brief contribution for an advanced philosophy seminar on the ontology of the self, specifically Daniel Dennett's analogy of the self as a center of narrative gravity — the view that the self is not an inner substance but a theoretical construct, comparable to a physical center of gravity, around which autobiographical narratives converge and from which we predict behavior. Our discussion has moved into a somewhat more technical framing that treats this center not as a static fiction but as a dynamically unstable attractor basin within a state-space of competing sub-personal narratives: fragments of bodily memory, social feedback loops, anticipatory projection, and silence that continuously reconstruct the point they appear to orbit. I would like a poem — exactly sixteen lines, arranged in four quatrains with an envelope rhyme scheme of ABBA in each stanza — that renders this unstable-attractor version of the idea with philosophical precision rather than mere allusion.

The poem should never use the word self, and the pronoun I should occur exactly once, placed at the precise center of the poem (the eighth line), functioning not as a speaking subject but as the empty gravitational point around which the other lines organize themselves. Each quatrain should draw on a distinct source of narrative input — for instance, bodily sensation, recalled dialogue, anticipated judgment, and an absence of narrative — so that the rhyme scheme's inward-then-outward motion mirrors how these fragments are pulled toward a center that lacks independent existence and then dispersed again. The form should carry the conceptual weight: the envelope rhyme should evoke convergence without consolidation, and the solitary I should read like the name of a missing object. The tone ought to be contemplative and unresolved, leaving suspended the question of whether this center is real, useful, or illusory, since that is genuinely where our seminar discussion stands.
