I'm working through a problem in applied ethics about whether individuals have genuine moral obligations to refrain from actions that contribute to large-scale harms when each contribution is, taken in isolation, causally redundant. The standard inefficacy objection—familiar from debates about climate emissions, meat consumption, and democratic voting—holds that if a harm is already overdetermined by the actions of others, my individual omission or commission makes no difference to the outcome, and therefore cannot be morally required or forbidden on purely instrumental grounds. I want you to write an essay that takes this objection seriously but examines a specific refinement that introductory treatments often neglect.

Specifically, focus on what we might call the "threshold-uncertainty structure" of real collective harms. In the stylized philosophical examples—ten thousand voters, a factory farm with a fixed order—agents are often assumed to know they fall in the redundant subset. But in actual climate, agricultural, and electoral systems, the critical threshold is not only unknown ex ante but probabilistically distributed: my flight might push a regional grid past a carbon budget, my purchase might cross a retailer's reorder point, or my vote might break a tie in a jurisdiction whose size I misjudge. The essay should weigh whether this epistemic opacity transforms the inefficacy objection from a decisive argument into a risk-management problem, or whether it instead reveals that our obligations depend on expected-value reasoning about marginal probabilities rather than on direct causal efficacy. Consider both the view that moral obligation survives only when we have reasonable confidence we are near the threshold, and the opposing view that uncertainty actually strengthens the obligation because it prevents us from treating ourselves as safely redundant. Reach a conclusion—defend either that the inefficacy objection fails in these threshold-uncertain cases (and on what grounds: expressive, deontological, or expected-utility), or that it succeeds and forces us to abandon individual-level moral obligations in favor of purely structural or collective remedies. In either case, justify your verdict against the best version of the other side.

Finally, be explicit about where your reasoning is most vulnerable: identify the specific premise—about the nature of moral risk, about the epistemic duties of agents in large systems, or about the relationship between individual intent and collective outcome—that, if shown to be mistaken, would lead you to reverse your conclusion. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I am interested in the argument itself, not in references.
