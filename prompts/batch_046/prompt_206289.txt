I have been thinking on my own about an old question I came across in Plato's dialogue called the Meno: why is knowledge supposed to be more valuable than true belief if both happen to point you in the right direction? I do not have any academic background, but I want a clear sorting tool that classifies the answers people actually hold by testing them against concrete cases, not by asking which philosophical label they prefer. Please write a taxonomy or decision tree in continuous prose only—no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and no other formatting devices whatsoever, as if you were writing a single long letter or email. The tree should begin from the shared premise that knowledge is genuinely better than lucky true belief, then branch through specific yes-or-no questions that separate positions in the logical space. The first branch should ask whether a belief formed by pure guessing that happens to be correct and is never questioned over many years should count as knowledge. The second should ask whether being able to explain your reason to someone else is essential, or whether private certainty is sufficient. The third should turn on what I will call inherited belief: if you take another person's true statement on trust without examining the evidence yourself, and that belief lives in your mind for a long time without ever being challenged, does it eventually mature into full knowledge, or does it stay borrowed opinion? Each split should be labeled by the claim that divides it, and each leaf should name an actual position—such as a stability-of-reasons view, a social-transfer view, or the view that knowledge is just belief anchored well—or else be marked unnamed if nobody occupies that spot. For every leaf, state in one sentence its most awkward commitment. Please also flag any two leaves that your tree genuinely cannot tell apart, and point out any combination of answers that seems either incoherent or simply unexplored rather than defended. I want this built from the questions and the combinations they allow, with the named positions placed into the spaces afterward, so that the instrument sorts people by what they believe rather than by what they call themselves.
