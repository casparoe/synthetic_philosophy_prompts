I cover the philosophy of risk and responsibility for a science and ideas publication. For an upcoming feature, I need a concise fictional scenario—roughly four hundred words—that dramatizes the problem of moral luck without rehearsing the familiar classroom examples. Invent a municipal system called the Contingency Ledger, which assigns citizens a numerical exposure debt calculated purely from the downstream statistical harm that flows from their ordinary, unremarkable choices—decisions made without foresight of those consequences and which they could not reasonably have anticipated. The debt does not trigger criminal liability; instead, it determines social standing, including access to shared resources, representation in local governance, and eligibility for mutual-aid pools. Write the story from the perspective of one individual who learns that her debt has suddenly tripled, not because her behavior changed, but because an unrelated event elsewhere in the causal network rewired the pathways the Ledger tracks. The narrative should render the moment she realizes that her moral status—how the community regards her as worthy of trust or burden—has been transformed by outcomes she neither intended nor controlled. After the story, please append a brief reflective note: identify where the fiction takes liberties with standard philosophical treatments of moral luck, particularly whether it collapses or invents variants of the familiar distinctions among resultant, circumstantial, and constitutive luck; and specify precisely which argument or empirical finding—for example, a rigorous demonstration that statistical correlation can never ground moral desert, or evidence that predictive risk models systematically reproduce social bias—would lead you to revise the premise that the Ledger’s allocations are genuinely unjust rather than merely unfortunate.
