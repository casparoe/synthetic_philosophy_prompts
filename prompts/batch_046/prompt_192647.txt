I'm writing an essay for a seminar on nineteenth-century social criticism and have been working through Kierkegaard's anonymous 1846 review En literair Anmeldelse—commonly translated as Two Ages or The Present Age—where he contrasts the reflective, passionless present age with the revolutionary age that preceded it. In one particularly dense section, he describes the public as an abstract, monstrous entity that devours everything and is nourished by the press: the press generates opinions without authors, distributes them without ownership, and thereby produces a realm in which everyone simultaneously has a view on every subject and no one stands behind any view in particular. He suggests that in such an age anyone can pronounce on anything, but the cost of this universal accessibility is that the opinion belongs to no concrete individual, so that the speaker is dissolved into the phantom audience even as he speaks.

What I'm struggling to understand is whether Kierkegaard treats the press as actively constructing this public ex nihilo, or whether he thinks the press merely accelerates a deeper leveling process that would occur regardless of media. There seems to be a tension in his account: on the one hand, he insists that the single individual is the only metaphysically real entity, standing in absolute isolation before God, while on the other hand he describes the public as possessing a kind of collective agency of its own—it devours, it levels, it suppresses action—almost as though it were a genuine subject rather than a mere aggregation of individuals. If the public is truly a phantom with no substance, why does he grant it causal power to stifle individual passion and responsibility? And does his critique implicitly rely on a normative theory of communication—something like the claim that authentic speech must be tied to the speaker's lived commitment and readiness to act—or is it meant to be purely descriptive sociology? I would appreciate help working through the logic of the passage, especially whether there is an unresolved contradiction between his metaphysics of the self and his sociology of the press, and if so, whether that contradiction undermines his critique or actually sharpens it.
