I'm trying to determine what I actually believe about the ethics of meat consumption and wild-animal suffering, judging by my intuitive verdicts on concrete cases rather than by any explicit theory. Here are the judgments.

Eating conventionally farmed chicken strikes me as worse than eating grass-fed beef from open rangeland. With chicken, I picture intense confinement suffering multiplied across many individuals, plus the rodent and insect deaths caused by growing and storing feed crops. With beef, one large animal dies, but if it lived on pasture that replaced wild herbivores, its presence may actually reduce net wild suffering from predation, starvation, and disease—so beef seems less bad, despite the single death.

Yet if that same beef comes from cattle raised on recently deforested land that now supports almost no wild mammals, my judgment flips: the cattle replace little wild suffering, and the land-use change itself destroyed habitats, so beef in this context feels worse than chicken fed on long-cultivated cropland.

A fully plant-based diet of grains, legumes, and vegetables seems best overall, though not morally pure—mechanized harvesting kills field rodents and insects, and stored grain requires pest control. Still, I think the total suffering is lower than in either meat scenario.

Wild-caught fish that lived free lives and died quickly, with minimal bycatch, feel acceptable to me—perhaps even preferable to plant agriculture if the fishing avoids broad ecological disruption. But farmed fish in dense pens with parasites and crowding feel as bad as conventional chicken.

Farmed bivalves—oysters and mussels—grown without feed inputs seem almost certainly permissible, even granting them minimal sentience, because they displace no wild animals and cause no crop-related deaths.

There is one case I keep returning to without resolution: beef from arid rangeland where wild herbivores would otherwise endure drought, starvation, and heavy predation. The cattle live relatively natural lives and are eventually slaughtered, but their presence might suppress a wild population that would suffer more severely and for longer. Is this genuinely the least harmful option, or am I rationalizing?

Finally, I find farmed insects—crickets, mealworms—worse than beef, despite their lower individual sentience, because the numbers are astronomical and the farming conditions involve crowding and abrupt killing; this sits uneasily with my willingness to accept beef from rangeland.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps weighing direct intentional harm, replacing wild suffering, or scaling suffering by sentience and lifespan—that captures all of these verdicts, or must I abandon one? What does the best principle say about the arid-rangeland beef case, and what does it predict for a hypothetical diet of only tree nuts and fruits that involve no annual harvest, no feed crops, and no displacement of wild herbivores?
