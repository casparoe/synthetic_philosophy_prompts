In our philosophy of value seminar, we have been discussing tranquilism—the view that well-being consists solely in the absence of unsatisfied craving and suffering, with achievements, relationships, and even pleasures carrying no intrinsic weight. I tried to formalize the view into an argument that seems to collapse into antinatalism and self-refutation, but I cannot identify the exact misstep.

(1) Define well-being as W = −[S + C], where S is suffering and C is unsatisfied craving; positive states contribute zero.
(2) Non-existence has S = 0 and C = 0, so W = 0, which is the global maximum—no life can score above it.
(3) Any striving life—pursuing knowledge, art, or attachment—generates craving (C > 0) and risks suffering (S > 0), so for every such life W < 0.
(4) Therefore, rationality requires preventing births (to avoid future W < 0) and, for those already existing, maintaining a sedated, craving-free state.
(5) But this proves too much: it makes defending tranquilism itself irrational, since philosophical argument is a form of striving that introduces craving for truth; it also renders education, care, and creation not merely worthless but positively harmful.
(6) The slide seems to happen somewhere between quantifying mental states (steps 1–2), comparing existence to non-existence (step 3), and deriving a prescription of inaction from a negative-value calculus (step 4).

Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost the tranquilist? I am especially reluctant to abandon the idea that suffering genuinely outweighs achievement, but I want to avoid the conclusion that the best world is an empty one.
