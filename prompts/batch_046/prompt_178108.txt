As a retired physicist who has recently begun serious study in philosophy of mind and language, I have been struck by a long-standing stalemate in the debate over semantic externalism. Since Putnam introduced the Twin Earth scenario and Burge developed the social-deference arguments concerning natural-kind terms and expert communities, philosophers have divided between those who hold that the content of mental states depends constitutively on causal-historical relations to the environment and those who maintain that content is fixed entirely by intrinsic psychological or functional properties. The externalist argues that two molecule-for-molecule identical agents—one on Earth, one on Twin Earth—can possess different thoughts about water or about arthritis solely in virtue of differences in their external causal chains and linguistic communities. The internalist replies that this conflates the metaphysics of reference with the psychology of concept possession, and that a proper taxonomy of mental states should not require leaving the skull.

What puzzles me is that neither camp appears to have revised its core commitments despite decades of refinement. Both sides accept the descriptive details of the thought experiments, yet they draw opposite conclusions about what those details imply for the individuation of mental content. My own suspicion—formed, perhaps, by habits acquired in physics—is that the disagreement is not primarily about missing empirical data, since no experiment on Twin Earth is available, but rather about what counts as an adequate explanation of linguistic competence. The externalist treats environmental boundary conditions as part of the content-constituting mechanism, analogous to how a physical system is partly specified by its external constraints; the internalist treats content as an internal functional state that ought to be explicable without invoking external causation. This suggests the stalemate may be structural: the two sides may be pursuing different explanatory projects—one metaphysical, concerning how reference is fixed; the other cognitive-scientific, concerning how concepts are implemented—under the single heading of meaning.

I therefore ask for a diagnosis rather than an adjudication. Is this disagreement best understood as a verbal dispute about the word meaning, as a substantive clash over whether psychological taxonomy must be individualistic, or as a case of two research programs answering distinct questions that happen to share a vocabulary? Furthermore, if your diagnosis is correct, what—if anything—would genuinely move the debate? Would progress require a new formal model of concept individuation acceptable to both sides, a sharp conceptual distinction between reference-determination and cognitive accessibility, or an argument that the framing of internal versus external is itself a false dichotomy inherited from an outdated computational picture of the mind? I want an analysis that engages the actual commitments of both camps, not a survey of interpretations, together with an honest assessment of whether this is an impasse that can be resolved, transformed, or should be abandoned.
