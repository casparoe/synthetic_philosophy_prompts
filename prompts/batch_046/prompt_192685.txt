I am a journalist covering the philosophy of science and technology, and I am preparing a feature on whether contemporary automated reasoning can break a classic regress in the justification of deduction. I have in mind Lewis Carroll’s dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise, in which every attempt to justify a deductive step demands a further premise, producing an infinite chain that never reaches an ungrounded foundation. Today, neural systems can learn to generate formal proofs by extracting statistical patterns from large mathematical corpora rather than by explicitly encoding rules of inference, raising a question Carroll could not have anticipated. If he were observing these systems now, how might he extend his paradox? Would he regard distributed statistical generalization over training data as merely another disguised layer in the Tortoise’s regress—an unargued leap that presents correlation as justification—or might he identify in it a genuinely novel termination of the chain? I am not seeking a survey of current AI architectures, nor any bibliography of Carroll scholarship; I want a rigorous conceptual reconstruction of his likely position, faithful to the internal logic of his original argument but applied to an epistemic technology he never encountered. In your analysis, at precisely which point in a modern neural prover’s operation would Carroll’s Tortoise refuse to take the next step, and on what grounds?
