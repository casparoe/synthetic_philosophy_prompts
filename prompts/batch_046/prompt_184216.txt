As a doctoral researcher in philosophy of history, I am examining a configuration of explanatory overdetermination that the standard literature on redundant and conjunctural causation does not seem to capture precisely. I am terming it interdependent sufficiency: a situation in which two causally independent pathways to a historical outcome are each individually sufficient, yet they are counterfactually interlocked—removing one alters the conditions under which the other operates—so that neither is strictly necessary and they are not merely redundant duplicates. Could you give me a concrete example from twentieth-century political history, preferably one in which historians genuinely dispute the relative explanatory weight of structural economic forces and contingent political agency, that exhibits precisely this architecture rather than ordinary overdetermination or simple interaction effects? After presenting the case, distinguish its causal structure from both redundant overdetermination and multi-causal interaction, and explain why it poses a distinctive problem for assigning explanatory weight in historiography. Please separate what is broadly accepted about the causal structure of your chosen example from interpretations that remain genuinely contested among philosophers of history and working historians, and do not present any particular weighting of the structural and agential factors as the established consensus.
