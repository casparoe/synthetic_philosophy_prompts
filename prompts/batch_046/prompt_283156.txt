My roommate summarized Burkean conservatism and Chesterton's fence like this: "Burke believed society is an organic partnership across generations — dead, living, and unborn — so institutions accumulate a prescriptive legitimacy just by persisting, kind of like evolutionary adaptations. Chesterton's fence is the practical version: if you see a fence in a field and can't explain its purpose, don't tear it down, since it probably blocks something you don't notice. Together they form a conservative epistemology: reform requires not just a good reason for change, but a complete functional explanation of the institution's current role, and if you can't provide one, that gap is itself evidence the institution serves a hidden purpose. So Burkeans think rapid reform is always irational — ignorance about purpose becomes proof of purpose, and the burden of proof is entierly on the reformer." Something about the jump from "be cautious" to "ignorance is proof" feels like a leap. Which parts are accurate, which ones conflate Burke's prescription with Chesterton's epistemic humility, and does the conclusion that ignorance proves purpose actually follow? Also, is it fair to say Burkeans reject rapid change in every case?
