I'm writing a dissertation chapter on explanatory norms in evolutionary biology and I'm stuck on this institutional pattern I keep seeing. In a lot of morphology and behavioral ecology journals there's basically an unwriten rule: if you report a trait, you have to lead with an adaptive hypothesis, and "spandrel" or "exaptation" only gets used as a fallback after reviewers reject the functional story. I was discussing this with my advisor and she pointed out that Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique wasn't just empirical but about what counts as a legitimate explanation in evolutionary science. So here's my question: what philosophical commitments about causation and scientific explanation does that peer-review practice actually presuppose? Does it treat natural selection as the default causal mechanism, with structural or developmental constraints seen as merely negative absences? Would taking spandrels seriously as primary explanations — not just residual categories — require a different philosophy of biology, maybe something closer to a constraint-based or structuralist ontology? And is the current practice actually consistent with explanatory pluralism, or does it quietly commit the field to adaptationist monism? I want to reconstruct the implicit theory of explanation here, not just get a summary of Gould-Lewontin, and I'm particularly interested in whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined — could it survive if we replaced the background view with something like a developmental-systems framing, or does it strictly need the adaptationist presupposition to make sense?
