In my upper-division bioethics course on reproductive ethics, we have spent several weeks on the tension between Julian Savulescu's principle of procreative beneficence and the liberal eugenics position associated with Nicholas Agar. The disagreement in our seminar has reached a frustrating impasse. Defenders of beneficence argue that parents who can screen embryos have a moral reason to select the one with the highest expected well-being, and that failing to do so is analogous to negligent harm. Proponents of liberal eugenics accept the permissibility of genetic selection but deny any corresponding duty, insisting that reproductive autonomy includes the right to refuse testing and that imposing an obligation to optimize risks reviving coercive eugenics in a new form. Both sides agree on the empirical facts about screening technology and on the badness of suffering; yet no one has changed position.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not about welfare versus liberty, but about two deeper issues that are rarely separated in the literature. First, there is a metaphysical question rooted in the non-identity problem: can a particular embryo that is never implanted be said to suffer a comparative harm, or is talk of harming a merely possible person conceptually confused? Second, there may be a hidden normative disagreement about whether reproductive choices should be evaluated ex ante by expected utility at the moment of decision, or ex post by the actual quality of the life that eventually results — a distinction that matters greatly when a child born with a disability lives a flourishing life that exceeds statistical predictions. A third possibility is that the sides are not answering the same question at all: one addresses the morality of individual reproductive acts, the other addresses the institutional design of genetic services and the risks of social stratification.

Is my diagnosis correct, or is there a substantive empirical or formal disagreement — perhaps about the heritability of complex traits or the limits of polygenic risk scores — doing the work beneath the surface? More specifically, is this primarily a verbal dispute about the scope of "beneficence," a disagreement about which cases count as data for moral evaluation, or an irreducible conflict of bedrock intuitions about genetic contingency and naturalness? And given your answer, is there any conceptual distinction — for instance, between selecting against severe monogenic disorders and selecting for polygenic enhancements — that could actually move the debate, or should we regard this as a permanently intractable clash of moral frameworks?
