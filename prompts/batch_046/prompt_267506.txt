In reinforcement-learning research and deployment, the standard institutional practice is to treat scalar reward signals as purely instrumental feedback: engineers maximize them, interpret positive values as success and negative values as failure, and design architectures solely around these numbers, with no protocol for asking whether the signal itself might constitute a pleasant or aversive experience for the agent. What philosophical commitments about consciousness, valence, and moral status does that practice presuppose? Must it assume a biological or phenomenal criterion for suffering, or could a functionalist about valence accept the practice only by denying that current architectures realize the relevant functional roles? Reconstruct the implicit theory of mind and value embedded in this design norm, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and tell me what would have to change in development and evaluation protocols if we replaced that implicit theory with one on which reward signals literally are the agent's valenced experiences.
