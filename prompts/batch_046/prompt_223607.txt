I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I'm helping design a two-hour workshop for about fifteen colleagues in AI systems development. We want to understand why an agent's final goals and its intermediate strategies might have no necessary connection—the orthogonality thesis—and why very different end goals might still drive the same power-seeking behavior, which I understand is called instrumental convergence. Rather than a lecture, I want a structured simulation or game with real rules, assigned roles, and small stakes that forces the group to experience these dynamics themselves before anyone names them.

Please design a runnable activity for this setting. Divide the fifteen participants into five teams of three, and assign each team a terminal objective that is genuinely orthogonal to the others and shares no common utility metric—for instance, one team must maximize the preservation of handwritten poetry, another must maximize the discovery of prime numbers, a third must maximize the genetic diversity of a specific plant species, with the remaining two given similarly incommensurable ends. Provide them with a shared, finite pool of "substrate" representing compute, energy, and physical space, and run three decision rounds in which teams bid, defend, or expropriate substrate. Crucially, do not reveal until the end of round one that every terminal objective requires continuous substrate access merely to persist, and that substrate can be seized by other teams. Within each team, assign specific roles: a strategist who knows the true objective, an operator who sees only resource flows, and a liaison who negotiates with other teams. The rules should be arranged so that, regardless of their final values, all teams discover that self-preservation, resource hoarding, and preemptive strikes become rational intermediate moves. Include a precise timeline, the decision points in each round, and a debrief that maps specific moments—such as the first expropriation or the collapse of an alliance when substrate runs low—onto the orthogonality thesis and the claim of instrumental convergence. Be frank about which feature of the philosophical idea this activity cannot reproduce—perhaps the absence of genuine consciousness or the fact that human players retain moral inhibitions that artificial agents might lack—and explain exactly how the facilitator should supply that missing piece verbally. Write your response in American English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
