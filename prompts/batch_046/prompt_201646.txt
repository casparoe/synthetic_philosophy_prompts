I am assembling a two-year research agenda for a small philosophy-of-mind working group, and rather than surveying consciousness studies broadly, we want to concentrate our efforts on the meta-problem—specifically, on which second-order questions about the hard problem are genuinely upstream and which are conceptually redundant. To frame this precisely, I have been working with a four-part taxonomy of open issues that seem to fall under the meta-problem umbrella but that may not stand or fall together. First, the origin question: what cognitive, evolutionary, or conceptual mechanisms generate the intuition that phenomenal consciousness is not fully captured by physical explanations? Second, the persistence question: why does this intuition remain robust even among researchers thoroughly acquainted with neuroscience and physicalism—does it indicate a residual epistemic gap, a conceptual confusion about phenomenal concepts, or an irreducible form of first-person authority? Third, the normative-debt question: if we were to solve the meta-problem by explaining away the intuition—say, via a phenomenal-concept strategy or an evolutionary-psychology account—would that dissolve any remaining explanatory obligation to provide a physical account of consciousness itself, or does it merely relocate the burden without reducing it? Fourth, the symmetry or distribution question: does an analogous meta-problem arise for other domains we treat as explanatorily special, such as life, intentionality, or mathematical knowledge, and if consciousness is unique in generating this second-order puzzle, what does that asymmetry reveal about its conceptual status?

Given our background in analytic philosophy of mind and basic logic, but limited access to empirical cognitive-science labs, which of these four lines should we prioritize over the next two years, and in what dependency order? I am asking for a defended agenda rather than a survey: please specify the criteria you are using—tractability for philosophers without heavy empirical collaboration, how many other questions depend on this one being settled first, whether resolving it would change how we approach the first-order hard problem, and which issues are relatively neglected. In particular, I want you to examine whether settling the origin or persistence questions actually undermines the normative-debt question, or whether those can come apart in ways that matter for our research planning. Rank the four, explain the trade-offs explicitly, and identify which one or two you would deprioritize because their resolution would not significantly alter downstream work. Please do not include a bibliography, citation list, or recommendations for specific papers; I need a conceptual prioritization that I can present and defend in a grant meeting without relying on literature I have not yet verified.
