I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about conflicts between the nyāya pramāṇas, going by gut feeling on cases. When direct perception (pratyakṣa) and inference (anumāna) clash over something right in front of me — like seeing a stick straight in water vs inferring it's bent — I think perception wins and kills the inference.

When testimony (śabda) clashes with non-apprehension (anupalabdhi) over something hidden — say a sage tells me there's an atom behind the wall but I don't percieve it — I think testimony still holds; the absence of seeing doesn't cancel the word.

But then there's comparison (upamāna). If I identify an animal as a cow because it matches a picture I was taught (upamāna), but my inference from its mechanical movements says it's a robot (anumāna), I feel like comparison should win? Or maybe inference should? I keep flip-flopping. Meanwhile, if I identify thunder by comparing the sound to past thunder (upamāna), but meteorlogical inference says there's no storm, I feel inference should win. So comparison vs inference feels different in the cow/robot case versus the thunder case, even though the structure looks the same.

Also, there's the case where anupalabdhi says there's no jar on the table because I don't see one, but a trusted witness says it's behind a curtain. I think testimony wins there too, but I'm less sure.

Is there a coherent principle that captures all of these — maybe about how close the pramāṇa is to the object, or about defeasibility conditions — with each clause tied to the case that motivates it? Or do I have to drop one of my verdicts to keep it simple? Please also say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: what if I percieve a faint shadow that could be a snake, but a reliable guide tells me it's just a rope (śabda), and I also fail to percieve any snake-like movement (anupalabdhi)? Which pramāṇa should dominate, and does that force me to revise any of my earlier takes?
