I just had a long argument with a friend about Quine's philosophy of moral values—specifically, whether his refusal to reduce ethics to physical science should be read as a pragmatic naturalism that tolerates normative posits, or as a form of anti-realism that relegates moral terms to a merely expressive or ideological role. We kept circling the same interpretive points: Quine treats mathematical entities as indispensable ontological commitments, yet in his writing on ethics he seems to place moral values on the ideology side of his ontology/ideology divide without explaining whether the divide functions identically in both domains. My working theory is that the stall is not textual ambiguity but a hidden disagreement about what counts as a legitimate posit: one side demands that anything without physical instantiation be eliminated or fictionalized, the other allows for practical indispensability without ontological weight. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a substantive disagreement concealed beneath it—perhaps about whether normative force can be borne by an ideological apparatus that carries no ontological commitment? I want a structural diagnosis that engages Quine's actual distinction between ontology and ideology, compares its deployment in mathematics and in ethics, and explains what, if anything, could resolve the impasse: a clarification of canonical notation for moral discourse, an analogy with Quine's treatment of intentional or modal idiom, or an argument that the question dissolves once the false dichotomy between real entities and mere fictions is abandoned. Please do not survey secondary literature; address the logic of the disagreement itself and tell me whether progress should be expected.
