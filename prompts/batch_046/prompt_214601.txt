I have drafted a short conceptual essay on corruption, clientelism, and cross-cultural judgment, and I want critical feedback on its coherence rather than on citation coverage or stylistic polish. The essay introduces two non-standard distinctions. First, it separates transactional corruption—an explicit exchange of official action for private benefit—from what I call reciprocal encumbrance: the gradual accumulation of social obligations through repeated gift-giving that never involves an explicit quid pro quo yet systematically biases institutional judgment. Second, it proposes an analytic tool I term the institutional reciprocity threshold, which holds that a gift becomes corrupt not when its value exceeds some fraction of local income, but when it crosses a boundary from personal or kin-based reciprocity into role-specific obligation, where the recipient’s institutional duties and the donor’s expectations become structurally incompatible. The essay then argues that Western anti-corruption frameworks embed a specifically contract-based, universalist ontology of the office-holder—as an impersonal agent whose private relationships must be fully separable from public function—rather than expressing a culturally neutral standard. It concludes that evaluating gift-giving practices in high-clientelism societies requires internal assessment against the institutional logic of the roles involved, not direct application of Western separation norms, though this does not collapse into relativism because some forms of encumbrance genuinely disable institutional functioning.

Please comment specifically on the following. First, does the distinction between transactional corruption and reciprocal encumbrance hold, or does it collapse because all sustained gift exchange is ultimately transactional at some deeper level? Second, is the institutional reciprocity threshold genuinely distinct from familiar ideas like conflict of interest or undue influence, or is it merely a relabeling? Third, is the claim that Western standards embed a particular moral ontology defensible without overstating cultural difference—could one defend universal separation norms on functional rather than metaphysical grounds? Fourth, identify any hidden premises or weak points in the argument, especially regarding how one determines the “institutional logic” of a role without circularity. Please do not supply a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I am testing conceptual structure without access to external sources, and I want to know whether the framework stands on its own reasoning.
