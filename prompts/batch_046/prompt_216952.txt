I am designing a session for an upper-level philosophy-of-law seminar—fourteen students, ninety minutes—on the Hart–Devlin debate over legal moralism. I want a structured legislative simulation rather than a standard discussion, arranged so that participants feel the gravitational pull of Devlin’s “disintegration” thesis and discover for themselves that Hart’s harm principle does not resolve the disagreement as cleanly as it first appears.

Design a runnable in-class activity with the following specifications.

The case to be legislated: “consensual cognitive co-authorship,” a procedure in which two consenting adults merge their autobiographical memory banks into a single, jointly authored life-narrative for artistic or therapeutic ends. It causes no physical injury, involves full informed consent, and produces direct benefits for participants. Nevertheless, it triggers widespread social revulsion because it dissolves the boundary between individual moral agents and undermines the narrative conditions for attributing praise, blame, and legal responsibility.

The setting and roles: Divide the class into three committees of four or five students each—Legislative Drafting, Social Impact Assessment, and Constitutional Constraints—plus two observers. Before negotiation begins, each committee must construct a quick “normative topology” map of the society they govern: they draw nodes for core norms (individual authorship, consent, artistic freedom, accountability, social trust) and draw edges showing which norms depend on others. The idea of normative topology—mapping cascading failures across moral boundaries—is introduced here as an unfamiliar framing device; students should not be able to fall back on pre-cooked opinions about standard cases such as drug use or sexual practice. Once the map is drawn, the Legislative Drafting committee proposes a statute (criminalize, license with strict oversight, or leave unregulated). The other committees cross-examine and may veto any provision that violates their mandate: Social Impact Assessment may invoke only arguments about institutional stability and indirect social effects; Constitutional Constraints may invoke only arguments about procedural neutrality and direct harm. All arguments must be drawn from a restricted “argument palette” printed on cards: Direct Physical Harm, Indirect Institutional Harm, Autonomy/Consent, Social Cohesion, and Procedural Neutrality.

The rules and timeline: Ten minutes for briefing and normative-map construction; thirty minutes for committee negotiation and cross-examination; fifteen minutes for a formal vote with brief justification; thirty minutes for debrief. There are no monetary payoffs, but there are status stakes: observers score each committee on consistency—whether the arguments used match the committee’s permitted palette—and the Legislative Drafting committee “wins” only if its statute survives both vetoes without being rewritten into incoherence.

What should happen: During negotiation, students should find that “Direct Physical Harm” is unavailable, that “Consent” supports deregulation, and yet that “Social Cohesion” and “Indirect Institutional Harm” keep re-emerging in disguised form—e.g., claims that dissolving authorship will eventually erode contract law, criminal testimony, or democratic accountability. The observers should record moments when a committee smuggles a moral premise (e.g., “this is just wrong”) into apparently neutral language (e.g., “this destabilizes the normative framework”). The activity should make participants experience Devlin’s pressure—the sense that some practices threaten society’s shared moral fabric even without identifiable victims—while also making them experience Hart’s resistance: that labeling a practice “disgusting” is not the same as showing harm.

The debrief: Map specific moments from the negotiation onto Hart’s and Devlin’s texts. Identify which committees collapsed into a liberal consensus too quickly and which ones could not escape the intuition that shared morality has independent institutional value. Be explicit about the feature of Devlin’s argument that this simulation cannot reproduce—namely, the empirical-sociological claim that a society actually requires a shared moral code to survive as a society, not merely that its members feel discomfort—and state that the facilitator must supply that claim directly. Also note what will predictably go wrong the first time: observers may dominate discussion, or the Constitutional Constraints committee may conflate “no direct harm” with “no regulation permitted,” collapsing the tension prematurely. Provide a brief facilitation fix for each failure.
