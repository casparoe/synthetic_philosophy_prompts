I am revising the syllabus for a graduate seminar in philosophy of religion, and after assigning David K. Lewis's essay on penal substitution—where he presses the thought that our legal and moral practices treat serious punishment as non-transferable in ways that appear to undermine substitutionary accounts of the atonement—I found that students kept retreating into familiar theological disputes rather than examining the conceptual architecture of Lewis's legal analogy itself. I want to reorient the discussion toward the open philosophical work that actually flows from that analogy, but I am unsure where the real payoff lies, especially for a student who has about three years and comes to the topic with a background in philosophy of law and ethics rather than in biblical studies or historical theology.

In particular, I have been wondering whether the argument gains or loses force when we move away from the standard criminal-law framework and instead examine analogues in other domains of legal responsibility: for instance, whether vicarious liability in corporate criminal law, the non-delegability of certain administrative penalties, or the conditions under which contract law permits novation of obligations might provide sharper or blunter instruments for testing whether punishment can coherently be borne by a party who did not commit the wrong. There is also the question of whether Lewis's point distinguishes penal substitution from satisfaction-type theories in a way that matters for broader debates in retributive justice, and whether his reasoning extends to collective or transitional-justice contexts where groups rather than individuals are the bearers of liability. Each of these directions injects a somewhat niche conceptual framing that is not usually central to the standard theological literature on atonement.

Given that a student in this seminar must produce original dissertation-level work without relying on web-accessible bibliographies or highly specialized historical scholarship, which of these open lines—refining the legal analogy across different domains of liability, testing its implications for the distinction between penal and satisfaction theories, or extending it to collective punishment regimes—deserves the most sustained attention? Please rank them, defend the ordering with explicit criteria (tractability within a dissertation timeline, dependence on prior work in philosophy of law versus philosophy of religion, and whether resolving the question would genuinely alter how philosophers think about responsibility or atonement), and indicate which you would deprioritize because its answer would leave the broader landscape largely unchanged. I am writing this as the guiding question for the seminar's final unit, so I want the reasoning to be rigorous enough to justify where I direct limited student energy.
