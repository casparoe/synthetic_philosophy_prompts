Consider the institutional design practice now visible in several AI governance frameworks, in which a system is not assessed directly on the utility or accuracy of each individual output, but is instead required to execute an internal two-stage loop: it generates a preliminary response, subjects that response to critique against a fixed normative constitution, and revises iteratively until the critique is satisfied. Proponents defend this architecture not by claiming that the constitutional principles determine the rightness of every token directly, but by arguing that a system embedded in such a self-corrective procedure will, across its operational lifetime, be more reliably aligned than one optimized for direct reward, accuracy, or user satisfaction. What philosophical commitments about normativity does this arrangement presuppose? Specifically, does it require indirect normativity, in the sense that primary normative authority attaches to the deliberative rule or procedure rather than to case-by-case evaluation of outputs? Does it also presuppose that idealized deliberation—counterfactual reasoning by a rational agent constrained by principles—carries greater normative weight than the system's actual causal behavior? Reconstruct the implicit theoretical framework, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, assess whether a direct consequentialist calculus applied to individual outputs could license the same architecture for entirely different reasons, and specify what structural changes would be necessary if one replaced indirect normativity with direct, action-guiding norms.
