I have come to accept that the precautionary principle is not merely a political heuristic but a rational default for technologies—such as stratospheric aerosol injection or engineered pathogens—whose risks are globally distributed and structurally irreversible. In conditions of deep uncertainty, where reliable probability distributions are unavailable, expected-utility reasoning collapses; drawing on my background in engineering, I see the principle as analogous to robust control, where one designs against worst-case disturbances within a bounded uncertainty set rather than optimizing for an average scenario. I hold that precaution is mandatory when harm is irreversible, causal pathways resist precise quantification, and inaction carries no comparable catastrophic risk. What would genuinely count against this view? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward: does the robust-control analogy fail because socio-technical systems are non-stationary and lack fixed uncertainty bounds? Does precaution generate symmetric demands that paralyze all action—the precautionary paradox? Have defenders quietly insulated the view by smuggling in implicit probability weights, rendering it unfalsifiable? Which objections would a committed precautionist concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as misapplying a domain-specific framework? I am particularly interested in whether irreversibility is a physical fact or a normatively loaded threshold, and if the latter, whether the principle collapses into a disguised value judgment rather than a rational default.
