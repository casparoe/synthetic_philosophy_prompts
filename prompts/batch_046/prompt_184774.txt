I've been trying to work through that brain-transplant thought experiment where a surgeon takes a man's brain out, keeps it alive in a vat, and lets his body walk around controlled by radio links. People often say the brain is like a computer hard drive and the body is like the laptop case — move the drive, and the files, which include you, go with it. I want to check exactly how far that comparison holds, because it feels like it skips over something important.

Can you map the parts out for me? What corresponds to the hard drive, the chassis, the operating system, the user, the power supply, and the backup copy? Then show where the mapping breaks down, especially for the question of where he actually is. A hard drive has no feelings and no point of view — can it really be split between two machines? In the story, there is also a computer duplicate running in parallel with the biological brain, reproducing its processing exactly. Does that match cloning a hard drive, migrating files, or something else? If both run at once, is that like two laptops with identical drives? And if the original brain is destroyed but the computer copy keeps running, is that like deleting the original drive after backing it up?

There is also the gradual-replacement idea: if my neurons are swapped out one by one for silicon chips, I supposedly stay myself, like replacing planks on a ship. Does the hard-drive analogy help with that, or does it fight against it? If the self is software, copying should be easy and identity should split — yet the intuition is that there should still be just one me. Does the laptop picture secretly assume there is a separate user who owns the hardware?

Finally, the story seems to suggest that my point of view sticks to wherever I am receiving signals and acting, not to wherever my brain physically sits. Is there an everyday analogy — maybe not involving technology at all — that captures that idea without falling apart?
