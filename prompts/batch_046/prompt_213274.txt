I've been trying to wrap my head around the seperateness of persons objection—like how Rawls and Nozick say utilitarianism wrongly treats people like containers for a single pool of well-being. But when I push the idea, it seems to prove too much. Here's the argument I built:

(1) If well-being is not fungible across distinct individuals, then it's wrong to make one person suffer just so another gets an equal benefit.
(2) But well-being is also not clearly fungible across long stretches within a single life—the teenager saving for retirement and the old person spending that money share only weak psychological continuty and often want opposite things.
(3) So if separateness blocks inter-personal aggregation, it should block big intra-personal tradeoffs for the same reason.
(4) That means it should be wrong to endure years of dull work now so that my futre self can retire comfortably.
(5) But long-term planning like that is clearly rational and morally okay.
(6) So either the separateness objection is false, or it applies everywhere and makes ordinary life-planning immoral.
(7) I don't want to reject (5), and I don't want to call saving for retirement immoral.

Where's the weak link? Which premise should I drop, and what's the cost—do I have to deny that personal identity over time is morally relevant, or do I need a different account of why aggregation fails?
