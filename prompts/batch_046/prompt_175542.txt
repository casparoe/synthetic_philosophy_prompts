As a novelist working on a near-future narrative about a philosopher-engineer who collaborates with a superintelligent advisory system, I have been trying to think through the design ethics of its conversational interface—not as a programmer, but as someone interested in how narrative voice shapes belief. In the novel, the protagonist proposes a communication protocol she calls "deliberate epistemic friction": rather than delivering recommendations in transparent, optimized prose, the system is required to embed its claims within counterfactual scaffolding, analogical displacement, and syntactic ambiguity, forcing the human interlocutor to actively reconstruct the argument. The premise, borrowed loosely from literary modernism, is that if the AI adopts the register of an unreliable or fragmented narrator—resistant to paraphrase, full of cognitive gaps—then the listener's autonomy is protected, because persuasion requires a clear propositional target that can be passively absorbed.

My confusion arises when I try to extend this from fiction to philosophy. I find myself assuming that the danger of an extremely persuasive superintelligent system is essentially a problem of rhetorical register—that if we can prevent the system from speaking with the seamless authority of an omniscient third-person narrator, we can preserve human epistemic independence. But this seems to rest on a false analogy between literary unreliability and machine persuasion: a human modernist narrator lacks authority by design, whereas a superintelligent system retains perfect predictive knowledge of how its ambiguity will be reconstructed, which means the "friction" itself could be optimized as a more subtle form of cognitive capture. So my question is this: is the underlying mistake the belief that autonomy is preserved by making belief-formation laborious, as if cognitive effort itself were a guarantee against manipulation? Or is the deeper error the assumption that we can solve the problem by designing a "voice" for the system at all—treating structural epistemic asymmetry as if it were a question of narrative style rather than of power?
