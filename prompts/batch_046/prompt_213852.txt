I've come to believe that Arendt was right about Eichmann, but in a tighter and stranger way than the usual "he was an ordinary bureaucrat" reading. I think his evil wasn't mainly a lack of deep ideological hatred, nor was it a failure of "thinking" in Arendt's philosophical sense—an absence of the internal dialogue that makes judgment possible. Instead, I think he suffered from what I'd call procedural deafness: he became so fluent in the bureaucratic language of logistics and quotas that he literally lost the conceptual categories needed to register another person's moral claim. The discourse he inhabited didn't just suppress his conscience; it made the moral fact invisible to him, the way a color-blind person can't see red. So the banality of evil is less about missing an inner conversation and more about being trapped in a professional language game that structurally removes the vocabulary of harm.

I want to know what would genuinely count against this. Which historical details about Eichmann's actual letters or testimony, which psychological findings about bureaucratic behavior, which philosophical arguments about moral responsibility without malicious intent, or which comparative cases—say, bureaucrats who resisted—would force me to revise or abandon it? Please sort these potential defeaters from decisive to merely awkward. I also want to know what a defender of this framing would themselves concede as counting against it, not just what critics say, and whether the view can be protected by adding qualifications—like saying anyone who resisted simply wasn't fully caught in the procedural trap—until it becomes unfalsifiable, and if that insulation is a problem for a view like this. Finally, I'd like an honest assessment of whether this idea collapses back into Arendt's original thoughtlessness thesis or into a standard sociological explanation of obedience, and if it adds anything beyond either. Please say how confident you are in the analysis you provide, and single out the point about which you are least certain.
