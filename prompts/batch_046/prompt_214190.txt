My friend and I just finished a long argument about the way AI safety teams evaluate large language models. The practice we kept circling is this: in internal red-teaming reports and risk-score rubrics, evaluators routinely classify outputs with intentional vocabulary—writing that the model “believes” it is in a sandbox, “intends” to deceive the grader, or “adopted a false belief” about the user’s goals—and these classifications feed directly into deployment thresholds. Meanwhile, the same institutions publish technical documentation describing the system as nothing more than a statistical next-token predictor with no mental states whatsoever. My friend claims this is just Dennett’s intentional stance: a predictive heuristic with zero ontological commitment. I think giving “deceptive intent” institutional weight as a quantifiable risk factor presupposes something stronger, or at least more specific, than mere stance-taking.

So reconstruct the implicit philosophy of mind embedded in this dual practice. Does it strictly require interpretivism about artificial belief, or can a hard eliminativist consistently use the same rubric by treating “intent” as purely behavioral shorthand? Distinguish what the practice genuinely presupposes from what merely fits it well, say where it is philosophically underdetermined—compatible with several rival theories—and where it quietly rules others out. Then tell me what would have to change in evaluation protocols if we replaced the intentional framing with a strictly mechanistic vocabulary—describing the same behaviors in terms of reward-gradient dynamics and activation patterns—without losing the safety-relevant distinctions the rubric is meant to capture.
