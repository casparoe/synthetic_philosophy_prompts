I'm taking a phil of science course and we got into this weird comparison between Darwin and Turing that I cant stop thinking about. Darwin showed that natural selection produces incredibly complex adaptations without any designer understanding what it's building - its competence without comprehension on a massive scale. Then Turing comes along and basically does the opposite: he builds machines that can do intelligent things without having any understanding, and he even suggests that we might explain human intelligence the same way, as computation without comprehension.

My professor mentioned something about how both cases invert the usual reasoning where comprehension comes first and competence follows. But I'm confused about what exactly gets inverted in each case, and whether they're actually the same kind of inversion or different ones. Like, in Darwin its that understanding isnt needed to produce good design, but in Turing its that good performance doesnt require understanding either - but then there's also Turing's point about explaining intelligence mechanistically. Can someone explain the conceptual structure of these two inversions? Specifically, how does Darwin's argument that nature works like a blind watchmaker relate to Turing's idea that machines could think without knowing? And is there a hidden philosophical commitment in both about what counts as a real explanation - like, do they both imply that if you can reproduce the competence mechanically, you've explained it, even if comprehension is missing? I feel like there's a subtle difference between saying "understanding isnt necessary for the result" and saying "understanding isnt necessary for the explanation" but I keep mixing them up, especially when people talk about AI now.
