As a novelist constructing a narrative around a philosopher of science who has spent thirty years watching the same arguments recycle, I find myself returning to the impasse over Goodman's new riddle of induction—the paradox introduced with the predicate grue—and wondering whether it is genuinely intractable or merely mislocated. The dispute, as I understand it, has long divided those who hold that projectible predicates must be anchored in a metaphysics of natural properties from those who hold that projectibility is nothing more than a habit of inference, shaped by practice and language, with no deeper ontological license. More recently, a formalist camp has entered, proposing that Bayesian learning over predicate spaces can dissolve the paradox by treating prior distributions over properties as empirically updatable rather than fixed in advance. My hypothesis is that this is not one debate but three: the metaphysicians and the conventionalists are arguing about what makes induction legitimate, while the formalists are solving a problem of representation that neither camp recognizes as the same question. I want you to commit to a diagnosis—substantive disagreement, verbal confusion about the word projectibility, hidden divergence in what counts as data, or an ill-posed question that should be abandoned—and defend it by reference to what each side actually says. In particular, address whether the Bayesian intervention changes the stakes or simply speaks past both sides. Then tell me, with equal commitment, what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves the paradox, an empirical result about human predicate learning, a formal proof that priors are unavoidable, or an argument that no progress is possible and the stalemate is a feature, not a bug, of the concept of induction itself. Do not lay out the options neutrally; take a position and give your reasons.
