Design an in-class activity for an 80-minute upper-level philosophy seminar of about 14 students on comparative moral philosophy. The goal is to make participants feel why Confucian role ethics treats relational roles (lun) as constitutive of moral identity rather than as applications of prior universal principles, before any lecture names the view.

The activity should be structured so that students first experience obligations emerging specifically from assigned relational positions—parent/child, elder/younger sibling, ruler/subject, friend/peer—rather than from abstract personhood. Second, they should discover that speaking or deciding in universalist language (e.g., “everyone deserves equal treatment regardless of relation”) produces not merely disagreement but a felt social and personal rupture, as if they have violated the conditions of their own standing. Third, they should encounter an assigned “Stranger/Guest” who lacks paired relational obligations and find that including this person requires extending specific role-based care—such as a ruler’s benevolence or an elder’s protection—rather than invoking universal rights.

Provide the exact rules, card-based role assignments, and spatial or token-based layout; a phased timeline (assignment, crisis, negotiation, intervention); and a concrete crisis scenario that forces these conflicts into open negotiation. The debrief should map specific moments onto Confucian concepts—particularly zhengming (rectification of names, where ethical failure is a failure of role-reality alignment) and graded affection (ai you chadeng)—contrasted with Mozi-style universal love (jian ai). It should also state frankly which feature of the view the activity cannot reproduce, specifically the long-term cultivation of virtue (xiushen) and the cosmological embedding of roles in tian, and explain how the facilitator must supply that gap verbally.

Include an honest note on what will predictably fail the first run—such as students breaking linguistic constraints, discomfort with the Stranger’s temporary exclusion, or resistance to treating assigned pairs as morally primary—and how to manage it without collapsing the exercise. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or invented quotations from texts; students should need no prior textual knowledge, and the framing should depend only on widely available conceptual distinctions rather than obscure interpretations. Inject the niche conceptual framing that moral failure here is not cruelty to outsiders but a dissolution of the distinctions that make care meaningful.
