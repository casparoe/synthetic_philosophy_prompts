I have come to accept a position I will call ecological virtue theory: it concedes the situationist finding that global, cross-situational character traits are largely illusory, but insists that virtues remain real as locally stable, ecologically anchored dispositions. On this view, to possess a virtue such as courage or honesty is not to display uniform behavior across contexts, but to maintain a robust, explanation-involving dispositional profile within a specific "moral ecology"—a bounded domain of social roles, emotional framing, and normative scaffolding. The trait is genuinely predictive and evaluatively central within that ecology, even if it fails to generalize, and its absence elsewhere does not undermine its reality inside the domain. Tell me what would genuinely count against this position. I am not looking merely for the standard situationist data; I want to know which specific observations, constructed cases, results from developmental or social psychology, or formal considerations about explanation and prediction it stands or falls with. Which of these defeaters would a committed ecological virtue theorist concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as missing the point? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward. In particular, does the view risk collapsing into either a thin form of situationism, where "virtue" is just a label for local behavioral regularities, or into traditional global-trait virtue ethics, should ecological boundaries turn out porous? And what would have to be true about moral education, blame, or practical reasoning for this middle position to be stable rather than ad hoc? I would personally find it most damaging if the view turned out to be descriptively indistinguishable from its rivals in every testable case, or if it could not account for the phenomenology of moral self-correction across ecological boundaries.
