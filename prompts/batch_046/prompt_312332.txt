I work in software engineering and have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been following the long-running dispute between illusionists and realists about phenomenal consciousness. By illusionists I mean those who hold that conscious experience consists entirely in complex representational and cognitive processes, and that our conviction that experiences possess intrinsic qualitative properties—what it is like to have them—is a widespread misrepresentation produced by introspective mechanisms. By realists I mean those who maintain that phenomenal properties are genuinely instantiated, that they are not reducible to representational or functional states, and that any adequate theory of mind must accommodate them as real features of the world.

For decades the debate has cycled through familiar moves—arguments from conceivability, from introspective authority, from neural correlates, and from the explanatory gap—without either side conceding ground. Both camps appear to accept the same neuroscientific findings and the same logical apparatus. This has led me to a working hypothesis drawn from my own field. In engineering, a system can generate accurate telemetry about its internal states, but it can also emit spurious self-diagnostic messages that mischaracterize ordinary processing as possessing special properties that have no corresponding runtime state. I suspect the illusionist-realist divide may not be primarily about ontology but about the epistemic status of first-person reports: realists treat introspective reports as authoritative logs indicating a distinct runtime state of phenomenal presence, whereas illusionists treat them as unreliable outputs from an introspection module that erroneously tags standard cognitive processing with a qualitative label. More specifically, I wonder whether the hidden disagreement concerns whether phenomenal consciousness requires a dedicated registration mechanism—a distinct neural or functional layer that makes an experience present to the subject—or whether talk of such presence is merely a mislabeled description of representational content being processed.

My question is not for a survey of interpretations. Rather, I want a diagnosis of why the debate has stalled and what kind of disagreement it actually is. Is my engineering analogy apt, or is it a category mistake? Is the dispute verbal—about the meaning of phenomenal or conscious? Are the two sides answering different questions, one about ontology and one about the reliability of introspection, under a single heading? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work—for instance, whether any architecture can implement registration without redundancy, or whether introspection is structurally incapable of the kind of error-correction that would expose illusion? Or is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, comparable to asking whether a running program genuinely feels its inputs?

Please diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually says about introspection, representation, and explanatory adequacy. Offer an honest assessment of whether anything—a conceptual distinction, an experimental result, a formal argument—could actually move the debate, or whether permanent stalemate is the rational expectation. If you think progress is possible, specify what form it would take; if not, explain why the structure of the disagreement makes resolution impossible in principle.
