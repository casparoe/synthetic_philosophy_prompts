As a retired physicist recently working through philosophy of science, I have been trying to formulate a consistent position on transcendental argumentation, and I would like a rigorous audit of my commitments before I present them to a discussion group. My position comprises the following five claims: (1) a modified transcendental argument holds that an objective thermodynamic entropy gradient is a necessary condition for the possibility of empirical measurement, since without distinguishable records and directed causal processes there could be no observation; (2) I am a scientific realist, holding that the entities and structures described by our best physical theories exist mind-independently; (3) I deny that transcendental arguments establish synthetic a priori truths; they expose only scheme-relative conceptual conditions that would dissolve if our conceptual framework were fundamentally revised; (4) I accept that the fundamental dynamical laws are time-reversible and contain no intrinsic arrow of time; and (5) I insist that scientific realism requires treating these time-symmetric fundamental descriptions as literally true, not as instrumental conveniences. Which pairs of these commitments are genuinely incompatible, which conflicts are merely apparent and dissolve on closer inspection, and how severely should each tension be ranked? For each genuine conflict, indicate the least costly revision, and identify the single change that would eliminate the greatest number of problems. In particular, address whether (3) undermines the force of (1), and whether (2) combined with (4) forces me to abandon the premise in (1).
