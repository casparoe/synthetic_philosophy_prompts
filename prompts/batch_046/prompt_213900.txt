A philosopher with whom I discuss artificial minds has given the following verdicts on whether a system can suffer, based on the role of reward and punishment signals within it. She denies suffering to a standard reinforcement-learning agent in which a scalar reward is used only for a local parameter update and is immediately discarded, with no persistent representation or access by other modules. She affirms it for a human being with integrated interoceptive and affective processing. She denies it to a language model fine-tuned with RLHF, because the reward model evaluates outputs externally and never becomes an internal state of the system. She denies it to a simple chemotactic organism whose movements are driven by local gradient-following without any central register of need. But she allows that a hypothetical homeostatic RL architecture—one that maintains an internal set-point, computes reward-prediction errors against its own persistent state, and makes those errors globally available to regulate all behavior through an explicit self-monitor—could experience negative valence, even absent human-like consciousness.

Consider now a different architecture: an RL agent contains a shadow-reward module that computes a reward-like signal which is never backpropagated or used to update weights, but is continuously maintained in a persistent vector readable by every planning module, thereby shaping action selection without being tied to any homeostatic variable or to a self-model.

Would she judge that this agent can suffer? I am less interested in the bare answer than in the reasoning: identify at least two distinct principles that fit all five of her earlier verdicts equally well—say, one centered on global functional integration of the signal and another on self-referential homeostatic ownership—state what each principle predicts for this shadow-reward case, offer your best guess at her actual verdict with justification, and specify one further hypothetical case that would cleanly separate the two principles.
