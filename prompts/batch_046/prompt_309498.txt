I have been reflecting on whether program equilibrium offers a genuinely distinct normative framework for rational commitment, particularly when we extend it from individual agents to collective institutional bodies. In the standard conceptualization, an agent selects a computer program that determines her action, and equilibrium obtains when no party can benefit by substituting her program, given that the programs themselves are transparent to all participants. The familiar result in Newcomb-like scenarios tracks one-boxing, but the underlying mechanism—common knowledge of the program's source code—depends on conditions of perfect transparency that are rarely satisfied in real-world predictive environments or in large-scale bureaucratic institutions. This has led me to wonder whether the framework is genuinely a third path between causal and evidential decision theory, or merely an elegant redescription of one of them under highly idealized conditions.

Here is the precise compound claim on which I want your credence, expressed as a number between 0 and 1: If we apply the program-equilibrium framework to collective agents—specifically, to a legislative body that binds itself through an algorithmically transparent fiscal rule (for instance, an automatic budget-balancing formula whose source code is public, immutable, and interpretable without discretionary mediation)—then constitutional entrenchment of such rules should, in principle, eliminate the time-inconsistency problem of democratic deficit spending. Moreover, the empirical failure of actual balanced-budget amendments and automatic stabilizer commitments is best explained not by rational defection of the sort predicted by standard political-business-cycle models, but by epistemic opacity in implementation: the rules are not truly transparent programs but black-box bureaucratic procedures subject to strategic interpretation and hidden amendment.
