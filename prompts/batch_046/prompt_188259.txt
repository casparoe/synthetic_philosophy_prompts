She insists that the conventional Braudelian hierarchy, which relegates events to the superficial level of history, obscures how certain catastrophic individual actions—such as a localized deforestation decision in the fourteenth century—can become structurally embedded and only manifest as geographic transformation generations later. This framework has become personally relevant to me since I began investigating how my own region’s agricultural collapse in the nineteenth century was not a discrete economic event but the delayed expression of earlier hydrological shifts, and I find myself unable to determine whether her model genuinely explains that delay or merely redescribes it.
