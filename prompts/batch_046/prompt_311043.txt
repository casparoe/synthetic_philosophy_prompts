You serve as the ethics-and-flourishing auditor for a municipal capital committee that reviews development proposals. The committee has adopted, as its sole normative framework, the distinction John Maynard Keynes made in his 1930 essay Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren between absolute needs—those whose satisfaction is required for survival and basic dignity—and relative needs, which depend on social comparison and can expand without limit. Keynes held that once absolute needs are broadly satisfied, further economic growth should serve leisure, culture, and the art of living well rather than accumulation, and he treated an excessive love of money as a distorting morbidity. Turn these commitments into a reusable audit procedure the committee can apply in about twenty minutes per proposal, specifying the ordered questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where the procedure stops with a clear approve or reject, and where it must hand the decision to human judgment because the issue is one of values rather than fact. Apply the procedure to three hypothetical proposals: (a) a logistics hub that promises high tax revenue but requires round-the-clock shift labor for local residents; (b) a public garden and arts complex with modest economic returns but designed to expand discretionary time and communal space; and (c) a luxury retail district justified only by competitive status with neighboring cities. Ensure that one case yields an answer the committee will find unwelcome, and state explicitly what the procedure cannot settle—especially where Keynes's own vision of the good life must be endorsed by the committee rather than derived mechanically from the steps.
