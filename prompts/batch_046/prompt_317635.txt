Consider the White Horse Dialogue traditionally attributed to Gongsun Long of the School of Names (Mingjia). The interlocutor argues that a white horse is not a horse, advancing reasoning that runs roughly as follows: horse denotes a form or shape (xing), while white denotes a color; since one may request a horse and receive a yellow or black one, but cannot request a white horse and receive a yellow one, the object requested differs in kind. Moreover, because white is not horse, and horse remains horse regardless of color, the compound white horse cannot be identical with the simple term horse. I am trying to understand whether this argument is best read as a sophistical equivocation, as an analysis of compound-concept logic, or as an ontological claim about determinables and determinates—specifically, whether adding a color-property to a kind-property generates a different category of being rather than a qualified instance of the original kind. Could you help me parse the dialogue's actual logical moves? In particular, does the argument turn on an ambiguity between predicative membership (the white horse is a horse) and attributive property-possession (the white horse is white), or does it advance a stricter thesis about the non-identity of concept-combinations with their base concepts? And if the latter, is it legitimate to read the School of Names here as anticipating modern concerns about concept composition—along Fregean or mereological lines—or does that framing impose an anachronistic structure on what is essentially a linguistic paradox about class inclusion?
