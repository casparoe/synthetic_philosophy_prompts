I have been working through a tension in the philosophy of history that I am calling mnemonic sovereignty: the claim that a community may possess legitimate epistemic authority over its own collective memory, even when that memory conflicts with the documentary record, because the memory functions as a performative foundation for the community's moral and institutional life rather than as a mere historical report. In a recent seminar discussion with colleagues, I found myself arguing that historians who uncover contradictory archival evidence should sometimes withhold or contextualize their findings in ways that preserve the narrative's functional role, but my interlocutors objected that this collapses the distinction between history and propaganda and undermines the moral standing of institutions justified by that memory.

Rather than asking for a bibliography or citation list—since I want to work through the conceptual structure directly—please describe the most important implications of taking mnemonic sovereignty seriously as a philosophical position. Specifically, address what it means for the professional obligations of historians who belong to the community in question; for the legitimacy of political or ethical institutions that derive their authority from the collective memory rather than from the corrected record; and for whether recognizing such sovereignty for one community creates an unacceptable precedent that would erode shared epistemic standards across different societies. I am especially interested in whether there is a principled way to limit this sovereignty so that it applies only to memories that are genuinely performative—those that become binding ethical truths through collective enactment—without extending it to every convenient fiction.
