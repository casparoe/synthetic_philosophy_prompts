As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am drafting a feature on how nineteenth-century critiques of publicity are being revived in contemporary analyses of digital attention economies. In the course of reporting, a colleague offered me this summary of Kierkegaard’s position, which I am considering quoting in the piece. The summary reads: Kierkegaard held that the press and the public constituted one continuous totalitarian apparatus that dissolved individual moral responsibility. The press functioned, he claimed, as an attention-market that commodified the reader’s reflexive consciousness for advertisers, thereby producing what he termed leveling—the collapse of every qualitative distinction into quantitative comparison. Since the crowd represents untruth, and since the press generates the crowd, Kierkegaard allegedly concluded that authentic Christian inwardness could not survive in any society that permitted an independent press; consequently, he treated the abolition of newspapers as a necessary condition for genuine faith. Before I include this in my article, I need a careful accuracy audit. Which elements are faithful to Kierkegaard’s actual arguments in his literary review of modernity and his later retrospective on authorship—particularly regarding abstraction, reflexivity, and the distinction between the crowd and the public? Which elements conflate separate claims he kept apart, import modern economic concepts he never employed, or present a standard objection as a direct conclusion? And does the inference that a free press is structurally incompatible with genuine inwardness actually follow from the premises he endorsed, or is it an overstatement that collapses under correction?
