I facilitate a 90-minute discussion group for about eight software engineers with no formal philosophy training. Design a runnable activity that makes us feel the pull of the boundary problem—who belongs to the demos—before anyone names it. Frame it as negotiating governance rules for an open-source protocol: anyone can submit code, but only some participants vote on changes that affect all users. Assign roles—core maintainer, occasional contributor, downstream user affected by updates, an automated bot that submits more patches than any human, and a fork seeking re-integration—then give the group a real, small decision (e.g., which of two actual snacks to order, with only “members” voting) and fifteen minutes to write admission criteria under time pressure. Arrange the rules and roles so contradictions emerge organically: they will likely begin with contribution-based criteria, then collide with the bot’s volume, the affected user’s lack of commits, and the fork’s ambiguous territorial status. Afterward, provide a debrief that maps each crisis onto a standard boundary argument—affected interests, democratic autonomy, presence or membership—notes the predictable failure modes (e.g., the group defaults to meritocracy and ignores the affected user; the bot forces them into arbitrary thresholds), and is explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce—such as future generations or non-human stakeholders—that I must supply verbally. Keep instructions practical and avoid any request for bibliographies or named theorists.
