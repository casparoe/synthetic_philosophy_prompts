I defend a reconstructed version of Anscombe's critique of the honorary degree awarded to Harry Truman: stripped of its theological framing, the argument holds that an institution's conferral of an honorary doctorate functions as an act of moral endorsement, and that this endorsement is conceptually incompatible with honoring an agent who directly intended the killing of non-combatants as a means to a strategic end. The wrong, on this view, is not merely Truman's but the institution's failure to maintain coherence between its own stated values—exemplary moral and intellectual achievement—and the object of its honor. I want an honest map of what would count against this position. Which premises about institutional intentionality, direct versus oblique intention applied to collective agents, or the propositional content of ceremonial honors are most vulnerable? Would the view collapse under a purely consequentialist account of institutional recognition, or if we deny that honorary degrees carry any moral-evaluative content? There is also an asymmetry worry: if direct intention to civilian harm disqualifies Truman, does the principle also disqualify virtually every modern head of state whose policies foreseeably caused civilian deaths, and is that generalization fatal or merely awkward? Rank the defeaters from fatal—those that reveal internal incoherence or collapse into an implausibly sweeping prohibition—to merely awkward qualifications. I am particularly interested in whether critics can show that institutions, lacking individual consciousness, cannot bear the kind of intentionality required for the direct/oblique distinction to apply to them at all, and whether that undermines the argument without rescuing Truman. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then develop the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
