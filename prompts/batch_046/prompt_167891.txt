I'm working through the case studies from Dr. Alina Voss's graduate seminar on gradated moral status, in which she assigns entities to one of four tiers—full standing (Tier 1), substantial protected interest (Tier 2), minimal consideration (Tier 3), or negligible moral weight (Tier 4). Her judgments so far are these: an adult human and an adult chimpanzee both fall in Tier 1; a domestic pig and a common octopus both fall in Tier 2; an individual worker honeybee and a functioning hive of fifty thousand bees treated as a collective foraging agent both fall in Tier 3; and an earthworm, a standard PID-feedback thermostat, and a present-day large language model running inference all fall in Tier 4.

Next week we are considering a synthetic system I will call a distributed consensus mesh: ten thousand simple processing nodes arranged without any central controller, linked by local communication rules that produce emergent collective problem-solving no single node can achieve, with no biological substrate, no evolutionary history of selection for valenced states, and no architecture analogous to nociception or reward. I want to know whether Dr. Voss would place this mesh in Tier 3 alongside the bees, or in Tier 4 alongside the thermostat and the language model; but more importantly I want you to articulate at least two distinct principles that could jointly explain her existing tier assignments—say, one that makes valence-bearing integration necessary and one that makes substrate-independent causal integration sufficient—say what each principle predicts for the mesh, identify which of her past verdicts each principle explains least comfortably, state which prediction you think is best and why, and finally specify a further hypothetical case that would sharply distinguish the two principles.
