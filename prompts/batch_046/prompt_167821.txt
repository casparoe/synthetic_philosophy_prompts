I spent most of last night arguing with my partner in the venture about whether we should commission an independent audit of our tutoring application, which uses an automated writing evaluator to provide feedback to students in under-resourced schools. We have scattered, unverified reports that the system penalizes non-standard dialects more harshly than standard ones, but no rigorous data. She insists we must run the audit immediately, arguing that to continue operating without knowing is morally indistinguishable from willful harm, and that the obligation to gather information is unconditional. I countered that if the audit reveals serious bias, we will be legally and ethically compelled to withdraw the product, which we lack the capital to rebuild, and that the students who currently depend on it will lose access with no replacement available. I also argued that since we could not act on the findings except by destroying the service, seeking the information serves no practical purpose and may cause net harm. She dismissed this as self-interested rationalization. I am genuinely unsure where the line falls. Is there a coherent philosophical principle that distinguishes legitimate strategic ignorance from culpable avoidance of knowledge? More specifically, is there an account—perhaps from decision theory or ethics—that permits, or even recommends, not seeking information when acquiring it would force a destructive choice that one lacks the means to remedy? And how would that account apply to our case, where the potential victims of ignorance are precisely the ones who would also suffer from the knowledge?
