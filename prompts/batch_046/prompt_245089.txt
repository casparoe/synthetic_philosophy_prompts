During a recent graduate seminar on American pragmatism, a fellow student rejected the idea of a mind-independent correspondence relation by invoking the slogan "truth is what works." She seemed to treat this as a knock-down argument: if a belief successfully guides action and produces desired outcomes, then asking whether it also "corresponds" to reality is either redundant or meaningless. I am skeptical that the slogan does that work, but I cannot articulate the ambiguity. Could you separate the precise claims hiding in "truth is what works," arranged from the weakest (tautological or definitional) to the strongest (a revisionary metaphysics of truth)? For each reading, specify what "works" denotes—predictive success, psychological satisfaction, moral or social utility, or convergence in inquiry—and say whether that reading is compatible with scientific realism, whether it distinguishes truth from warranted assertibility, and whether it actually supports dismissing correspondence theories rather than merely supplementing them. In particular, contrast Peirce's long-run convergence view with James's account of practical "cash value," and identify which of these, if either, is being invoked when the slogan is used to end an argument about whether successful false beliefs are possible. I want the result as a menu of interpretations with verdicts, not a single defended reading, so that I can identify exactly which thesis needs defense in our discussion.
