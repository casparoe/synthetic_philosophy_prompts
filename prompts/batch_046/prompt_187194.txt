I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and there's this occurance in policy debates that's been bugging me. People — experts, pundits, sometimes my own editors — will dismiss a study or trial by saying "even if it came back the other way, I wouldn't change my recommendation, so it's not worth the cost." They frame it like a hard-nosed cost-benefit rule: information only has value if it can actually flip your decision. But then the same person will grab onto a shaky preliminary result that supports them and treat it like decisive weight, even though they just finished arguing they're already past the point where new evidence should matter. I've seen it in climate policy, vaccine discussions, and energy debates; it's not random hypocrisy, it feels like a specific rhetorical move that borrows the formal-sounding logic of decision-theory and uses it as a one-way shield.

Is there an established name for this exact asymmetry — applying the "wouldn't change my mind" filter only to data that threatens your view? Or am I just describing motivated reasoning with extra steps? If there's no precise term, can you coin one and define it with real boundary cases: how it differs from plain confirmation bias, from strategic ambiguity, and where its edges are? I care more about the carving than the label.
