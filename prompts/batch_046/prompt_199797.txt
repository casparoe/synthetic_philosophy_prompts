I'm delivering the opening address at an interdisciplinary conference on moral psychology and institutional design, attended by philosophers of emotion, legal theorists, cultural anthropologists, and several policymakers who are not academics. The audience is split between specialists familiar with debates on reactive attitudes and practitioners who need to understand why the distinction matters for actual institutional reform. The conference is explicitly asking whether guilt is the better moral emotion compared to shame, and—more controversially—whether a democratic culture can rationally choose one over the other through constitutional-level design rather than leaving the matter to historical contingency.

Please write a twenty-minute speech that takes a definite stance on both questions and does real structural work rather than serving as decorative framing. Structure the argument around one central conceptual distinction: repair directed at a specific wrong and oriented toward future action, versus repair directed at the self's social standing and oriented toward visibility and status. The speech should not dismiss shame as primitive, nor should it celebrate guilt as purely rational or culturally neutral. Instead, defend the view that guilt is the more defensible moral emotion for a pluralistic society, but argue that a culture cannot cleanly choose guilt without simultaneously constructing the social conditions—strict privacy norms, restorative rather than display-oriented justice practices, and educational frameworks focused on conduct rather than identity—that make guilt psychologically stable. Without those supporting conditions, attempting to institutionalize guilt collapses into a performative version of the very shame it seeks to abolish.

To test this claim, include one concrete, somewhat speculative scenario: imagine a democratic society that, following a national commission, amends its public education curriculum and restorative justice protocols to suppress shame-based rituals and amplify guilt-based mechanisms, treating this emotional architecture as an explicit cultural choice comparable to constitutional language rights. Use the scenario to examine whether such a choice is coherent, just, and sustainable over generations, or whether it secretly depends on the status-monitoring mechanisms of shame that it claims to have eliminated. The speech must land for the experts in the room—who will notice if the distinction between agent-focused repair and status-focused repair is blurred or if the social conditions for guilt are treated as afterthoughts—without losing the non-specialists, who should leave with a clear sense of why this is not merely an academic taxonomy but a question about how a society organizes its moral life. Reach a clear conclusion on both the normative superiority of guilt and the possibility of genuine cultural choice, and justify it within the speech itself. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not quote any specific text verbatim.
