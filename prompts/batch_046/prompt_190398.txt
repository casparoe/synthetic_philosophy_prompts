As I have recently taken on a mediating role within a professional collective, I have heard colleagues invoke Adam Smith’s account of sympathy through a mirror analogy: they claim that sympathizing is simply reflecting another person’s emotional state back onto oneself, that this reflection allows one to calibrate one’s own response, and that the impartial spectator is therefore only the aggregate polished surface of these individual reflections, possessing no independent normative authority. From this they conclude that Smithian ethics amounts to emotional mimicry without critical distance. I want to audit this analogy rigorously. Please lay out the correspondence table—what plays the role of the mirror, the reflected image, the reflective surface, and the observing judge—then identify the precise points where the mapping fractures, especially in light of Smith’s view that sympathy involves a judgment of propriety rather than a replication of feeling, and his distinction between sympathizing with an agent’s immediate sentiments and sympathizing with the reactive sentiments of those affected by the agent’s conduct. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion—that the spectator lacks authority beyond collective emotional echo—survives once these disanalogies are made explicit, and whether a different analogy better captures how sympathy and the spectator actually function together in Smith’s framework.
