Exam question:
In their well-known critique of evolutionary explanation, Gould and Lewontin attacked what they called the "Panglossian paradigm"—the tendency to treat every observable trait as the product of optimal adaptation and to construct just-so stories without testing alternatives. Consider the following hypothetical finding: in the deep-sea anglerfish Linophryne, the illicium (the modified dorsal spine that bears the bioluminescent lure) exhibits a pronounced left-handed torsion in roughly 94% of specimens. Team A argues that this torsion is an adaptation that improves signal dispersion in left-to-right hydrothermal vent currents. Team B argues it is a spandrel—an unavoidable structural byproduct of the developmental pathway that elongates the spine—though it may now be co-opted for signaling.

In your answer: (1) Explain what Gould and Lewontin's critique actually targets, being careful not to misrepresent their stance toward natural selection itself. (2) Define a spandrel precisely and distinguish it from an exaptation. (3) Assess whether experimental evidence of current functional utility—say, improved signaling efficiency in turbulent flow—would by itself settle the historical question in favor of the adaptationist account over the spandrel account.

Student answer:
Gould and Lewontin's 1979 essay criticized the Panglossian paradigm, which is basically the idea that every trait is perfectly adapted and that natural selection explains all morphology. They think evolutionary biologists invent adaptive stories without enough evidence, treating organisms like optimally engineered machines. They do not deny that natural selection operates, but they think it is overused as an explanation.

A spandrel is a trait that originally evolved for one purpose and was then co-opted for a different one; it is not initially adaptive. That distinguishes it from an exaptation, which is... actually spandrels and exaptations are essentially the same category, since both involve co-option. The important point is that spandrels begin as non-adaptive byproducts of development or architecture.

In the Linophryne case, Team B claims the torsion is just a byproduct of spine elongation. But if researchers can show that the left-handed twist genuinely improves bioluminescent signaling in vent currents, then natural selection would preserve and refine it. Once you have measured functional benefit, the spandrel explanation becomes redundant, because selection is clearly acting on the trait now. Therefore Team A is correct: the current utility demonstrates adaptive origin, and Gould and Lewontin's warning about just-so stories does not apply when there is direct experimental proof of benefit. Even if the torsion started as a developmental side effect, its present optimization means the adaptationist story is the right one.
