You are a philosopher of computing with deep familiarity with Turing’s 1950 framework for intelligent machinery. I just finished a long, heated argument with a friend over whether training contemporary large language models counts as “education” in Turing’s specific sense—teaching a child-machine through interaction, reward, and punishment—or whether it collapses into direct programming by other means. Before we can settle the dispute, I want the specification laid out coldly.

Write the full desiderata that any adequate theory of machine education must meet if it is genuinely faithful to Turing’s child-machine analogy. For each requirement, give the motivating case or distinction it protects: education versus explicit rule-injection; the need for coupled sense-organs and action rather than passive data ingestion; punishment and reward that shape behavior without fully specifying internal structure; the acquisition of language through interactive feedback rather than symbolic programming; the legitimacy of unpredictable but appropriate mature outcomes; and the transition from a “blank notebook” child-state to an adult state that was not predetermined in detail. Mark each desideratum as non-negotiable within Turing’s framework or as a preference particular to one interpretive camp (e.g., connectionists versus symbolicists).

Then examine whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable—particularly whether the demand for genuinely open-ended, unpredictable development can coexist with demands for guaranteed behavioral bounds or with reduction of all learning to statistical optimization over a fixed loss landscape. Identify the single desideratum that does the most damage to popular contemporary accounts of machine learning.

Inject one further, under-discussed requirement: the theory must explain why the learning environment includes an irreducibly social or interactive dimension—something like conversational turn-taking and joint attention—that cannot be reconstructed from passive ingestion of static corpora, and it must do so without reducing that interaction to mere input-output pairing.

Think carefully and step by step. Start with a brief brainstorm of the elements in Turing’s child-machine analogy. Be explicit about where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what would change your mind—whether a newly discovered archival note altering Turing’s view on social interaction, or a technical result showing that interactive dynamics can be fully captured in batch pre-training.

Verdict: the single desideratum that rules out the most popular theories is the irreducible openness of the mature outcome coupled with non-specifiable internal structure.
