I'm preparing notes for an interdisciplinary seminar on the ethics of dramatizing real people, and I want to check whether my current package of commitments actually holds together before I defend it. My position is roughly this: (a) I think we owe the dead a negative narrative constraint — a biopic or historical fiction should not attribute to a real, named figure interior motives or values that flatly contradict their well-documented commitments, since that misrepresents the historical record in a way that harms both the dead's legacy and the audience's understanding; (b) I also hold that once someone has been dead for roughly a century and a half, this negative constraint largely dissolves, leaving only an ordinary epistemic duty not to deceive viewers about established facts; (c) at the same time, I maintain that inventing private psychological states — inner monologues, unrecorded emotional reactions — is always a form of fiction with no special moral weight, because consciousness is in principle inaccessible and all such portrayal is equally speculative; (d) nevertheless, I believe that depicting documented suffering primarily for audience gratification or dramatic spectacle is a form of instrumentalization that remains wrong regardless of how long ago the person died, since it does not depend on the dead being able to experience harm; and (e) I want to apply a counterfactual-authorization standard — a portrayal is permissible only if we have reason to believe the subject, knowing artistic conventions and their own biography, would have consented to it.

These five points feel right individually, but I'm not sure they form a consistent set. I can see an immediate friction between (c) and (a): if all interior portrayal is equally speculative fiction, then attributing incompatible motives is just another fictional choice, not a distinctive betrayal of the dead's legacy. There's also tension between (b) and (d): if the moral obligation to the dead decays with time, leaving only epistemic duties, why does the prohibition on instrumentalizing suffering survive that decay? And (e) seems to sit awkwardly with both (a) and (b) — counterfactual consent requires knowing the subject's values, yet (b) suggests those values lose normative pull over time, while (a) treats documented values as permanently binding in one respect. That said, I suspect some of these conflicts are only apparent. For instance, (a) might concern misrepresentation of the public record rather than access to private consciousness, which could allow it to coexist with (c). Please audit the full set: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank the real tensions by severity, specify for each what the least costly revision would entail and what it would sacrifice, and then recommend the single change that would resolve the most inconsistencies with the least damage to my core concerns. Be specific about which commitments pull against which, and do not treat all conflicts as equally serious.
