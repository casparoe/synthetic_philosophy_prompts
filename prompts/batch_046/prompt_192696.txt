There's this split you see in a lot of administrative systems: officials and the people they regulate exchange standardized seasonal gifts—think set-price envelopes or ceremonial boxes—that are technically banned by the anti-corruption code copied from Western models, but in practice everyone knows the price range and treats it as maintaining harmony rather than buying a specific permit. Meanwhile the formal audit rules say any private benefit to a public servant is corruption unless it's trivial. So the lived practice seems to run on the idea that obligations can be relational and non-transactional, and that blurring public and private isn't automatically wrong if it's reciprocal and visible. What philosophical commitments does that practice preseuppose about the nature of office—does it need a clientelist view, or a non-liberal ontology where roles carry inherent reciprocal duties? Is it consistent with the formal system's universalist claim that corruption is boundary-crossing regardless of culture? And if you rebuilt the audit rules around the practice's implicit theory instead of importing the Western standard, what would change—would you legalize ritual gifts but regulate reciprocity, or abandon the idea of one global definition altogether? I want to see where the commitments land, not a bibliography.
