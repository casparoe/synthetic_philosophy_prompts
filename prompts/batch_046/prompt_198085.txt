I have been reading about the problem of universals without any formal philosophical training, and recently a knowledgeable acquaintance summarized for me what she called a structural resemblance account. According to her, this view denies that there is any single universal property—like redness or roundness—that is literally present in many different objects. Instead, objects belong to kinds only because their particular, non-shared properties stand in resemblance relations that form a network or structure. She argued that this solves the classical one-over-many difficulty, since no single entity needs to be in multiple locations at once; resemblance is just a relational fact among particulars. She further claimed that on this account, two objects that share every particular property must be identical, which resolves the problem of indiscernibles without appealing to universals or bare particulars. Finally, she proposed that this philosophical picture maps cleanly onto clustering algorithms in data science, where individual data points are nodes and similarity metrics are edges, suggesting that resemblance nominalism is not just metaphysically parsimonious but computationally tractable. I am unsure which of these claims belong to genuine philosophical positions and which are extensions or distortions she added. Could you separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what she has likely misrepresented? In particular, do resemblance nominalists actually avoid the one-over-many problem in this way, or do they still face the objection that resemblance itself functions like a universal? And is the analogy to graph clustering philosophically illuminating, or does it confuse a metaphysical claim with a practical tool?
