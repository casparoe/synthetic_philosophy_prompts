I'm drafting a post for a rationalist forum on whether everyday conventions—tipping, queueing, jaywalking—generate genuine moral obligations and whether visitors must observe local versions. I want a formal taxonomy tree that sorts positions by the answers they give to concrete diagnostic questions, not by the labels they prefer. Start with the root split: when someone violates the convention, is the harm they cause conceptually independent of the convention (e.g., physical risk, coordination failure that could be solved another way), or is the harm constituted entirely by breaking the shared expectation itself? From each side, branch on whether an outsider's ignorance or lack of participation in the local social contract defeats the obligation, weakens it, or leaves it fully intact. Then branch on whether the obligation's content is indexed to the local descriptive norm, the local legal code, or a universal harm-calculus that merely varies in application. At every leaf, provide either a standard philosophical or political-theory name if one genuinely fits, or mark the position unnamed, and in one sentence describe its most awkward commitment—for instance, requiring travelers to memorize local queue norms before entering a grocery store, or treating jaywalking in Tokyo and Berlin as morally identical despite radically different risk and visibility profiles. Use these concrete separation cases: a tourist in Japan who fails to queue for a train, a local who cuts the same line, a visitor in a mandatory-tipping culture who refuses on principle, and a local who jaywalks when no vehicles are present. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate on these cases. Also identify at least one logically open combination of answers—especially the view that accepts convention-constituted harm but denies any binding force on visitors regardless of visibility or risk—that your tree leaves unoccupied, and briefly diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, instability, or mere neglect. Frame every node as a direct question a reader could answer without knowing in advance which philosophical position it corresponds to.
