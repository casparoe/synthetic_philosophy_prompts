I hold a modified finitist view: concrete reality never contains an actual infinite plurality, only potential ones, but I add that Hilbert's hotel commits a category mistake rather than revealing a paradox. In concrete domains, part-whole relations are metaphysically primitive while cardinal comparison is derivative, so applying one-to-one mappings between a proper subset and the whole misapplies formal arithmetic to open-ended pluralities. Cantor's theorem governs well-defined sets in formal systems, not concrete collections lacking definite totalities. What would genuinely count against this position? Rank the defeaters by decisiveness: formal results that compel cardinal arithmetic onto concrete plurality; arguments that my part-whole priority collapses into contradiction or renders size-talk impossible; physical or cosmological findings that require actually infinite structures; and conceptual pressures that force me to concede "potential infinity" is indistinguishable from "actual infinity in practice." I want an honest assessment of whether defenders can always retreat to the concrete/abstract distinction in a way that insulates the view, whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a metaphysical claim of this kind, and which objections even a committed proponent should concede as damaging rather than dismissible.
