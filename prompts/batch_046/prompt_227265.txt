Having spent four decades in experimental physics and only recently turned to philosophy of science, I find myself with strong but unsystematic judgments about whether scientific taxonomies correspond to real joints in nature. I am not asking for a historical survey, a bibliography, or citations of particular philosophers; I want to know whether my intuitions cohere around a defensible principle, and precisely where they must be revised.

Here are my verdicts, case by case.

First, gold—element 79, with its natural mixture of isotopes—is unambiguously a natural kind. The isotopic composition varies by sample, yet nothing about the variation changes the kind membership. By contrast, heavy water (D2O) still belongs to the kind water; the substitution of deuterium for protium does not create a new substance, only an isotopically heavy variant.

Second, the mammalian class Mammalia strikes me as genuinely carved at the joints, despite the monotremes' egg-laying. The shared reproductive physiology and deep evolutionary history seem to mark a real boundary, not a conventional one.

Third, synthetic superheavy nuclei—those with atomic numbers well beyond fermium, produced only in accelerators and decaying within milliseconds—leave me uncertain, but leaning toward denial. They possess a definite proton count and predictable electronic structure, yet they have no stable presence in natural processes, no ecological or geochemical role, and exist only as laboratory artifacts. I am inclined to say they are not natural kinds, even though their atomic number is precise.

Fourth, the salamander populations arranged in a ring around California's Central Valley present a different challenge. Adjacent populations interbreed freely, but the two terminal populations, where the ring closes, are reproductively isolated. Here I judge that there is no single natural kind encompassing the entire ring; rather, the taxonomic unit fragments into locally interbreeding populations. The boundary is not a hidden joint but a gradual transition.

Fifth, the category "planet," particularly as applied to Pluto after the 2006 reclassification involving an orbit-clearing criterion, does not strike me as a natural kind. The criterion is dynamical and admits of clear borderline cases; it seems to reflect a conventional decision about dynamical dominance rather than a discontinuity in nature.

Sixth, despite the fuzziness introduced by brown dwarfs, I still hold that "star" denotes a natural kind. Sustained hydrogen fusion defines a physically real regime, and the boundary with substellar objects, though gradational, tracks a genuine causal discontinuity in stellar physics.

Seventh, functional ecological roles—such as "keystone species," exemplified by sea otters in kelp-forest dynamics—are not natural kinds. The role is relational and can be occupied by different taxa in different ecosystems.

Eighth, "microplastic"—defined administratively as polymer particles smaller than five millimeters—strikes me as particularly troublesome. The size threshold is arbitrary, the polymer chemistry is heterogeneous, and yet these particles share distinctive causal powers: bioaccumulation, adsorption of pollutants, and resistance to degradation. I keep reversing my judgment: sometimes it feels like an administrative convenience, other times like a causally unified kind analogous to "heavy metal."

The tension I cannot easily resolve is this. I deny that synthetic superheavy elements are natural kinds despite their precise atomic structure, yet I affirm that gold is a kind despite isotopic variation, and I deny that "planet" is a kind despite Pluto's definite physical properties. Meanwhile, I affirm "star" despite brown-dwarf gradation. So origin and stability seem to matter for synthetic nuclei but not for isotopes; conventional boundaries seem fatal for planets but not for stars.

What principle, if any, captures these eight judgments? I want a precise formulation—whether a single criterion or a small family of conditions—stating for each clause which of the above cases motivates it. The principle should deliver clear verdicts on cases I have not mentioned: for instance, whether "dark matter" (defined only by gravitational effect, with unknown particle nature) counts as a natural kind; whether a gene defined purely by its functional effect in one organism but lacking sequence homology in another belongs to one kind; and whether "quantum entanglement" is a kind of correlation or merely a descriptive label for a statistical relation.

Please also state honestly which of my eight verdicts your principle must overturn, and whether saving all eight requires a principle so complex or ad hoc that it should be rejected in favor of a simpler account that sacrifices one judgment—particularly my oscillating view on microplastics or my asymmetry between planets and stars.
