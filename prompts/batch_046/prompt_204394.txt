I'm putting together a six-month fellowship proposal for a conceptual research project, and I'd like to sharpen the framing before I submit it. The domain is universality and epistemic dominance, specifically through what I'm calling the problem of ascription universality: the idea that when we attribute a mental state, a normative commitment, or a rational standing to an agent, we implicitly commit ourselves to treating that agent as falling under universal constraints—constraints that should apply across all relevantly similar contexts. I'm interested in whether this universality actually undermines epistemic dominance or merely relocates it. More precisely: if two communities use the same universal framework to ascribe incompatible beliefs or normative statuses, does the disagreement necessarily become hierarchical—one side correctly applying the universal norm, the other failing—or can there be genuine cross-ascription conflict that preserves parity? The existing literature tends to treat universality either as flattening all difference into a single framework, which makes dominance inevitable, or as a purely formal feature of logic that leaves dominance unexplained. What neither side addresses well is the ascription gap: the moment when the universal commitment embedded in attribution conflicts with the local conditions under which attribution is actually made.

The project would be philosophical rather than empirical—no fieldwork, no surveys—because the open question is logical and normative, not anthropological. I want to analyze the conditions of successful ascription: what must be true about an agent for us to consistently attribute a belief or a wrongness to them, and what happens to that consistency when the agent operates within a framework that denies the applicability of our universal quantifier. Rather than asking whether culture A is right and culture B is wrong, I want to model three possible structures of conflict—category error, scope disagreement, and normative inversion—and determine for each whether it entails that one community occupies an epistemically dominant position. The approach differs from the obvious alternatives: I'm not proposing a comparative study of belief systems, nor am I defending a global relativism that denies universality altogether. Instead, I'm testing whether universality itself can be disaggregated into formal and substantive components, and whether only the substantive component carries the risk of dominance.

A useful deliverable would be a working taxonomy with clear failure conditions: if all cross-ascription conflicts reduce to empirical disagreements about non-normative facts, then universality has no distinctive philosophical work to do and the project fails. If, however, we can isolate conflicts that are genuinely structural—arising from the quantifier conditions rather than from missing information—then we will have shown that epistemic dominance is not an inevitable byproduct of universal ascription but depends on a specific, contestable move within it. The scope is realistic for six months: a 40-page conceptual paper mapping the taxonomy, a workshop presentation testing the framework against two constructed cases, and a short methodological appendix explaining how the analysis applies to contemporary debates about rationality attribution without requiring citation of specific ethnographic studies. What I need is feedback on whether this framing is both tractable and distinct enough from standard discussions of moral relativism or epistemic humility to justify the fellowship.
