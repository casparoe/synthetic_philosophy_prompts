I am a journalist covering science and ideas, and I am preparing a private ninety-minute session for a reading group of policy journalists and science communicators, none of whom have formal philosophical training. The subject is Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism, together with his observation that in certain acts of rescue there can be one thought too many — an intrusive layer of moral reasoning that undermines the immediacy of the act.

Rather than explaining the ideas to me, please plan how I should teach them. First, set out the dependency structure: which conceptual element must be established before the integrity objection can be grasped, and in what order should the components — ground projects, the demands of impartial maximisation, and the alienation that results — be introduced?

Second, propose a carrying example drawn from investigative or scientific practice, not the familiar scenario of a husband saving his wife. The example must display the structural feature Williams requires — a ground project that an impartial calculus would require the agent to sacrifice — and it must also possess the emotional immediacy that makes the idea of an intrusive moral thought intelligible. Specify what the example must include and what it must avoid.

Third, map the misconceptions. I anticipate that the group will initially take integrity for mere professional consistency, will interpret ground projects as especially strong preferences, and will read Williams as licensing simple selfishness. Indicate the order in which these misunderstandings are likely to surface, and state precisely where in the explanatory sequence each should be headed off.

Fourth, advise what should be left out at this level — I suspect that the meta-ethical distinction between agent-relative and agent-neutral reasons is too technical for this audience — and propose one closing question that will reveal whether they have genuinely understood the objection, rather than merely sympathised with it.

Please present your plan in British English. Do not supply bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the response should be usable by someone without access to external sources.
