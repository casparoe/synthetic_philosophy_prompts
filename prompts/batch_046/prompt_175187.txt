Write a short story of no more than 400 words exploring the ethics of dramatizing the dead through the concept of a "posthumous editorial veto." Center the plot on a screenwriter adapting the life of an obscure nineteenth-century municipal reformer who left a sealed letter directing that any future portrayal preserve a specific false account of her administrative failure. The letter explains that the myth protects her descendants' social standing and secures the endowment of a charity she founded. The screenwriter must decide whether to honor the dead woman's explicit instruction, correct the record, or seek a compromise, recognizing that each path violates a different obligation—to the dead's autonomy, to historical truth, or to the living beneficiaries sustained by the fiction. After the story, append a brief note of no more than 80 words distinguishing settled considerations (for instance, that harming the dead differs conceptually from harming living persons) from genuinely contested positions (such as whether the dead retain interests capable of being violated, or whether autonomy extends beyond death), and flag where the story takes liberties with or leans on contested readings of posthumous harm. Do not present a contested philosophical view as consensus.
