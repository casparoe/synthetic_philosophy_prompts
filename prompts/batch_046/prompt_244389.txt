I am writing to seek conceptual clarification about a dispute that has arisen in my department’s governance committee, which I believe turns on an underexamined tension in theories of agency. One colleague, M, has administered our graduate admissions process for over a decade using an identical multi-stage protocol: preliminary screening by a fixed rubric, followed by a structured interview schedule, then a ranked vote conducted in closed session. This year, several stages have become technically redundant—the preliminary screening is now automated, and the interview schedule conflicts with a revised university calendar—yet M insists on executing every stage precisely as originally designed, producing redundant paperwork and delaying decisions, and defends this by saying that altering the protocol would violate her commitment to being “the kind of administrator who does not improvise.” Another colleague argues that this is not autonomy but a form of behavioral rigidity analogous to an organism repeating a fixed motor sequence despite environmental change, and that genuine self-governance requires the capacity to revise one’s own decision procedures in light of counterfactual perturbations to their inputs.

What strikes me is that both sides treat flexibility and rigidity as opposing indicators of free will, yet neither addresses whether there might be a distinct form of autonomy that consists precisely in binding oneself to a procedure that one refuses to revise even when it becomes suboptimal—something like procedural-identity preservation. I want to understand whether M’s refusal to adapt is best interpreted as an exercise of self-binding autonomy, or as a failure of the responsive flexibility that free will requires. More specifically, does the capacity for free agency depend on being able to alter one’s internal decision-rules in response to changed circumstances, or can an agent maintain her authority precisely by maintaining procedural continuity regardless of utility? And if there is a meaningful distinction between “being rigid because one is running a script” and “being rigid because one has chosen a non-revisable rule,” what in the concrete behavior would allow us to tell the two apart? I am less interested in practical advice about the admissions timeline than in mapping the conceptual structure of the dispute and identifying which philosophical commitments each interpretation of M’s behavior actually requires.
