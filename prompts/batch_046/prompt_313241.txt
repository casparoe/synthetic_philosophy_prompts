I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of art and cultural heritage studies, drafting a narrative exercise for my dissertation workshop on representational ethics. Would you please write a short story—around 600 to 800 words—related to the problem of what obligations, if any, we have to the real dead when we dramatize their lives?

The story should center on a playwright commissioned to adapt the reconstructed private journals of a recently deceased, largely forgotten political philosopher into a one-act drama. The philosopher's estate has denied access to the original papers, so the playwright works from fragmentary archival notes and secondhand accounts. The central invented scene involves the philosopher confessing to an estranged colleague that the central thesis of her most famous treatise was motivated not by philosophical conviction but by a long-buried personal grievance against a rival—something psychologically plausible but entirely undocumented. The playwright understands that this fabrication will likely harden into the definitive popular image of the philosopher, yet it supplies the dramatic coherence the commission demands.

I want the narrative to unfold through the playwright's interactions with two figures: the philosopher's adult child, who attends rehearsals uninvited and carries a sealed letter whose contents might either confirm or undermine the invented scene; and the director, who insists that emotional authenticity justifies strategic invention. Rather than resolving into a clear moral verdict, the story should inhabit the ambiguity between illuminating a life and colonizing a death—treating the dead philosopher's posthumous reputation as something that can be neither fully protected nor entirely appropriated without remainder.

Please write in American English, avoid framing the tension as a simple cost-benefit analysis, and do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to specific real-world texts or scholars.
