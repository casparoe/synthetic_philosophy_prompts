I am helping design evaluation criteria for long-range scientific forecasts, and Malthus has recently become my central test case. Here is an argument that seems to prove either that Malthus was never genuinely scientific or that falsifiability is useless for long-run predictions.

(1) A predictive theory is falsified when its specified outcome fails to occur under the conditions the theory itself identified.
(2) Malthus specified that population growth would outpace food supply and produce catastrophic positive checks, absent technological or moral restraint.
(3) For roughly two centuries the predicted checks did not occur, largely because technological and institutional changes raised food supply.
(4) If we allow those changes to count as failures of the specified conditions that save the theory, then any failed long-run prediction can be saved by citing some unforeseen intervening factor.
(5) So allowing the rescue makes long-run predictions unfalsifiable in principle.
(6) Popper held that unfalsifiable theories are not scientific; he criticized Marxism for immunizing itself against refutation in exactly this way.
(7) Yet many scholars now defend Malthus as right but early, claiming resource limits will eventually vindicate him.
(8) Therefore, either Malthus was never scientific, or Popper's falsifiability criterion fails for long-run predictions.

The conclusion feels too strong. If I must choose, I want to keep (2) and (7). Where does the argument slip? Is the move from (3) to (4) an illicit generalization—do we need an independent principle to distinguish legitimate condition-failures from ad hoc rescues? Or is the ambiguity in (1) about what counts as a condition the theory specified? If I reject (5) instead, am I conceding that climate and demographic forecasts are immune to refutation?

Please identify the weakest step, explain why it looked plausible, and price the alternatives—especially whether saving Malthus requires abandoning falsifiability for other long-run predictions. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of science, and do not present a contested reading of Popper or of Malthus's scientific status as the consensus view.
