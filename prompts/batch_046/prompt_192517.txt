I have no formal training in philosophy; I spent fifteen years in hospice care and have spent the last three years trying to articulate something I kept noticing inside long, intimate obligations. Next month I have been invited to present at a university ethics workshop, and I want to defend a specific claim about Bernard Williams’s worry that saving one’s spouse can involve one thought too many—the reflective step of asking whether one is permitted to do so. I think this problem is not confined to sudden rescue cases. In sustained caregiving—looking after a parent through years of dementia, for instance—any explicit moral framework, even non-utilitarian ones such as care ethics or virtue ethics, does not enter as a single wrong thought but as a serial accumulation. Each daily assessment—am I being fair, are my motives pure, is my care proportionate to duty—is individually reasonable, but together they produce what I call moral wear: a gradual shift in which direct, uncalculated presence is replaced by monitored obligation. I call the boundary at which this shift becomes structurally irreversible, rather than merely tiring, the threshold of calculated presence.

My audience will be about twelve people: mostly analytic moral philosophers who work on partiality and personal relationships; two Kant scholars who hold that obligation is constitutive of love rather than destructive to it; one virtue ethicist; and a clinical ethicist accustomed to principlism in hospital settings. Most will be familiar with Williams’s argument. They are skeptical of claims drawn from caregiving intuition alone, but they take conceptual distinctions seriously and will press me on whether I have described a genuine structural feature or only a psychological fatigue.

The sketch of my argument is this. In the acute case, the wrong thought is singular and reflective: it is permitted for me to save her. In sustained care, the wrong thoughts are distributed across time: repeated micro-assessments of moral adequacy. Because none is dramatic, none is resisted, yet their cumulative effect transforms the relationship from one of direct bond to one of moral management. I claim this is not a failure of individual character but a feature of sustained intimacy placed under any explicit normative framework.

Please prepare me for the objections I will actually face in this room, ordered by how likely they are and how damaging they could be. For each, phrase it as that specific audience would phrase it—in the vocabulary of analytic ethics, Kantian practical philosophy, virtue theory, or clinical principlism. Give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and note where the reply is weakest or concedes ground I should not want to lose. Tell me which objections I should concede up front, which I must hold firm, and identify the one reply that absolutely has to land if my proposal is to survive the session. Finally, include the question I am hoping nobody asks—the one that exposes the weakest point in my own position—and advise me how to answer it without collapsing the distinction between moral wear and ordinary exhaustion.
