I'm currently enrolled in an upper-level seminar that crosses evolutionary biology with philosophy of science, and we've been working through whether the classic units-of-selection debate can be sharpened by holding two conditions for genuine selective causation simultaneously rather than treating them as interchangeable. The first is an interventionist condition: a unit counts as selected only if counterfactual or experimental manipulation of its properties systematically alters evolutionary outcomes in the relevant population. The second is a mechanistic condition: a unit counts only if it is physically embedded in the causal processes—developmental, ecological, or reproductive—that actually produce and maintain the trait in question.

Could you rank the three principal candidate units—genes, individual organisms, and groups—on a scale from 1 (fails both conditions clearly) to 5 (satisfies both without tension) for each of the following three evolutionary scenarios: (a) standard multicellular organisms undergoing directional selection on a morphological trait; (b) eusocial insect colonies in which reproductive division of labor is stable; and (c) the evolution of altruistic behaviors that reduce the actor's direct fitness but increase the fitness of recipients. That gives nine ratings total.

For each rating, provide a brief justification, and specifically highlight cases where the interventionist and mechanistic conditions pull apart—where a unit might be said to be selectively causally relevant in the counterfactual sense but does not figure in the actual physical machinery of selection, or vice versa. I am not asking for a literature survey or a reading list; I want a conceptual assessment of where the tension between these two conditions is sharpest and whether any of the three units consistently outperforms the others across all three scenarios.
