I am working through the fifth part of Descartes' Discourse on the Method (published 1637), particularly the argument in which he asserts that if one constructed a machine possessing all the external organs of a monkey or any other irrational animal, there would be no grounds for distinguishing its actions from those of the living creature—save for the absence of a rational soul. The passage moves rapidly from an anatomical comparison, in which the body is likened to a clock or automaton governed by the arrangement of its parts, to a metaphysical conclusion that animals therefore lack thought in any form worthy of the name. What I find difficult is the intermediate step: Descartes grants that animals feel, move, and respond to stimuli in ways structurally analogous to human sensation, yet he denies them any form of true cognition. I want to understand whether this denial is meant to cover only propositional or rational thought, or whether it extends to phenomenal awareness itself. Specifically, does the passage contain an implicit distinction—perhaps between the mechanical arrangement of organs that produces behavior and the habit of reason that transforms sensation into judgment—such that an animal could possess something like sentience without possessing the kind of reflexive, representational consciousness that Descartes reserves for minds? Or does the clock analogy commit him to the stronger view that there is literally nothing it is like to be an animal? I am not looking for a survey of the standard Cartesian position on animal suffering; I am trying to determine whether the internal logic of this particular argument allows for a modular separation of affective experience from rational cognition, and whether such a separation, if present, undermines or reinforces his broader dualism. Could you help me parse that specific tension?
