I'm working on a colum about why 19th-century metaphysics still sneaks into modern debates about language and reality. Last week I was chatting with a philosopher friend about Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition, and I realized I don't actually understand the move he makes. So here's the thing: I get that if you build a proposition out of terms plus an external relation, you need another relation to connect that relation to the terms, and then you're off to the races with an infinite regress. Bradley's answer, as I understand it, is that relations are internal—already inside the terms somehow. But that just raises a weirder problem for me: if the relation is internal, then the terms aren't really separate pieces that need gluing, they're already parts of one big unified thing. So does Bradley solve the regress by showing that propositions are genuinely unified, or does he just collapse everything into a kind of holistic mush where you can't tell where one term ends and another begins?

Could you explain the conceptual issue here—specifically how Bradley's internal-relation view is supposed to preserve both the unity of a proposition and the distinctness of its parts? I want to understand whether the "unity problem" is really about missing glue, or about glue that's so pervasive it makes distinction impossible. And here's a slightly offbeat angle I'm wondering about: do modern defenders of structured propositions—those who talk about relations being "unsaturated" or having binding logical form—face a version of Bradley's mush-objection? Or does that kind of formal binding actually block the regress in a way Bradley missed? Don't worry about citations, I just need the conceptual map clear in my head, especially since I'm trying to write for readers who haven't taken a logic course since college.
