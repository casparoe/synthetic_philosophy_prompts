Having spent thirty years in physiological ecology before turning recently to philosophy of mind, I have tried to assemble a view of emotion that respects both neuroscience and conceptual rigour. My present position is roughly this:

1. Emotions are essentially interoceptive predictive models—brain-generated inferences about allostatic state under the free-energy principle—rather than judgments or bare sensations.
2. These predictive models carry genuine evaluative content: they represent situations as beneficial or threatening, not merely cause appropriate behaviour.
3. The specific emotional categories one can experience depend partly on the conceptual and linguistic resources one possesses; there is no culture-free taxonomy of basic emotions.
4. Nonetheless, core affective states—approach, withdrawal, arousal—are present in non-human mammals and human infants, long before conceptual maturity.
5. Moral emotions (indignation, guilt, gratitude) evolved primarily for social coordination and reputation management, so they are unreliable indicators of mind-independent moral facts.
6. The phenomenal character of feeling afraid or elated is irreducible to the interoceptive computation itself, even if that computation fully explains the causal and functional profile.

Do these hang together? Please audit them for tension: identify which pairs genuinely conflict, rank the conflicts by severity, and for each suggest the least costly revision. Also point out any tensions that dissolve once the distinctions are drawn properly. Finally, tell me which single commitment, if revised, would clean up the largest number of problems.
