I'm preparing a fifty-minute seminar for third-year philosophy students who have just read Parfit's absent-minded driver problem in a course on rationality and practical reasoning. They understand the payoff structure—exiting guarantees arrival at the cost of a small delay, while continuing risks missing the destination entirely—but they've collapsed into a predictable cluster of confusions: they treat the puzzle as a psychological failure of memory that a better mnemonic could fix, they slide between the ex ante planning perspective and the ex post decision point without recognizing that the tension is structural rather than personal, and several are importing intuitions from Newcomb's problem that don't map onto this case. I want the deliverable to be a teaching plan, not the lecture itself: the precise dependency order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so that the paradox registers as a feature of rational planning rather than a glitch in reasoning; the single carrying example to build around (I'm weighing a modified scenario involving an automated laboratory backup protocol that a technician may or may not have already triggered, where the "absent-mindedness" concerns system-state rather than autobiographical memory, but I'm unsure whether this preserves the logic of strategic self-binding or quietly turns it into a causal prediction problem); the misconceptions likely to arise at each stage and the exact point in the sequence where each should be defused; a concrete check for understanding that reveals whether students have grasped the structural tension or have merely memorized the "take the exit" conclusion; and an explicit list of what formal machinery—game trees, backward induction, dynamic consistency conditions—I should name and set aside at this level.

Please invite pushback: if my framing assumes there is a clean pedagogical path through a problem that is genuinely paradoxical in a way that resists orderly unpacking, or if my modified laboratory-protocol example actually collapses the distinction between strategic reasoning and causal reasoning that makes the driver problem philosophically interesting, say so plainly rather than constructing a neat plan around a confused premise. I also want an honest assessment of which feature of the problem any classroom explanation at this level necessarily flattens or omits.
