Here's an argument I've been working through, and I can't tell whether it contains a hidden slide or whether it's valid and I simply have to reject one of the premises. (1) In standard Frankfurt-style cases, an agent decides to perform X on her own, though an external controller stands ready to force X if she shows signs of wavering; she lacks alternative possibilities for the action itself, yet seems fully responsible because the decision flows from her own uncoerced deliberative mechanisms. (2) The standard compatibilist reading is that responsibility tracks sourcehood—whether the action stems from the agent's own values and reasoning—rather than leeway, the bare ability to have done otherwise. (3) Now consider a nested variant: Agent M's entire deliberative architecture was deterministically constructed by a chain of external designers, each implanting values in the next, with M at the end. M acts with full internal coherence and identifies with her values, but she never influenced the chain that produced them. (4) By the sourcehood criterion in (2), M is responsible, since her actions flow from her present, authentically experienced values. (5) But that seems wrong: when the very source of one's will was deterministically fixed from outside—as in severe brainwashing or artificial construction—we typically withhold full responsibility. (6) So to preserve the intuition in (5), the compatibilist must require that sourcehood itself not be deterministically produced; the agent must possess some form of alternative possibility regarding the origin or constitution of her will. Call this source-level leeway. (7) Once source-level leeway is admitted, the original principle of alternative possibilities returns—not for the particular action, but for the agent's will-forming history. (8) Apply this recursively: if source-level leeway is required for the will's origin, then the conditions providing that leeway must themselves not be deterministically fixed, generating either an infinite regress of sources or an uncaused cause. (9) Therefore, the Frankfurt strategy proves too much: it either extends responsibility to deterministically constructed agents (giving up 5) or collapses back into a version of PAP at the meta-level (giving up 7), with the regress in (8) as the unavoidable price. Where does this go wrong, if it does? If I must choose, I'd rather keep (2) and (5) than (7) or (8), but I want to know exactly what I'm paying. And if the argument rests on a confused premise—for example, if the distinction between action-level and source-level leeway isn't as sharp as I'm treating it, or if diagnosing the flaw requires settling deep controversies about source compatibilism that can't be adjudicated here—please say so plainly instead of just picking a step to reject.
