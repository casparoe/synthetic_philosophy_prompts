I’ve noticed a recurring pattern in how people defend the special status of instrumental music—music without words, titles, or programs. They begin by insisting that it does not depict, symbolize, or stand for anything in the world of appearances: it does not show a landscape, narrate an event, or illustrate a concept the way a painting or poem might. Instead, they say it expresses something deeper and more direct—perhaps the fundamental impulse or inner nature of reality itself—bypassing images and ideas entirely. But then, almost immediately, they try to explain how this direct expression works by mapping musical elements onto worldly phenomena: the deep bass becomes an analogue for gravity or the inertia of inorganic matter; the melody becomes human striving or desire; harmonic resolution becomes the reconciliation of opposing forces in nature. So the theory claims to be non-representational at its core, yet its own explanatory machinery is built entirely out of representations and structural analogies. I first ran into this while reading an older philosopher—Schopenhauer, I believe—who argued that music is unique among the arts because it copies not the world of appearances but the Will itself, yet who also describes melody, harmony, and bass in terms that mirror natural processes and human psychology. Is there an established name for this specific tension, where a claim to direct, unmediated expression of essence is articulated only through mediated structural analogies? How does it differ from ordinary musical symbolism, from program music, and from simple metaphor? If there is no standard term, what should we call it, and where exactly is the boundary between a legitimate structural explanation of direct expression and a contradiction of the original claim? I am not looking for a bibliography; I want to know whether this carving of the problem already exists, and if not, how to define its edges precisely.
