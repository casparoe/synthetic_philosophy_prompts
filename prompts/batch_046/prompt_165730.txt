Recently, after committing to part-time volunteer work at a legal-aid clinic, I was asked to take on several complex cases that would push me well beyond my agreed hours. This left me unsure whether I was merely fulfilling a professional obligation or actually doing something supererogatory, and it sparked a disagreement between two colleagues, Maya and Jonas, that I could not settle.

Maya argued that within a helping profession there is no sharp boundary between duty and going beyond it: the role itself is defined by open-ended responsiveness to need, so extending my hours is just being a good volunteer, not performing a heroic excess. Jonas countered that every role has institutional and contractual limits, and without them supererogation collapses into "being excellent at one's job"; we praise people for stepping outside their domain—say, a lawyer volunteering in a disaster zone—precisely because that crosses a clear boundary, whereas staying late at one's own clinic is ambiguous at best. Maya replied that Jonas imports an overly legalistic conception of roles; professional ethics are normative rather than contractual, and they can extend as far as need requires, so the line is vague but not absent. Jonas conceded the vagueness but insisted that clear cases remain on both sides, and he challenged Maya to identify one act within her own professional domain that she would unambiguously call supererogatory rather than merely exemplary duty. Maya suggested donating half one's salary to fund the clinic, but Jonas observed that this shifts the act into the financial domain, leaving untouched the original question about labor within the role. He concluded that Maya's view makes supererogation domain-relative in a way that severs it from its usual ties to personal sacrifice and genuine non-obligation.

Please adjudicate point by point: who is right about the nature of professional duty, the relevance of domain boundaries, and whether vagueness undermines the duty/supererogation distinction? Also say what both miss—especially whether there can be domain-internal supererogation that is not reducible to exceptional role performance, and whether the agent's personal cost should play any independent classificatory role.
