I am attempting to determine whether my judgments about the moral equality of combatants form a coherent pattern or contain irreconcilable tensions, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them and where it must fail.

Consider the following pairings, where in each instance I ask whether the two parties stand in a relation of moral equality with respect to the permissibility of lethal force in hostilities.

First pairing: Two uniformed regular soldiers, one serving in a clearly unjust war of territorial aggression, the other in the just defense against it. They engage in conventional battlefield combat. My verdict: they are morally equal; neither wrongs the other merely by fighting.

Second pairing: The same structural situation, but the soldier on the unjust side was conscripted under credible threat of execution for his family, holds no commitment to the war's aims, and takes active measures to limit harm to noncombatants. My verdict: the moral equality still holds, even though his personal culpability is substantially diminished.

Third pairing: The just-side fighter is a volunteer with full information; the unjust-side fighter is a highly paid mercenary fighting with evident enthusiasm. My verdict: equality persists; differences in motivation and compensation do not dissolve the symmetry.

Fourth pairing: The unjust-side fighter operates a remote weapons system from another continent, faces no physical risk, wears no uniform, and targets the just-side fighter, who is a conscripted infantryman exposed to direct fire. My verdict: equality is broken. The remote operator lacks the reciprocal vulnerability that, in my view, grounds the special moral status of combatants.

Fifth pairing: Both fighters belong to states that lack just cause, yet each is defending his own village from invasion by forces associated with the other state. My verdict: they are equal to one another, but the equality is structurally different from that in the first pairing—closer to mutual wrongful defense than to symmetric permission within a unified framework of war.

Sixth pairing: A soldier on the just side kills an enemy combatant who has dropped his weapon, raised his hands, and begun moving toward a surrender point, though he has not reached it or uttered formal words of surrender. My verdict: the killing is impermissible, and the wrong consists precisely in the fact that the victim has exited the class of persons against whom lethal force is permitted—indicating that equality is conditional on remaining within an active combat role rather than governed by a separate, independent prohibition.

These judgments seem to support equality across differences in justice of cause and personal motivation, yet deny it on grounds of physical risk asymmetry, recognize a distinct form of equality among mutual wrongdoers, and treat equality as role-dependent and revocable. Is there a single principle that accommodates all six verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If not, which verdict should be abandoned in favor of simplicity, and what does the most defensible principle predict for a case not listed here—specifically, a uniformed soldier fighting for an unjust cause who has been captured, escaped captivity, and is now using lethal force against his former captors solely to regain freedom?

Please state the principle precisely, indicate which of my verdicts it cannot preserve, and apply it to the escape case.
