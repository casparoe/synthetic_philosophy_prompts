I serve on the State Constitutional Reform Commission, and I write to you from the double position of commissioner and novelist — someone who thinks about institutions not only as mechanisms but as stories with characters, dependencies, and hidden plots. We are required to circulate a formal decision memo by Thursday recommending whether to advance Proposal 14 to the legislature. The proposal creates a hybrid chamber: two-fifths of seats filled by stratified random lot, with single, non-renewable eight-year terms and a permanent bar on reelection or subsequent appointment; the remaining seats subject to hard lifetime term limits of three terms, followed by a mandatory decade-long exclusion from state office; and, most unusually, a provision that every term-limited former legislator must then serve four years as a paid institutional memory advisor to the lot-selected members, with full committee access and the authority to lodge binding procedural objections. The philosophical tension is sharp. The design claims to advance republican liberty — freedom understood as non-domination — by breaking careerist control, yet it embeds that same career class inside the new chamber’s procedural architecture, potentially reducing the citizen-legislators to subordinate figures in a narrative written by their predecessors. Defenders say the advisor role is a necessary guardrail against amateur error and preserves democratic continuity; critics say it manufactures exactly the kind of structural dependency that republican liberty condemns, while also complicating any simple account of negative liberty or positive self-mastery for the citizens who are drawn by lot. The memo must recommend one concrete course — approve, reject, or approve with enforceable conditions — with reasoning that addresses both the democratic theory and the concrete power dynamics created by the advisor mechanism, and it must treat the strongest opposing consideration with genuine intellectual honesty rather than dismissal. Please draft the document as it might be circulated before Friday’s vote: formal in tone, actionable in its bottom line, and written for commissioners who must decide whether this particular hybrid of sortition, term limits, and retained career influence is a reform worth sending to the ballot.
