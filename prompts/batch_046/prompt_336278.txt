The disagreement over counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail in decision theory appears to have stalled: evidential decision theorists hold that rational agents should take the bet in Newcomb-style scenarios because choice and outcome are probabilistically linked, whereas causal decision theorists reject this as a category error between correlation and causation, contending that XOR blackmail exposes the implausibility of evidential reasoning by conditioning blackmail on logically exclusive outcomes. Despite decades of refinement, neither camp substantially revises its commitments. Is this impasse primarily verbal — a dispute over the semantics of rational choice — or does it mask a substantive divergence about whether decision rules must satisfy counterfactual stability across modal neighborhoods? More specifically, do the sides implicitly apply incompatible criteria for when a counterfactual dependency is genuine, with causalists demanding physically mediated causal chains and evidentialists requiring only coherence under a fixed decision procedure, such that they are actually addressing distinct questions about strategic reasoning? If the disagreement is substantive, what kind of formal result — perhaps regarding the modal robustness of decision procedures under XOR conditions — could resolve it? And if it is partly verbal or anchored in non-negotiable intuitions, what does that suggest about the prospects for integrating counterfactual reasoning into a unified account of rational agency?
