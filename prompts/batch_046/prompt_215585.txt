Write a short research proposal—approximately 1,000 words, suitable for a philosophy graduate workshop or a small interdisciplinary fellowship—addressing the following question: If Hofstadter is correct that an I is a pattern capable of partial persistence in other brains—embedded, for instance, in the predictive models and emotional schemas that close others maintain—can that distributed instance possess diachronic moral status independent of the original brain, or is it necessarily a parasitic projection? Frame the problem through a specific tension in Hofstadter's account: he treats the self as a substrate-independent strange loop, yet he describes its survival in others as contingent on the host brain's continued functioning, which suggests that distributed persistence is always asymmetrical and derivative. Your proposal should (1) state the precise question, distinguishing ontological persistence from functional persistence and clarifying whether a distributed I could ever generate normative claims of its own; (2) explain why standard personal-identity theories—psychological-continuity accounts and narrative-identity approaches—fail to resolve this tension, particularly because they assume either a single locus of consciousness or a fully symmetric relational structure; (3) propose an analytical approach that introduces the concept of fractal persistence—the idea that a self-pattern can be nested at different scales across brains without being token-identical—to determine whether asymmetrical, partial instantiation can support independent moral accountability; (4) describe a concrete deliverable, such as a conceptual taxonomy of three modes of distributed selfhood (projection, symbiotic loop, autonomous fragment) and the criteria that would place a given case in each; (5) specify what would count as failure, for example if every distributed instance collapses into either full personal identity or mere representational content; and (6) provide an honest scope assessment for a single-semester project, identifying which parts must remain conceptual rather than empirical. The proposal should be written in American English, should avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citation-dependent literature reviews, and should discuss positions and arguments conceptually rather than relying on specific editions or page references.
