At a workshop on economic ethics, two attendees, Elena and Marcus, argued about whether a proposed municipal "care-token" system belongs on the natural or unnatural side of Aristotle's distinction between household management and wealth-getting. In the system, residents earn tokens by providing eldercare or childcare; tokens are redeemed for equivalent services; and any unspent balance is bought back by the city at year-end and destroyed, with the buyback rate adjusting to demand.

Elena said this is natural exchange: the tokens mediate genuinely needed labor, the buyback is only a liquidity safeguard analogous to converting goods back into coin, and accumulation is impossible because tokens expire.

Marcus said it is chrematistics disguised: the floating buyback rate makes the token behave like interest-bearing capital, wealthy residents can hoard tokens to drive up the rate, and artificial scarcity makes exchange-value govern rather than use-value—exactly the inversion Aristotle criticized when he treated money as barren and unsuited to breeding more money.

Elena answered that expiration rules prevent hoarding, so the token cannot serve as capital.

Marcus replied that expiration creates speculative trading around anticipated rate changes, and that Aristotle's point was not biological reproduction but treating a barren medium as if it were productive whenever profit is sought from the medium's movement rather than from the goods it carries.

They asked a bystander to referee. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: say who has the stronger argument at each stage, identify what either party is missing about Aristotle's framework, and state who is more right overall. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
