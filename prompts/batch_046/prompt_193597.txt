A comparative cognition researcher, Dr. Lena Okonkwo, has published a consistent track record of judgments about phenomenal consciousness: she holds that adult humans, pigs, and cuttlefish are conscious; that chickens, individual honeybees, and the nematode C. elegans are not; that a bee colony operating as an integrated information-processing unit is conscious; that a current large-scale artificial language model is not; and that a decerebrate frog exhibiting reflex withdrawal is not. I am less interested in her specific answers for three new cases than in the underlying rule she is running. The new cases are: a lobster with a distributed ganglionic nervous system but documented individual recognition and problem-solving; a Portuguese man o' war, a colonial organism composed of specialized zooids functioning as a single unit; and a synthetic cerebral organoid grown from human stem cells—roughly fifty thousand neurons, displaying spontaneous synchronized oscillations in vitro but lacking sensory input, motor output, or evolutionary history. For each of the leading candidate rules—integrated information theory applied across centralized or distributed architectures; a scale-modified precautionary principle that raises the evidential threshold for entities existing in vast numbers; a functionalist criterion tied to flexible adaptive behavior; and any other rule that fits her earlier verdicts equally well—state what it predicts for the lobster, the man o' war, and the organoid. Then give your best guess at her actual verdicts with reasoning, identify which case or comparison would best distinguish the leading rules from one another, and say whether her framework genuinely treats the scale of insect populations as a reason to lower or to raise the bar for attributing sentience.
