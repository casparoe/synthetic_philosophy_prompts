During a seminar discussion, a fellow student offered a summary she had encountered in an unnamed popular article. According to her account, the problem demonstrates that externalist theories are viciously circular: one begins with a single perceptual belief that lacks prior justification, uses its apparent success to infer that one's perceptual faculties are generally reliable, and then appeals to that inferred reliability to justify both the original belief and countless others. She maintains that this circularity is peculiar to reliabilist externalism, since internalist theories require direct introspective access to mental states and therefore cannot use a belief's success to license its own source. Furthermore, she argues that the only way to block the circle—requiring an independent check on reliability—forces the reliabilist to demand prior reasoning for every perceptual belief, which collapses the view into a form of foundationalist coherentism.

I suspect that she has overstated the uniqueness of the problem to externalism, and that the alleged collapse into coherentism may be a misrepresentation, but I am not sufficiently confident to identify every error myself. Could you assess whether her summary is fair? I would like you to take a committed position: distinguish precisely which parts capture the genuine structure of the bootstrapping objection, which parts are simplifications or overstatements, and which are genuinely mistaken—particularly regarding internalism's supposed immunity and the claim about collapsing into coherentism. Please do not merely lay out the options; give me your definite judgement on whether her conclusion that reliabilism is self-defeating survives once the premises are corrected, and explain why.
