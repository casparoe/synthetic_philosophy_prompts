The debate over whether a statue and the lump of clay that constitutes it are one object or two has persisted for decades, with the familiar positions—strict identity, constitution without identity, eliminativism about artifacts, and four-dimensionalist fusion—each well articulated and each facing well-rehearsed counterexamples involving repair, destruction, and modal properties. My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not primarily ontological but methodological: the sides differ on what a satisfactory account of persistence must explain—whether it must preserve spatiotemporal continuity of matter, preserve form under part-replacement, or merely underwrite ordinary judgments of sameness—without any shared criterion for why one explanatory target should dominate. Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden formal disagreement—perhaps about the transitivity of constitution, the logic of parthood, or whether artistic form introduces genuinely new modal constraints—doing the substantive work? Diagnose the impasse by engaging what the main camps actually commit themselves to regarding change and identity, and say whether the dispute is verbal (about the word 'object'), substantive but incommensurable (different questions masked by shared vocabulary), or genuinely resolvable. If resolvable, specify what would count as progress: a formal result in mereology, an empirical finding about human object-categorization, a distinction between natural-kind and artifact persistence conditions, or a reconceptualization of the problem that dissolves the coincidence intuition. If not resolvable, argue whether that failure signals that the question is ill-posed—say, because 'the statue' and 'the clay' answer to different explanatory projects—or merely that it is hard in a way that philosophy should learn to live with.
