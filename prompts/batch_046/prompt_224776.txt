I'm a phd student in phil of religion and I'm stuck on this debate about Kierkegaard's media critique that's been going since the 90s. One side — call them the institutionalists — reads his attack on the press in A Literary Review and those journal entries as an early sociology of mass media: newspapers manufacture an abstract 'public' (Offentligheden) that dissolves individual responsibility into anonymous opinion. The other side — the existentialists, I guess — says that's missing the point; the press is just one occasion for the leveling process, and the real issue is spiritual despair and the loss of self before God, not media tech. Both sides cite the same passages, everyone agrees he hated newspapers and that the crowd was dangerous, and yet they talk past eachother for decades.

My hunch is part of the stall is verbal: 'the public' means 'a social formation produced by scalable communication' for one camp and 'a metaphysical state of anonymity in modernity' for the other, so they're answering different questions — mechanisms vs conditions — under one heading. But I also wonder if there's a hidden empirical question: whether his critique is tied specifically to print culture and one-to-many broadcast, or whether it applies to any scalable network including social media. That feels answerable from the texts, but nobody frames it that way.

So I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this substantive, verbal, or are they genuinely answering different questions? What's settled — do we agree he distinguishes the crowd from the public, or that anonymity is spiritually dangerous? — and what's genuinely contested? And given your diagnosis, what would move things: a conceptual distinction, a closer textual argument, or should we accept the question is ill-posed? Please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this, and don't present one camp as the consensus.
