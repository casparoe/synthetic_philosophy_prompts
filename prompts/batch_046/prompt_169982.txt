Having spent three decades in condensed-matter physics before turning recently to philosophy, I have developed a habit of treating conceptual problems as structural ones, and I find Gödel's incompleteness theorems especially striking in this regard. I understand that the first theorem establishes that any consistent formal system with sufficient arithmetic strength must contain statements that are true in the standard interpretation yet unprovable within the system, and that the second theorem shows such a system cannot demonstrate its own consistency. What I am struggling to grasp is the precise boundary of this result when one attempts to apply it outside mathematics—specifically, to normative or ethical reasoning.

In my recent reading I have begun to imagine whether one could formalize a system of moral permissibility as a consistent, recursively enumerable axiomatization that embeds basic arithmetic for comparative judgments. If such a deontic architecture were genuinely subject to Gödel's constraints, then it seems there must exist specific normative propositions—what I have come to think of as Gödelian moral residues—that are objectively true within the intended interpretation but permanently beyond derivation within any consistent ethical calculus. Should we therefore regard these residues as obligations that must be accessed through some non-derivational faculty, or does the attempt to extend Gödel's result into ethics fail at a precise point—perhaps in the move from syntactic well-formedness to deontic content, or in the absence of a uniquely intended model for moral language? I would be grateful for a careful clarification of what these theorems do and do not establish when translated into normative domains, particularly regarding whether they genuinely generate this category of unprovable but true moral statements or whether the analogy collapses before reaching any practical deliberative consequence.
