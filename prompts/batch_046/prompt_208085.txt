Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with two refrains that alternate through the tercets and then stand together in the final couplet—that enacts the conceptual conflict between Isaac Newton’s own theology and the Enlightenment caricature later imposed on him. On one side, take Newton’s actual position, developed in his unpublished theological manuscripts and in his correspondence about planetary dynamics: divine presence is not spatial extension but an immediate, active causal power exercised everywhere, and the regularities of nature depend on continuous or periodic divine governance rather than on a self-sufficient mechanism (he worried, for instance, that gravitational perturbations would eventually destabilize the orbits without such correction). On the other side, take the “clockmaker” image his readers invented: a remote deity who constructs the world-machine and lets it run without further intervention. Give the first refrain to Newton’s anti-spatial, actively governing presence; give the second to the clockwork metaphor he rejected. Structure the poem so that the alternating refrains in the tercets keep passing each other without convergence, and so that the closing quatrain, where both lines appear side by side, demonstrates their incompatibility—if presence is immediate causal power everywhere, the gears are not running alone; if they are running alone, presence collapses into distant architecture. Make it precise enough that someone familiar with Newton’s anti-Trinitarian commitments and his concern about orbital instability recognizes the historical view being defended, not a generic deism. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or source lists; let the form itself carry the argument.
