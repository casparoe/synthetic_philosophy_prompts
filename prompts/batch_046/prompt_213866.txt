Question:
In Politics I.8–10 and Nicomachean Ethics V.5, Aristotle distinguishes oikonomia (household management directed towards a natural end) from chrematistike (the unlimited acquisition of wealth), and discusses the invention of coinage (nomisma) as a response to the incommensurability of goods in exchange. Assess whether, on Aristotle’s account, the introduction of currency necessarily corrupts the proportional reciprocity (antipeponthos) that governs just exchange within a political community, or whether it can remain a neutral instrument serving the natural limit (peras) of household acquisition. In your answer, explain the specific sense in which interest-bearing lending (tokos) is regarded as unnatural, and relate this to Aristotle’s conception of autarkeia—carefully distinguishing its application to the individual household from its application to the polis as a whole.

Student answer:
Aristotle distinguishes oikonomia, the art of managing a household with a natural end, from chrematistike, which has no natural limit because it aims at the accumulation of coin rather than goods for use. In Politics I.9 he argues that money was invented for exchange (metablētikē) because goods are incommensurable by nature; the coin measures demand (chreia) and thereby makes proportional reciprocity possible. Currency is therefore not corrupt in itself—it is an instrument that allows households to reach the natural limit of self-sufficiency by trading surplus for needed goods.

The corruption enters with chrematistike, which treats coin as an end. Because coin is conventional (kata nomon) rather than naturally limited like food or livestock, it can be accumulated without physical bound. The extreme case is tokos, interest-bearing lending. Aristotle regards this as unnatural because money is barren (agonos) and cannot reproduce; to make it generate offspring violates its essential nature as a medium of exchange, not a source of generation.

This relates to autarkeia. The individual household is not fully self-sufficient, but the polis achieves autarkeia precisely through the division of labour and exchange among households. Thus inter-household exchange is not a departure from nature but the political completion of natural self-sufficiency. The error of chrematistics is therefore not exchange itself, but the failure to recognise that the household’s need for goods is limited, whereas the desire for coin is unlimited.

Nevertheless, Aristotle thinks that measuring value through nomisma introduces a structural risk for justice in exchange. In Nicomachean Ethics V.5 he holds that just exchange requires arithmetic proportion—adding and subtracting goods according to their fixed worth—whereas currency, being conventional, can obscure the true ratio between goods. Consequently, while coin is necessary for the polis, it permanently threatens to replace the natural proportion of needs with an abstract quantitative measure, making chrematistike almost inevitable once money is adopted.
