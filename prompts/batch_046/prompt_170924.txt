Design a 90-minute seminar activity for 12 graduate students in moral philosophy that forces them to inhabit the tension between ranking historical atrocities and the intuition that such ranking is obscene. Introduce a new, purpose-built evaluation framework—the Index of Unmendable Harm—scored along four dimensions: irreversibility of damage, scale of deliberate intentionality, collapse of internal moral categories, and intergenerational rupture. Do not anchor this index in any named existing theory or citation. Divide the students into three committees of four; provide each committee with anonymized case files describing four mass atrocities (the Holocaust, the transatlantic slave trade, the Holodomor, and the Rwandan genocide) rendered only as Case A through Case D with structurally parallel descriptions. Require each committee to distribute exactly 100 commemorative-resource points across the four cases using the Index, defending both an ordinal ranking and a cardinal allocation. After 40 minutes of deliberation, have the committees present; then reveal the real-world identities behind the cases. In the debrief, map each dimension of the Index onto a philosophical justification for comparison, show how the cardinal allocation compelled participants to treat incommensurable sufferings as fungible, and pinpoint the moment when groups either suppressed internal disagreement to reach a number or refused quantification and thereby failed to complete the task. Include an honest prediction of what will go wrong on the first run—likely false precision or paralytic silence—and advise how the facilitator should intervene without resolving the tension. Finally, state frankly which feature of the "ranking is obscene" objection the rules of the activity cannot reproduce—specifically the direct moral testimony of survivors and descendants—so that the facilitator knows what must be supplied externally rather than generated by the game.
