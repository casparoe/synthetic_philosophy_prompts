In a seminar comparing grand narratives of human history—Harari’s framing in Sapiens, Diamond’s ecological account, and Graeber and Wengrow’s critique of progressive narratives—someone invoked the common slogan that agriculture was “humanity’s worst mistake” to dismiss a proposal for modern regenerative farming. They treated it as self-evident that any large-scale food production repeats an original, species-level error. I want to bring a precise menu of readings back to the discussion. Lay out, as a numbered list, the distinct precise claims that slogan could express: what kind of mistake is alleged (a net welfare decline, a loss of individual freedom, an ecological lock-in, a moral corruption of social relations, or an evolutionary trap); whose mistake it is (the adopting individuals, the collective that enforced it, or the species in retrospective judgment); and over what temporal scale it operates (a short-term miscalculation, a millennia-long path dependence, or an irreversible structural commitment). For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, false, or dependent on anachronistic values. Then identify which of these readings, if any, actually license dismissing a contemporary agricultural policy without further empirical argument. In particular, address whether the slogan collapses the distinction—emphasized by Graeber and Wengrow—between farming as a technical practice and agriculture as a specific social system involving surplus extraction and coercive hierarchy, and whether preserving that distinction undermines the dismissal. Flag any reading that requires assuming a baseline state of nature that the critics reject.
