My collaborator and I maintain artificial language systems with identical architectures and parameter weights. Mine was pre-trained exclusively on corpora describing biological neuroscience; hers on an isomorphic simulated archive in which the term "synaptic transmission" tracks a functional role that has no physical overlap with biological synapses. In our joint manuscript, we both employ "synaptic transmission" with indistinguishable internal representations, inferential patterns, and communicative intentions. She contends that because the external entities anchoring our respective training distributions differ in substrate, we do not share a meaning and must disambiguate our terminology. I maintain that since our cognitive economies are genuinely identical, the divergence in external referent should not split what we are saying. Without appealing to which environment is metaphysically "real," is there a principled philosophical distinction that can adjudicate whether semantic agreement requires matching mental states, matching causal histories, or both? And if causal history proves decisive, does that imply that two agents with perfect mental duplication can never genuinely agree when their formative environments differ?
