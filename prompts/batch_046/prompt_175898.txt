I'm preparing remarks for a graduate seminar on moral philosophy and want a consistency audit of the position I've developed, because I suspect there may be hidden tensions I'm not seeing clearly. My view combines several commitments drawn from the care-ethical tradition but arranged in what may be an unstable configuration. First, I accept the core care-ethical claim that moral reasoning should begin with concrete, particular relationships rather than with abstract, universalizable principles—so I reject the priority of impartial justice as the foundation of ethics. Second, I maintain a sharp distinction between caring for, which involves direct practical engagement, emotional attunement, and responsive labor, and caring about, which is merely a diffuse affective orientation toward distant others; I take only the former to generate the strong, specific obligations that care ethics valorizes. Third, I hold that moral knowledge within caring relationships is primarily receptive—acquired through attentive listening, contextual judgment, and emotional openness—rather than discursively justified or principle-derived, which means that obligations arising within care often resist full articulation to outsiders. Fourth, despite this, I want to defend ambitious universal institutional measures—such as socialized childcare, paid caregiving leave, and the de-gendering of domestic labor—on explicitly care-ethical grounds, applying them to entire populations regardless of direct relational ties. Fifth, I reject the public-private distinction that has historically confined care to the domestic sphere, treating care instead as a matter of public justice and political structure.

The tension I sense is between the epistemic and normative particularism of commitments two and three, on one hand, and the universal structural ambitions of commitment four, on the other. If strong obligations and reliable moral knowledge are tied to direct caring-for relationships and to receptive attentiveness within specific bonds, then on what basis can I justify imposing universal caregiving institutions on strangers whose particular needs I have not listened to and with whom I share no caring bond? Does my reliance on receptive, non-discursive moral knowledge in the third commitment undermine my ability to argue for the policies in the fourth without smuggling in the very kind of abstract, principle-driven reasoning I reject in the first? Furthermore, does the distinction in the second commitment leave me with only weak, sentiment-based reasons—mere caring-about—to motivate the redistribution in the fourth, thereby collapsing the normative force of my institutional commitments? I want you to examine these five commitments systematically: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but can be reconciled, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each tension, specify what the least costly revision would be—whether modifying the epistemology in the third, softening the caring-for and caring-about boundary in the second, or qualifying the universalism in the fourth—and tell me whether there is a single, minimal adjustment that restores coherence without forcing me to abandon either the particularism of care ethics or the political ambition of the fifth commitment.
