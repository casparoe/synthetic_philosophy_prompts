After a long argument with a friend last night about Machiavelli’s views on fortune and virtù, I’m still unsettled about how to read a specific passage in The Prince. We kept returning to the river metaphor, but we couldn’t agree on whether Machiavelli is describing a genuinely anticipatory form of political agency or merely a reactive prudence that adjusts after circumstances have already shifted. Here is the passage that started our disagreement:

"I compare her to one of those raging rivers, which when in flood overflows the plains, sweeping away trees and buildings, bearing away the soil from place to place; everything flies before it, all yield to its violence, without being able in any way to withstand it; and yet, though its nature be such, it does not follow therefore that men, when the weather becomes fair, shall not make provision, both with defences and barriers, in such a manner that, rising again, the waters may pass away by canal, and their force be neither so unrestrained nor so dangerous."

My friend reads this as a straightforward argument for adaptive flexibility: you wait for the weather to become fair, then build barriers. I think that misses the temporal structure Machiavelli is sketching—the barriers are built during calm precisely so that they constrain future floods, which sounds more like a feed-forward, control-theoretic model of virtù than like reactive adaptation. At the same time, Machiavelli also claims that a prince who relies entirely upon fortune is lost when it changes, and that a cautious man often fails because he cannot deviate from his nature when circumstances demand adventure. So is virtù here a stable dispositional capacity to engineer institutional defenses in advance, or is it an unstable capacity for timely self-transformation?

Could you help me understand the passage? In particular, does Machiavelli’s river image support a control-theoretic interpretation of virtù—one in which the prince constrains future contingency by building barriers before the flood—or does it ultimately reduce virtù to a reactive adjustment of method to the “spirit of the times”? And how should we reconcile that with his claim that fortune governs half our actions but that free will remains intact?
