I am drafting a philosophical interlude for a novel in which a reclusive moral philosopher, Dr. Elin Voss, has left a scattered record of verdicts on moral luck. From her essays I have reconstructed the following positions: she judges that the drunk driver who actually kills a pedestrian is more blameworthy than the equally negligent driver who, by pure chance, harms no one; she holds that two agents with identical reckless intentions deserve precisely the same moral praise, even when only one succeeds in rescuing a child through an unforeseeable accident; she maintains that a surgeon whose careless hand slips and kills a patient is culpable for the death itself, not merely for the carelessness, despite the slip being genuinely unforeseeable; and yet she insists that a whistleblower who accidentally exposes corruption through a misaddressed email merits praise only for her conscientious drafting, not for the exposure that resulted. For my narrative I need her response to a new case: an autonomous logistics algorithm, designed without any moral intent and subjected only to stochastic variation in weather data, reroutes a shipment that saves a remote village from famine in one run, while an identical parallel run reroutes differently and the village starves. The programmers exercised standard due diligence, and the system possesses no mental states. Would Dr. Voss hold the programmers more blameworthy in the starvation outcome, or more praiseworthy in the salvation outcome? Would she apply moral luck to the algorithm itself, deny that moral luck applies without an appropriate agential substrate, or treat the programmers as insulated from outcome-based assessment? Which of her prior rulings serves as the closest analog, and is she implicitly running an asymmetrical principle by which blame tracks outcomes while praise tracks intentions, a unified mental-state isolation with an ad hoc exception for physical harm, or a substrate-dependent restriction of moral luck to embodied agents? Finally, what single variation—say, a case in which the programmers knowingly deployed an opaque system they could not audit, or one in which a human dispatcher overrode the algorithm’s recommendation—would best distinguish whether her view rests on mental-state isolation or on substrate-dependence?
