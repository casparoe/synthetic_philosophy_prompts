I'm working through debates about gradual change and categorical language, and I've noticed a recurring argumentative posture that I can't place precisely in the literature. The pattern goes like this. Someone is pressed on where, say, a heap ends and a non-heap begins, or where baldness starts. Rather than denying there's a boundary, they insist there must be one specific point — "there's a grain that makes the difference" — but they simultaneously claim this point is in principle undetectable, not because our instruments are weak, but because the property itself doesn't come with a warning sign. What's distinctive is that they don't use this hidden cutoff to defend precise judgments; instead, they use it to license a kind of suspended judgment across a wide middle range, saying "it's genuinely one or the other, I just can't say which," while still treating clear cases at the extremes as unproblematic. In a second instance, the same posture appears with color terms: they reject both "there's no fact of the matter" and "we could find out with better lighting," and instead hold that the boundary is sharp but epistemically inaccessible in a stronger sense — not hidden behind noise, but structurally invisible to anyone applying the concept.

This feels adjacent to epistemic theories of vagueness, but it isn't quite that: the speaker isn't claiming we know there's a sharp line and merely fail to know where it is; they're claiming the line is there, it determines membership, yet it's not the kind of thing that could ever be known or pointed to, and this isn't presented as a limitation of our minds but as a feature of the concept's application. It also isn't straightforward contextualism, because they don't shift standards with context; they hold one fixed standard that happens to be permanently occluded.

Does this specific combination — metaphysical sharpness plus in-principle epistemic inaccessibility used to justify agnosticism about the middle range — have an established name? If it does, how does it differ from standard epistemic accounts of vagueness and from contextualist or supervaluationist treatments? If it doesn't, can you propose a term, define its core commitments, and specify its boundary cases — particularly how it behaves differently from a view that simply denies vagueness altogether or one that treats vagueness as purely linguistic?

Please answer in American English, and don't just provide a label: I want the phenomenon carved out from its neighbors, with clear conditions for what counts as an instance and what doesn't.
