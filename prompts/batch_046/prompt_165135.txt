Read Shakespeare’s Macbeth through the lens of Johannes Kepler’s philosophy of astrology, particularly the position he developed in his 1601 treatise on the more certain foundations of the subject. Kepler accepted a restricted form of celestial influence—what he called the action of stellar species on terrestrial matter and human temperament—while firmly rejecting judicial astrology’s claim to determine individual futures, insisting that the rational soul remains free and that all stellar effects are subordinate to divine providence. Focus on the Weird Sisters’ prophecies and Macbeth’s interpretation of them: does the play dramatise a Keplerian distinction between a genuine but general celestial correlation and a deterministic prediction of particular deeds, or does it collapse that distinction? Examine Macbeth’s first encounter with the witches, Banquo’s sceptical resistance, and Macbeth’s soliloquy on feeling secure after the second prophecy, asking where Kepler’s framework illuminates a character’s error—treating a broad celestial influence as a necessary cause of a specific murder—and where the play resists the reading by making prophecy seem genuinely efficacious. Consider also whether Macbeth’s tragedy turns on a confusion between physical temperament and rational will of the kind Kepler sought to police. Please separate what is well established about Kepler’s astrological writings from what remains genuinely disputed among historians of science—especially whether his species mechanism was intended literally or heuristically, and whether his theological reservations about stellar determinism were sincerely held or partly strategic—without presenting either contested view as the consensus. Conclude by identifying what this philosophical lens reveals about Macbeth that a purely literary interpretation misses.
