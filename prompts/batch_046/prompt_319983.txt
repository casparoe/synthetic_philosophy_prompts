Consider the position I will call split-layer deference. The standard Principal Principle directs an agent to align her credences with the objective chances she assigns to outcomes. I propose splitting this into two independent channels: structural deference, which applies to propositions about physical outcomes independent of the agent's reasoning, and epistemic deference, which applies to propositions about the reliability of the agent's own cognitive processes, weighted by her second-order confidence in rationality. On this view, if you assign an objective structural chance of 0.7 to a quantum event and an objective epistemic weight of 0.6 to the soundness of your reasoning about that event, your all-things-considered credence should combine them multiplicatively rather than applying a single deference coefficient. What would have to be true for split-layer deference to fail? I want an honest map of its exposure: which formal results (for example, about Dutch-book vulnerability, self-locating belief, or Newcomb-style cases) threaten it specifically; what intuitive cases show that multiplying structural and epistemic weights yields the wrong credence; whether the position risks unfalsifiability by allowing defenders to reweight the layers after the fact; and whether advocates of the standard undivided Principal Principle should treat the split as a clarification or as a corruption. Please distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that is merely awkward, and assess whether the view has been insulated in ways that are philosophically problematic.
