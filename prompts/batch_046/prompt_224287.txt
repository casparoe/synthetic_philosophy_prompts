i've been arguing with a friend about whether Benatar's asymmetry survives once you stop treating existence and non-existence as neat binary states and start thinking about conditional creation, partial suffering, and internal compensation within a life. I'm not convinced my own intuitions dont line up, but here they are.

If I could bring someone into being with a life that's mostly good — net wellbeing clearly positive — I think it's morally okay but not required. The fact that they'd miss that good life if I don't create them doesn't seem like a loss, which sounds like standard Benatar. But if the life involves serious suffering, I think there's a strong reason not to create them, stronger than the mere permissibility in the first case.

Where it gets messy: suppose I can create a being who'd suffer a little bit but then experience a huge amount of pleasure that more than makes up for it. I still feel a pull not to do it, as if the initial suffering is a real moral cost that the later pleasure doesn't fully cancel. But then consider an already-existing being who's suffering a bit: I can either remove the suffering or add a ton of pleasure. Here I think removing the suffering is clearly good, but adding the pleasure is just nice, not obligatory — so suffering seems to have an asymmetrical weight even inside an existing life? And yet in the creation case I was treating suffering as something that can't be cancelled by pleasure, while in the existing-being case I'm treating them as comparable but with suffering having priority. There's also this weird edge case: if I actively prevent a being from coming into existence in a world with slight suffering, that feels wrong in a way that merely failing to create them in a pleasant world doesn't feel wrong in reverse. Is that agent-regret, or part of the principle?

Can you extract a principle — or a small set of candidates — that captures these verdicts? Be honest about which one I have to drop, especially the tension between the creation case and the existing-being case. And please say what your principle predicts for a scenario I haven't listed: a being whose consciousness is split so that one part experiences intense pleasure and another part experiences mild suffering at the same time, with the pleasure quantitatively outweighing the suffering but neither being causally linked to the other. Write in British English, and don't just list Benatar's original premises — I want to see whether there's a deeper structure here, not just the usual pretraining summary.
