I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving active research, have worked through moral philosophy systematically but without institutional affiliation. I am applying for a four-month independent study fellowship, and I need a formal research proposal for a single chapter of approximately eight thousand words on Bernard Williams's integrity objection and the case of an agent having one thought too many. The proposal should state a precise research question, explain why existing answers leave it open, describe the method and justify it against more obvious alternatives, specify what success and failure would look like, and include an honest assessment of scope for the timeline.

My framing departs somewhat from the standard literature. The usual treatment takes Williams's objection to be a broad indictment of impartial moral theories—utilitarianism and Kantianism—because they require the agent to justify her actions from an impersonal standpoint, thereby alienating her from her ground projects. I want to examine whether this framing conflates two demands that an impartial theory might impose: a deliberative demand that the agent represent her commitment in third-person, categorical terms during reasoning, and an evaluative demand that an external standard apply impartially in retrospective assessment. I will call the first representational mode-switching, and ask whether Williams's objection retains force against theories that impose only the second. The precise question is whether an agent can maintain full first-person, indexical commitment to a ground project—saving her partner, for example—while her action is governed by an impartial norm that never enters her own deliberative representation, and whether Williams still has an independent argument that such non-activating norms corrupt integrity.

Why the current debate does not settle this: commentators have generally assumed that any impartial moral theory requires explicit justificatory access within the agent's reasoning. But philosophy of action distinguishes norms that guide performance from norms that assess it. If impartial norms can function solely as assessment conditions—analogous to physical conservation laws that constrain outcomes without entering the dynamical equations—then Williams's examples do not clearly demonstrate a corruption of integrity, but only the corruption of an over-intellectualized moral psychology. Existing positions leave this open because they rarely separate the logical structure of a theory from its psychological implementation conditions.

My method will be conceptual reconstruction rather than historical scholarship. I will extract the logical form of Williams's original examples and the canonical responses, then apply the distinction between deliberative transparency and evaluative transparency—a distinction drawn from discussions of observer-dependence in philosophy of science, applied here to moral reasoning. For each reconstructed position, I will ask whether a model of skilled, non-rule-following agency can accommodate the impartial norm without mode-switching. This avoids the obvious alternatives of defending Williams against every objection or cataloguing all replies; instead, it attempts to dissolve part of the debate by showing that the objection targets a specific conjunction of impartiality and transparency, not impartiality alone.

The deliverable is a polished chapter arguing that at least two prominent impartialist frameworks can be formulated in versions that avoid representational mode-switching, and that Williams's intuitive force depends on an implicit assumption of deliberative transparency. The project fails if the analysis collapses into the familiar claim that all impartial theories are alienating, since that would show the mode-switching distinction adds no explanatory power. I will also test whether the distinction clarifies puzzles about supererogation and moral remainder, but I will restrict the domain to personal relationships and ground projects, avoiding extension to full particularism.

Scope and feasibility: four months is adequate for this narrow conceptual task because the primary textual basis is limited and the conceptual tools—indexical representation, transparency conditions, skilled agency—are drawn from philosophy of mind and philosophy of science, which I have studied independently. The principal risk is scope creep into a defense of particularism; I will guard against this by maintaining strict focus on transparency conditions within impartial theories. I will supply all citations and references in the final draft; please do not include a bibliography or literature review in the proposal itself.

The tone should reflect the analytical rigor of someone with a scientific background who is new to professional philosophy: precise framing, honest scope assessment, clear success and failure conditions, and no pretended mastery of the secondary literature.
