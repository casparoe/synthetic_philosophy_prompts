For a postgraduate philosophy seminar of about twelve students, meeting for seventy-five minutes, design a structured role-play and deliberation activity that makes participants feel the live force of Kripkenstein’s rule-following paradox—not merely as an abstract puzzle about private mental contents, but as a problem about the normativity of rules within a linguistic community. Centre the exercise on an invented, slightly arcane institutional framework: a fictional “Board of Lexical Adjudication” that governs whether neologisms may enter a technical register, operating under a founding statute (call it Rule 7) that has been applied consistently in all recorded past cases. Divide the students into three subgroups, and give each subgroup a subtly different “official history” of how Rule 7 has been interpreted—histories that are each fully consistent with the explicit record of past votes, but that diverge on whether the rule tracks an explicit criterion or a dispositional pattern in the Board’s practice. Present the groups with a genuinely novel borderline case that has never arisen before, and require each subgroup to reach a collective verdict on the correct application of Rule 7, then defend it before the others.

Arrange the rules and incentives so that every subgroup can justify its verdict as consistent with all observed past behaviour, yet no subgroup can appeal to a hidden fact—whether in the minds of past Board members or in the rule itself—that independently settles the correct answer. Use a small, non-monetary payoff scheme (for example, points that determine speaking order and voting weight in the final plenary) so that participants genuinely care about being “right” and experience the frustration of having no independent tribunal to appeal to. Provide a clear timeline, assigned roles (e.g., Chair, Recorder, Dissenter), the minimal materials needed, and instructions that prevent the facilitator from explaining the philosophical point until the debrief.

In the debrief, map specific moments in the activity onto parts of Kripkenstein’s argument: identify the point at which participants realise that every possible application is compatible with past practice (the skeptical argument), and the point at which they fall back on communal assertibility conditions to restore order (the skeptical solution). Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run—for instance, one subgroup insisting that its interpretation is objectively correct despite the parity of histories, or participants conflating the skeptical paradox with ordinary disagreement—and advise the facilitator on how to handle this without delivering a lecture. Finally, state frankly which feature of Kripkenstein’s view the activity cannot reproduce—such as the metaphysical status of meaning-facts or the philosophical distinction between the skeptical argument and the skeptical solution as a matter of justification rather than social dynamics—and explain how the facilitator must supply that element verbally.

Write the response in British English. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the activity should be entirely self-contained and runnable without reference to external texts.
