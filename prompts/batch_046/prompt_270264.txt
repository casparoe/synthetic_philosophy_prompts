I am writing for an upper-division seminar on Buddhist metaphysics and personal identity, and I have constructed a chain of reasoning that seems to show the doctrine of anattā undermines not only the self but the coherence of karma and dependent origination themselves. I want to preserve both anattā and a robust account of karmic continuity, but I cannot locate the precise misstep, and the conclusion feels like it proves too much.

(1) Anattā holds that there is no permanent, partless, enduring self; what we call a person is only a dependently originated stream of momentary mental and physical aggregates (skandhas).

(2) If there is no enduring self, then the being that performs an action at t₁ and the being that experiences its result at t₂ are not numerically identical.

(3) Moral responsibility and karmic accountability require that the subject who receives reward or punishment be numerically identical to the agent who acted.

(4) Therefore, anattā seems to entail that no one is ever karmically responsible for past deeds.

(5) Buddhist ethics presupposes that actions have moral weight precisely because they shape the future conditions of the same continuing being.

(6) So anattā appears to collapse the motivational structure of Buddhist practice.

But the argument does not stop there. If I apply the same reasoning consistently across domains:

(7) Conventional entities generally—chariots, flames, rivers—are also nothing over and above successive moments or parts arranged in functional continuity.

(8) If numerical identity over time is required for any causal or moral relation to hold, then not only personal karma but all causal relations become incoherent, since no cause is numerically identical to its effect.

(9) Yet pratītyasamutpāda asserts precisely that conditions and results are linked by causal efficacy without requiring a permanent substrate.

(10) So if I reject (3) for persons in order to save karma, consistency seems to demand that I reject it for all phenomena, which would destroy causation altogether; but if I keep (3), I must abandon anattā.

Where exactly does this chain fail? Is (3) smuggling in a substantialist assumption about identity that Buddhism already rejects? Or is there a hidden modal slide between “not numerically identical” and “not the appropriate bearer of results”? I would prefer to keep (1) together with the ethical framework built on karma, but I want to know which step to reject and what that rejection costs—particularly whether it forces me into a two-truths framework in which conventional continuity is sufficient for karma but insufficient for personal identity, or whether it implicitly commits me to an Abhidharmic account of momentariness and immediate causal contiguity (samanantara-pratyaya) that I have not explicitly endorsed.
