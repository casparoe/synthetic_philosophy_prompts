I'm prepping a discussion for my philosophy of identity seminar and we've been stuck on Oscar Wilde's The Picture of Dorian Gray, specifically how it seems to run the Ship of Theseus backwards. Usually the paradox is about replacing planks until nothing original remains, but here Dorian's body never changes — the portrait in the attic takes all the physical and moral wear — so its like the ship stays intact while a hidden twin absorbs every replaced part. I'd like you to read the novel through that inverted identity puzzle: are Dorian and the painting temporal parts of one four-dimensional self, or are they separate entities bound by psychological continuity? Pay close attention to the scenes where he hides the portrait away and the final moment when he stabs it and dies — does destroying the material record of corruption collapse the identity, and does that support bodily continuity or actually undermine it? I want the reading to be honest both ways: don't just use the book to illustrate the paradox, and don't distort the paradox to save the plot. Where does Wilde's version illuminate the debate about moral versus physical parts, and where does the story resist being mapped onto standard theories?

Also, my study group kept asking whether Dorian's psychological continuity — he remembers his sins, he feels guilt — is enough to keep him the same person even though his body is 'replaced' by the painting's aging, or whether the painting functions as a kind of moral counterpart in the Lewisian sense. I think there's something genuinely weird about the ending that standard Ship-of-Theseus talk misses, but I can't put my finger on it. If you think the novel actually breaks the analogy rather than extending it, say so.
