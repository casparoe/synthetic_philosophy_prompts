I'm studying debates over national curriculum reform and have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern that seems more specific than the standard concepts I know, though I may be missing the precise vocabulary. A state introduces a contemporary civic-education framework—say, one that treats linguistic uniformity or a particular secular order as foundational national traits. Historians object that these traits, in their current explicit form, are largely twentieth- or twenty-first-century constructions, and they document clear discontinuities with earlier practice. Rather than denying the record, defenders concede that the explicit institutional form is recent, but insist it expresses a deeper logic that was always implicitly present in the nation's constitutional culture, collective habits, or self-understanding. Once that implicit continuity is asserted, the modern framework is presented not as an innovation but as a clarification or recovery. Crucially, defenders then cite the framework's present stability or popular acceptance as retrospective evidence that the implicit logic was genuinely operative all along—so current success validates the historical narrative, and the historical narrative validates current policy.

I am familiar with Hobsbawm's analysis of invented traditions and Anderson's account of imagined communities, yet this loop feels distinct: it is not merely the fabrication of antiquity, but a circular legitimation in which a modern institutional fact is used to retroactively ground an essentialist past, which in turn justifies the institution. Does this configuration have an established name in political theory, sociology of knowledge, or the philosophy of history? If it does not map neatly onto existing concepts, I would welcome a careful distinction—how it differs from an ordinary invented tradition, from retrospective coherence, and from straightforward essentialism—together with a proposed term, its defining conditions, and its boundary cases. I am less interested in a bare label than in a precise carving of what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
