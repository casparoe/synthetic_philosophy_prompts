Here is an argument assembled in a seminar on supererogation that now seems to erase the very distinction it was meant to protect, and I cannot locate the exit. (1) An act is supererogatory if and only if it is morally valuable but not morally required of the agent. (2) What is required of an agent is determined by the balance of moral reasons that apply specifically to her, given her capacities and circumstances. (3) Moral reasons of the same type and strength are interpersonally fungible: if reason R applies to agent A with strength s, and reason R' applies to agent B with strength s and identical content, then R and R' generate the same normative status for A and B respectively. (4) Take the canonical case: a lone bystander can save a drowning child at moderate risk to herself. The reason to save the child applies with full strength, so she is required to act; if she does more than the minimum, the excess is supererogatory. (5) Now suppose ten equally able bystanders are present. By (3), each faces the same reason with the same strength. (6) If each faced an unreduced duty to save the child, then nine would be required to stand idle once one acted, which is absurd; therefore the presence of others must dilute each individual's reason, reducing it from a duty to a meritorious option. (7) Apply this dilution logic within a single agent's life. An agent faces innumerable small reasons to benefit others—giving directions, donating minor sums, volunteering brief periods—each weak in isolation but collectively enormous in value. (8) By the reasoning in (6) and (7), none of these small reasons reaches the threshold of duty, yet together they form a vast field of meritorious options. (9) But there is no principled, non-arbitrary boundary between the diluted small reasons and the undiluted large ones; any cutoff is conventional. (10) Hence either we reject dilution and make every beneficent act a duty, or we apply dilution consistently and make none a duty; in either case, the category of the supererogatory collapses. I am inclined to preserve (1) and (7)—the intuition that others' presence genuinely alters my obligations—so I need to know which premise to surrender and what that surrender costs. Where exactly does the reasoning fail?
