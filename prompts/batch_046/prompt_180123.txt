(3) If rationality requires that closure, then it's required for any decision problem with counterfactual structure, not just sequential games.

(4) So a rational agent must have fixed preferences and beliefs about every unreached world, and can't update on new info without revising beliefs across infinitely many counterfactual branches, which violates standard Bayesian constraints.

(5) Worse, if two agents can only recognize each other as rational after mapping responses across every possible deviation, then rational social interaction is epistemically impossible.

(6) So backward induction, read as a normative closure condition, makes rational cooperation self-defeating.

(7) But cooperation clearly happens, so the closure condition must be false.

(8) Without it, though, backward induction collapses, and with it goes the standard fix for Newcomb-style problems and any sequential consistency axiom.

(9) So either rationality is incompatible with social life, or we have to ditch a huge chunk of decision theory.

Where do I pull the thread? Which premise looks fine but actually slips — maybe a scope shift between game-theoretic common knowledge and general Bayesian closure?
