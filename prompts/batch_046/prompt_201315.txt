I am a doctoral student in a course on rationality and epistemic game theory, and I want to understand why a debate in our reading group has remained deadlocked for over a year despite broad agreement on both the formal results and the behavioral data. The dispute concerns the failure of backward induction in real agents playing finite games of perfect information—particularly centipede-style interactions. One faction, which I will call the accessibility camp, argues that the failure is fundamentally epistemic: human agents lack the representational architecture to stabilize the infinite hierarchy of beliefs that common knowledge of rationality requires. They grant that subjects often hold accurate first-order beliefs about an opponent's rationality, but they claim that second-order and higher-order representations are noisy, unstable, or cognitively inaccessible. They point out that rationalizability technically requires only mutual belief, not full common knowledge, and they take this formal point to show that real agents operate under bounded epistemic conditions rather than rejecting rationality itself. The other faction, the transparency camp, accepts all of these empirical and formal claims. They agree that representational capacity is bounded and that mutual belief is the correct formal standard. But they insist that the accessibility camp confuses a representational bottleneck with a normative one. They propose that strategic reasoning requires not merely representing another agent's rationality, but treating it as normatively authoritative for one's own choice—a condition they call normative transparency. They note that when experiments provide explicit reasoning prompts that augment representational capacity, backward induction rates do not significantly increase, which they take as evidence that the obstacle is not access but uptake: agents do not treat the opponent's projected reasoning as binding. The two sides therefore agree on the formal distinction between mutual belief and common knowledge, on the behavioral data from laboratory games, and on the cognitive limits of belief representation. Yet neither has moved. The accessibility camp says transparency is just a redescription of failed representation at a higher order; the transparency camp says representation is irrelevant without normative uptake. They seem to be answering different questions under the single heading "why does backward induction fail in practice," but I am unsure whether this is a verbal disagreement, a hidden empirical dispute about the neural or computational mechanisms of belief revision, a disagreement about what counts as a reason for action, or a clash of bedrock intuitions about whether rationality is essentially representational or normative.

My own hypothesis is that part of the stall is verbal—"common knowledge" is being used sometimes as an epistemic condition and sometimes as a normative one—but I also suspect there may be a hidden empirical question about whether explicit reasoning prompts genuinely augment second-order representation or merely change the salience of cooperative norms, and I worry that the question may be ill-posed if it assumes a single explanation must cover both representational failure and normative resistance.
