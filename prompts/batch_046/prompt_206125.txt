I am a high school student preparing an independent research essay on cognitive taxonomy, specifically Daniel Dennett's classification of minds into Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures. Recently, in a study group with older students, I witnessed a sustained disagreement between two participants — I will call them Maya and Leo — about whether the Gregorian category marks a genuinely new kind of mind or is merely a socially enriched version of the Popperian kind. I have reconstructed their exchange below from my notes and would like your help adjudicating it.

Maya began by summarizing the four kinds as nested evolutionary achievements: Darwinian creatures are hard-wired by natural selection; Skinnerian creatures can learn through individual reinforcement; Popperian creatures can generate and test hypotheses internally, taking risks in thought rather than in action; and Gregorian creatures add language and culture to that framework. She then argued that Gregorianity does not represent a qualitative jump comparable to the earlier transitions. Language, she said, is simply the most powerful thinking tool available, but it still operates within the Popperian mechanism of generating and selecting representations. The only real difference, in her view, is that Gregorian creatures inherit some representations from culture rather than inventing them personally — a quantitative expansion of available data, not a change in the underlying cognitive architecture. She concluded that Dennett overstates the break: a solitary Popperian creature with an unusually large memory and symbolic notation could, in principle, perform the same kinds of reasoning, only more slowly and with greater individual effort.

Leo responded that this misses the structural significance of cultural transmission. He agreed that Gregorian creatures are Popperians who possess language, but insisted that language and culture introduce a second inheritance system — analogous to Dawkinsian memes — that operates on a different timescale and with different selection pressures than either genetic evolution or individual learning. Because Gregorian creatures can acquire complex thinking tools, such as mathematical notation, logical methods, or scientific frameworks, without having generated them through their own internal trial and error, their cognition is not merely faster but scaffolded by an external evolutionary process. Leo argued that this creates a genuinely new selection environment: the creature's hypotheses are partly shaped by centuries of cultural selection, which means the generate-and-test loop is distributed across generations. He suggested that this is precisely why Dennett treats Gregorianity as a separate kind rather than a well-equipped Popperian variant.

Maya pushed back by asking whether scaffolding is anything more than a metaphor for having richer inputs. She pointed out that even a solitary scientist uses instruments, written notes, and libraries — external aids — yet we do not say her mind is structurally different from another scientist who works from memory. The mechanism, she maintained, remains internal simulation; culture just provides a larger database of representations to simulate with. She also raised a boundary problem: if Gregorianity requires cultural inheritance, then the line becomes blurry. A chimpanzee that learns nut-cracking techniques from its mother exhibits some social transmission, yet we do not classify it as Gregorian. If the boundary is continuous, Maya argued, then Gregorianity is a matter of degree, not a distinct kind.

Leo conceded that the margins are indeed blurry — some animals exhibit limited social learning that approaches the threshold — but he maintained that there is a meaningful cutoff, perhaps the capacity to participate in a cumulative culture where modifications are systematically preserved, refined, and recombined across generations. He argued that Maya's reduction treats culture as an input stream to an individual mind, whereas for true Gregorian creatures culture is constitutive: they think in languages and conceptual frameworks that they could not have invented alone, which changes not just what they think but what kinds of thoughts are available to them at all. He finished by noting that if Gregorianity were only quantitative, there would be little reason to distinguish it as a fourth kind rather than treating language as one more Popperian tool.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For Maya's summary of the taxonomy, her argument about quantitative versus qualitative change, Leo's appeal to cultural evolution and scaffolding, Maya's counterexample about instruments and social learning in animals, and Leo's claim about constitutive culture, please say who is closer to being right, what qualifications are needed, and where both might be missing something. In particular, please separate what is broadly settled among philosophers and cognitive scientists who work with Dennett's framework from what is genuinely contested interpretation, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. I am especially interested in whether the Gregorian-Popperian boundary is sharp or continuous, and whether culture is best understood as an input to an individual mind or as part of the mind's architecture itself.
