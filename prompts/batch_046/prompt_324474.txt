Consider the French institutional arrangement in which public-school teachers, as civil servants, are barred from displaying religious symbols; students in state schools are prohibited from wearing conspicuous religious symbols; the state nonetheless funds private confessional schools under contract and preserves religious buildings as national heritage; and the Concordat regime remains in force in Alsace-Moselle. What philosophical conception of citizenship, equality, and the boundary between public and private does this specific combination of rules presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it depend on a layer-cake model of the self, in which religious identity is a private substrate that must be masked in civic space, or can it accommodate a web-like model in which identity is constituted through visible, embodied practices? Carefully distinguish what the arrangement strictly requires from what merely coheres with it. Assess whether the practice is internally consistent, or whether it tacitly invokes two incompatible frameworks—one that treats visible religious expression as a form of domination over the shared public sphere, and another that treats such expression as a legitimate cultural pluralism deserving state subsidy. Finally, if this underlying philosophical commitment were replaced by an Anglo-American multiculturalist view that treats visible religious expression as fully compatible with equal citizenship, what specific institutional changes would necessarily follow in rules governing teacher employment, student dress, and heritage preservation?
