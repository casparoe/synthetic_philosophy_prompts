I have been working on notes for an essay about epistemic dependence on large language models and what I take to be a specific, under-described form of understanding erosion that standard epistemology tends to overlook. Rather than framing LLM dependence as a simple reduction in factual knowledge or in practical skill, I propose a tripartite distinction among modes of AI-assisted cognition: tool-use, in which the system extends working memory or computation without displacing the user’s judgment; delegation, in which the user actively transfers evaluative authority to the model for particular tasks; and assimilation, in which the model’s outputs are incorporated into the user’s belief system without any retained access to the reasoning steps that produced them. My central claim is that assimilation generates what I call opaque competence—the ability to apply concepts correctly in familiar contexts coupled with a systematic failure when surface features change—precisely because the user never internalized the underlying inferential structure. I further argue that this erosion is asymmetric across domains: it is more severe in conceptual fields such as philosophy or mathematics than in empirical ones, since in conceptual domains the justificatory path is partly constitutive of understanding, whereas in empirical domains external verification pathways typically remain available. I want to avoid collapsing this argument into the familiar worry that search engines or calculators make people intellectually lazy.
