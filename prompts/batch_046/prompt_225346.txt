For my seminar in the moral philosophy of urban space, I need a designed conceptual scenario that isolates whether local conventions about place generate genuine moral obligations or merely coordination norms. Please construct a thought experiment that satisfies the following specifications.

Present a realistic case—set in a municipal planning review, not in science fiction—in a single paragraph. A visiting planner, with no residential or economic stake in the neighborhood, evaluates an affordable-housing proposal that fully satisfies universal criteria of accessibility, density, and carbon neutrality. The proposal violates a long-standing, democratically ratified local convention: a solar-access covenant that preserves direct sunlight on a shared waterfront promenade. The residents defend this covenant not through property title or economic exclusion, but through what I will term "indexical place-rights"—a non-transferable moral entitlement to the continued character of shared spatial goods that derives from long-term participatory embeddedness in the place rather than from general welfare maximization or an impersonal right-to-the-city framework. Applying that universal accessibility framework consistently, the planner approves the development; the residents invoke indexical place-rights to reject it. The planner must not be malicious or ignorant, and the residents' opposition must be structurally irreducible to disguised NIMBYism.

After presenting the scenario, please do the following: (1) state the specific judgment each party reaches and trace the reasoning path that leads there; (2) identify exactly which structural feature forces the two frameworks to diverge—whether it is the indexicality of the entitlement, the non-substitutability of the solar-access good, or the planner's absence from the participatory history; (3) demonstrate what happens if that feature is removed, and whether the conflict then collapses into a standard distributive dispute over housing versus aesthetics; (4) outline how a defender of indexical place-rights would respond to the objection that such rights are only parochial preferences dressed in moral language, and indicate whether that defense requires accepting a form of moral particularism about place. Please avoid bibliographies, citations, named authors, or references to specific texts; the exercise should stand entirely on its own conceptual structure.
