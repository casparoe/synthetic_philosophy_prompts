Consider the contrastive framing common in Great Divergence literature: "why Britain and not China?" Rather than asking for a causal narrative, examine whether this question is conceptually well-posed at all. One concern is that it treats British industrialization as the event demanding explanation while treating Qing China's sustained agrarian trajectory as an implicit baseline, which may conflate the absence of a specific transition with the presence of an inhibitory condition. A second concern is comparator arbitrariness: without a prior theory of necessary conditions, the set of possible counterfactual cases—France, the Netherlands, Mughal India—expands without principled limit, risking an unfalsifiable multivariate comparison. Explain whether a coherent distinction can be drawn between explaining an occurrence (industrial takeoff) and explaining a non-occurrence (the absence of an analogous takeoff), such that the asymmetry is preserved without teleological smuggling. If the framing is salvageable, specify what constraints on the comparator class and on the modal status of the counterfactual—e.g., holding constant coal endowments, global trade integration, or institutional inheritance—are required to prevent the question from dissolving into a mere list of contingent differences.
