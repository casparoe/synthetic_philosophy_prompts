A colleague offered me the following summary of John von Neumann's comparison between neural and digital computation: von Neumann maintained that the brain is fundamentally a binary digital computer realized in neurons; that its notorious unreliability and stochastic noise are design flaws that massive redundancy only patches over; and that his theory of self-replicating automata proves consciousness is mechanistically reducible, because any system complex enough to reproduce itself must necessarily possess cognitive capacities. From this, my colleague infers that modern neuroscience—which emphasizes analog signaling, graded potentials, and intrinsic neural noise—has not merely updated von Neumann's analogy but empirically falsified it, revealing that his mechanist philosophy of mind rested on an obsolete digital metaphor. I think this characterization may conflate separate parts of von Neumann's work and misread his attitude toward analog processes and error statistics. Which elements of the summary accurately reflect the arguments he advanced in his brain-computer comparison, which are subtle distortions—particularly regarding the logical versus analog character of neural processes and the relationship between self-replication and cognition—and does the contemporary neuroscience objection actually target the specific comparison he defended, or does it defeat only a stronger thesis he never held?
