I was arguing with a friend about Bentham's essays on sexuality and the law, specifically how he treats acts that society calls 'unnatural', and I realized there's a pattern I keep seeing that feels like a named fallacy or a recogized tension in utilitarian ethics, but I can't place it. Bentham argues that homosexual acts shouldn't be criminalized because they don't cause physical harm to anyone involved, and he explicitly rejects the idea that they violate some natural law. But then, in the same breath, he warns that if these practices become visible they might cause 'alarm' or 'disgust' in the public, which he treats as a kind of secondary harm that the law might need to manage. It's not hypocrisy exactly — he's being consistent about applying the harm principle — but there's this weird loop where the only harm he can identify is the harm caused by society's own reaction to the act, and he uses that reaction to justify keeping the act hidden or regulated rather than protected. I've seen this in other contexts too, where a reformer uses a harm-based framework to defend a stigmatized practice but ends up treating the stigma itself as a fixed parameter that the practice must accommodate. Does this loop have a name? Is it a recognized problem in Bentham scholarship specifically, or in liberal political theory more broadly? I'm not asking for a bibliography, just whether there's an established concept for this kind of self-undermining harm calculus, and if not, how you'd distinguish it from plain strategic compromise or from just accepting that social costs matter.
