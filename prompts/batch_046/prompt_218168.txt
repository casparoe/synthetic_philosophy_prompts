I'm taking a seminar on nineteenth-century liberalism and empire, and I've become puzzled by a specific feature of British colonial administration in India. The government applied what was known as the doctrine of lapse: when a princely ruler died without a direct natural male heir, the state was annexed on the grounds that sovereignty was a revocable administrative trust rather than hereditary property. This practice coexisted with the official justification—associated with thinkers like Mill—that British rule was temporary tutelage for peoples not yet capable of self-government. What implicit theory of political value and legitimacy does the doctrine of lapse presuppose? Must it rest on a functionalist view of sovereignty as an office whose continuity serves aggregate welfare, or could it be grounded in a liberal theory of property rights in governance, or in a civilizational-stage theory of who may hold sovereign authority? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and say whether it is philosophically underdetermined—compatible with rival frameworks—or essentially tied to Mill's tutelary liberalism. Finally, describe what an annexation rule built on purely consent-based sovereignty would have to change about the doctrine's core logic.
