I've been working through ideas in distributive justice on my own, trying not to simply repeat what I vaguely recall from popular writing, and I'm stuck on what looks like a contradiction in how philosophers discuss the leveling-down objection. As I understand prioritarianism, it assigns greater moral weight to benefits that go to people who are worse off—something like applying a concave transformation to each person's well-being and then summing the results. If that's correct, then lowering the well-being of someone near the top should reduce their contribution to the total without helping anyone else, so prioritarianism should reject destructive leveling. But here is where my reasoning seems to turn around: in a simple two-person case with one person at welfare level 10 and another at 100, egalitarianism just demands equality; it doesn't contain any internal calculus that rewards harming the well-off unless equality is actually achieved. Prioritarianism, by contrast, builds in a comparative weighting scheme that constantly measures how far each person is from the others, which feels like it should make downward equalization attractive whenever the concave curve makes the loss at the top seem smaller than the gain in parity. So why do philosophers present the leveling-down objection as a problem specifically for egalitarianism and claim that prioritarianism escapes it? Am I wrong to think that the weighting function itself creates a hidden incentive to pull the top down, or is there an unstated restriction—perhaps that prioritarian weights apply only to actual benefits received rather than to relative standing—that does all the defensive work? I'd like to know whether my intuition about a hidden leveling-down risk in prioritarianism is just a mistake in applying the weights, or whether the standard defense quietly relies on a constraint that hasn't been made explicit.
