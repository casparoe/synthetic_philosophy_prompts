I'm trying to sort out what I actually think about calling things genocide, both in the legal sense that Lemkin originally pushed for and in the looser moral/political sense people use now. I was talking with friends about this last week and realized my gut verdicts don't line up neatly, especially once you factor in how governments avoid the word for strategic reasons.

Here are my takes on some cases. The Armenian mass killings of 1915: genocide, full stop, legally and morally, and I think denying it is mainly poltics. The Holodomor in Ukraine in the 1930s: morally it feels like genocide because the famine was engineered against Ukrainians as a group, but I'm less sure about the legal box since intent is murky and some historians push back hard. The Rwandan killings in 1994: clear genocide, no ambiguity. The Khmer Rouge years in Cambodia: I split it - the targeting of Cham Muslims and ethnic Vietnamese is genocide, but the mass killing of ethnic Khmers based on class is horrific but not genocide under the usual definintion, even if the death toll is enormous. The Herero and Namaqua genocide in German South West Africa around 1904-1908: yes, both categories, and it's strange that it's less named in public memory. Then there's what I'd call "paper dissolution" - imagine a state that never kills anyone directly but legally dissolves a minority's collective status, seizes all communal land, and mandates the seperate dispersal of families into the majority population so the group can't reproduce socially. I think this should count as genocide morally, and maybe even legally if you read Lemkin broadly, but I'm genuinely not convinced.

But here's where I'm stuck and going back and forth. The residential school systems for Indigenous children in Canada and Australia: forced assimilation, family seperation, high death rates, but not always direct extermination intent. Sometimes I think it's cultural genocide and should be named as such; other times I worry that stretching the word that far makes it useless for cases like Rwanda. And then there's the politics of naming atrocities that aren't quite at that scale - like if a government carries out systematic ethnic cleansing with killings in the hundreds but not thousands, and activists call it genocide while international lawyers hesitate. I tend to side with the activists morally but think the legal precision gets lost, which feels like a contradiction I can't shake.

So what's the principle behind these verdicts? Is there one account that captures why I say yes to Armenia and Herero, split on Cambodia, hesitate on Holodomor, want to include paper dissolution, and waffle on residential schools? Or do I have to drop one of these verdicts to keep things coherent? And what should I say about a case I haven't listed - say, a state that uses AI-driven predictive policing to systematically incarcerate every male member of a specific clan, not killing them but permanently breaking kinship structures? Does that fall under my principle or outside it?

Please say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you're least sure of.
