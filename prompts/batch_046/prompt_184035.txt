Hey, I'm trying to get my head straight on the statue/clay thing for a discussion group and want a consistency check before I open my mouth. Here is where I stand (probably a mess):

a) I'm basically a hylomorphist about artifacts: the statue and the lump aren't identical because they have different forms. The form of a statue isn't just shape; it also includes being recogntion'd as that specific artwork by the relevant art-world practice — institutional status is literally part of the form.

b) There's an asymetric grounding relation: the statue exists in virtue of the clay being arranged and recognized that way, but not vice versa. And I buy the principle that if the ground is destroyed, the grounded thing usually goes with it, unless something weird is going on.

c) For persistence, I think artifacts carry a material anchor condition: to stay the same statue, at least 40% of the original material substrate has to remain continuously present. If you gradualy replace every molecule (Ship of Theseus style) but keep shape and recognition going, once you drop below 40% original matter it's a replica, not the original. The clay itself dies earlier — I'd say once over 50% of its original molecules are gone, that particular lump is destroyed and replaced by a numerically different one.

d) If you suddenly crush the thing, the statue is destroyed and what's left is just a new lump with the same old molecules but zero art-status.

e) I don't think identity is ever vague. Either it's the same statue or it isn't; there's no fuzzy middle.

f) Because form is partly conventional, I think two physically identical lumps in different instituional contexts could be different statues, or one could be art and one just junk.

So, tensions. There's an obvious-looking one between (c) and (d): crushing leaves 100% of the original molecules, so by my 40% rule shouldn't the statue survive? I think that dissolves once you notice form is destroyed too — no shape, no recognition — so that's not a real conflict.

The ones I'm actually worried about: (b) and (c) together seem to clash with gradual replacement. If the original clay-lump is destroyed once >50% is replaced, and the statue is grounded in that specific lump, then by (b) the statue should die right then. But by (c) it keeps going as long as 40% remains, even though its original ground is gone. So either grounding can shift bases, which I never endorsed, or I'm wrong about one of them.

Also (a) and (c) feel like they're pulling in opposite directions. If form is partly institutional, why should the material substrate matter so precisely? I could imagine the museum swapping out 70% of the clay gradualy but keeping catalog entry and shape intact, and by my lights it's now a replica — but if form is conventional, shouldn't the institution's say-so be enough? So there's a tension between the conventionalist part of my form-view and the material-essentialist part of my persistence conditions.

Can you audit this? Rank the tensions by severity, say what the cheapest fix is for each, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most mess without me having to abandon hylomorphism entirely. Also tell me if there's a hidden problem I missed — I don't want citations or a reading list, just whether these hang together.
