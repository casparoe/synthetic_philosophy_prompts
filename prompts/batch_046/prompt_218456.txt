I'm advising the ethics review board for a regional federation of platform cooperatives—worker-owned digital labor platforms—and we need a reusable audit procedure to evaluate whether a given cooperative genuinely eliminates Marxist exploitation and alienation, or merely redistributes them. We convene monthly, have roughly two hours per proposal, and our reviewers are practitioners—co-op members, labor lawyers, one philosopher—rather than Marx scholars. The stakes are concrete: a positive review unlocks public procurement contracts, but we currently have no framework for distinguishing structural exploitation from contractual inconvenience, or for identifying alienation that persists despite democratic ownership. I need something they can run without consulting Capital or the early manuscripts.

The procedure should be grounded in Marx's two core critiques: exploitation as surplus extraction through control of the means of production, and alienation as the separation of the worker from the product, the labor process, human species-being, and fellow workers. But it must also operationalize a distinction our board has adopted but never formalized—the difference between "extractive alienation," where surplus or decisive control is captured by an external party owning the technical or financial infrastructure, and "distributive alienation," where surplus remains inside the cooperative but decision-making over the conditions of labor is structurally separated from the workers who endure those conditions. This axis is not standard in textbooks, yet it is central to our cases.

Please construct a step-by-step decision procedure my reviewers can run in sequence, with explicit stopping rules: at each step, state what evidence is required, what counts as sufficient, and whether the step yields a definitive failure, a conditional pass, or a mandatory hand-off to human judgment because the question is one of values rather than observable structure. The procedure must survive simplification—reviewers will not parse dense economic texts under deadline—but it must preserve distinctions that actually matter, such as the fact that democratic profit-sharing does not by itself eliminate exploitation if the cooperative still depends on an external algorithm or capital structure that determines the pace and content of work. Apply it to three proposals: (1) a delivery cooperative where drivers collectively own the dispatch software but a venture partner holds an irrevocable veto over pricing algorithms and route optimization; (2) a graphic-design collective where members share revenue equally and vote on clients, but are required to use a proprietary generative-AI interface that structures every creative interaction with the client and standardizes output; (3) a cleaning cooperative that is fully worker-owned and self-managed, but operates in a sector with such thin margins that members routinely work unpaid overtime to maintain contracts, with no external owner to blame.

For each case, show exactly where the procedure stops, what judgment call it forces, and—crucially—include at least one case where the procedure returns a result the board will find unwelcome or counterintuitive, explaining why the framework demands that result despite the cooperative's democratic form. Finally, specify the hard limits of the procedure: which questions about dignity, autonomy, or meaningful work it explicitly cannot settle, and where a reviewer must sign off personally rather than delegate to the checklist.
