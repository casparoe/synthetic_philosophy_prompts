I'm putting together a kinda weird syllabus for a philosophy-of-logic reading group that meets in my basement every other thursday. We're tired of the usual routine where a sentence just declares itself false, so I cooked up a variant I'm calling the "echo liar": someone writes What I am writing right now is not true, but then imediately erases the second half, leaving only What I am writing right now is followed by a blank space. The claim seems to point at its own missing conclusion, so whether it's false depends on whether the erased part still counts as part of the statement's content, which makes the syntax/semantics boundary feel pretty shaky.

Can you rank these five approaches—correspondence theory, coherence theory, deflationary minimalism, paraconsistent logic in the style of Graham Priest, and Tarski's hierarchical language-level solution—from best to worst at handling the echo liar? Give each a tier (S, A, B, C, or F) and in one sentence say why it lands there. I'm not asking for a bibliography or anything, just your gut philosophical take, and if one of them clearly collapses the distinction between syntax and semantics in a way that makes the erased text irrelevant, mention that in its sentence.
