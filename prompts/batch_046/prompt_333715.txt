I've been trying to formalize the conditions for ontology identification, and I keep returning to what looks like a strict accessibility principle: two putative entities or structures are genuinely ontologically distinct only if there exists some physically permitted observation or inference—actual or counterfactual—that could distinguish them. Where all such pathways are permanently blocked—whether by an event horizon, by quantum no-signalling constraints, or by fundamental computational opacity—the corresponding descriptions should be treated as notational variants rather than competing ontologies. Applied consistently, this seems to dissolve standard debates about black-hole interiors, about empirically equivalent quantum interpretations, and about hidden-variable configurations that reproduce identical statistics. Yet these are treated in physics and philosophy as substantive questions about what exists. Is the error in assuming that ontology requires this kind of epistemic openness? Or is there a defensible account—say, through structural reference, theoretical-role semantics, or causal-chain anchoring—that fixes ontological commitment independently of observational access, without collapsing into an unmotivated realism about every mathematically possible variant?
