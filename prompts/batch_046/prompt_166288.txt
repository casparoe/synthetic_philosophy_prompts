You serve as the senior evaluation officer for a pilot residential program governed by tranquilism: the principle that only the absence of craving and of suffering carries non-instrumental value, and that every other apparent good—social belonging, creative achievement, intellectual understanding, moral virtue—has value solely as an instrument to reduce craving or suffering, with zero independent weight. You review proposed four-week therapeutic modules for incoming residents and must approve, deny, or flag each for conditional approval. You have thirty minutes per proposal, no access to external literature, and your decision must be co-signed by the clinical director, who has no philosophical training but must defend it to a funding board.

Write a reusable procedural checklist—ordered tests, decision nodes, and explicit stop points—through which an officer can apply tranquilist evaluation consistently without philosophical expertise. The procedure must: (i) distinguish genuine instrumental reduction of craving and suffering from mere substitution of one craving for another (e.g., a module that eliminates social anxiety but instills craving for continued access to the module, or for the identity of being a "deep practitioner"); (ii) reject appeals to intrinsic goods (dignity, character, knowledge for its own sake) as justifications unless they are shown to causally reduce craving or suffering; (iii) specify the evidence an officer can gather in thirty minutes (resident self-report, behavioral indicators, clinician observation) and weight it; (iv) mark precisely where clinical judgment cannot be eliminated by the checklist; and (v) state what the procedure cannot settle, requiring the officer and director to sign off jointly.

Include three worked applications: (1) a module that clearly passes; (2) a module conventionally considered valuable (e.g., narrative-identity reconstruction or aesthetic-immersion training) that the procedure must deny or severely restrict because its benefits are intrinsic rather than tranquilist; and (3) an ambiguous case—such as a social-reconnection protocol that sharply reduces suffering but generates a subtle, persistent craving for relational validation—where the procedure yields a recommendation you find personally unwelcome but must accept if tranquilism is taken seriously, with the point of residual uncertainty clearly noted.

Finally, state how confident you are in the procedure's fidelity to tranquilism, and single out the one conceptual or empirical point about which you are least certain.
