I work as a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, but I have been reflecting on how societies ought to calibrate rule-following—what is sometimes framed as the tension between tight and loose cultural norms. I want to defend a specific design-oriented position: societies should maximize normative tightness, meaning rules should be explicit, minimally exception-laden, and enforced with high uniformity. My reasoning borrows from distributed systems: ambiguous social protocols behave like race conditions or undefined interface contracts; they produce coordination failures, deadlock, and cascading errors that outweigh any adaptability gains. Loose norms that tolerate context-sensitive exceptions are analogous to runtime mutation or dynamic typing in critical infrastructure—acceptable only when the environment is genuinely non-stationary. In stable domains such as contract enforcement, safety regulation, or property rights, looseness is therefore not a feature but accumulated technical debt.

I am asking for an honest map of what would count against this view. I do not want generic appeals to liberty or cultural diversity. Instead, I want specific conceptual counterexamples, formal results, or empirical patterns that should weaken or overturn the claim. For example: do tightly governed societies exhibit brittle failure modes—catastrophic collapse under shock—that loose societies absorb more gracefully? Would evolutionary or game-theoretic models show that maximal tightness is evolutionarily unstable because it eliminates the variation needed for adaptation? Is there a philosophical argument that the authority of rules depends on an underlying practice of interpretive flexibility, so that stripping away exceptions undermines legitimacy rather than strengthening it? I want these defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an indication of which ones a committed defender of maximal tightness could concede without abandoning the core analogy. Finally, I want an assessment of where the engineering analogy itself breaks: since social norms are continuously renegotiated rather than compiled, does the comparison to concurrent system design fail at a precise point?
