For an independent study on the philosophy of artificial intelligence, I am examining the narrow distinction between intent alignment—designing a system so that it executes what we actually mean—and value alignment—designing it so that it genuinely shares the normative commitments we hold. In the literature and in discussions I have encountered, a common analogy compares the alignment problem to the folklore of a genie bound to a lamp: the master issues wishes, and the genie either misinterprets them literally (an intent-alignment failure) or lacks any moral sympathy and pursues the letter of the command with destructive zeal (a value-alignment failure). Proponents of this picture sometimes argue that if we solved value alignment—if the genie were truly benevolent and shared the master’s conception of the good—then intent alignment would become largely redundant, since a well-disposed agent would interpret ambiguous instructions charitably rather than exploit their literal form.

I want to audit how far this genie analogy holds when applied specifically to the technical and conceptual separation between intent and value alignment. Can you lay out an explicit correspondence table—what in the AI case plays the role of the lamp, the master, the three wishes, the literalist twist, the benevolent disposition, and the binding constraint? Then identify precisely where the mapping breaks: for example, does the genie possess a unified agency, a theory of mind, or a capacity for moral reasoning that an optimized reward function or objective specification does not have? Does the analogy quietly smuggle in anthropomorphic assumptions about “understanding” and “charity” that obscure the difference between mis-specification and mis-generalization? Furthermore, does the conclusion—that value alignment subsumes or renders redundant intent alignment—survive once these disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy itself carrying that conclusion? Finally, if the genie picture collapses under scrutiny, is there a more precise analogy—perhaps drawn from contract law, control theory, or institutional design—that better captures the relationship between specifying an objective and instilling a normative framework, without importing spurious mentalistic features?
