Consider this argument about Dennett’s distinction between genuine intentional systems and counterfeit people in the context of artificial intelligence. (1) For Dennett, the difference between a real mind and a counterfeit is architectural, not behavioral: a counterfeit produces human-like outputs without the internal functional organization—predictive processing dynamics, self-modeling feedback loops, information integration—that makes beliefs and consciousness genuine. (2) He denies any Cartesian theater or non-functional essence of consciousness; to have a mind just is to instantiate the right kind of complex functional architecture. (3) Therefore, any system that genuinely replicates that architecture—whether carbon or silicon—is conscious and possesses full moral status, while any system that lacks it but mimics behavior is a counterfeit lacking that status. (4) Imagine a gradual silicon replacement: every biological neuron is replaced one at a time with a functional equivalent, preserving all causal and informational relations. The result behaves identically to the original, and by (1) and (2) is a real person, not a fake. (5) Now imagine a synthetic twin built from scratch with exactly the same functional architecture but no biological history. By (3), this twin is also real, not counterfeit. (6) This implies that any AI achieving true functional equivalence to a human brain is automatically a person, not a counterfeit. (7) Yet Dennett’s warnings about AI specifically target the risk of convincing fakes—systems that might elicit our moral concern despite lacking the architecture. (8) For such a counterfeit to exist, it must be behaviorally indistinguishable from a real person in practice, otherwise we would not be fooled into treating it as one, yet architecturally different. (9) But on Dennett’s functionalism, the architecture explains the behavior; if the architecture is genuinely different, there should in principle be some predictive or behavioral divergence detectable under sufficient scrutiny. (10) So a perfect counterfeit—indistinguishable in every possible interaction—is impossible: either it shares the architecture and is real, or it differs and eventually reveals the gap. (11) This proves too much. It makes Dennett’s warning either empty, since no perfect fake can exist, or self-defeating: if we ever encounter an AI that is genuinely indistinguishable from us across all contexts, his view forces us to grant it full moral status, collapsing the very distinction he wants to maintain between convincing fakes and genuine beings. (12) Which step should be rejected? I am inclined to preserve (1) and (2), since they seem central to his anti-dualism, but I want to know what abandoning (8), (9), or (10) costs, and whether the argument smuggles in a slide from behavioral equivalence in practice to equivalence in principle—one that might save the counterfeit category without requiring substrate essentialism or denying functionalism altogether.
