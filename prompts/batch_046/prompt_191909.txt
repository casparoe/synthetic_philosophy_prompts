I'm planning a 90-minute graduate workshop in philosophy of history with twelve participants arranged in three boards of four, seated seminar-style. The goal is to make them feel the force of the demarcation problem—specifically why there is no clean algorithmic separation between legitimate chronological revision and pseudohistory—before anyone names the problem. Please design a structured role-play with explicit rules, anonymized materials, and a point-based payoff scheme that forces the issue into action rather than discussion.

Each board receives three anonymized dossiers describing chronological revisions: one legitimate case involving radiocarbon calibration debates around the Thera eruption chronology; one pseudohistorical argument in the style of Fomenko's New Chronology that applies statistical correlation methods to star-catalog longitudes in Ptolemy's Almagest, concluding that the catalog was actually compiled in the medieval period and that ancient Near Eastern chronology is substantially compressed; and one transparently conspiratorial claim about extraterrestrial architects of the pyramids. Every board also gets an identical five-criterion demarcation checklist—falsifiability, peer-reviewed publication status, consilience with adjacent disciplines, methodological transparency, and ontological parsimony—and must classify each dossier as either "admissible historical revision" or "pseudohistory," then distribute one hundred imaginary grant points among the three. The rules should engineer a specific failure: the Fomenko-style dossier should score highly on methodological transparency and statistical falsifiability because it offers precise, computable predictions about stellar positions, while the legitimate radiocarbon case should appear ambiguous on consilience and peer consensus because active disciplinary disagreement exists. After public justification and voting, reveal the true origins. Then require boards to renegotiate allocations using only the checklist, without reference to the revealed labels. Include a precise timeline, a list of minimal physical materials, the incentive structure, a debrief that maps each collapsed criterion onto the formal demarcation problem, an honest account of what the exercise cannot reproduce—namely the long-term institutional gatekeeping dynamics that actually maintain disciplinary boundaries in practice—and a prediction of what will go wrong the first time you run it (for instance, boards treating peer-review status as a proxy for authority rather than epistemic quality, or refusing to reallocate after the reveal) together with guidance on how to handle that resistance.
