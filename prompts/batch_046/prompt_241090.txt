I'm running a philosophy workshop for about 12 people — mix of grad students and curious non-academics — one 90-minute session, no grades. I want them to feel why "are white lies wrong?" gets complicated when you put Dutch-style directness next to Japanese-style indirectness, not just debate it from armchairs. I was thinking about how indirectness can be less about hiding truth and more about jointly maintainence of a social frame, and I invented this distinction: "surface agreement" where everyone pretends to accept a claim so the interaction keeps moving, versus "depth divergence" where the disagreement is real but deliberately bracketed for the group. I want the activity to make them hit that wall physically, not conceptually.

Design a structured role-play or game. The group has to co-design a shared space — say a community garden layout — but each person gets a hidden card with a real preference and a communication protocol: Direct (must voice objections plainly), Indirect (must never say "no" explicitly, only redirect or soften), and Transparency (must flag any surface agreement they think is a white lie). There should be small real stakes: points for both design quality and for "group harmony" scored secretly by you, so they feel pulled in two directions. The rules should be arranged so that they run into the tension themselves — probably the Direct people bulldoze, the Indirect people stall, and the Transparency people blow up cooperation — before anyone names the problem. Then a debrief that maps each crash onto the philosophical question: is the wrongness of a white lie about deception, or about breaking a shared practice of acknowledgment? Include the surface-agreement vs depth-divergence twist in the debrief mapping, and say what feature of long-term cultural embedding this 90-minute game can never reproduce and you have to supply verbally. Also tell me what will definately go wrong the first time — maybe dominance dynamics or silence — and how to fix it on the fly. No bibliographies, just the runnable design.
