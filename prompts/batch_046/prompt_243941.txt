I am a high school senior organizing a 90-minute workshop for about sixteen peers in our school’s applied-ethics seminar; we have read some basic material on personal identity over time but have not studied medical ethics or legal instruments in depth. I want you to design a structured role-play that makes participants feel, through their own decision-making, the tension between honoring an earlier self’s directive and recognizing a present self’s apparently competent, reflected-upon preferences—without using the familiar dementia or sudden-trauma narrative that appears in every textbook. Instead, build the scenario around a constructed but philosophically serious framing: the subject has undergone a documented, multi-year “value-recalibration protocol,” deliberately revising core life priorities through guided reflection, and has left an advance directive that activates only when the subject reaches a quantitative “value-coherence index” of 0.85 with the new framework; the proxy group is given partial, noisy measurements of this index and must vote on whether to enforce the old directive or honor the current expressed wishes, knowing that the threshold itself was chosen by the earlier self precisely to prevent premature override. Split the group into rotating roles—proxy decision-makers with hidden information cards, an observer who records justifications, and a “subject” who communicates only through scripted preference-statements—so that participants construct the conflict themselves before any facilitator names it. Include precise rules for how hidden cards are revealed across rounds, how votes translate into binding or non-binding outcomes, a timeline with opening setup, deliberation rounds, and a final reveal, a debrief that maps specific moments onto the problem of earlier-self authority versus present autonomy and points out where any sharp cutoff becomes arbitrary, and an honest note about which feature—likely the phenomenological sense of being one continuous subject—the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by discussion. Please begin with the activity rules and role assignments themselves, with no preface or commentary, and then present the timeline, debrief, and limitations.
