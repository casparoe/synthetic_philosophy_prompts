I am taking an advanced civics course, and we have been studying how societies institutionalize their relationship to the past. Our instructor described a national curriculum framework—apparently adopted in several provinces—called the Tri-Modal Heritage Protocol. Under this protocol, every secondary student must complete a single capstone project evaluated on three equally weighted criteria: first, a monumental narrative that identifies heroic founders and derives present obligations from their example; second, an antiquarian archive that documents local artifacts with exhaustive neutrality and forbids any evaluative framing; and third, a critical demolition that exposes the ideological function of both the heroic narrative and the archival neutrality. The puzzling part is that the rubric requires all three to be synthesized into one coherent statement of civic identity, and students are penalized if the three sections contradict one another. I find this strange: it seems to demand that I simultaneously worship, catalog, and annihilate the same historical material, then pretend the result is harmonious.

What philosophical commitments about the purpose of history for life does this institutional practice presuppose? Does it assume that monumental, antiquarian, and critical history are not rival modes with incompatible advantages and dangers, as Nietzsche argued, but rather complementary lenses that naturally converge on a single usable past? Or does the synthesis requirement smuggle in a hidden fourth view—perhaps that historical consciousness is valuable only as a therapeutic tool for present self-affirmation, regardless of which mode produces it? I want a committed answer: reconstruct the implicit theory of history embedded in the rubric, say whether it is internally coherent, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and tell me whether Nietzsche's framework would judge this protocol as a healthy use of the past or as the kind of historical excess that paralyzes life. If the synthesis requirement were dropped and each mode were taught separately, what exactly about the practice's underlying philosophy would have to change?
