I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I've been reading about Frankfurt-style counterexamples to the principle that moral responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise. For decades, philosophers have debated whether these cases genuinely show that a person can be responsible without alternative possibilities, or whether defenders of the principle can always identify a flicker of freedom—some minimal choice point that technically remains available. The literature seems stalled: every counterexample receives a flicker defense, every flicker defense receives a refined counterexample, and neither side concedes ground. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about moral responsibility at all, but about what counts as a genuine possibility within a deterministic system—analogous to whether an un-triggered exception handler in a running program counts as an available branch for the purpose of attributing choice. Is that right? Or is there a hidden empirical or conceptual disagreement about agency, causation, or the structure of choice that I am missing? Diagnose the stall concretely: do the sides differ about facts, about standards for what counts as data, about the meaning of being able to do otherwise, or about bedrock intuitions that neither can argue for?
