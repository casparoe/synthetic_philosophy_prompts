I'm putting together a feature on how medical autonomy holds up when the brain changes, and I got stuck on an arguemnt that broke out at a bioethics roundtable I covered back in March. Two people, Dr. Elena Voss and Marcus Reed, were debating a case I'll call L — a composer who filed an advnace directive saying if she ever lost auditory emotional processing, specifically if music stopped feeling like anything, she shouldn't recieve feeding tubes or hydration. That condition has now occured through a rare degenerative thing, but instead of being miserable she's developed this intense, apparently real attachment to tactile geometric patterns; she spends hours running her fingers over textured walls and seems genuinely content. Voss said the directive still binds because the evaluative framework that wrote it is gone, and letting the current state veto it is basically letting a stranger rewrite your will. Reed said that's missing the point — the directive was meant to prevent suffering from living without meaning, and since she now has meaning, just in a different sensory lane, the trigger isn't actually met; enforcing it just imposes a dead composer's aesthetic hierarchy on someone who doesn't share it.

Voss fired back that contentment isn't flourishing, and Reed was smuggling in a value call that geometric pleasure equals musical joy. Reed answered that Voss was doing the exact same thing in reverse, treating sound-based experience as the only valid currency of a good life, and that if we take autonomy seriously we have to ask which self is actually speaking — the one who signed the paper, or the one who's here now and would suffer if we pulled the tube. They kept circling and neither would concede. Can you walk through there exchange move by move and rate who's right at each step? And tell me who is more right overall, plus whether both of them are missing something important about how authority works across these kinds of value shifts?
