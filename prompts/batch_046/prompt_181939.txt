In my philosophy-of-knowledge seminar the debate over Plato’s Meno problem—why knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief—has stalled in a way that feels structural rather than evidential. Every participant accepts the standard Gettier and lottery counterexamples, and nobody denies that knowledge excludes certain kinds of luck. The division is between those who locate the surplus value in cognitive achievement (you deserve credit because the belief flows from your own competence) and those who locate it in modal stability (the belief holds across nearby worlds in a way lucky belief does not). For three semesters the same exchange repeats: the achievement side treats stability as a by-product of competence, the stability side treats achievement as a psychological gloss on modal robustness, and neither side has engaged the empirical literature on cognitive attribution. Is this a verbal disagreement about what counts as “value,” a case of two groups answering different questions—one about the nature of knowledge, one about why we prize it—or a substantive deadlock over whether epistemology must answer to psychology?
