I'm writing a paper for my metaphysics and rational-choice seminar, and I want to check whether I'm right that Kavka's toxin puzzle implicitly presupposes endurantism. The setup is familiar: an agent can receive a substantial reward tomorrow solely in virtue of having formed, tonight, a genuine intention to drink a mildly toxic but non-lethal substance tomorrow morning; once the reward is deposited, however, she will have no further reason to actually consume it, since the payout does not depend on execution. The standard analysis treats this as a paradox about diachronic rationality—how one can rationally form an intention one knows one will not rationally carry out. But it strikes me that this framing only gets its grip if we assume that a single, numerically identical substance persists unchanged from the moment of intention-formation to the moment of action, so that "my future self" is literally the same agent bound by my present commitment. If perdurantism is correct, by contrast, there is no enduring agent at all—only a present temporal part that forms the intention and a distinct future temporal part that faces the cup. On that metaphysical picture, the present part's rational calculus should not be constrained by the welfare or obligations of some merely causally related later part, and the "paradox" dissolves into a straightforward case of one part benefiting at no cost to another. So my question is: doesn't Kavka's puzzle actually depend on an endurantist ontology of personal persistence, and wouldn't adopting perdurantism show that there is no single diachronic rational agent who needs to reconcile intending with acting?
