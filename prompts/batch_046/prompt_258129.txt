I work as a software engineer without formal training in philosophy or political science, but I have been tracking a dispute that has persisted for well over a decade and now seems frozen: whether countries such as Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and more recently the United States are undergoing democratic backsliding. On one side, comparative-politics researchers and monitoring institutions treat the erosion of judicial independence, media pluralism, and electoral neutrality as measurable indicators that a regime is sliding away from democracy even when elections continue to occur. On the other side, domestic defenders, some scholars, and partisan commentators argue that this framing is essentially a normative or ideological label applied to legitimate democratic choices, that the indicators are arbitrarily weighted, or that the concept is so vague it can be stretched to cover any government one dislikes. The same case studies, the same counter-examples, and the same methodological objections are recycled repeatedly, and as far as I can tell no one changes position.

My working hypothesis—drawn from engineering rather than political theory—is that part of the stalemate resembles what reliability engineers call specification drift paired with an observability failure: the formal outputs of the system (elections, parliamentary votes, executive appointments) continue to look correct, while the internal control structures, failure-mode tolerances, and redundancy mechanisms have shifted in ways that standard dashboards do not capture well. I do not know whether this analogy identifies a real structural feature of the debate or is merely a metaphor.

Please diagnose the disagreement rather than deliver a verdict on any specific country. Is it substantive—about empirical facts, about the correct weighting of institutional features, or about whether there exists a hidden threshold separating democracy from non-democracy? Is it verbal—a dispute about the meaning of democracy or backsliding that could in principle be dissolved by stipulation? Or are the sides answering different questions under a single heading—perhaps one about normative democratic ideals and one about empirical regime classification—without recognizing the divergence? Use what each side actually says in the literature and in public commentary to support your diagnosis. If a hidden formal or empirical question is doing the work—such as a problem of aggregation, boundary conditions, measurement validity, or the distinction between gradual degradation and abrupt failure—identify it explicitly.

Finally, tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis. Would resolution require a new formal model of institutional degradation (perhaps borrowing from reliability engineering or machine-learning drift detection), a refined taxonomy that separates distinct failure modes, an empirical study that settles a specific factual dispute, or a change in the question itself? Or is the impasse a sign that the concept of democratic backsliding is ill-posed and should be abandoned or replaced by a more precise framework? I am not asking you to classify Hungary, Poland, or the United States; I am asking for a diagnosis of why the debate is stuck and what kind of resolution—if any—is structurally possible.
