I'm prepping questions for next week's podcast — my guest is an ethicist who defends a kind of prioritarianism she calls "sphere-bound lexical priority." The basic idea is that prioritarian weighting only applies inside distinct domains like health, education, and civic standing, and you can't trade gains across domains to justify leveling down inside one. She thinks this blocks the classic leveling-down objection because reducing the well-off in sphere A doesn't help the worst-off in sphere A, and you can't offset that by boosting them in sphere B.

I'd like eight to ten interveiw questions that go deeper than just restating her view. Specifically:

- At least one pressing the classic leveling-down scenario: we can take resources from the well-off in health but waste them, leaving the worst-off unchanged. How does sphere-bound priority treat that? And a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that spheres are incommensurable.
- At least one on whether her view ever permits deliberately lowering someone's education level to equalize health outcomes — or whether that's exactly the kind of cross-sphere leveling she wants to forbid. Follow-up assuming she invokes a lexical ordering.
- At least one on what happens when the worst off are already at a floor in every sphere: does prioritarian weighting collapse into strict equality, or into sufficientarianism? Follow-up for the answer she'll probably give.
- One question that forces her to distinguish her view from sufficientarianism, since both seem to care about floors.
- A couple more that probe the edges: maybe one about whether her domains are fixed by nature or by social convention, and one about how she handles cases where a single policy affects multiple spheres at once (like a tax that funds both clinics and schools).

Keep the tone conversational — this is a podcast prep doc, not an exam. You can have a typo or two. No need for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Definately don't ask her to name texts.

Please end with a single line starting 'Bottom line:' that captures the sharpest tension running through the questions.
