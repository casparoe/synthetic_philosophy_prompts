I keep running into a particular methodological deadlock in bottom-up historical research—the kind that reconstructs reasoning from parish registers, court depositions, or workshop account books rather than from state papers or institutional archives—and I want to know whether it has a precise name, or whether I am just describing a familiar tension in an over-specific way.

Consider two concrete patterns. In one study, rural tenants in a specific region during the 1720s decide whether to petition a magistrate or resolve a boundary dispute informally. Their reasoning is coherent and practical, but it operates through a localized calculus involving seasonal labor needs, kinship obligations, and an informal notion of “fair dealing” that does not correspond to the legal categories in the magistrate’s records, nor to “class consciousness,” “resistance,” or “market rationality.” In another case, artisans in a single urban workshop develop a cooperative practice governing tool-sharing and debt that cannot be described as either resistance to capital or deference to authority; it only makes internal sense relative to the specific debts, personal histories, and physical layout of that room.

In both instances, the researcher can document the local reasoning with precision. The deadlock arises when attempting to connect these findings to larger-scale change—say, the erosion of seigneurial authority or the transformation of craft organization. The local logic is not merely different in content from the explanatory vocabulary of macro-history; it appears structurally incompatible with it. Translating the tenants’ or artisans’ reasoning into terms like “state formation,” “class struggle,” or “hegemonic negotiation” distorts its internal coherence, while refusing to translate leaves the episode isolated, reducing the study to antiquarian detail that cannot be aggregated into any causal narrative about broad transformation.

This feels sharper than the generic micro-macro gap. Is there an established concept for exactly this scaling failure—where local agents employ a form of practical reasoning that cannot be mapped onto elite or state-level frameworks without losing its coherence? How does it differ from ordinary incommensurability, from the charge of antiquarianism, and from the simple claim that bottom-up sources are too fragmentary to support generalization? If no precise term exists, can you coin one with clear boundary conditions—for instance, distinguishing cases where the mismatch is structural from cases where local reasoning is merely poorly documented?
