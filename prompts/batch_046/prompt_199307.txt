I'm designing a module on distributive justice for an advanced undergraduate seminar, and during a pilot discussion two students—Jordan and Riley—got locked in a sustained disagreement about how luck egalitarianism applies to career-related social capital. They were comparing two freelance illustrators, Maya and Leo, who both freely chose the same risky profession and invested comparable effort. Maya was born into a well-connected family with ties to publishing houses; Leo grew up in economic precarity with no professional network. Maya secured steady commissions while Leo struggled to find clients. Jordan and Riley agree that luck egalitarianism distinguishes brute luck (unchosen circumstances) from option luck (the outcomes of genuinely voluntary choices), but they diverge sharply on where the line falls here. Here is how the exchange unfolded. (1) Jordan said Leo's lack of professional connections is a paradigm case of brute luck—he did not choose his family or its social capital—so a consistent luck egalitarian should support compensation that brings Leo's prospects closer to Maya's. (2) Riley replied that Leo also chose a career path—freelance illustration—where success is unusually sensitive to exactly those network effects. By selecting a high-risk, network-dependent field rather than a more stable occupation, Leo assumed responsibility for the differential risk. The connections themselves may be brute luck, but the decision to enter a domain where they matter so much transforms the poor outcome into option luck. (3) Jordan countered that this reasoning conflates the choice of career with the resources available within it. Even holding the career constant, Maya's network is an unchosen advantage that tilts the playing field. If compensation is denied whenever a person made any prior choice, then virtually all social advantages become non-compensable because every life path involves prior choices. The theory needs to neutralize background conditions, not excuse them by pointing to downstream choices. (4) Riley answered that Jordan is sliding toward full equality of outcome disguised as luck neutrality. There must be a cutoff: compensation applies only when unchosen circumstances severely constrain or undermine the capacity for choice, not whenever they create differential advantages within an otherwise voluntary career. Leo's choice was genuinely free; he merely faced harder conditions, and facing harder conditions is part of what it means to take responsibility for a risky choice. Without such a cutoff, no one is ever responsible for anything, since every choice is causally embedded in an unchosen history. (5) Jordan responded that Riley is smuggling in a perfectionist conception of autonomy—treating choices as free only under ideal conditions. Luck egalitarianism was developed precisely to reject that picture: people make choices in conditions shaped by luck, and justice requires neutralizing the luck without denying that the choice occurred. To say Leo assumed the risk while ignoring that the risk was distributed by brute luck is to abandon the theory's core commitment. (6) Riley concluded that Jordan is correct that choices are embedded in unchosen histories, but wrong that this makes every embedded outcome compensable. The theory requires a principled distinction between circumstances and choices, yet in practice they are entangled in ways that resist clean separation. Rather than compensating for every network gap, a workable luck-egalitarian policy should identify specific structural barriers that genuinely undermine autonomy—such as exclusion from basic education or credit—while treating differential success within a freely entered profession as a matter of option luck, even when background advantages play a role.
