I am studying how modern states construct historical genealogies, and I have noticed a recurring asymmetry for which I lack a precise term. Consider two medieval revolts in the same territory: both are led by local elites against a distant imperial authority, both fail, and both leave comparable archival traces; yet when the rebels share the linguistic and religious profile of the present-day nation-state, official commemorations treat the uprising as an early expression of national consciousness and build monuments around it, whereas when the rebels belong to a group now classified as a minority or rival ethnicity, the identical evidence is filed away as parochial unrest or banditry and vanishes from school curricula. I want to isolate this specific move—the selective assimilation of past resistance into a national narrative based on retroactive ethnic or cultural matching—rather than collapse it into general invented tradition, strategic forgetting, or presentist historiography. Does this asymmetry have an established name in the study of nationalism and collective memory? If there is no dedicated term, propose one with a concise definition and boundary conditions that distinguish it from ordinary myth-making and from the broader construction of imagined communities. Please answer in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
