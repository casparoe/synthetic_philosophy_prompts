I was arguing with friends on a forum about whether judging past figures by current norms is always a failure of historical imagination, and the conversation kept circling back to the 2020 toppling of the Edward Colston statue in Bristol without anyone distinguishing the layers of the problem. The standard framing is presentism: activists apply contemporary anti-racist standards to a seventeenth-century slave trader, while defenders insist we must understand Colston within the moral economy of his era. But I think this misses something structurally important. There is normative presentism—evaluating Colston the man by our values—and then there is what we might call institutional presentism: asking whether a present-day civic institution should continue to honor a figure through a monument erected in 1895, decades after his death, as part of a Victorian project of civic identity rather than a direct memorial to his actions. The statue was not a neutral historical record; its 1895 installation and its 2020 removal are both acts of institutional self-definition. I want you to analyze the Colston episode—the 1895 erection, the 2020 removal, and the subsequent 2022 museum display of the damaged statue—through the lens of presentism in historiography. Specifically: do the activists depend on normative presentism about Colston himself, or on institutional presentism about Bristol's current values? Do the defenders' appeals to historical context actually address the institutional question, or do they change the subject? What does the case show about whether citizens acting as historical agents can escape the standards of their own time, and is there a disciplined, unavoidable form of presentism that is necessary for institutional coherence rather than a cognitive distortion?

Please be precise about which features of the episode each form of presentism explains and which it misdescribes—does normative presentism help us understand why the statue was targeted in 2020 rather than in 1950, and does institutional presentism illuminate why the 2022 museum display was chosen over destruction or re-erection? I am also interested in where the presentism framework falls silent. For instance, does it provide any guidance on whether the statue should have been destroyed, preserved horizontally with contextual plaques, or returned to its pedestal? If not, what additional normative commitments—about democratic representation, the ethics of public memory, or the ontology of monuments—are being imported to fill the gap? Finally, does the episode suggest that historians can genuinely bracket their own moral standards when writing about the slave trade, or does it confirm that any selection of what to commemorate is already saturated with present values, leaving only the question of whether we acknowledge that saturation explicitly?
