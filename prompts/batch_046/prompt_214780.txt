I am drafting the final exam for my upper-level undergraduate course in philosophy of biology and naturalism, and I need one essay question that students must complete in 75 minutes and that should run to about 700 words. The course has covered Darwin's evolutionary explanation of moral sentiments, his account of sympathy and social instincts as the root of ethical behavior, and his private correspondence on religious belief, but I want a question that rewards conceptual analysis rather than summary. Specifically, I want students to confront what I am calling the authority-preservation problem: Darwin traces moral norms back to evolutionary origins—parental affection, social instincts, and the gradual extension of sympathy—yet he also maintains that human morality achieves a kind of disinterested concern for others that appears to claim authority over the very mechanisms that produced it. At the same time, his well-known agnosticism rejects revealed religion and a personal deity, though his letters occasionally gesture toward an unknown first cause behind natural law. The exam question should ask whether Darwin's evolutionary genealogy of morality undermines the normative authority of moral judgments, and whether his residual metaphysical openness offers any resources to preserve that authority, or whether the two commitments—naturalistic genealogy and cautious theological openness—simply remain in unresolved tension. Please provide: (1) the exam question itself, clearly framing the task for a closed-book setting; (2) a model answer of approximately 650 words that executes the specific conceptual moves required; and (3) a marking scheme totaling 25 marks, built around five must-hit points with marks assigned to each, that distinguishes an A-level response from a B-level response by naming the exact analytical steps each must contain—such as separating genealogical explanation from normative justification, identifying the specific role of sympathy-extension versus impartiality in Darwin's moral psychology, and evaluating whether the first-cause idea functions as a metaphysical stopgap or is genuinely irrelevant to ethics. For each point, include a brief one-line note on the characteristic mistake students make. The question should be answerable without consulting external texts, citing specific editions, or referencing secondary literature, since students will have no access to sources during the exam.
