In my philosophy of mathematics seminar this term, a fellow student offered the following summary of Skolem's paradox: "Skolem showed that every first-order axiomatization of set theory possesses a countable model; consequently, within that model there exist sets that the model itself regards as uncountable, even though the entire domain contains only countably many objects from an external perspective. From this, it follows that first-order logic is inherently incapable of expressing genuine uncountability, since any formalization can be reinterpreted to falsify cardinal claims about the actual domain. The paradox therefore demonstrates that set theory lacks a fixed, interpretation-independent subject matter—its objects are merely artifacts of symbolic manipulation—and that working mathematicians ought to abandon first-order ZFC in favor of second-order logic if they wish to secure an absolute, non-relative conception of infinity."

How fair is this account? I am looking for a precise audit: claim by claim, which statements accurately reflect the Löwenheim-Skolem theorem and its standard model-theoretic interpretation, which ones illicitly move from a result about the existence of nonstandard models to metaphysical conclusions about "symbolic manipulation" or the absence of fixed subject matter, and which ones misstate what defenders of first-order set theory or of the relative interpretation of the paradox would reply. Specifically, does the inference that first-order logic "cannot genuinely express" cardinality confuse internal quantification within a model with external quantification over the domain? Does the recommendation to replace first-order ZFC with second-order logic actually resolve the underlying issue, or does it merely shift the locus of relativism without eliminating it? And if the metaphysical gloss is removed, does any sound argument for abandoning first-order axiomatizations remain?
