In debates over whether patriotism is a virtue or a vice, I have repeatedly encountered a particular argumentative architecture that strikes me as unstable, yet I am unsure whether the literature on partiality or associative obligations has given it a specific diagnosis. The pattern runs as follows. A defender begins with an analogy to familial loyalty: just as it is morally appropriate for a parent to rescue her own child before a stranger's, so too is it appropriate for a citizen to prioritize her compatriots. This analogy derives its force from the intimacy, direct reciprocity, and non-voluntary embeddedness characteristic of family life. When critics respond that modern nation-states are anonymous, territorially vast, and lack the face-to-face reciprocity that renders family partiality defensible, the defender typically does not concede the analogy's limits. Instead, she pivots to an institutional justification: patriotism is warranted by shared civic structures, mutual contribution to public goods, or participation in a common political culture. The difficulty is that these institutional grounds apply with equal or greater force to long-term residents, tax-paying immigrants, or regional cooperators who are not citizens, and they bear none of the intimate, pre-political features that made the family comparison initially compelling. The result is not a single argument with auxiliary premises but a kind of dual-track defense: the family analogy supplies the moral warmth and intuitive plausibility, while the institutional account supplies the resilience against scale-based objections, with little effort to reconcile them or to explain why the second track does not dissolve the exclusivity the first was meant to justify.

I am not describing mere equivocation, since both tracks are sincerely endorsed, nor the standard motte-and-bailey, since neither is an indefensible exaggeration of a defensible core; they are closer to two independent justifications that borrow credibility from each other without integration. Does this configuration have an established name in political ethics? If not, should it be classified as analogical overreach, as a failure of consistency within partialist ethics, or as a distinct failure mode—perhaps one we might call "justificatory code-switching"—and what boundary conditions would separate it from legitimate pluralism about patriotic reasons? I ask not for a bibliography but for a conceptual carving: what exactly this pattern is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. Please state explicitly what consideration would lead you to revise your judgment—whether, for example, a convincing demonstration that institutional reciprocity genuinely reproduces family-scale obligations, or evidence that the family analogy was intended only as heuristic illustration rather than as a justificatory premise—and identify the point in your reasoning where error is most likely. Provide your answer in a single paragraph of no more than 300 words.
