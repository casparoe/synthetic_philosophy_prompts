I am finalizing the examination for my graduate seminar in philosophy of mind, and I need an essay question that probes whether students genuinely understand the structural tension in epiphenomenalism rather than merely rehearsing the familiar causal-exclusion objection. The prompt should center on the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if phenomenal properties are causally inert with respect to physical processes, then judgments about those properties appear to be causally disconnected from the states they purport to describe, which seems to undermine the reliability—or even the intelligibility—of first-person reports.

However, I want the question to go further. It should require students to interrogate an often-unstated premise of the paradox, namely what we might call the transparent access assumption—the idea that phenomenal knowledge requires direct, unmediated acquaintance with its object, such that any causal gap between state and judgment immediately severs knowledge. Students should be asked to evaluate whether abandoning this assumption in favor of a mediated, inferential model of introspection actually dissolves the paradox or merely relocates it: if phenomenal judgments are theoretical inferences from behavioral and functional cues, then the epiphenomenalist owes an account of why those inferences track phenomenal properties rather than functional surrogates, and whether this collapses epiphenomenalism into a form of functionalism.

To raise the stakes without relying on obscure literature, include a comparative thought experiment: imagine an artificial cognitive architecture designed so that its qualitative states are strictly causally inert relative to its self-monitoring outputs, which are generated entirely by parallel functional mechanisms. The system produces accurate-seeming reports about its experience, yet those reports are never caused by the experiences themselves. Students should address whether applying the paradox of phenomenal judgment to this system reveals a genuine incoherence in attributing phenomenal knowledge to it, or whether the absence of paradox for the artificial case demonstrates that the human paradox was always an artifact of an inflated conception of first-person authority.

Please construct a single essay question suitable for a 75-minute graduate exam, requiring approximately 1,200 words. Provide a model answer of about 700 words that works through the conceptual moves cleanly, and a marking scheme organized around four essential argumentative moves, each worth 5 marks (total 20). For each move, specify precisely what the answer must do, assign the 5 marks, and include a one-line note on the characteristic failure mode—for instance, conflating causal inertness with logical incoherence, or treating the artificial-system analogy as decisive without addressing the attribution disanalogy. The question and answer should be self-contained: they must not depend on specific page citations, reading lists, or niche theoretical labels that a well-prepared student could not reconstruct from conceptual reasoning alone.
