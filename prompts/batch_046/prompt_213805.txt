Right, I’ve recently inherited my aunt’s little gallery in Bristol (she died suddenly last month) and I’m trying to decide what to hang. It’s made me think about whether you can seperate the art from the artist, and I’ve realised my gut feelings are a mess. I’d like to know if there’s actually a principle behind them.

Here are my verdicts on five cases. Most feel clear, but there’s one pair I keep flipping on.

1. A landscape painter who was vicious to his apprentices, but the paintings are serene and treat nature with reverence. Verdict: still genuinely good art. The cruelty belongs to the man, not the canvas.

2. A biography that’s beautifully written, but the author fabricated primary sources. Verdict: not good work. The deception is baked into the craft.

3. A protest song with incredible lyrics and melody, but the songwriter genuinely believed in violence against civilians and meant the song to recruit people. The ideology is woven into the metaphors. Verdict: bad art. The wrongness isn’t incidental; it’s doing aesthetic work.

4. A film directed by someone who bullied the cast terribly, but the film itself is about resilience and the performances are raw partly because of the real tension on set. Verdict: I keep going back and forth. Sometimes I think the suffering made the art possible and that taints it; other times I think if the film genuinely condemns that harm, it transcends the production. Right now I lean it’s not good art, but I’m genuinely unstable.

5. A symphony that incorporates melodies lifted directly from an unknown composer who was never credited, but integrates them into something technically masterful. Verdict: not good art. The theft is structural — you can’t hear the piece without hearing the stolen parts.

So my pattern seems to be: if the immorality is in the message or the structure, it ruins the art; if it’s just in the artist’s private life, it doesn’t. But case 4 messes that up, because the bullying is in the production, not the message, yet I still feel tainted. Meanwhile case 1 involves cruelty to people who are kind of present in the work, but I still say it’s fine.

Is there one principle that saves all five? Or do I have to drop one? I’ve started calling it “moral echo” when the wrongness resonates inside the experience of the work, versus “moral background” when it’s outside — but I made that up and I don’t know if it holds.

What would your principle say about a case I haven’t listed: a novel that’s morally neutral in content and well-written, but was written solely to launder money for an arms dealer? The writing is innocent, but the reason the book exists is corrupt.

Please say precisely which of my verdicts your principle can’t accommodate. And please end with a line beginning "Verdict:" followed by the core principle in one sentence.
