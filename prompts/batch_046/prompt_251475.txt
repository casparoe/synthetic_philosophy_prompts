I am currently enrolled in an upper-level course on the philosophy of historiography, and I wish to develop a focused research proposal—approximately six thousand words, for one semester—on Whig history and the temptation to write the past as progress toward the present. Most existing critiques, as I understand them, treat Whig history primarily as an epistemic distortion: historians impose present conceptual categories onto past agents and thereby misrepresent what those agents were actually doing. What I want to investigate is whether Whig framing also operates as a kind of normative intuition pump, one that makes present moral and political arrangements feel not merely preferable but historically inevitable, thereby suppressing the sense that development could have taken genuinely different paths.

The precise question is whether the rhetorical force of Whig historiography can be decomposed into three distinct mechanisms rather than one: first, anachronistic concept attribution; second, teleological causal explanation; and third, a mechanism I shall call “normative closure,” whereby the identification of a particular trajectory as “progress” is used to foreclose debate about whether the values that define our present deserve their authority. I suspect that standard critiques conflate these, and that separating them reveals normative closure to be doing independent philosophical work—particularly in narratives about the development of scientific, medical, or legal authority—work that survives even when the first two errors are corrected.

The approach would be conceptual analysis supported by close reading of passages from well-known general histories, paired with reconstruction—using available primary-source fragments—of the conceptual schemes those passages marginalise. I am not seeking an exhaustive survey or a bibliographic review; rather, I want to test whether the three mechanisms can be distinguished in practice, and whether normative closure can be resisted without abandoning all evaluative judgment about the past. A successful result would be a clear typology together with an argument about which mechanism is active in different historiographical contexts, plus an honest assessment of whether resisting closure requires abandoning the very idea of learning from history. The project fails if the distinctions collapse, or if the primary material is too fragmentary to sustain the reconstruction. The scope is deliberately limited to avoid overreach. Please write the proposal in British English, and do not include citations, reading lists, or bibliographies—I will supply those myself.
