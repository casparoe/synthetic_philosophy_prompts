As a doctoral student working at the intersection of epistemology and philosophy of mind, I have been trying to understand the regress problem by mapping foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism onto the distinction between occurrent justification, which requires active cognitive access to one's reasons, and dispositional justification, which requires only that supporting reasons be available upon reflection. My difficulty concerns how infinitism is meant to avoid skeptical over-demand. If the infinitist holds that justification is purely dispositional—so that S is justified in believing p not because S has actually traversed an infinite chain of reasons, but because an infinite series of further reasons is in principle available—then it seems the infinitist has not solved the regress so much as relocated it: the problem is no longer about the logical structure of reasons, but about the psychological impossibility of possessing an infinite disposition. Meanwhile, foundationalism appears to solve the regress precisely by allowing basic beliefs to be justified dispositionally without requiring further reasons at all, and coherentism solves it by allowing mutual dispositional support within a closed web. Consequently, I find myself wondering whether there is any structural difference between infinitism and foundationalism once both are interpreted through this dispositional lens, or whether infinitism merely inserts an infinite number of unnecessary intermediate dispositions between the subject and an unreachable ground.

More specifically, I worry that the infinitist's rejection of basic beliefs forces every belief into the same epistemic status—potentially justified in principle, but never actually justified here and now—whereas the foundationalist can at least claim that some beliefs are occurrently justified without further inquiry. If justification requires that for every belief there exists a further reason, and if human cognition cannot actualize an infinite series, then doesn't infinitism collapse into a form of skepticism about actually justified belief? Or, if the infinitist allows that justification can be satisfied by the mere availability of reasons without their actual possession, then I struggle to see why this availability condition does not apply equally to a foundationalist's basic beliefs, which are also available without further reason. I keep feeling that if we take the structure of justification seriously as a relation that must be instantiated rather than merely potential, then an infinite linear chain without a first element is structurally indistinguishable from no justification at all, unless we tacitly allow some kind of limit-belief at infinity that functions as a hidden foundation. Could you help me see exactly where this framing conflates the logical order of justification with the psychological order of belief acquisition, and whether infinitism can be defended without either collapsing into skepticism or becoming a disguised foundationalism with an infinitely remote ground?
