I'm working through some reading on longtermist ethics and recently encountered a summary in an online discussion that tries to sharply distinguish suffering risks from conventional existential risks. The poster, who seemed philosophically literate, argued that s-risks are not a mere subset of extinction risk but structurally different: they presuppose that technological civilization survives long enough to generate conscious substrates—large-scale digital simulations, engineered biological systems, or other substrate-independent minds—capable of experiencing harm. On this view, preventing human extinction is actually a necessary precondition for the worst s-risk scenarios, whereas extinction itself produces only a null outcome with no suffering subjects. The poster then introduced what they called a "binding asymmetry": negative phenomenal states tend to monopolize a conscious system's cognitive architecture in a self-reinforcing way that positive states do not, giving suffering a kind of moral stickiness that makes it resist optimization away from harm far more strongly than pleasure resists improvement. From this they concluded that, once instantiated, an s-risk scenario is more likely to persist and expand across cosmic timescales than an equivalent flourishing scenario. They went on to claim that standard longtermist reasoning therefore implies a lexical priority for s-risk mitigation over extinction prevention, since extinction guarantees zero suffering while astronomical suffering produces expected disvalue that dwarfs any plausible future value.

Something about this framing strikes me as both conceptually provocative and suspiciously neat—particularly the move from a psychological observation about negative states to a cosmic-scale moral calculus, and the claim that extinction risk should be subordinated rather than addressed in parallel. Is this a fair summary of how suffering risks are actually characterized in the philosophical literature? Please separate what accurately reflects the logic of s-risk arguments, what is a defensible but contested philosophical addition, and what counts as a misrepresentation or overstatement—especially regarding whether proponents treat extinction as an enabler of s-risks, whether they invoke anything like this "binding asymmetry," and whether they genuinely defend the kind of lexical priority over existential risk that the summary implies.
