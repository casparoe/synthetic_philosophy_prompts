I've been turning over in my mind whether an enormous number of barely-noticeable harms can ever amount to something truly terrible, or whether concentrating harm on one person is always worse regardless of the totals. Here are the judgments I keep returning to, and I can't tell if they point to one idea or if I'm just contradicting myself.

If one innocent person is tortured for an hour, that is clearly among the worst things on this list.

If every human being currently alive receives a single dust speck in the eye for one second, that strikes me as a global annoyance, not an atrocity. Even extending it to everyone who will ever live doesn't change the feeling: it still doesn't come close to the torture case.

But now consider a permanent, imperceptible loss—say, one color-sensitive cell in the retina stops working in each person, with no one ever noticing the change individually. Distributed across billions, that somehow feels more morally weighty than the dust speck, even though the suffering summed across all people might be smaller.

If, instead, one person suffers that same single-cell loss every day for fifty years—accumulating a lifetime of imperceptible damage but never experiencing it as damage—I think that is wrong, yet far less wrong than torture, and perhaps not much worse than suffering it once.

The case that breaks my intuition is a hypothetical machine that runs continuously and, with each cycle, induces exactly one second of very mild anxiety in one randomly chosen person, never repeating on the same individual. Over time it touches every person who ever lives, but no one ever knows the cause, and no one ever feels more than that isolated second. My instinct is to call this a distributed nuisance, not a catastrophe. Yet if the same total anxiety were concentrated—giving one person one second of mild anxiety every second for their entire life—I feel pulled to call that a serious harm, perhaps approaching the gravity of torture, though I genuinely cannot settle on either view.

Finally, writing a false note saying the check is in the mail when it is not strikes me as wrong in a different register altogether—more a failure of honesty than a harm—so I take it that aggregation is not the only moral axis here.

Is there a single principle that explains why distributed imperceptible damage feels different from concentrated imperceptible damage, why total summed suffering doesn't seem to be the measure, and why awareness or the identity of the bearer matters so much? Or must I abandon one of these judgments? I would also like to know what such a principle predicts for a case I have not mentioned—say, a policy that saves a million lives but requires every citizen to endure one second of imperceptible cognitive fatigue every year, with no one ever linking it to the policy.
