The phrase was invoked most recently after I declined to add a service charge in a tipping culture, ignored an informal queue that locals seemed to treat as optional, and crossed a road against the pedestrian signal in a city where jaywalking is widespread though technically unlawful. I am convinced the proverb is being asked to do too much work, but I lack the vocabulary to separate its possible meanings.

Please treat tipping, queueing, and jaywalking as three different kinds of social convention — one that allocates economic burdens, one that manages fair ordering, and one that regulates shared risk — and lay out the distinct precise principles that "When in Rome, do as the Romans do" might stand for in each category. For each reading, please specify:

- whether it concerns prudence, a universal moral principle applied locally, a context-dependent moral obligation, or mere social coordination;
- whether it applies uniformly across all three behaviours or differently to each;
- whether the claim is trivially true, substantive but defensible, or false;
- and, crucially, whether it actually entails that a visitor must conform, or whether opting out can be justified.

Present the readings as a menu rather than selecting a single interpretation. I do not require bibliographies, named philosophical theories, or citations to specific texts; I want a practical framework I can apply directly while travelling.
