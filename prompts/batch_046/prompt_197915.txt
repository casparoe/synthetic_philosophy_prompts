I’ve been working through the rationalist and decision-theoretic literature on moral uncertainty, and I ran across a forum synthesis that tries to tie the whole debate together. The author writes: “The standard framework is maximizing expected choice-worthiness, or MEC: you assign each moral theory a credence, map every outcome onto that theory’s internal value scale, multiply, and sum. But this requires intertheoretic value comparison—placing deontological wrongness and consequentialist utility on a common cardinal scale. Most philosophers now regard this as impossible without smuggling in a neutral meta-theory, so MEC has been largely abandoned in practice. The leading replacement is a moral-parliament model, in which each theory casts votes proportional to its credence and you select the action with the highest weighted vote total. The problem is that with three or more competing theories this generates Condorcet cycles—A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A—so there is no stable choice. Because cyclical preferences are incoherent, the parliament model fails. The only remaining stable strategy is therefore an epsilon-lexicographic rule: if your credence in any deontological constraint exceeds an arbitrarily small epsilon, you must assign that constraint infinite negative weight, which collapses every decision into simple deontological compliance whenever a constraint is in play.”
