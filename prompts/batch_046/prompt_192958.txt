hey, i'm working on this argument for a seminar on immigration and welfare states and it keeps running away from me. here's the chain:

(1) a domestic welfare state is only justified if there's a closed reciprocity loop—citizens tax themselves and draw benefits as co-authors of the scheme, not as strangers.

(2) if borders are genuinely open with no assimilation period or contribution waiting-period—no "integration debt"—then anyone can enter, claim benefits, and never have contributed, which breaks that loop for existing members.

(3) so either the state closes the loop by restricting entry, or the welfare scheme stops being reciprocal and becomes pure global charity.

(4) but pure global charity doesn't need a state boundry at all; it's cosmopolitan redistribution, not a welfare state.

(5) therefore open borders are incompatible with maintaining a welfare state in the justified, reciprocal sense.

(6) now apply the same logic inside a federal country: if provinces have different welfare regimes and people can move freely, then someone can move from a low-benefit province to a high-benefit one, claim benefits, and break the provincial reciprocity loop.

(7) to prevent that you'd need internal residency requirements—basically internal borders—for welfare access.

(8) but we reject internal borders as violations of citizenship, so we must reject the reciprocity justification for welfare altogether.

(9) but if we reject reciprocity, then there's no special reason to prefer domestic redistribution over global redistribution, so the welfare state loses its moral distinctiveness and collapses into charity.

i feel like something slipped between (2) and (6)—maybe i'm treating physical borders and administrative borders as the same kind of thing?—but i can't name the mistake. i really want to keep (1) because it explains why people feel betrayed when benefits go to newcomers without prior contribution. which step should i reject, why did it look okay, and what do the standard positions—cosmopolitan, liberal-nationalist, and the "welfare magnet" empiricists—each have to pay to reject it?
