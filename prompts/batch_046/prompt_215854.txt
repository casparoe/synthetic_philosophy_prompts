Question: David Lewis analyzes convention as a solution to coordination problems sustained by common knowledge and conditional preferences. Some philosophers have tried to extend or replace Lewis's framework with evolutionary game-theoretic models of norm emergence. Assess whether evolutionary dynamics can capture the normative force that Lewis attributes to conventions—specifically, the idea that deviating from a convention is not merely disadvantageous but, in some sense, wrong or mistaken. In your answer, explain Lewis's conditions for convention and discuss at least one significant difference between Lewisian and evolutionary explanations of why agents follow regularities.

Student response: Lewis defines a convention as a regularity in behavior within a population that solves a coordination problem, where everyone prefers to conform given that others do, there is a salient alternative, and all of this is common knowledge. The normative force comes from the fact that once established, deviating frustrates mutual expectations—it's not just inefficient but violates a kind of implicit contract among participants. This makes Lewis's view essentially a rational-choice theory of norms: conventions bind us because we have collectively, if tacitly, agreed to them.

Evolutionary accounts, like those developed by Skyrms or applied to language by Millikan, replace this rational-choice framework with selection dynamics. In these models, regularities emerge because they confer reproductive or strategic advantage, not because agents recognize them as solutions to coordination problems. Consequently, evolutionary norms lack the normative dimension Lewis describes: following them is adaptive, not obligatory. A speaker who violates a linguistic convention isn't breaking an implicit agreement; she's just using a strategy that hasn't been selected for. So while evolutionary models explain the stability of norms, they cannot capture the distinctively normative expectation that others ought to conform—that's unique to Lewis's common-knowledge framework.
