As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying moral and political philosophy, I find myself facing a practical dilemma in a local cooperative that seems to mirror debates I have been reading about, though I cannot place the exact parallel.

Several years ago, my neighbors and I founded a shared fabrication workshop. Each household contributed an equal share of the initial capital—tools, raw materials, and a precision CNC mill that I donated from my former laboratory. At the outset, we adopted what I then called a maximum-entropy allocation rule: every member was entitled to access resources in proportion to their initial contribution, with adjustments only for wear and tear calculated by a depreciation formula I derived from thermodynamic analogies.

Over time, members began exchanging services and equipment through purely voluntary agreements. One neighbor, an exceptionally skilled machinist, gradually accumulated a disproportionate share—roughly sixty percent by current valuation—because others freely chose to transfer tools and materials to him in exchange for custom fabrication work. No one was coerced; every transaction was documented and unanimous.

Recently, I proposed reinstating the original calibrated allocation, arguing that the present disparity violates our founding principle and creates a structural imbalance that undermines collective governance. The machinist objected that he holds legitimate title to everything he received through consensual exchange, and that any forced reallocation would constitute an unjust expropriation.

I am trying to determine whether there exists a genuinely principled justification for enforcing the initial allocation against the outcome of these free transfers, or whether my instinct to rebalance is merely an aesthetic attachment to my thermodynamic model. Could you work through the relevant reasoning—without merely invoking named authors or schools of thought—and advise me on the strength of each position?
