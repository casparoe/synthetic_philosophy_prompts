I am a retired physicist who has begun studying philosophy in earnest over the past two years. I now sit on the advisory council of my retirement community, where we have become entangled in a dispute that feels less like a disagreement over property and more like a logical trap.

Two longtime residents, both substantial donors to our shared library, are deadlocked over a proposed renovation. Resident A has threatened to withdraw her entire donated collection—roughly forty percent of our holdings—if the renovation proceeds without her approval. Resident B has countered that he will file a formal grievance with the regional oversight board, which automatically freezes all community funding for capital projects and delays every other improvement for at least eighteen months. Both threats are credible, and both parties would suffer significantly if they followed through. To break the deadlock, the board proposes a binding escalation mechanism: if either party makes a formal move—filing the grievance or initiating the withdrawal—the library is automatically locked for thirty days with no human override possible, and both parties lose their voting rights on library matters for six months. The reasoning is that making the consequences of escalation so severe and automatic will deter either side from pulling the trigger, thereby forcing compromise.

As I listened, I could not shake the feeling that we were constructing an artificial doomsday switch—deliberately worsening our own position if a line is crossed, solely to make that line uncrossable. I want to understand whether this is a rational strategy for achieving cooperation, or whether it falls into the paradox where stability depends on committing to actions that are, taken alone, irrational and destructive. Specifically, if rationality requires that I be able to carry out only actions I actually want to carry out, then removing my own option to retreat seems like a failure of rationality rather than an achievement. But if rationality is instead about the outcomes my commitments produce in others, then binding myself might be the wisest move.

Can you advise me on whether such a mechanism is philosophically defensible? Please treat the situation in its own terms, without assuming I have correctly named the underlying analogy, and apply the relevant theory to the concrete details: the automatic lock, the forfeited voting rights, and the fact that both parties are already worse off if either threat succeeds. I want to know whether there is a coherent account of rational commitment that justifies this design, and what I must give up if I reject it.

Please say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Write your answer in American English.
