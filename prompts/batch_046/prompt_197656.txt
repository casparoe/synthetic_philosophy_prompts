Consider the following exam question in metaphysics and practical reason, and then the student response submitted for grading.

Question: Some philosophers propose an “authority-weight model” of diachronic rationality: each temporal stage of an agent assigns deliberative weight to other stages strictly in proportion to psychological continuity, with no intrinsic calendar-time discount factor. A critic objects that this model collapses into pure time preference, since psychological continuity decays exponentially with temporal distance, so the weights end up tracking calendar time rather than relation strength. Evaluate whether this collapse argument succeeds, and whether the authority-weight model can genuinely distinguish itself both from pure time preference and from reductionist accounts of personal identity that treat special concern for one’s future self as a rationalized convention rather than a relation-sensitive commitment.

Student answer: “The authority-weight model is basically pure time preference in disguise, because continuity always fades over time. If you care about your future self in ten years less than in one year, that is just discounting the future. So the model solves nothing. Parfit showed that if personal identity is just psychological continuity, then there is no reason to care more about my future self than about a stranger. So reductionism and the authority-weight model both say special concern is irrational. But people do care more about near futures, so the model describes actual psychology. The collapse argument fails because continuity can stay high across long gaps if values remain stable, but that only means the discount rate is not constant, which is still pure time preference with a variable rate. Therefore the model cannot distinguish itself.”
