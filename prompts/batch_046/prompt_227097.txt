I am a PhD student in historical methodology. For a graduate seminar of about ten students meeting for ninety minutes, design a structured simulation that makes participants feel—before anyone names the concept—that microhistory and elite/state-centered history are not complementary scopes but competing causal regimes. Inject the niche framing of “archival scale-lock”: the idea that the granularity of surviving sources constrains the kind of causation you can legitimately attribute, so aggregating micro-evidence into structural explanation is not additive but lossy and transformative.

Split the group in two and assign each half a reconstructed archive about the same localized 1848 food riot. One side receives only state-scale materials—prefectural reports, tax rolls, ministerial correspondence—while the other receives only micro-scale fragments: one butcher’s ledger, a torn petition with partial signatures, a parish burial record. Give them forty minutes to produce a causal explanation of the riot’s origin and significance. Then require them to present to each other and jointly vote on a single two-sentence “official account,” with the rule that either side may veto any claim that relies on evidence absent from its own archive.

Specify the debrief: map the veto moments onto the problem of aggregation, the suppression of anomalous detail onto the epistemic cost of macro-categorization, and the failed or coerced vote onto the claim that the two histories operate with incommensurable ontologies rather than different zoom levels. Tell me what will predictably go wrong—the state-scale group likely dominates verbally because its categories sound more authoritative—and how to correct it by giving the micro-scale group the final word in the vote. Finally, include an honest note about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce—specifically, the long-term archival survival bias that makes micro-sources scarce in the first place—which you should instruct me to supply in the closing remarks. Keep the design free of required readings, citations, or bibliographies.
