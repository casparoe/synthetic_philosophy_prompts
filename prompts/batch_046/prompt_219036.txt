You are delivering the opening remarks at a one-day workshop titled "Norms Without Minds," attended by philosophers of action and evolutionary biologists in roughly equal measure. The theme is whether rationales—reasons that explain or justify—can float free of any reasoning agent's mental states. Structure the address so that the distinction does real work: distinguish a genuinely free-floating rationale from two common disguises, namely causal-selection stories that borrow normative vocabulary and mentalistic projections onto non-cognitive systems. Speak to both halves of the room without simplifying for either; the philosophers should see that the challenge is not merely about stripping mind from explanation, and the biologists should see that functional talk need not be anthropomorphic. To ground the abstraction, sketch one concrete case—perhaps a synthetic gene-regulatory circuit or an institutional practice—showing what a non-mental rationale would have to look like to count as more than metaphor. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or specific citations. If the framing itself is confused—if the idea of rationales without reasoners collapses into one of the two disguises or is incoherent—say so plainly instead of smoothing it over with rhetorical balance. Aim for about eight minutes, formal but not lecturing.
