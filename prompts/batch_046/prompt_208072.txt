I have been observing a particular argumentative maneuver in discussions that draw on Borges's conceptions of time — especially those that treat his branching-path narrative and the idea of eternal recurrence or circular time as live philosophical options rather than mere literary metaphors. The pattern is not a synthesis of linear and circular time, but a sustained, unreconciled deployment of both. In one register, time is branching and irreversible: every decision actualizes one path and nullifies others, which seems to secure a robust notion of contingency and individual responsibility. In another register, time is circular or simultaneous: all moments coexist, repetition is inevitable, and distinctions between agents or choices collapse. The distinctive move is that both registers are kept active within the same argument. For example, when the problem is whether a particular historical actor could have done otherwise, the branching model is invoked to establish that alternative paths were genuinely open; but when the problem shifts to whether that actor is ultimately responsible or distinct from others who made different choices, the circular model is invoked to dissolve those distinctions. A second instance appears in discussions of personal identity across time: the branching ontology isolates a self at each node, preserving numerical difference, while the circular ontology collapses all nodes into a single eternal subject, and the tension between them is used not to resolve but to suspend the question of whether identity is preserved or obliterated. Is there an established name for this specific practice — maintaining two incompatible temporal ontologies in suspension and using the gap between them to block definitive conclusions about agency, contingency, or identity? I am not satisfied with a bare label. I want to know whether the phenomenon is recognized at all, how its boundaries are drawn — for instance, whether both ontologies must be held literally or one may be metaphorical, and whether the suspension must be intentional or can occur unwittingly — and what distinguishes it from ordinary philosophical eclecticism, dialectical tension, or strategic ambiguity in argumentation.
