As a novelist reading around John von Neumann’s speculations on self-replicating automata and the endurance of technological systems beyond their originating civilizations, I keep encountering a pattern in historical examples that I cannot name precisely. A technology is designed to solve a problem articulated within a specific conceptual framework—say, mechanical computation shaped by ballistic mathematics, or an automaton conceived within an obsolete organizational theory. The framework collapses or is forgotten, yet the technology persists, and over generations it reshapes the surrounding problem-space so thoroughly that later thinkers can no longer state the original problem except through the technology’s own categories. This persistence is not merely practical, as in path dependence or lock-in, because it does not simply constrain future choices; rather, it retroactively renders the pre-technological conceptual world unintelligible or naive, colonizing the very conditions under which alternatives could be conceived. I have seen analogues in how early computing architectures silently informed later philosophies of mind, and in von Neumann’s suggestion that a sufficiently complex machine might survive the mathematics that designed it. Is there an established concept for this specific mode of technological survival—where endurance entails the erasure of the conceptual world that made the technology originally intelligible—or is it discussed only under broader headings such as technological determinism or black-boxing? How should it be distinguished from function creep, infrastructure inversion, or ordinary auxiliary hypotheses? And if no precise term exists, how might one delimit the phenomenon so that its boundaries are clear? I should add that if my framing conflates technological persistence with conceptual history in a way that renders the phenomenon illusory, or if isolating it as I have described is incoherent, please say so plainly rather than accommodating the premise.
