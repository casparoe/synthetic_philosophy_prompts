I'm trying to identify whether there is an established concept for the following pattern in accounts of tragic art. Consider two people encountering the same sorrowful work: one who has previously endured a closely analogous real loss, and one who has not. The first frequently describes the experience as valuable because the work gives form to a grief she could not herself articulate; the second typically reports only distress or distance. Meanwhile, standard explanations of why we enjoy sad art invoke mechanisms—cathartic release, safe practice with negative affect, or the cognitive satisfaction of comprehending profound themes—that are meant to apply universally, regardless of biography. The pattern is that the biography-dependent explanation is treated as legitimate by those who possess the matching history, yet it is rarely proposed as a general resolution to the paradox, since it makes aesthetic appreciation contingent on private trauma and risks reducing the work's value to personal therapy. At the same time, it differs from ordinary character identification, because the viewer may value the work precisely for articulating an experience she lacked the language to express herself. Does this configuration have a specific name in aesthetics or philosophy of emotion? How should it be classified in relation to the paradox of tragedy—as a rival theory, a boundary condition, a category mistake, or something else? And what exactly distinguishes it from both universalist explanations and from simple empathic identification?
