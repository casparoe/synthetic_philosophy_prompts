Design a decision-tree taxonomy for a philosophy-of-biology seminar that classifies a researcher’s actual explanatory commitments—rather than their self-identified label—regarding morphological traits that appear to lack adaptive function. The tree should take as input answers to concrete, case-based diagnostic questions and terminate in exactly eight named positions. The root commitment is: “Consider a mature morphological feature F in species S—e.g., the male nipple, or a non-functional vestigial eye in a cave-dwelling organism—that currently confers no detectable fitness advantage.” From this root, construct branching nodes that ask, in order: (a) whether F is a necessary structural byproduct of an adaptive architecture (the strict spandrel thesis) or whether its presence is developmentally contingent; (b) if it is a spandrel, whether it persists mainly because of shared ontogenetic pathways with adaptive traits, because of selective neutrality coupled with genetic hitchhiking or pleiotropy, or merely as evolutionary dead weight that selection has not yet purged; (c) for cases where F is not a spandrel, whether the researcher denies that genuinely non-adaptive traits persist at all (functionalist skepticism about hidden adaptations) or instead holds that every trait is either currently adaptive or was previously adaptive and has since been co-opted (the exaptation-only view); and (d) within the exaptation branch, whether co-optation requires that F possessed prior adaptive potential, or whether a trait can be exapted from a genuinely neutral spandrel. The eight terminal leaves must be: (1) hard Gould--Lewontin pluralist—spandrels are widespread and routinely mistaken for adaptations; (2) strict adaptationist with exaptation escape—denies persistent true spandrels and explains apparent ones away via exaptation; (3) developmental-constraint primacist—non-adaptive traits are ontogenetic byproducts of conserved pathways, not architectural spandrels; (4) neutralist with hitchhiking—persistence explained by linkage or pleiotropy rather than architecture; (5) exaptation-only—no true spandrels exist, only current adaptations or exapted prior adaptations; (6) evolutionary dead-weight—spandrels exist but only as transient, unpurged residue; (7) functionalist skeptic—apparent non-adaptation always reflects incomplete knowledge of hidden function; and (8) one unoccupied or incoherent combination of answers that the tree logically permits, which you should diagnose briefly (for example, a view that asserts both that F is a necessary architectural spandrel and that its later co-optation requires prior adaptive potential in F). Every internal node must be phrased as a concrete claim or case question—such as, “If eliminating F’s developmental precursor also eliminates an adaptive trait elsewhere in the same organism, does that better explain F’s persistence than the mere absence of negative selection pressure?”—so that a respondent can answer without knowing which leaf the answer selects. Build the tree from the logical space of answer combinations first, then map the named positions onto the leaves; flag any pair of leaves that your diagnostic questions cannot genuinely separate. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. The audience is graduate students in philosophy of biology, and the tree should be compact enough to present in a single seminar handout.
