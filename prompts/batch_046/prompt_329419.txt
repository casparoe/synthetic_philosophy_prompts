For my philosophy-of-marriage seminar, I am writing a response paper that reads James Baldwin's Giovanni's Room through the debate over what marriage is for—specifically, the regulatory view (marriage as a state mechanism for kinship and economic stability) against the expressive view (marriage as public recognition of a pre-existing romantic union). Rather than applying these categories directly, I want to develop what I am calling a "marriage-shaped absence": the idea that David's engagement to Hella and Giovanni's total exclusion from any marital framework do not simply leave a neutral gap, but actively generate a negative institutional space that structures the characters' suffering. Could you help me construct a faithful reading along these lines? I need you to show how the scenes surrounding David's engagement to Hella and the final parting with Giovanni dramatize this absence without ever naming it, and to say whether Baldwin implies that same-sex relationships in the 1950s were ethically deformed by being denied access to the very form that might have stabilized them. I also want to address whether this reading risks reducing the novel to a prefiguration of same-sex marriage rights, and whether the text's attention to economic dependency—David's reliance on Hella's family resources versus Giovanni's poverty—complicates any claim that extending marital recognition alone would have resolved the tragedy.
