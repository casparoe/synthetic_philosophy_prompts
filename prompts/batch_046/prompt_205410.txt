I was discussing Buddhist philosophy with two colleagues after a seminar on personal identity, and they became locked in a disagreement about whether anatta is compatible with any meaningful sense of self over time. (1) Elena argued that the Buddhist denial of a permanent self entails that there is literally no subject who persists through change: the skandhas are like a flame passed from candle to candle, so asking whether I will experience future suffering is as confused as asking whether this flame is the same one lit yesterday. (2) Marcus replied that Elena treats conventional identity as nothing, when in fact the Buddhist tradition clearly operates with dependently originated persons—designated by name, bound by karma, and capable of moral progress—so anatta targets only an illusory metaphysical substrate, not the functional self required for ethics and narrative. (3) Elena then pressed a harder point: if the conventional person is just a causally linked stream of aggregates with no underlying owner, there is no principled reason why prudential concern should extend to future aggregates but not to causally unrelated ones; she introduced a hypothetical case in which a meditator enters complete mental cessation for a century, after which aggregates resume with full memory restoration—if this is still me, she asked, why is a perfectly accurate neural upload not also me, given that both preserve causal-informational continuity without any enduring substance? (4) Marcus conceded that the boundary is genuinely hard to draw but insisted that karma and intentional continuity provide a principled distinction: the resumed meditator inherits karmic traces and volitional patterns of the prior stream, whereas an upload is a copy, not a continuation, because the original causal chain of mental formations has been broken. (5) Elena responded that this pushes the problem back onto what counts as breaking a chain, and that Marcus is smuggling in a hidden essence—call it a karmic signature—that does exactly the metaphysical work anatta was supposed to reject; Marcus countered that no hidden essence is needed, only the fact that Buddhist soteriology treats liberation as occurring within a single continuum, not across duplicates, which shows the tradition itself treats causal continuity as identity-constituting without requiring an owner. They turned to me and asked me to referee, and I found myself genuinely unsure who is right about which point. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating, state who is closer to being right overall, and identify the crucial conceptual issue both are neglecting. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
