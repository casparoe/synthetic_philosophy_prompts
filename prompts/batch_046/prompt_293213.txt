I'm currently reading for a module on aesthetics and the epistemology of art, and I've found the literature on aesthetic testimony particularly elusive—not because the arguments are obscure, but because they seem to rely on analogies that are never fully unpacked. In our recent seminars, the comparison that keeps resurfacing is that judging a work of art is rather like tasting wine: just as one cannot genuinely know whether one will enjoy a particular vintage solely by reading a sommelier's tasting notes, so one cannot come to understand the aesthetic value of a painting or a symphony through another person's report alone. The analogy is routinely deployed—by both students and, implicitly, by several authors we have read—to license a strong anti-testimony conclusion, namely that second-hand aesthetic reports are at best heuristic prompts to seek direct experience, and at worst a form of aesthetic self-deception that substitutes social conformity for genuine sensibility.

I should like to audit this analogy properly rather than simply endorse or reject it. Could you first provide an explicit correspondence table, mapping the sommelier, the taster's palate, the vineyard or terroir, the act of tasting itself, and the social setting of the table onto their aesthetic equivalents? Then, please identify precisely where the mapping fractures—distinguishing those disanalogies that genuinely threaten the anti-testimony conclusion from those that are merely decorative or incidental. I am particularly keen to know whether the analogy survives once we inject a slightly less familiar case into the framework: algorithmic curation systems, such as recommendation engines that predict one's preference for a film or musical recording on the basis of behavioural data rather than articulated reasons. Does such an algorithm function as a kind of mechanical sommelier, as a substitute for direct tasting, or as an altogether different epistemic category? And once these fractures are fully visible, does the sceptical conclusion about aesthetic testimony still stand, or was the analogy carrying the entire argumentative burden? If there is a more accurate comparison—perhaps drawn from scientific instrumentation or from the structure of moral testimony—I should also like to hear it, together with an assessment of whether it avoids the same pitfalls.
