Please explain the central conceptual tension between the Milgrom-Stokey no-trade result and the standard epistemic justification of financial markets—namely, that prices aggregate dispersed private information. Give an intuitive but precise account of why, if agents share a common prior and are commonly known to be rational, the observation of a market-clearing price should in principle eliminate any informational asymmetry that would motivate further trade, and yet real markets persistently exhibit both price movement and volume. I want a gut-level understanding of where the analogy between information revelation and preference revelation breaks down, particularly with respect to common knowledge conditions. To push this into less familiar territory, inject the following scenario: suppose agents do not merely hold private information about an asset’s fundamental value, but also hold private, heterogeneous beliefs about the reliability and interpretive framework of the price signal itself—call this meta-informational heterogeneity. Does this hierarchical structure allow rational agents to continue trading after observing the price without violating common knowledge of rationality, or does it merely relocate the no-trade problem to a higher-order level, such as common knowledge of meta-rationality? Finally, assess whether this suggests that the epistemology of markets requires a non-flat, iterative model of belief revision, or whether it reveals a category mistake in applying static common-knowledge conditions to dynamic, noisy price discovery.
