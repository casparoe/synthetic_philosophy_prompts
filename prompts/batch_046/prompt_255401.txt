As a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, I have been trying to formalise my intuitions about biological death for a biomedical-ethics discussion. I tend to model an organism as an integrated control system: death is the irreversible failure of its core controller, not merely the loss of peripheral signals. My commitments are these:

(1) Death is an objective biological fact, not a social or legal construction; it consists in the permanent loss of an organism’s capacity for integrated, self-sustaining functioning.

(2) The whole-brain death criterion best captures this failure mode, whereas circulatory death is only a practical proxy—once central integrative control is lost, peripheral circulation is irrelevant to the system’s status.

(3) A patient in a permanent vegetative state with intact brainstem functions remains alive, since autonomic integration persists; consciousness is not the relevant state variable.

(4) Death must be technology-independent in principle: if a future intervention could restore central integration and circulation, then the interval we labelled “death” was actually a suspended state, not genuine termination.

(5) Medical and legal institutions ought nevertheless to adopt brain death partly because it permits timely organ transplantation—though I concede this sounds like letting institutional interests shape a biological boundary.

(6) I reject constructivist or culturally relativist accounts; there is a mind-independent fact about whether the integrated system has collapsed.

Please audit whether these hang together. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which are only apparently so, and what is the severity ranking? For each real conflict, state the cheapest revision and what it costs. Then tell me which single commitment, if revised, would resolve the greatest number of tensions. Finally, say how confident you are in this audit, and identify the specific point about which you are least sure.
