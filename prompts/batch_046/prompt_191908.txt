me and my roommate got in a huge argument last night about whether misleading someone is actualy as bad as lying. he works in ad compliance and said theres this sharp institutional line—if an ad literally says something false, the regulators can shut it down, but if you just arrange the stats and visuals so people draw the wrong conclusion on there own, thats usually okay unless its materially deceptive. he thinks that makes sense because lying breaks a cooperative rule, but misleading is more like letting someone trip over there own reasoning.

but that got me wondering what theory is actualy baked into that practice. does it presuppose that falsifying is morally different from what some people call epistemic exploitation—where you manipulate the information environment so the listener forms a false belief through there own rationality? and is the practice consistent with itself? in court, perjury only covers false statements under oath, but lawyers can ask leading questions that are technically true yet designed to spin the jury. the system punishes a witness for a technically false yes but rewards the lawyer for technically true framing.

if we took the view that strategic ambiguity and selective framing are just as bad as falsehood—maybe on kantian grounds, or because both violate epistemic autonomy—what would have to change? would ad law need to ban implication altogether? would cross-examination rules change?

can you reconstruct the implicit theory behind that regulatory and legal split, say whether it strictly needs the lying/misleading distinction or if its just an enforcement convenience, and tell me what'd have to change in practice if we replaced it with a view that treats manipulation of context as equivalent to lying? im especially curious whether the practice secretly relies on a gricean picture about violating maxims of quality vs quantity, or whether thats just post-hoc rationalization.
