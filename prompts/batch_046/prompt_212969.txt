A colleague in a rhetoric seminar summarized Douglas Hofstadter's well-known satire essay on gendered language—commonly referred to as the Person Paper—as follows. They claimed that Hofstadter demonstrates that masculine generics such as mankind or the default he operate as purely syntactic agreement markers, entirely devoid of semantic gender content, analogous to grammatical number rather than natural gender. From this, they argued, Hofstadter derives that objections to such language commit a category error: they confuse anaphoric resolution rules with lexical selection restrictions, treating syntactic machinery as if it carried ideological entailment. The colleague further concluded that the essay's parallel between racialized language and gendered language proves that all default gender in pronouns is pragmatic noise, and therefore that feminist attempts at pronoun reform mistake grammatical convention for moral failure, a mistake Hofstadter allegedly exposes to show that syntax is autonomous from social practice. I have reservations. I recall that Hofstadter's satire was designed to highlight an inconsistency—namely, that people who dismiss sexist language as just grammar rarely extend the same tolerance to racially loaded terms—rather than to defend the semantic emptiness of gendered pronouns. But my colleague insists that the technical distinction between agreement-driven anaphora and referential gender encoding is implicit in the essay's logic, even if Hofstadter never uses that vocabulary. Is this summary fair? Please distinguish what accurately captures Hofstadter's rhetorical strategy and analogical reasoning, what is a defensible modern reframing using contemporary linguistic concepts, and what appears to misrepresent his position—particularly regarding whether he regards masculine generics as semantically null, whether he endorses the autonomy of syntax, and whether the essay's conclusion supports the claim that language reform is conceptually misguided rather than socially necessary.
