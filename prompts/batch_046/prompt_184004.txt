I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I caught a sharp exchange at a workshop on decision-making last month. Two researchers -- Dr. Reed and Dr. Park -- got into it over coffee and wouldn't drop it, so I want someone to actually referee what they said.

(1) Reed started by saying Simon's satisficing model works descritpive because people pick an aspiration level, search until something clears it, and stop. The whole point, she said, is that they don't compute probabilities or maximize expected utility; they just use a local stopping rule.

(2) Park said that's fine for static worlds, but real option spaces shrink while you search -- job offers expire, apartments get rented out. He proposed what he called a decay-weighted threshold: your aspiration level should drop over time using a simple urgency heuristic, not an optimization calculation. He insisted this is still satisficing, just with a moving bar.

(3) Reed shot back that if your bar responds to the shrinking environment, you're implicitly weighing the cost of continued search against expected remaining value. That's dynamic programming, she said, which is exactly the computation Simon wanted bounded rationality to avoid.

(4) Park replied there's no computation happening. The decay is driven by an affective signal -- anxiety about missing out -- and the brain applies a linear reduction rule. No probabilities, no expected utility weights. So it's ecological rationality, not economic rationality.

(5) Reed said anxiety is just an internal price signal. If affective states can shift your threshold, then every rational choice can be redescribed as satisficing with feelings, which makes the theory unfalsifiable.

They turned to me and asked who's right. Can you go through thier exchange move by move -- rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justify briefly, and say who's right about what? What's the actual conceptual fault line they're dancing around? And is there something both miss about how bounded rationality actually handles time-sensitive environments? Don't give me a bibliography or a reading list -- just adjudicate the arguments as they actually made them.
