In debates over democratic boundaries, people frequently analogize the demos to a members-only cooperative—specifically a housing cooperative with both equity-owning members and long-term leaseholders who live on the premises, are fully bound by its bylaws, and bear its costs, yet hold no voting shares in its governance. Closure advocates invoke the equity members: democracy requires a stable, defined body of joint authors with mutual responsibility for collective decisions, just as a cooperative requires identifiable shareholders to prevent dilution of accountability and to protect the value of the common asset. Cosmopolitan or “all-affected-interests” advocates invoke the leaseholders: anyone subject to the cooperative’s rules and embedded in its territory has a legitimate claim to a vote, regardless of formal title, because being governed without representation violates the same principle that would justify giving leaseholders a board seat. The analogy is used to license either a restrictive or an expansive view of citizenship and territorial jurisdiction.

How far does this cooperative model actually hold? First, provide an explicit correspondence table—what in democratic theory plays the role of the board of directors, the equity members, the leaseholders, the bylaws, the common property, the admission procedures, and the mechanisms for expulsion or withdrawal? Second, identify where the mapping breaks: democratic authority is typically non-voluntary at birth and applies coercively to non-members within territory in ways cooperative rules do not apply to outsiders; democratic goods are not fungible assets like real estate; and democratic obligations are not reducible to contractual consent. Third, separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the closure and cosmopolitan arguments from those that are merely cosmetic—ask, in particular, whether the analogy quietly imports property-rights and contractual assumptions that democratic theory should reject. Finally, do either of the conclusions—that democratic membership should be closed like a shareholders’ meeting, or open like a stakeholder board—survive once the disanalogies are on the table? Or does the analogy carry the normative work that the arguments themselves cannot support? If the cooperative model fails, suggest what a more appropriate analogy for democratic boundaries would be and why it avoids the same distortions.
