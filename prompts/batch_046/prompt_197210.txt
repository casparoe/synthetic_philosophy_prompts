You serve on the Academic Governance Board of a mid-sized university that must decide by next month whether to retain or abolish the Common Examination — a ninety-year-old requirement that every graduating senior, regardless of major, must orally defend a single self-chosen question before a panel of three faculty from disciplines outside their own. The exam has no standardized rubric; panels award only Pass or Fail with a brief narrative justification, and failure rates have hovered near eight percent for decades. Proponents, including several emeritus faculty, argue that the exam's very arbitrariness is its virtue: it creates an unpredictable friction point that prevents students from optimizing solely for disciplinary metrics and preserves a shared sense of intellectual risk across the university. Opponents — including most department chairs and student government — contend that in an era of specialized accreditation and student debt, an opaque, non-curricular hurdle serves only as an arbitrary barrier with no demonstrable learning outcome, and they ask the Board to vote for abolition, with the option to replace it with a voluntary interdisciplinary capstone.

Draft the Board's formal decision memo, addressed to the Provost and to be circulated before the vote. Recommend a specific course — retain, abolish, or retain with structural reform — and provide reasoning the Board could defend to both constituencies. In particular, take seriously the Burkean and Chestertonian claim that long-standing institutions may serve functions invisible to present users, applying it specifically to this exam rather than invoking it as a generic slogan; address the concern that functional opacity can become an unfalsifiable excuse for institutional inertia. Include an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, a concrete implementation timeline, and a clear condition or metric that would trigger reconsideration within five years.
