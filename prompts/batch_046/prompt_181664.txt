Two interlocutors were debating whether karma is best understood as a backward-looking theory of moral desert or as a forward-looking pedagogical mechanism. The first maintained that karma is essentially retributive: virtuous and vicious actions produce corresponding experiences not merely as incentives but because the agent deserves them, with the intentional quality of the act serving as the vehicle of desert across the continuity of rebirth. The second objected that this renders karma vulnerable to moral luck—if a malicious intention is thwarted by external circumstances so that no harm occurs, the desert-based view must either deny that desert was satisfied or invoke invisible causal mechanisms, both of which appear ad hoc. She proposed reading karma instead as calibrated moral feedback: pleasure and suffering shape future perceptual and ethical dispositions, so desert plays no independent explanatory role.

The first replied that stripping away desert collapses karma into a utilitarian training program, losing its distinctive claim that the moral quality of an action is intrinsically tied to the agent's subsequent experience. He introduced a further case: an enlightened being who, having exhausted all personal karmic residue, voluntarily assumes the suffering of others out of compassion. If karma is purely pedagogical, this transfer is inexplicable—there is no learner being shaped—whereas on a desert view the being is not violating justice but is rather acting like a guarantor who assumes another's debt, since the burden is voluntarily taken rather than imposed.

The second countered that this very example undermines the desert interpretation. If suffering can be voluntarily assumed and thereby dissolved without being earned by the one who experiences it, then suffering is not a necessary expression of prior moral failure, and the link between action and consequence is conventional rather than metaphysically required. She added that if personal identity across lives is not a substantial self but a causal-psychological continuum, then talk of one being "deserving" another's suffering becomes incoherent, since there is no enduring subject to bear the desert.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: evaluate the initial distinction between retributive and pedagogical karma, the moral-luck objection, the enlightened-being counterexample, and the final argument about voluntary assumption of suffering coupled with the continuity of personal identity. Indicate who is right about which specific point, whether either side neglects an important distinction—such as between karmic result and karmic seed, or between collective and individual desert—and which interpretation, desert-based or pedagogical, better withstands the objections raised.
