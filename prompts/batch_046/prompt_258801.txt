I'm writing an essay for my philosophy of AI seminar on the frame problem, and I'd like comments before I submit the draft. My central claim is that standard formal solutions—McCarthy's situation calculus and Reiter's successor-state axioms—resolve the representational frame problem, that is, how to encode economically that most fluents do not change after an action, but they leave untouched what I call the practical frame problem: an agent can possess correct frame axioms and still fail to deploy them under time pressure, resource limits, or shifting contexts. To develop this, I draw an analogy to what I term moral frame inertia in human practical reasoning. Just as an AI needs explicit axioms to avoid computing every non-effect, human moral cognition seems to operate with implicit moral frames that treat biological humans as salient moral patients and entities like distant future beings or current artificial systems as background; revising these frames requires a kind of cognitive labor that parallels the computational cost the frame problem describes. I then ask whether this analogy reveals that the frame problem is not peculiar to artificial agents but a general feature of bounded rationality—any system with limited processing capacity needs framing mechanisms, and the real difficulty lies not in static representation but in the dynamics of frame revision under action. I want to be careful not to misrepresent Reiter's axioms, which I understand solve the representational problem on its own terms, and I am unsure whether the move to moral cognition is a legitimate extension or a category mistake that conflates formal architecture with normative commitment. Please tell me where the distinction between representational and practical frame problems is sharpest, whether the moral analogy illuminates anything about bounded rationality or merely confuses cognitive economy with moral prejudice, and—most importantly—what argument or empirical result from cognitive science or decision theory would change your mind about whether there genuinely is a practical frame problem distinct from the representational one. Please also say where my reasoning is most likely to fail, especially in the transition from AI formalism to human moral psychology.
