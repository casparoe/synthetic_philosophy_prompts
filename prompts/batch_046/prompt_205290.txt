In a bunch of East Asian hospitals there's this protocol: if a patient gets a terminal diagnosis, the doctor tells the family first and asks them whether the patient should even be informed. The family can veto disclosure entirely, and the staff treats that as binding — no need for the patient's own consent. But the same hospital will make that exact patient sign an individual consent form for setting a broken arm or getting stitches. So we treat personal autonmy as absolute for minor stuff but basically irrelevant for life-altering news. What view of the self, or of moral obligation, does that double standard preseuppose? Is it just Confucian role ethics in disguise — that you're a relational node, not an isolated rights-bearer — or can a liberal individualist framework accommodate it? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the practice actually needs that view or is compatible with several, and tell me what the protocol would have to change if we replaced the relational picture with a straight individualist one.
