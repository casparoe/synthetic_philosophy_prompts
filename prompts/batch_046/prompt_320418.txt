I work as a senior software engineer on a platform infrastructure team, with no formal background in philosophy or decision theory. We are formalizing a protocol for evaluating proposals to replace human-reviewed deployment gates with automated approvals in our CI/CD pipeline. The stakes are high—production outages, security breaches, and rollback failures—yet our current process is ad hoc, and I need something my team can run repeatedly without philosophical training and with clear sign-off rules.

I have encountered the framework of HCH—Humans Consulting HCH—as an idealization of deliberation in which a complex judgment is decomposed into smaller, largely independent sub-questions that can be resolved separately and then aggregated, together with the related notion of factored cognition. I want you to translate this framework into a practical, reusable decision procedure for our team. The deliverable should be a step-by-step protocol: the ordered questions to ask for each proposal, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, the explicit stopping conditions where the answer is no or where human sign-off is mandatory, and the precise points where the question is one of values rather than technical fact and judgment cannot be eliminated.

Please include a concise set of test conditions or decision rules that a reviewer without theoretical training can apply. Then run the procedure on three concrete proposals: (a) automating syntax-only lint checks; (b) automating security vulnerability assessments for third-party dependencies; and (c) automating authorization to roll back a production deployment when an outage is detected. Include one case—likely the rollback case—where the procedure yields a recommendation to automate that I will instinctively resist, and explain why. Also state clearly what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of proposals it misclassifies, and where independence between sub-questions breaks down.

Additionally, I am considering injecting an adversarial review step—assigning one reviewer to argue against automation regardless of their private view—to preserve the independence required by factored cognition. Incorporate this into the procedure and flag its limitations.

Finally, please be explicit about your own epistemic position: state what argument or finding would change your recommendation (for example, evidence that factored judgments in engineering teams are systematically correlated rather than independent), and identify where your reasoning is most vulnerable to failure, particularly regarding the adversarial step and the translation from an idealized deliberator to our actual team structure.
