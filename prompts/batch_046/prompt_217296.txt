I'm presenting at next week's interdisciplinary workshop on "Attention, Judgment, and Democratic Institutions"—hosted jointly by the political theory and comparative politics groups—to defend a reconstructed reading of Simone Weil's 1943 essay on party abolition. My thesis is that the essay is not a proto-deliberative or anarchist manifesto but a phenomenological diagnosis of how party membership structurally disables pesée, the mode of judgmental weighing that requires an uncollapsed interval between belief and assent. I argue that party loyalty operates as pesanteur—a collective gravity that eliminates this interval by substituting group identity for individual attention—and that this explains pathologies in modern "platform parties" that empirical polarization studies miss: not disagreement itself, but the foreclosure of the mental space in which disagreement can be genuinely weighed. I strip the argument of Weil's theological vocabulary (decreation, affliction, grace) but retain the structural claim about attention and collective belonging. The audience will be mostly empirical political scientists and deliberative democrats skeptical of metaphysical imports, plus one intellectual historian of twentieth-century French philosophy who knows Weil's Cahiers intimately. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually face, ordered by likely damage, each phrased in the vocabulary this audience uses (institutional feasibility, deliberative legitimacy, comparative party-system analysis, textual fidelity), with a concise two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. Specify what I should concede up front about the normative limits of the framework, what I must hold if the thesis is to survive, the specific question about the Cahiers that I hope the historian does not raise, and the single reply that has to land for the proposal to survive a hostile empirical turn.
