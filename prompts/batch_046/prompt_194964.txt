Write a dialogue between two interlocutors—call them Mara and Elias—who have just left a graduate seminar on Kierkegaard's account of Abraham and the teleological suspension of the ethical. They should debate whether Abraham's willingness to raise the knife over Isaac represents an authentic transcendence of universal moral norms in the name of a singular relation to the absolute, or whether it instead exposes the dangers of allowing private, incommunicable conviction to override ethical accountability. Mara should defend the suspension view with precision: she should argue that the ethical is not annihilated but placed in abeyance by a higher teleology, that Abraham's silence is not an absence of justification but the necessary form of a faith that cannot be mediated through universal language, and that the knight of faith differs fundamentally from the knight of infinite resignation precisely because Abraham expects Isaac back by virtue of the absurd. Elias should resist this with equal rigor, insisting that if the ethical can be suspended without public criteria, then moral agency collapses into subjectivism; he should press Mara on how we can distinguish a genuine teleological suspension from a mere pathological exception, and he should argue that Abraham's refusal to speak to Isaac or Sarah is not inwardness but a rupture of the interpersonal obligations that constitute ethical life. Their exchange should be genuinely dialectical—each must address the other's specific claims rather than retreating to slogans. For added concreteness, have them discuss whether Abraham's action, if imitated in a modern context—say, a parent who refuses medical treatment for a child on the basis of an unverifiable divine command—should be understood through the same structure of suspension, and ask each character to explain where, if anywhere, the analogy breaks down.
