I sit on our university's behavioral-research ethics committee. We are reviewing a proposal that constructs a live analog of Kavka's toxin puzzle. Each participant receives $200 and is told that if they form a sincere, documented intention—verified through a structured interview and a signed declaration—to forfeit $150 to an anonymous peer, they will be awarded an additional $800; they are then explicitly released from any obligation to actually surrender the money. The investigators claim this transparently tests whether rational agents can form "disconnected intentions" (intentions they do not expect to execute) without the deceptive framing common in social-psychology studies. Opponents on the committee argue that the design exploits a rationality paradox to induce cognitive dissonance about one's own agency, that measuring "sincerity" of intention creates coercive pressure to perform belief for payment, and that standard informed-consent procedures do not adequately cover the distinctive risk of asking subjects to rationalize an intention they predict they will abandon. A further condition divides subjects: half are told the peer is real and identifiable, half that the peer is purely hypothetical, in order to test whether social presence affects the rationality of intention-formation.

Draft the committee's decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—addressed to the principal investigators. Provide a clear, actionable bottom line and reasoning they could actually respond to, including an honest treatment of the strongest argument for the opposing view. Specifically address whether reliance on the toxin-puzzle structure introduces an ethical risk that conventional consent and debriefing procedures fail to cover. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
