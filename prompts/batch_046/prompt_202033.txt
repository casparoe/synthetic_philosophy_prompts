In seminars on belief revision, Jeffrey conditionalization is frequently likened to adjusting the gain knobs on an audio mixer: each channel corresponds to a cell of the partition, the internal mix within that channel represents the prior conditional probabilities, and the knob-turn represents the new weight assigned by the uncertain evidence, so that the overall sound retains its original tonal balance even as its loudness profile shifts. The analogy is often invoked to argue that Jeffrey updating is uniquely conservative — it respects all internal conditional relations while accommodating soft input — and therefore preferable to outright replacement of beliefs. I want you to audit this comparison rigorously: lay out the full correspondence table (channels, knobs, the engineer, the master bus, the signal); identify the points of breakdown, especially where the mixer picture assumes linear, independent adjustments and cannot represent evidence that bears on the partition boundaries themselves or on the likelihood ratios linking cells; examine what the analogy quietly imports about the separability of internal structure and external weight that Jeffrey's rule does not in fact guarantee; and tell me whether the conservative conclusion survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the mixer board was doing all the argumentative work. If the analogy fails, suggest a more apt mechanical or informational image that captures both the rigidity of the conditional structure and the flexibility of the marginal redistribution.
