I'm currently helping design a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of mental disorder, and in preparing a unit on interactive kinds I've become stuck on what seems like a perennial stall in the philosophy of psychiatry. The debate concerns Ian Hacking's looping effects: critics argue that because classifications like borderline personality disorder or ADHD reshape the self-understanding and behavior of those classified—producing feedback dynamics that do not occur in conditions like diabetes—the categories are pragmatic instruments rather than natural kinds. Defenders reply that all human kinds loop to some degree, that looping is compatible with causal efficacy, and that rejecting psychiatric reality on these grounds confuses the dynamics of a kind with its ontological status. Both sides invoke the same clinical histories and the same conceptual framework, yet after years of exchange in journals and panels neither camp has moved.

What makes this personally urgent is a hypothetical I've been testing with colleagues. Imagine we formalize a new diagnostic category—call it "reflexive attention fragmentation"—defined not by a biological marker but by a specific pattern of self-monitoring that patients learn to perform precisely through the structured clinical interview itself. If patients begin to exhibit the pattern only after being asked about it in the protocol, and if clinicians stabilize the protocol because it reliably produces the pattern, we have a loop in which the classification instrument is not merely measuring but scripting the causal structure of the kind. My working hypothesis is that the longstanding debate stalls because both sides treat looping as either corrupting reality (making the kind artifactual) or enriching it (making it dynamically real), but neither has a framework for loops where the diagnostic instrument is constitutive of the kind's causal structure. Is that correct, or is the disagreement actually about hidden standards—whether ontological commitment requires mind-independence, whether predictive stability is sufficient, or whether clinical utility should constrain metaphysics? I want a committed diagnosis: what kind of disagreement is this, supported by what each side actually claims about feedback, classification, and reality, together with an honest assessment of whether a conceptual distinction, an empirical study, or a reframing could actually resolve it, or whether the stall indicates we are asking the wrong question altogether.
