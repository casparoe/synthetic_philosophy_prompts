Design a structured classroom simulation for an upper-level metaphysics seminar of about twelve students running ninety minutes, centered on Leibniz’s identity of indiscernibles and Black’s symmetric universe containing only two qualitatively identical spheres. The exercise should run in three phases arranged so that participants commit to a position before they discover the pressure against it. In phase one, split the group into two teams and assign roles—relational cartographer, symmetry defender, and boundary observer—giving each team identical sets of index cards and a rule that they may describe their universe using only relational properties among the spheres and no absolute spatial coordinates; have them record a shared description asserting that the two spheres are strictly indiscernible. In phase two, withhold a second sheet containing a distant boundary that is present in both worlds but oriented differently relative to each sphere; then reveal that in each team’s world there is an imperceptible directional relation between each sphere and this boundary—preserved under all internal observation yet distinguishing the spheres structurally—without allowing the teams to observe the boundary’s orientation directly. The teams must now revise their descriptions and debate whether this hidden relation renders the spheres discernible, and whether discernibility requires observational accessibility or merely the existence of a structural difference. Use seminar-participation points as payoff: award consistency between a team’s initial and revised descriptions rather than “correctness,” so that defenders of indiscernibility face an incentive cost if they abandon their first commitment without argument. The facilitator should say almost nothing philosophical until the debrief. In the debrief, map the initial construction onto Black’s scenario, the hidden directional relation onto debates about purely structural or relational properties, and the final debate onto whether Leibniz’s principle should apply to differences that are metaphysically present but epistemically inaccessible; predict that students will conflate “we cannot detect a difference” with “there is no difference in being,” and instruct the facilitator to interrupt this by asking them to state the condition under which two descriptions denote the same world rather than the same observable state. Be explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce: Black’s original case presupposes a universe with no external reference frame whatsoever, whereas this exercise necessarily introduces a distant boundary to generate the hidden asymmetry, so the facilitator must supply verbally that in the pure thought experiment even that boundary is absent, and ask whether removing it collapses the structural distinction or merely hides it further. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not invoke specialized frameworks that participants would need prior training in; the hidden directional relation should be invented and fully specified within the prompt’s rules rather than borrowed from an obscure theory.
