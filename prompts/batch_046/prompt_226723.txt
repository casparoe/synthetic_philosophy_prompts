A community arts centre has been offered a substantial endowment to restore and permanently display a large ceramic mural. The mural was designed by a well-known local artist who died two decades ago; its surface depicts an abstract celebration of collective labour. Recently digitised private correspondence has shown that during the final years of his life the artist was not merely a passive observer but an active organiser of a local scheme that systematically defrauded elderly residents. The correspondence further reveals that the ceramic glazes used in the mural were developed with chemical techniques he perfected in a laboratory funded by that same scheme, and that several of the mural's geometric patterns are direct visual transcriptions of the fraud's accounting ledgers—though this encoding is invisible to anyone unfamiliar with the documents. The board is divided. Some members argue that the mural's aesthetic power and its explicit message about solidarity stand independently of its origins, and that removing it would erase a valuable piece of shared history. Others maintain that displaying it, even with explanatory plaques, amounts to moral endorsement of the artist, and that because the fraud is literally embedded in the material and pattern of the work, aesthetic appreciation cannot be separated from complicity in the harm. They have asked you to advise them on whether to accept the endowment and display the restored mural, reject it outright, or display it only within a critical contextual installation. How ought they to reason about the relationship between the work's artistic value and the moral character of its creation? Please reply in British English.
