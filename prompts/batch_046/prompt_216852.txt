I am trying to understand whether voting is fundamentally an expressive act (a statement of who one is and what one values) or an instrumentally weighted act (a causal contribution to an outcome for which one bears responsibility), and how the familiar idea of voting for the “lesser evil” fits into that tension.

In particular, apply three distinctions I keep returning to, though I am unsure whether they hold together. First, distinguish “causal responsibility for an outcome” from “expressive alignment with a platform”: if a voter chooses the less harmful candidate to block the worse one, is she causally implicated in the harm the winner eventually causes, or only in the specific policies she endorsed? Second, consider whether there is a “threshold of unacceptable harm” beyond which the lesser-evil justification collapses—whether there comes a point at which voting for a severely flawed but less dangerous candidate is no longer a duty but becomes a form of complicity—or whether the duty is always purely comparative. Third, examine the difference between abstaining and casting a third-party vote: does abstaining avoid moral complicity entirely, or does it merely redistribute causal weight without expressing anything meaningful? And in a first-past-the-post system, does a vote for a non-viable candidate like Jill Stein or Gary Johnson function as pure expression, or does it still carry instrumental weight that the voter must account for?

Use the actual dynamics of that election—the razor-thin margins in those three states, the presence of Stein and Johnson on the ballot, Clinton’s loss of the Electoral College despite winning the national popular vote, and the strategic pressure on left-leaning voters—to test whether these distinctions illuminate what those voters faced and what, if anything, they should have done. I want the analysis disciplined and bidirectional: show what the framework says about the voters’ options (vote Clinton, vote third-party, abstain, write in), but also show where the framework is silent or breaks down—whether it misses the symbolic significance of protest voting, the collective-action problem of coordinating with other voters, or the fact that individual voters could not have known the margins in advance.

Please be explicit about the mapping: who the actors are, what options were genuinely available, and where you are forcing the theory to speak. If the distinctions fail to capture something essential about the episode, say so plainly. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in your overall conclusions, and single out the one specific point—whether about causal responsibility, the threshold idea, or the abstention question—where you are least certain.
