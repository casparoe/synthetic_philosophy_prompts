Analyze the Athenian assembly's reversal of its decision to annihilate Mytilene in 427 BC—first voting to execute all male citizens and enslave the women and children, then, after a second day of debate led by Diodotus, narrowly voting to spare the general population and execute only the ringleaders—through the lens of democratic self-correction and the commitment problem in collective punishment. The episode is well documented: Cleon had argued that destroying Mytilene was necessary to preserve imperial credibility and deter other allied cities from revolt, framing severity as a rational investment in future compliance, while Diodotus countered that excessive punishment would not prevent rebellion but would destroy Athens's strategic and moral advantages, urging moderation on instrumental rather than sentimental grounds. The reversal came only after intense emotional and rhetorical contest, and Thucydides emphasizes both the volatility of the crowd's passions and the practical urgency of sending a second trireme to intercept the first, which had already departed with orders for total destruction.

Map the analysis with discipline: identify the decision-makers and their available options (immediate annihilation, moderated punishment, or procedural reversal), specify what the payoff structure looked like for Athens's imperial control versus its self-image as a hegemon that governed through consent rather than terror, and articulate precisely where a theory of democratic self-correction predicts that an assembly should update rationally in light of new arguments without undermining its own authority. Then account for what the theory misses or misdescribes—whether the reversal demonstrates genuine epistemic humility or instead reveals a structural defect, namely that direct democracy lacks credible commitment mechanisms and therefore cannot stabilize punitive intentions long enough to be strategically coherent, rendering any "correction" operationally hollow. Finally, consider what the near-failure of the reversal (the first ship's head start) reveals about the theory itself: does the fact that the assembly eventually changed its mind confirm that democratic deliberation can overcome initial emotional consensus, or does the logistical race to undo the decision show that democratic correction is always too late to be meaningfully effective? Be explicit about where the framework is silent—particularly regarding the role of individual oratorical skill, the material logistics of imperial communication, or the psychological dynamics of collective guilt—rather than forcing the theory to speak on points it was never designed to address.
