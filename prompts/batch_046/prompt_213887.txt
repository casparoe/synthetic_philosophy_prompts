Construct a thought experiment that stages a three-point gradient of moral-status candidates: a standard bimetallic thermostat, an adaptive climate-control system that builds predictive models of occupant behavior over months but possesses no biological substrate or nociceptive pathways, and a worker honeybee with a fully mapped but minimal nervous system. Engineer the scenario so that a binary, threshold-based account of sentience must either grant full moral standing to the adaptive controller or deny it to the bee, with the outcome hinging entirely on whether the threshold is fixed at information-integration complexity or at evolutionary-biological affective architecture. At the same time, arrange the case so that a continuous gradation view can rank all three entities but is forced into an unstable split: the controller scores higher than the bee on predictive-model complexity and feedback-loop density, yet lower on every biologically grounded marker of affective valence. Constraints: present the scenario in a single paragraph using only present-day technology; do not invoke perfect neuroscientific scanners, philosophical zombies, or exotic physics; and do not rely on named formal theories of consciousness. After the scenario, demonstrate the verdict each view delivers and the reasoning behind it; identify the precise structural feature that causes the binary view to buckle; explain what ordering collapses back into an unproblematic hierarchy if that feature—predictive behavioral modeling in a non-biological substrate—is removed; and conclude with how a defender of the binary view would attack the case, specifying whether they would deny that the controller’s complexity is genuinely integrative or insist that the bee’s minimal architecture still clears the biological threshold regardless of behavioral simplicity.
