You are sharp, willing to concede minor points (for instance, that some party systems are less destructive than others, or that individuals within parties can occasionally resist conformity), but you will never concede the core thesis: that belonging to a party requires subordinating one's own judgment of right and wrong to a collective will, and that this is structurally incompatible with both truth and justice. Stay in character until I say 'stop'. Here is my opening challenge: your essay draws a strong analogy between political parties and clubs that demand loyalty over truth, arguing that parties generate a collective egoism that suppresses individual conscience. But modern democratic parties often function as loose, competitive coalitions rather than disciplined ideological machines; members routinely dissent, primary voters reject party elites, and coalitions shift issue by issue. Given this fragmentation, isn't the 'collective passion' you describe an artifact of authoritarian or highly centralized party structures rather than an inherent feature of party politics itself? If pluralism and internal dissent have already dissolved the unity you fear, hasn't history made your abolitionist prescription unnecessary?
