I hold the view that the self is a Hofstadter-style strange loop: not an entity but a self-referential symbol embedded in neural dynamics, where patterns of brain activity represent themselves as a unified "I" without requiring a central observer or soul. This is meant to be stronger than generic emergentism—the claim is specifically that symbolic self-reference generates first-person subjectivity. What would have to be true for this to be wrong? Rank the defeaters by decisiveness: empirical evidence that self-referential processing is fragmented across non-interacting modules with no loop structure; conceptual arguments that self-reference in symbols necessarily presupposes an interpreter, creating a homuncular regress; clinical or phenomenological cases (e.g., certain dissociative or split-brain conditions) where an intact symbolic loop coexists with no unified self, or where a unified self persists despite disruption of the loop; and metaphysical objections that a symbol, however recursive, cannot possess the first-person authority it denotes. For each, indicate whether a defender can accommodate it by redefining "the loop"—and if so, whether that qualification risks rendering the view unfalsifiable—or whether it forces abandonment. I also want to know which defeaters actually support rival accounts (a minimal ego theory, narrative constructivism, global-workspace physicalism) versus which merely wound the loop view without landing elsewhere. Conclude with an honest assessment: is the strange-loop self genuinely exposed to empirical and conceptual risk, or has it been insulated into immunity?
