Consider the following extension of origin essentialism, which I now find suspicious because it seems to prove that no one survives normal biological development. (1) If a person P is essentially the product of the particular sperm S and egg E that actually formed P, then P is essentially tied to the specific genetic and material constitution of the zygote produced by S and E. (2) During embryogenesis and throughout life, somatic mutations, epigenetic modifications, and cellular replacement alter the genetic profile of P's body; the genome of the adult is not identical to the genome of that original zygote. (3) Essential properties, if they are truly essential, must be preserved in every possible world in which P exists. (4) Therefore, in any world where the zygote's genome differs—even through ordinary mutation—the resulting individual is not P; by the same token, the adult whose cells now carry altered genomes is not identical to the zygote-originated P. (5) Hence origin essentialism entails that personal identity is destroyed by the very processes that constitute normal human growth, which is absurd. (6) So origin essentialism must be false, or so restricted that it applies only to the moment of conception and not to the persisting person. Where exactly does this reasoning go wrong? I would prefer to keep (1) and deny (5)—that is, I want to maintain that we have essential origins without accepting that mutations dissolve identity—but I need to know whether the error lies in (2)'s empirical claim about genetic change, in (3)'s modal principle, or in an illicit slide from the identity of the origin to the identity of the genome across time. If I reject one of these, what does that cost me?
