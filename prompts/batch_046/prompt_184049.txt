I'm preparing an independent research essay on the ethics of procreation, and I've constructed an argument that starts from the standard procreation asymmetry but ends somewhere I think is absurd. I need help diagnosing where the chain breaks.

(1) The procreation asymmetry holds: if a life would contain net suffering, we have a strong moral reason not to bring that person into existence; but if a life would contain net happiness, we do not have a corresponding reason to bring them into existence.

(2) If suffering is the only factor that generates a reason in creation decisions, then suffering-prevention must take lexical priority over happiness-creation in all contexts, not only in deciding whether to conceive.

(3) So, in allocating my resources—time, money, attention—preventing suffering in existing people always outweighs any project that merely creates or promotes happiness, including procreation, art, education, or friendship pursued for their own sake.

(4) By the same reasoning, I should devote every disposable resource to suffering-prevention and should never pursue non-instrumental goods; indeed, maintaining my own health or comfort is only justified if it directly serves the reduction of others' suffering.

(5) But this implies that morality demands we live as suffering-prevention machines, abandoning all personal projects and never reproducing unless reproduction itself reduces suffering.

(6) That conclusion is implausible: moral life clearly allows for non-instrumental goods, personal commitments, and limited obligations.

(7) Therefore, premise (1) must be false, and there is no genuine asymmetry—either we have symmetric reasons in both directions, or the asymmetry applies only in a restricted domain.

My instructor suggests the error is not in (1) but in the slide from (2) to (3), perhaps an equivocation between "reasons that apply to potential people" and "reasons that govern resource allocation among actual people." I want to know which step to reject. If I keep (1) and reject (3) by restricting the asymmetry to creation contexts only, does that save the view, or does it make the asymmetry arbitrary—just a special exception with no deeper justification? And if I reject (2) instead, what kind of pluralist framework about reasons do I need to adopt, and does it collapse the asymmetry altogether? Please identify the weakest link, explain why it seemed plausible, and price out what I have to pay for giving it up versus giving up (1).
