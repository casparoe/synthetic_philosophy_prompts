Please take on the role of an expert philosopher of science and mind, with particular familiarity in evolutionary epistemology and philosophy of artificial intelligence, and help me construct a conceptual taxonomy for my independent study project. I am a high school junior writing a paper on what I am calling the "competence-without-comprehension inversion": Darwin's observation that natural selection produces exquisitely adapted functional structures—eyes, immune defenses, navigational behaviors—without any representing, intending, or understanding agent directing the process; and Turing's parallel observation that a purely mechanical symbol-manipulating system can exhibit behavior that, in humans, would require understanding, without itself grasping the meaning of its operations. Before you construct anything, please spend a few sentences brainstorming the main logical axes along which people disagree about these inversions—for instance, whether the absence of comprehension is genuinely eliminated or only relocated, whether the biological and artificial cases should be treated symmetrically, and whether recognizing such competence undermines the normative value we assign to human understanding.

Now please build a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts philosophical positions on whether, and in what form, competence without comprehension is genuinely possible. The root should be a single concrete diagnostic question that a student could answer without knowing any philosophical labels—something like whether they would genuinely attribute navigational competence to a bacterium that solves chemical-gradient problems through purely mechanistic receptor dynamics, or whether they think such attribution always secretly borrows comprehension from the evolutionary environment. From there, branch through three specific distinctions I want included: first, whether the inversion applies symmetrically to Darwinian evolution and to Turing-style computation, or is restricted to one domain; second, what I am calling "the inheritance problem"—whether the comprehension apparently missing from the system is genuinely eliminated or only displaced onto its causal history (the programmer, the evolutionary niche, or the collective user community); and third, what I call "functional opacity"—whether a system's competence should be treated as autonomous and genuinely its own even when we cannot recover or reconstruct the missing comprehension from within the system's internal architecture. Each leaf should carry a name (or be marked "unnamed" if no standard view occupies it) and one sentence describing its most awkward or counterintuitive commitment. Where your tree generates combinations of answers that no actual philosopher holds, please note them and briefly diagnose whether they are incoherent, merely unexplored, or unstable for some other reason. Also please flag any pair of named positions that your tree cannot cleanly separate using only these branches. The taxonomy should contain between six and eight leaves, should be phrased for an audience of advanced high school students, and should avoid asking for citations, bibliographies, or references to highly technical literature that would require outside verification.
