Design a 75-minute classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy seminar of about 14 students that makes them feel the force of Moritz Schlick’s distinction between play (activities pursued as ends in themselves) and work (activities pursued only as means to external ends), and that lets them experience a further problem I will call residual instrumentalization: the tendency for an activity’s internal framing to stay metricized even after the metrics have been officially withdrawn. The students should not need prior reading on Schlick. The activity must unfold through rules, materials, and real small stakes rather than through facilitator lecture, so that participants first encounter pure play, then watch it become instrumentally rationalized, then try to return to pure play, and only afterward discover that the “free” round was being covertly evaluated.

Specify the setting precisely: 14 students, one seminar table, no digital devices needed. Give me the materials list, the exact sequence of phases with time allocations, the payoff and reward structures at each stage, and the interaction rules (who talks to whom, what is visible, what is hidden). In Phase 1 (about 15 minutes) students should engage in a simple physical task—folding paper figures, arranging colored tiles, or stacking cups—under conditions of explicit non-evaluation. In Phase 2 (about 15 minutes) the same task continues but a visible point system, leaderboard, and speed/accuracy metrics are introduced; the facilitator should reward efficiency openly. In Phase 3 (about 15 minutes) the facilitator announces that all points are abolished, the leaderboard is erased, and students may return to doing the task however they wish, with no evaluation whatsoever. Unbeknownst to them, Phase 3 is actually being observed for “authentic playfulness” against hidden criteria; in Phase 4 (about 10 minutes) the facilitator reveals this covert audit and announces that the student(s) rated highest in Phase 3 will receive a minor but real stake—specifically, the right to choose the seminar’s discussion text for the following week. The remaining time should be a structured debrief.

In the debrief, map each phase onto Schlick’s distinction: Phase 1 as play, Phase 2 as work, Phase 3 as the attempted recovery of play, and Phase 4 as the revelation that instrumental framing has persisted invisibly. Explain what residual instrumentalization adds to Schlick’s own account—namely, that removing external rewards does not reliably restore an end-in-itself orientation because participants may have already internalized an evaluative gaze. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run: for instance, some students will continue competing in Phase 3 despite the abolition of points, others will try to perform “playfulness” for the hidden observer in Phase 3 once they sense ambiguity, and the group may split between those who feel betrayed by the covert audit and those who deny they changed their behavior. Say how the facilitator should handle each failure without breaking the experiential point.

Also include an honest note on which feature of Schlick’s view the activity cannot reproduce: his claim that youth possesses a natural, pre-reflective capacity for play that aging and socialization gradually erode, and that this youthful orientation is the true locus of meaning. Explain why this feature must be supplied by the facilitator in discussion rather than enacted, given that the seminar participants are adults and the activity is deliberately artificial.
