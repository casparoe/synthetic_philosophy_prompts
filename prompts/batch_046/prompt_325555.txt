Ok so I've been trying to defend a kind of heretical middle ground in the memory/forgetting debate — not Margalit's usual ethics of remembering, not just Rieff's skepticism about theraputic culture, but something more specific: that after genuine collective repair there's a catagorical (not just pragmatic) duty to move toward institutionalized forgetting, what I'm calling "archival silence." The idea is that once a wrong is acknowledged and materially repaired, maintaining the trauma as a living civic identity actually freezes both sides in a static moral script. It traps descendents of victims in permanent victimhood and denies perpetrators the possibility of moral transformation. Remembering becomes not justice but a kind of moral vanity — you keep the wound open to maintain narrative purity.

What would count against this? I want more than the standard "if you forget you repeat history" line — that's predictable and I think usually overstated. I'm looking for deeper defeaters: maybe something about how collective identity actually requires continuity with past harm, or cases where formal forgetting was tried and backfired in ways that don't fit the repetition narrative. Is there a hidden tension between saying "repair is complete" and still needing to remember that repair happened? And does this view accidentally collapse into the very therapeutic individualism Rieff critiques, just scaled up to groups? Rank the objections from fatal to merely awkward. Also be honest — has this position been qualified into unfalsifiability? Like, if I can always say "real repair hasn't happened yet" whenever forgetting fails, is that a problem for a view of this kind? What's the carrying case that makes it plausible, and what's the case that breaks it?
