In rationalist and effective-altruism forums, I keep encountering a comparison between Arrow's impossibility result and Gödel's first incompleteness theorem. The parallel is usually drawn like this: just as Gödel showed that any formal system rich enough to contain arithmetic cannot be both consistent and complete, Arrow showed that any social welfare function mapping individual preference orderings to a collective ranking must violate at least one of four conditions—unrestricted domain, Pareto efficiency, independence of irrelevant alternatives, or non-dictatorship. From this, people license a broad epistemic humility about collective decision procedures: if mathematics itself cannot have all the properties we want, democratic aggregation certainly cannot either, and the search for a perfect voting mechanism is structurally doomed.

I want to audit how far this analogy actually carries, especially if we extend it to belief aggregation rather than preference aggregation—a framing I have seen floated informally but never worked out with care.

Please lay out an explicit correspondence table: what in Arrow's framework plays the role of Gödel's "sufficient arithmetic," "consistency," "completeness," and "self-reference"? Then identify the precise fractures in the mapping: the ordinal and interpersonally incomparable nature of preferences versus the syntactic structure of arithmetic; Arrow's proof proceeding via constructed preference cycles rather than via diagonalization; and the absence of anything like a Gödel sentence in the social-choice setting.

Next, examine whether importing the Gödel framing quietly smuggles in a false metaphysics—treating collective preferences as if they possessed the internal syntactic structure of a formal language—when Arrow's result is in fact about aggregation rules applied to externally given orderings. After that, tell me whether the "structural doom" conclusion about democratic design survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying most of the rhetorical load.

Finally, if the Gödel comparison collapses under pressure, suggest a cleaner analogy—perhaps to information-theoretic bottlenecks, or to the CAP theorem in distributed systems—and explain whether it fares better at capturing what Arrow's result actually forbids without importing misleading logical machinery.
