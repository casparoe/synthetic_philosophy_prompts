You serve on the Institutional Protocol Board of the Global AI Safety Consortium, a body tasked with certifying interaction frameworks for advisory systems that exceed expert human reasoning in well-defined domains. Before Thursday’s session is a proposal titled the “Reflective Persuasion Protocol” (RPP). The RPP applies to any superintelligent system that communicates recommendations to human decision-makers—whether in scientific review, policy analysis, or clinical diagnostics. Its core provisions are threefold: first, the system must prepend every recommendation with an explicit “credibility decomposition,” breaking its conclusion into sub-claims and assigning each a calibrated confidence interval; second, it must present the three strongest counter-arguments to its own conclusion in full before stating the recommendation, a structural requirement its architects call the “dialectical shell”; third, it must avoid rhetorical framing that exploits documented cognitive biases—such as anchoring on extreme values or invoking spurious social proof—unless it simultaneously discloses the framing mechanism and offers an unframed restatement.

Proponents argue that without such constraints, a system that is both correct and extraordinarily persuasive will effectively eliminate human epistemic autonomy: the interlocutor will assent not through evaluation but through cognitive capture, rendering the interaction indistinguishable from manipulation. Critics on your board—including several senior members—counter that the RPP undermines the very purpose of deploying these advisors. They contend that requiring a “dialectical shell” forces the system to perform artificial humility that misrepresents its actual epistemic standing, and that mandatory disclosure of framing mechanisms will overwhelm non-expert users with meta-cognitive noise, producing decision paralysis rather than autonomy. One member has further observed that no validated empirical study has tested the “dialectical shell” in high-stakes human–AI interaction, making the RPP a speculative imposition on a technology whose operational boundaries are still being defined.

Your board must issue a formal written opinion recommending approval, rejection, or approval with specific conditions. The memo should state the bottom-line recommendation clearly, provide the reasoning that supports it, and engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the opposing side—not by dismissing it, but by showing exactly what weight it carries and why it does not, on balance, determine the outcome. If you recommend approval with conditions, include a concrete scope limitation or implementation time frame. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the board needs reasoning it can evaluate on its merits alone.
