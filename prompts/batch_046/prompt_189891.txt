I have been working through the heterogeneity problem for hedonism—the familiar objection that the pleasures of tasting wine, grasping a mathematical proof, and feeling relief after danger share no discernible phenomenal quality, which makes it puzzling how they could all be tokens of one and the same intrinsic good. Lately I have been considering whether the problem can be dissolved by distinguishing phenomenological heterogeneity from what we might call normative fungibility: the capacity of any pleasure, regardless of its sensory profile, to be substituted for any other in rational deliberation about well-being. If normative fungibility holds, then the heterogeneity objection seems to rest on a category mistake, conflating how pleasure feels with its axiological function. So my question is this: if we grant that pleasures are normatively fungible in this way, why do philosophers continue to treat phenomenal heterogeneity as a substantive threat to hedonism rather than as a confusion between descriptive psychology and normative theory? Is there an implicit assumption that fungibility itself requires some hidden phenomenal uniformity, or is the persistence of the objection simply a failure to separate the metaphysics of pleasure from its role in practical reasoning?
