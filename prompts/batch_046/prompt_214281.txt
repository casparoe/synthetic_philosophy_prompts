Q: Epiphenomenalism holds that conscious mental states have no causal power over the physical world. Explain the paradox of phenominal judgement — specifically why, if my mental states don't cause physical behavior, it seems impossible for me to genuinely judge or verbally report that i'm experiencing them. Then apply it to this twist: imagine a neural interface that lets a subject say 'i see red' whenever a specific cortical region fires, but that firing is triggered by an external stimulator rather than by any actual red-experience. Under epiphenomenalism, is the subject's report about a phenomenal state or only about the physical trigger? Defend your answer.

student answer (20 pts):

Epiphenomenalism is the view that mental states are like smoke from a train — they come along for the ride but don't steer anything. The paradox of phenominal judgement, first raised in Chalmers' discussion of self-knowledge, is that if my saying 'i'm in pain' is caused entirely by brain processes and not by the pain itself, then my judgment isn't actually caused by the thing it's about. So i never really know my mental states; i just have physical reports that happen to line up with them. It's kind of like Mary's room but applied inward — Jackson showed that knowing all physical facts leaves out knowing what it's like, and here we see that even reporting what it's like is physically driven.

In the implant case, the subject says 'i see red' because the stimulator fired. Since under epiphenomenalism all reports are physically caused anyway, this isn't really different from normal life. The mental state of seeing red is still there in the background, correlated with the brain state, so the report is still about the phenomenal state, just indirectly. The paradox only bites if we think mental states have to directly push words out of our mouths, but correlation is enough for reference. So the report tracks the experience even though the experience doesn't cause the report, which shows the paradox isn't actually a problem.
