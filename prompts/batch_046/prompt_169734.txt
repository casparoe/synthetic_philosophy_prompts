I'm presenting next week at a cross-disciplinary philosophy seminar, defending a non-standard reconstruction of Gongsun Long's White Horse Dialogue and, by extension, the methodological aims of the School of Names (Mingjia). My thesis is that the argument should not be read—contrary to the standard analytic treatment—as an ontological paradox about universals versus particulars, nor dismissed as mere sophistry, but reconstructed as an early analysis of what I call "aspectual framing": the claim that predicating a perceptual qualifier like "white" upon a kind-term like "horse" does not simply describe the subject but shifts the conditions of categorical identity, so that the discourse operates with two distinct intentional objects rather than one object with two properties. From this I draw the broader conclusion that the School of Names was not primarily engaged in logic or ontology but in investigating how naming-acts construct discursive boundaries rather than tracking pre-given realities.

The audience is precisely divided and therefore dangerous in two directions. One side consists of analytic metaphysicians and philosophers of language who will regard any interpretive claim about Warring States texts with immediate suspicion of anachronistic projection; they care about whether I am smuggling in modern notions of intentionality, speech-act theory, or perceptual framing that have no textual warrant. The other side comprises two specialists in classical Chinese philosophy who read the dialogue in its received classical form and are fiercely protective of textual fidelity; they will attack whether my reading can survive comparison with the actual grammatical structure and terminological choices of the key lines without importing distinctions alien to the period. I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a polished defense: list the objections in roughly the order they will come, each phrased exactly as that camp would put it, ordered by how much damage they would do to the proposal. For each, indicate what I should concede up front, what I must hold, a reply calibrated for about two minutes, and an explicit note on where that reply is weakest. Crucially, include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—likely the one that forces me to defend whether "aspectual framing" can be articulated using only the conceptual resources available to the text's original context without collapsing back into the universal-particular reading I am trying to escape. The reply should be usable by someone speaking in real time to a skeptical room, not a written essay.
