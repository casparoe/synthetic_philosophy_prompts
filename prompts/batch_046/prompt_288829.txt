I am a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of mathematics, and I find myself returning to James Thomson's lamp and the general metaphysics of supertasks. For a discussion group I am leading, I wish to examine the film Groundhog Day through this lens. The protagonist, Phil Connors, appears to live through an indefinite—perhaps actually infinite—sequence of identical days, gradually refining his behavior until he is released. I ask you to treat this narrative structure as a candidate supertask: an apparently infinite process of moral self-correction that reaches a determinate terminus within a finite span of objective time. Where does the analogy to Thomson's lamp hold, and where does it break? Specifically, if we model Connors's character state at the point of release as analogous to the lamp's state after an infinite sequence of toggles, is there a fact of the matter about whether he is transformed (the lamp is on) or unchanged (the lamp is off), or does the film deliberately exploit the same indeterminacy? I want to push the reading further with a somewhat technical framing: consider the loops not as a simple potential infinity but as indexed by ordinals—there is loop 0, loop 1, and eventually an omega-th loop that completes the sequence, followed perhaps by loops beyond omega. Does the film's narrative logic allow for such a transfinite indexing, and would the existence of a limit-ordinal loop change our assessment of whether his final state is genuinely the result of a completed supertask rather than merely a very long finite iteration? Point to particular scenes that either support or resist this interpretation, and be honest about where the philosophical machinery distorts the film's humanistic concerns and where, conversely, the film offers an intuitive grasp of completion-at-infinity that abstract argument alone rarely conveys. I am teaching this to non-specialists, so I want to avoid misleading them about either the metaphysics or the film's actual themes.
