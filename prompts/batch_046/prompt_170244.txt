I am delivering the opening remarks this Friday for a one-day workshop I am convening at the university’s public-policy center—roughly twenty-five people, split between social-choice theorists and local civic organizers, with a few graduate students in between. The session is on how groups form collective judgments when institutional rules require them to justify decisions with reasons rather than simply casting a final vote. I need the remarks to run about six minutes, and they must feel welcoming rather than like a lecture, but the philosophical problem should shape the structure of what I can and cannot say.

What makes this personally urgent is that I just finished serving on a three-person neighborhood zoning committee that tried to operate exactly this way. We were required to evaluate a proposed development on three separate criteria—environmental impact, affordable-housing contribution, and historical preservation—and then issue an overall recommendation. Individually, each of us was fully consistent: two members found the environmental risk acceptable and the housing contribution sufficient, though we divided on preservation. When we aggregated our judgments premise by premise using majority rule, the premises pointed clearly toward approval. Yet when we took a direct vote on the recommendation itself, the same two members who had endorsed the premises opposed the project. The contradiction was not inside any of us; it was produced by the aggregation procedure itself. I want the remarks to use this experience as the narrative spine, not as decoration. That means I should not be able to claim that “the committee reached the wrong answer,” since there is no single committee mind independent of the rule we chose to apply. At the same time, I cannot treat the contradiction as a merely technical glitch, because for the organizers in the room it represents a real failure of democratic accountability: reasonable people, acting in good faith, generated a collective judgment that none of them individually endorsed.

Please write this speech in a reflective, conversational tone appropriate for a mixed audience. The experts should recognize the discursive dilemma and see its institutional implications—particularly the tension between premise-based aggregation, which feels more rational and transparent, and its vulnerability to strategic manipulation of the premises—while the non-experts should come away understanding why a group of consistent individuals can still produce an incoherent collective outcome. The idea should do real structural work: it should constrain what I can claim about collective wisdom, about the authority of our committee’s report, and about whether democracy requires more than majority rule. Do not include citations, bibliographies, named scholars, or reading lists. Do not quote anyone verbatim. End by framing the day’s central question—whether any aggregation procedure can respect both individual consistency and collective coherence—without answering it, and do so in a way that makes the activists feel the problem is theirs, not just an academic puzzle.
