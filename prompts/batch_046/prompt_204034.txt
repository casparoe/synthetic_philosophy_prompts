The concrete case: In 1922, an ethnographer acquired forty-seven wax-cylinder recordings of ritual songs from a community in what is now southeastern Nigeria (the Kwara recordings). The physical cylinders are chemically unstable; high-fidelity digital preservation was completed in 2018 with university funding. The Kwara Cultural Council — a recognized body of descendants and ritual practitioners — has filed a petition. They do not seek physical repatriation of the cylinders; they lack climate-controlled storage and prefer that the museum retain the originals for conservation. Instead, they request three things: (1) a binding institutional declaration that the museum holds the recordings in trusteeship rather than absolute proprietary ownership; (2) a community-controlled access protocol that prohibits public playback, exhibition, or secondary digitization without explicit council approval, with violations treated as breaches of institutional duty rather than mere policy infractions; (3) a funded, ten-year co-curatorship program in which council-nominated scholars share authority over cataloging, interpretation, and future preservation decisions.

The museum's legal counsel confirms that acquisition in 1922 was documented and that no current statute mandates return. The director argues that conceding veto power over access violates the museum's charter obligation to make collections available for research and education, and that adopting trusteeship language creates uninsurable legal liability and destabilizes donor agreements. Two committee members support full compliance with the petition; two others warn that conceding governance over access sets a precedent that could paralyze the museum's entire ethnographic holdings.

Please approach this as an expert in philosophical ethics and museum governance, reasoning step by step.
