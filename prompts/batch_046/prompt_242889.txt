Here is my opening challenge. I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been trying to map constitutional design onto system architecture. In doing so, I have drawn a distinction that I do not recall seeing in the literature: procedural precommitment versus ontological precommitment. Procedural precommitment fixes decision rules—like hard-coding access controls—while ontological precommitment fixes the very categories of entities entitled to consideration—like freezing the data schema that defines a "rights-holder." Constitutional texts do both, but my concern is that Ulysses-contract reasoning justifies only the first. A future generation might legitimately discover that the ontological schema is incomplete—perhaps synthetic agents, ecological systems, or future forms of consciousness should be included—yet the constitutional "API" pins an outdated ontology. This creates what in engineering we call catastrophic technical debt: the cost of refactoring the foundational schema is prohibitively high because every downstream law depends on it. So I ask: if your defense of precommitment applies to ontological as well as procedural binding, how do you avoid locking in moral error? And if it applies only to procedural rules, then isn't the scope of constitutional precommitment far narrower than defenders usually claim?
