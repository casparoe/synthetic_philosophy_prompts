In discussions of democratic reform—particularly those advocating lottocracy or legislative sortition—I repeatedly encounter the claim that election is an aristocratic mechanism whereas selection by lot is genuinely democratic, often glossed with the idea that randomly chosen citizens are literally the people rather than representatives acting on behalf of others. I want to understand exactly what thesis or theses this pairing expresses. Could you separate the distinct precise claims hiding in it: for example, a claim about descriptive representation (that a random sample statistically mirrors the demographics of the population); a claim about the elimination of campaign-driven selection biases and professional political classes; a claim about direct rule through rotation of office; and an epistemic claim about the quality of deliberation in mini-publics? For each, indicate whether it is a near-platitude, a defensible but substantive empirical thesis, or a conceptual mistake—especially with regard to whether being randomly selected actually dissolves the principal-agent problem or merely disguises it, and whether identifying a temporary sample with the people as a whole commits a fallacy of composition once binding decisions affect those who were never in the pool. Finally, indicate which of these readings is actually needed to support replacing elected legislatures with sortition bodies, and which ones do no work at all.
