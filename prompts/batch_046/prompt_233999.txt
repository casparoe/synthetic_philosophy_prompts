ok so I built this argument for a study session and now I think it proves too much, maybe that consequentialism is unusable if you take the Solomonoff prior seriously. Here it is step by step:

(1) The universal prior gives every computable hypothesis a weight that falls off exponentially with its Kolmogorov complexity.
(2) That includes hypotheses where some tiny action I take now -- like typing a specific digit -- determines whether trillions of agents exist in bliss or agony in distant computable worlds.
(3) Those hypotheses still get strictly positive weight, however small.
(4) Consequentialism says I should maximize expected utility, weighting outcomes by how likely they are.
(5) Because the utility gap in those distant worlds is effectively unbounded, the product of tiny weight times enormous utility dominates everything local.
(6) So for any choice I face, there's a computable model where picking option A versus B is the difference between cosmic salvation and cosmic ruin.
(7) Therefore rational choice should always be about finding which option correlates with the best far-future simulation-world, not about ordinary morality.
(8) But that's paralized -- I can't actually compute which digit-pattern saves the multiverse.
(9) So either the prior is malign and we should reject it, or consequentialism collapses into cosmic lottery reasoning.

Which step should I reject? I'd like to hang onto (1) and (4) if possible. Is the error in (5) -- maybe infinitesimal times infinite isn't well-defined? Or in (6) -- maybe causal decision theory blocks worlds I can't influence? Or is (3) misleading because the prior assigns weight but not credence we should act on? I want to know what giving up each one actually costs.
