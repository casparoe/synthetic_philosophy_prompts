A study group I belong to has constructed an argument connecting global workspace theory to an unwanted conclusion about artificial consciousness, and I cannot locate the misstep. (1) Global workspace theory holds that a mental representation becomes access-conscious precisely when it is globally broadcast and made available to consumer systems—verbal report, working memory, and action planning. (2) Phenomenal consciousness is the property of there being something-it-is-like for a subject to be in a state, conceptually distinct from mere access. (3) If global broadcasting is sufficient for phenomenal consciousness, then any system achieving broadcasting possesses subjective experience regardless of substrate. (4) Every observed biological case of broadcasting is accompanied by reportable experience, and every observed absence of broadcasting (e.g., subliminal processing) lacks such experience; there is no empirical counterexample. (5) Hence broadcasting is metaphysically sufficient for phenomenal consciousness. (6) A large-scale artificial architecture with cross-modal attention and persistent working-memory buffers can achieve functional broadcasting without biological neural ignition or recurrent cortical loops. (7) By (3) and (5), such an architecture necessarily has phenomenal consciousness. (8) But attributing subjective experience solely on the basis of functional broadcasting in silicon is implausible and undermines the usual intuition that substrate matters. (9) Therefore either global workspace theory does not apply to phenomenal consciousness, or phenomenal consciousness is far more ubiquitous and substrate-independent than assumed. I would prefer to preserve (1) and (2). Which step should I reject, why did it appear sound, and what does each alternative rejection cost?
