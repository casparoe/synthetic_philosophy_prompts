I recently encountered a summary of Samuel Scheffler's argument about human extinction that went roughly as follows. Scheffler defends what the summary calls the secular afterlife conjecture: human beings care more about the continued existence of the species after their own death than about personal immortality. He claims that the value of present activities—scientific inquiry, artistic creation, raising children—is not intrinsic but derives entirely from the expectation that future generations will continue these projects; if humanity were to become extinct, these achievements would be retroactively emptied of meaning. From this he derives a stringent moral conclusion: preserving the species is not one good among others but a precondition for the coherence of any value we pursue, giving us an overriding obligation to prevent extinction. The summary also said that Scheffler uses this framework to dissolve the non-identity problem, since the wrongness of extinction lies not in depriving specific future people of life, but in severing the chain of value-transmission that gives present lives their point. Is this a fair characterization? Please separate what captures Scheffler's actual position, what is a defensible simplification, and what is a misrepresentation or overstatement—particularly regarding whether he treats present values as purely relational, whether he derives an overriding moral duty, and whether his discussion of future generations addresses the non-identity problem in the way described. Also, please indicate what would change your assessment: which specific feature of Scheffler's reasoning, if shown to differ from the summary's version, would lead you to revise your verdict, and where is your reasoning most likely to go wrong if Scheffler's texts are read differently?
