I'm designing a taxonomy for a philosophy seminar on interpretations of Keynes's ethical vision of the good life, particularly as developed in his 1930 essay on long-run economic possibilities and in related reflections on work, leisure, and the drive for accumulation. I want the tree built from logical distinctions rather than named scholars, with concrete cases at each node so that someone can navigate it without already knowing the labels.

Start with the root commitment: once basic material needs are satisfied, is continued economic growth primarily harmful, neutral, or beneficial for human flourishing?

From there, branch on the following diagnostic questions, each paired with a concrete scenario:

1. Are "relative needs" (status, comparison, social rank) insatiable by human nature, or only by contingent social structures? Test case: a society achieves abundant leisure, yet every freed hour is spent on zero-sum status competition—fashion, luxury display, positional goods. Does this society approximate the good life?

2. Is the value of a shortened work week mainly negative (freedom from labor as disutility) or positive (freedom for specific non-instrumental activities—fellowship, art, science, local governance)? Test case: technology eliminates all necessary labor, but also dissolves the clubs, universities, and civic associations through which such activities were traditionally cultivated.

3. Can competitive consumption itself be part of the good life if pursued within limits, or does any competitive drive corrupt leisure? Test case: people work minimal hours but spend their income on ever-escalating but culturally meaningful rivalry—say, patronage of the arts driven by prestige rather than pure enjoyment.

The leaves should include: a standard post-work utopian reading; a pessimistic reading that relative needs make growth perpetual and the good life unreachable; a negative-leisure reading that values rest over cultivated activity; a positive-leisure reading that specifies the content of flourishing; a structuralist reading that holds relative needs are culturally malleable and capitalism must be replaced by a culture of sufficiency; and at least one unnamed combination—e.g., the view that insatiable relative needs are actually constitutive of excellence and discovery, hence compatible with the good life despite Keynes's suspicion of the love of money. For each unnamed leaf, note whether it seems incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored.

Please flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot cleanly separate (for instance, the utopian and positive-leisure views may overlap on many answers). The audience is graduate students in philosophy or political theory. Aim for six to eight leaves across four or five branching questions, with no bibliographies, citations, or lists of secondary literature.
