On a scale of 1 to 10, rate how defensible each of the following three formulations of American exceptionalism remains once the comparison class is expanded beyond Europe to include settler societies with comparable origins—specifically Canada, Australia, and Argentina—rather than treating the United States as sui generis: (1) the historical thesis that the absence of feudal legacies made democratic consolidation uniquely probable; (2) the normative claim that American constitutional liberalism represents a teleological endpoint for political development; and (3) the ideological thesis that exceptionalist rhetoric functions primarily to legitimate unilateral foreign intervention. For each score, provide a single sentence explaining whether the formulation can be reconstructed without suppressing the divergent institutional trajectories of those comparator cases. Then rank the three by their dependence on what might be called comparative silence—the strategic omission of similar-origin societies with different outcomes—to maintain internal coherence, and in two sentences explain why the highest-ranked formulation requires that silence most acutely.
