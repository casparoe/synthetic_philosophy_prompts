If Simone Weil—who in her well-known posthumous essay argued for the abolition of political parties on the grounds that they manufacture collective passions and substitute group loyalty for individual judgment—had lived to observe the 21st-century emergence of “platform parties” (political movements that maintain no formal membership, publish no unified program, and exist primarily as algorithmically distributed networks of parasocial leader-follower bonds and micro-targeted emotional triggers)—how would she likely have assessed them?

Address specifically whether she would treat these formations as an intensification of her critique, in which the suppression of individual judgment now operates without even the disciplinary structure of a party, or as a qualitative break that escapes her framework altogether because there is no collective doctrine to enforce conformity. In answering, draw on her broader conceptual vocabulary—the distinction between gravity and grace, the moral necessity of attention, her suspicion of collective entities that replace individual thought, and her analysis of déracinement—to evaluate the shift from programmatic party loyalty to modular, algorithmically curated “affective subscriptions” to discrete political postures. Would she regard the rootlessness of digital political identity as a new form of uprooting, or as a phenomenon that her philosophy of rootedness and attention does not adequately cover?
