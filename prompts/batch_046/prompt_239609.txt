I'm a junior serving on my school's student government ethics committee, and in my philosophy elective we've been working through Plato's Laws—specifically the Athenian Stranger's proposal that properly regulated symposia serve as a civic test, lowering inhibitions so that citizens reveal whether reason still governs spirit and appetite. Outside of class I was discussing with my friends whether this idea could be operationalized for modern peer leadership reviews, since we interview club officer candidates every other Tuesday in about fifteen-minute windows, with teachers only signing off afterward. We obviously cannot use wine, but the instructor mentioned that Plato's framework depends less on the substance than on the structure: a deliberately loosened social context in which habitual control is relaxed and the soul's ordering becomes visible.

I want a reusable decision procedure—an ordered checklist or flowchart in words—that applies this Platonic principle to evaluating candidates during unstructured group exercises, like ambiguous problem-solving tasks or informal retreats. The procedure should preserve the three-part soul distinction that runs through Plato's psychology—reason directing spirit and appetite—while translating it into observable behaviors a high school reviewer can record in real time. It needs to specify what counts as evidence of proper ordering versus disorder, and it must handle hard cases where the mapping is unclear: for example, distinguishing natural reticence from disciplined restraint, or separating provoked reactions from revealed character when the group dynamics themselves are chaotic. Mark explicitly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and state the constraints—fifteen minutes, no access to private records, need for teacher co-signature, and the risk of false positives.

Please apply the procedure to three hypothetical candidates: one who dominates discussion but offers a genuine apology afterward; one who remains largely silent yet reliably completes assigned tasks; and one who becomes excessively accommodating, abandoning previously stated positions to maintain harmony. The last case should yield a negative recommendation despite the candidate's apparent pleasantness, since that is the kind of result I want to check for consistency. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do—whether it can substitute for academic integrity assessments, whether it applies outside leadership selection, and whether it is vulnerable to reviewers who confuse emotional expressiveness with moral disorder.
