There's this weird asymmetry in how museums, auction houses, and courts treat originals, forgeries, and reproductions, and I can't figure out what philosophical view makes sense of all of it together.

If a painting is revealed to be a forgery — same materials, same technique, experts praised it for years — its market value collapses and institutions sometimes literally destroy it. But an authorized print pulled from the original plate, signed and numbered, can sell for thousands even though it's physically near-identical to other prints in the same edition. Meanwhile, in music we treat a recording or a score as fully the work, with no special aura around the composer's handwritten manuscript; but in painting, if the original canvas is destroyed, a perfect digital reconstruction is still just a substitute, and people talk about an irreplaceable loss.

So what theory of value or authenticty is actually embedded in these practices? Does it presuppose some indexical relation — causal contact with the artist's hand — that explains why the forgery is worthless and the original is sacred? Or is it really about social institutions of authentication and deception? There's also the legal side: forging a signature is a crime, but painting in the exact style of a dead master and selling it honestly is legal. That suggests the wrongness isn't about copying the aesthetic properties but about misleading people about causal history. Yet if the wrongness is just deception, why does the revealed forgery stay worthless even after everyone knows it's fake?

And then there's the cross-domain inconsistency: we treat painting as autographic — the physical object is the work — but music as allographic, where the score is just instructions. Is that distinction doing real philosophical work here, or is it a post-hoc rationalization for practices that come from different traditions?

Can you reconstruct the implicit commitments? Not just "people like originals," but what view of art, identity, or value has to be in place for these patterns to be coherent. Point out where they're inconsistient — like why substrate matters in one art form but not another — and say whether one theory can cover it all, or if we're running on conflicting intuitions that don't actually hang together.
