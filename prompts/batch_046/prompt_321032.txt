As someone who worked for decades in experimental physics before turning recently to philosophy of science, I have noticed an asymmetry in how research institutions evaluate empirical claims. In mature disciplines with dense theoretical structures—quantum optics, enzymology, or classical thermodynamics—reviewers and funding panels typically treat mechanistic explanation as a necessary condition for accepting novel results; a large, reproducible statistical effect is often dismissed as insufficient or even suspicious if it cannot be integrated into the existing causal framework. In contrast, within newer or interdisciplinary domains—complex adaptive systems, high-dimensional climate modeling, or large-scale epidemiological forecasting—the same institutions routinely accept robust predictive correlations in the absence of any comparable mechanistic narrative, regarding explanatory opacity as an acceptable cost of forecasting power.

What philosophical commitments about scientific knowledge and belief does this institutional practice presuppose? Does it commit us to the view that inside-view assessment—judgment rendered from within a well-established theoretical framework—carries an epistemic authority that outside-view statistical aggregation, however reliable, can never fully replicate? Or does the pattern instead presuppose a domain-relative doctrine of epistemic modesty: namely, that where theoretical understanding is deep, we ought to be more modest about accepting results that violate it, whereas where understanding is shallow, we ought to be modest about demanding explanations we do not yet possess?

Reconstruct the implicit theory of scientific belief that licenses this double standard. Determine whether the practice is philosophically consistent—whether it can be sustained by a single coherent view of explanation and prediction—or whether it quietly combines incompatible commitments, treating explanation as constitutive of knowledge in one domain and mere prediction as sufficient in another. Finally, specify what concrete changes would be required in peer review protocols, funding criteria, and the institutional demarcation between established and emerging fields if we replaced the implicit view with a uniform standard: either requiring mechanistic accounts across all domains, or accepting purely correlational evidence everywhere without theoretical integration.
