As a software engineer with no formal philosophical training, I have been trying to understand Machiavelli's distinction between fortune and virtù by formalising it in control-theoretic terms: fortune as exogenous stochastic perturbation, and virtù as the feedback mechanism that corrects political trajectory despite that noise. From this framing I have derived an argument that seems to prove too much, and I cannot identify the faulty step.

(1) Virtù is defined solely as the capacity to impose form upon fortune — that is, to reduce the variance that random events introduce into political outcomes.
(2) If a ruler succeeds in reducing variance, then the observed stability is fully explained by the feedback mechanism (virtù) operating upon the noise (fortune).
(3) But if fortune is genuinely stochastic, then any particular successful correction might have failed under a different realisation of the noise; hence the ruler's success is partly attributable to the favourable draw of noise rather than to the mechanism alone.
(4) Therefore, praising a ruler for virtù is indistinguishable from praising them for having encountered favourable noise.
(5) If praise requires that the praised feature be the sole or dominant cause of the outcome, then virtù is never genuinely praiseworthy.
(6) Machiavelli clearly intends virtù to be genuinely praiseworthy.
(7) Hence Machiavelli's framework is contradictory: either fortune is not stochastic (in which case there is nothing for virtù to correct) or virtù is not praiseworthy.

I should prefer to keep (1) and (6). Which premise or inference should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically, must I abandon the control-theory analogy, or can I preserve it by redefining virtù so that it remains compatible with partial causal contribution from fortune?
