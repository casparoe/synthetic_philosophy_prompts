I have recently been auditing an informal philosophy seminar with several fellow novelists, all of us working on first-person narratives in which the protagonists seem actively to engineer their own confinement rather than suffer it passively. In my own manuscript, a translator of autobiographical fiction keeps insisting that her voice is purely transparent, even as she meticulously selects which memories to render and which to suppress; I want to use Sartre’s account of radical freedom and the structure of bad faith—specifically the simultaneous denial of facticity and transcendence—to help my writing group see this not as hypocrisy but as an organized existential project. I am asking for a teaching plan rather than a lecture: the precise dependency order in which the components must be introduced so that radical freedom is not mistaken for simple voluntarism and bad faith does not collapse into dramatic irony; the single carrying example, drawn from narrative craft rather than the familiar service-industry scene, that must illustrate both poles of self-deception and what it should possess and omit; the misconceptions this audience of non-specialist writers will predictably generate—perhaps conflating freedom with an absence of external constraint, or treating self-deception as a failure of insight rather than an active stance—and the point in the sequence where each should be intercepted; a concrete check for understanding that demonstrates they have grasped the structural idea rather than merely adopted its vocabulary; and an explicit statement of what should be deliberately excluded at this level, such as the full ontological argument for the for-itself, together with an explanation of why its absence will not make the account feel truncated.

The audience is a small workshop of fiction writers with no background in phenomenology, meeting for a single ninety-minute session; they need to leave able to recognize in their own characters a self-imposed closure that operates as freedom rather than against it. Please design the explanatory arc accordingly, keeping the framing literary so that the philosophical machinery serves the craft problem rather than decorating it, and avoid recommending any texts, bibliographies, or further reading since none of us will have access to a library before we meet.
