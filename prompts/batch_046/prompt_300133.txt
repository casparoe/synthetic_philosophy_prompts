I've been grading papers on whether numbers should count in rescue cases—basically the Taurek problem, but my students keep bringing up weird edge cases and now I'm not sure what I actually think. Here are my verdicts, for better or worse. Case one: I can save either one stranger or five strangers from a burning building, same risk and effort on my part. I save the five; that feels definatly right. Case two: same choice, except the one is my sister and the five are strangers. I save my sister, and I don't feel like I'm making a moral mistake leaving the others. Case three: I have one dose of medicine that can cure either one fully conscious patient in terrible pain or ten patients in a permanent vegetative state with no chance of recovery. I give it to the one conscious patient; multiplying bodies that lack awareness doesn't seem to multiply moral weight. Case four: a malfunctioning teleporter has produced five physically distinct duplicates of a single person—same memories, same personality, but separate bodies. I can save all five duplicates or one unrelated stranger. I'm torn; part of me says five lives are still five lives, part says it's essentially one mind copied. Case five: I can save either one child now, knowing she'll survive and later rescue twenty others, or save those twenty now but the child dies. I save the child, which feels like indirect counting matters, but I also think if the choice were the child plus ten others versus just the child, I'd take the larger group, so numbers aren't totally dead.

Is there a single principle that captures all of this without just being ad hoc? I want a committed answer—pick a principle, defend it, and tell me which of my verdicts it has to throw out if any. Also, write it out as continuous prose, no headings or bullet points, just like an email. And tell me what your principle says about the case I keep going back and forth on: should I save one person in excruciating pain or five people in brief, mild discomfort?
