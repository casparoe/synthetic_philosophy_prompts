I am leading a single ninety-minute seminar for advanced philosophy undergraduates who have studied Cartesian skepticism in detail but have not yet encountered Wittgenstein's hinge epistemology as developed in On Certainty. I do not want a direct explanation of hinges for me; I want a pedagogical plan for how I should introduce the idea to them. First, lay out the precise order in which the conceptual components must be introduced — the failure of a particular skeptical strategy, the structural need for propositions that stand fast, the distinction between hinges and both empirical beliefs and logical truths, and the non-foundationalist role hinges play — and justify the dependency relations (what must be grasped before what can be introduced). Second, identify the single carrying example that should carry the session: evaluate whether the classic hinge that the earth has existed for more than five minutes is adequate here, or propose a different hinge that better avoids being mistaken for an empirical generalization or for a Cartesian foundation, and specify exactly what features the example must possess and what features it must lack. Third, map the misconceptions this audience will likely form — specifically the view that hinges are merely unexamined beliefs, that they serve as Cartesian foundations, and that they are in principle immune to revision — and indicate the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be preempted. Fourth, provide one check-for-understanding question that tests whether students have grasped the structural function of hinges rather than merely acquired the terminology. Finally, state explicitly what should be omitted at this level — for instance, specific textual exegesis from On Certainty, biographical details about Wittgenstein, or comparisons to contemporary naturalized epistemology — and justify each omission. The audience has no prior exposure to hinge epistemology, and the session must not rely on outside reading.
