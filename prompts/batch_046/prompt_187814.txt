I've been working through some arguments about socially constructed kinds—race, gender, and money in particular—and I keep running into a specific oscillation that I can't place within the standard taxonomy of moves. In one register, an interlocutor insists that because a category is socially constructed, its boundaries are arbitrary and therefore it lacks the kind of natural authority that would justify institutional enforcement: they might say that racial taxonomy is a historical fiction, that gender binaries are performative artifacts, or that fiat money has no intrinsic value and is therefore a kind of collective delusion. But then, in a different register—often within the same discussion—they defend the continued institutional use of that very category by appealing to the real, durable effects that its construction has produced: racial health disparities that require race-based clinical guidelines, gendered labor patterns that justify gender-targeted economic policy, or monetary networks that generate real obligations despite having no commodity backing. The shift is not a simple correction or a clarification about social construction not meaning unreal; rather, it treats the constructedness of the kind as a reason to dismiss its normative weight in one domain while treating the constructedness of its effects as a reason to preserve or even deepen its institutional role in another.

What makes the pattern distinctive is that the two registers are not separated by a principled boundary—say, between ontology and pragmatics—but are deployed opportunistically. The interlocutor does not argue that race is biologically unreal but socially indispensable in a consistent framework; instead, they invoke unreality to block one kind of institutional claim (e.g., that racial identity should determine legal status) and invoke constructed reality to support a different kind of institutional claim (e.g., that racial data should guide resource allocation), without accounting for why the same feature—being socially made—should dissolve authority in the first case and generate it in the second. I've considered whether this is just strategic essentialism in reverse, or a variant of reification, or perhaps an instance of the non-identity problem applied to kinds rather than persons, but none of those quite capture the double use of constructedness itself as both solvent and cement. I am also not asking for the standard construction-is-compatible-with-reality reply; I am asking whether there is an established name for this specific asymmetrical deployment, where the artifactual status of a kind is used to undermine its legitimacy in one institutional context and simultaneously to underwrite its legitimacy in another.

If there is an established concept in the literature on social kinds—perhaps drawn from philosophy of race, feminist metaphysics, or the social ontology of money—that names exactly this oscillation, I would like to know it, along with how its proponents distinguish it from plain contradiction, from context-sensitive pluralism about kinds, and from the more familiar move of treating constructed categories as real enough for practical purposes. If there is no established term, I would appreciate a proposed coinage with a precise definition, clear boundary conditions that separate it from strategic essentialism and from ordinary institutional pragmatism, and perhaps an indication of whether the pattern is more prevalent with some kinds (race, gender) than with others (money, legal status). I am not looking for a bibliography or a survey of the field, but for a conceptual carving that captures the edges of the phenomenon.
