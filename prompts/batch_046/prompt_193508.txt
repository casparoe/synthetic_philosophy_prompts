Exam Question: Philosophers traditionally distinguish perfect duties (strict, with determinate content), imperfect duties (broad, leaving latitude in fulfillment), and supererogatory acts—those that are good but not required. Consider Dr. Chen, a physician with no special obligation to a particular patient, who spends her vacation performing emergency surgery in an active crisis zone, enduring substantial personal risk and financial loss. Some theorists propose that certain acts are "structurally supererogatory": they do not merely sit above a threshold of duty, but they alter the moral landscape such that subsequent, similar acts by the same agent become less praiseworthy—a phenomenon of "moral saturation." Others defend "ledger-neutral" supererogation, holding that each act is evaluated independently and repetition never diminishes supererogatory status.

First, explain the standard three-part distinction, using Dr. Chen's case to illustrate each category's boundaries. Second, evaluate whether structural supererogation is a coherent addition to moral taxonomy, or whether it illegitimately imports considerations of moral accounting into an act-focused framework. Third, present and respond to one significant objection that a Kantian might raise against the very possibility of supererogation, being careful to distinguish the Kantian treatment of imperfect duty from a flat denial of all action beyond strict obligation.

Student Answer:

Supererogation designates acts that are morally valuable yet not morally obligatory, occupying a space beyond both perfect duties—such as the prohibition on deception, which admits no latitude—and imperfect duties—such as beneficence, which permits discretion in when and how one aids others. Dr. Chen's decision to operate in a crisis zone is paradigmatically supererogatory: she is not specifically required to assist these patients, and the personal costs she bears confirm that her act exceeds what duty demands. Following J. O. Urmson's classic distinction, this makes her a "hero" rather than a "saint," since heroes undertake actions that involve notable risk and difficulty, whereas saints fulfill moral demands with ease.

With respect to structural supererogation, the view maintains that once an agent performs one supererogatory act of a given kind, further identical acts lose their supererogatory status and become imperfect duties—a kind of moral saturation. I reject this as incoherent. If an act is genuinely beyond duty, its repetition cannot generate a new obligation; otherwise, the initial performance was merely the first discharge of an imperfect duty rather than a true exceeding of duty. The ledger-neutral alternative, which evaluates each act in isolation, avoids this problem. A Kantian objection, articulated in Kant's Groundwork, holds that the moral law allows no actions that transcend its demands: all beneficent conduct falls under the imperfect duty to promote the happiness of others, leaving no conceptual room for supererogation. Structural supererogation attempts to accommodate this concern by allowing repetition to convert supererogation into duty, but this concedes too much: it treats moral praise as a depletable resource, which is essentially a consequentialist framework of aggregate moral accounting rather than a deontological one. Consequently, we should retain the standard taxonomy with ledger-neutral evaluation and reject structural supererogation.
