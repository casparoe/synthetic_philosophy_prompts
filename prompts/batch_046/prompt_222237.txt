I'm currently enrolled in an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness—the view that phenomenal properties do not exist as they appear in introspection, and that conscious experience systematically misrepresents its own nature. While studying for a unit on introspective authority, I noticed a specific evaluation protocol used by my university's disability and mental-health services for students requesting accommodations for conditions such as chronic migraine, dissociative episodes, or severe anxiety disorders. The protocol requires applicants to submit both first-person phenomenological reports and third-party behavioral assessments, but the rubric explicitly weights observable functional impairment above subjective severity ratings. More specifically, it contains a clause permitting clinicians to discount or override a student's self-report when they identify what the form calls phenomenal misdescription—cases in which the applicant describes their experience using vocabulary that conflicts with the clinician's theoretical model of the condition (for instance, describing a migraine aura in terms of visible spatial distortions rather than in terms of cortical spreading depression). The practice therefore treats subjective phenomenology as epistemically relevant yet corrigible, and it appears to license external theoretical frameworks to correct first-person reports rather than merely supplement them.

What view of phenomenal consciousness and of introspective knowledge does this institutional practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology embedded in the evaluation rubric: does it treat phenomenal reports as fallible descriptions of real properties, as constructed narratives with no direct ontological weight, or as something else? Assess whether the practice is philosophically coherent—particularly whether it can consistently maintain both that phenomenal consciousness is authoritative enough to require documentation and that it is unreliable enough to be overridden by clinical theory. Say whether this presupposition aligns with illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, or whether it instead commits the institution to a direct realist or transparency thesis that illusionism rejects. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates well, and identify where the protocol is philosophically underdetermined, such that rival theories of consciousness could license the same policy. Finally, describe what would have to change in disability-evaluation practice if we replaced its implicit view with a rigorous illusionist framework—especially regarding the status of phenomenal misdescription, the relative weight assigned to subjective reports versus behavioral markers, and the legitimacy of clinician correction.
