The debate over what Descartes's cogito actually establishes has been stalled for decades between a minimal interpretation—that it proves only the occurrence of a thinking event, with "I" functioning as a logical placeholder—and a maximal interpretation—that it establishes the existence of a substantial, enduring thinking self. Both sides work with the same passage in the Meditations and accept the same rules of inference, yet neither has conceded ground. I want a diagnosis rather than an adjudication: is the disagreement verbal (about what "substance" means), substantive (about the conditions for self-reference), or are the sides answering different questions—one epistemic, about indubitability, and one metaphysical, about constitution—under a single heading? My working hypothesis is that a hidden divergence is doing the work, involving what I will call indexical anchoring: the minimalists treat self-reference as achievable without any pre-conceptual point of view, while the maximalists insist that successful use of "I" requires a non-derivative, first-personal fixation of reference that only a substantial self can provide. Given this, are they really disputing Descartes's text, or disputing whether consciousness is fundamentally object-directed? Support your diagnosis with what each camp actually says, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress—a formal result about self-reference, a phenomenological distinction, a change of question—or whether the stalemate shows the original formulation is ill-posed, and if so what should replace it.
