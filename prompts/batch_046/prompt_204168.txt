Since a recent diagnosis of progressive cognitive fatigue has forced me to integrate my episodic memory, scheduling, and reasoning almost entirely with an external digital ecosystem—voice memos, searchable notes, and algorithmic reminders—I have found myself in debates about the slogan I keep hearing in philosophy of mind discussions: the mind extends beyond the skull. In one context it was used to claim that deleting my primary archive would be ethically comparable to damaging brain tissue; in another it seemed to mean only that cognition causally depends on tools; in a third it was treated as a thesis about where knowledge is literally stored. Could you disentangle the distinct precise claims this slogan might be making—separating metaphysical claims about the constitution of mental processes from causal or functional claims about cognitive dependence, and from epistemic claims about the location of knowledge—indicating for each whether it is a trivial platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or false? In particular, identify which reading is actually required to support the argument that erasing my integrated digital system is closer to harming my mind than to discarding a conventional diary, and say whether that reading survives standard objections about the mark of the mental or about the asymmetry between internal neural processes and external artifacts. Finally, please state what specific argument or empirical result would change your assessment—whether, for example, a convincing intrinsic-boundary criterion for mentality that excludes all external structures, or evidence that my integration with these tools remains merely causal rather than constitutive, would compel you to abandon the stronger constitutional interpretation in favor of a weaker one.
