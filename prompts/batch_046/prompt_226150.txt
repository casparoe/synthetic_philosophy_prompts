I'm drafting a novel where the protagonist lives by this philosphy she calls tranquilism—she believes the only real good is the absence of craving and suffering, and that pleasure is basically just a loan you have to pay back with more want later. I was arguing with my writing partner about whether this makes her a negative utilitarian in disguise, since both seem obsessed with cutting down the bad stuff, but she insisted she isn't trying to save the world, she's just trying to empty herself. Then my friend asked the obvious thing: if absence is literally the only value, why wouldn't she be glad when someone dies peacefully? Dead people have zero cravings, zero pain—pure absence achieved. I told him she wouldn't, because she thinks non-existence isn't the same as being peacefully empty; it's more like a blank page that doesn't count as 'absence of suffering' because there's no one there to be free of it. But now I'm second-guessing whether that distinction is actually consistent with her own rules. If she admits that being alive with no wants is somehow better than not being alive at all, hasn't she smuggled in a hidden positive value for existence that tranquilism was supposed to reject? Or is there a real philosophical difference between 'peaceful non-existence as a state' and 'just nothing' that saves her from contradiction? I need this for a dialogue in chapter three where she wrestels with it, and I want her to sound sharp but not like she's just reciting a wikipiedia entry. Where exactly does my reasoning go off the rails—am I confusing a personal practice with a moral theory about the world, or is there a deeper glitch in how I'm applying the idea?
