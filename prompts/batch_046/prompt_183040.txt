I've been going in circles with my study group about workfare, benefit fraud, and the whole "undeserving poor" framing -- my friend keeps saying conditionality is just common sense, I keep saying its mostly a stigma machine that hides labor-market failures. We want to write up an actual adversarial collaboration doc instead of yelling past each other.

Treat side A as the Conditionality/Deservingness camp: they think strict work requirements, fraud checks, and sanctions preserve program legitimacy and prevent moral hazard, and that "deservingness" tracks a real moral distinction between people facing structural barriers and thier free-riding. Side B is the Unconditional/Structural camp: they think fraud is massively overstated relative to actual rates, that administrative policing costs more than it saves, that workfare conflates unemployment with moral failure, and that "deservingness" is mostly rhetorical cover for class bias and budget cuts.

We want three parts. First, a numbered list of claims both can sign -- precise, not vague. Include that fraud rates are usually low compared to public perception, that work requirements do boost short-term employment in some contexts, that sanctions and eligibility errors cause real hardship, and that program legitimacy partly depends on public perceptions regardless of accuracy. Second, a disagreements table phrased so each side accepts the wording of its own view and the other accepts it as a fair description. The cruxes should cover: (a) whether deservingness is a deontic moral category with independent weight, or just a predictive heuristic about behavior change; (b) whether post-reform employment gains in big welfare-to-work programs are causally due to conditionality or to macroeconomic trends and selection effects; and (c) whether the harm from sanctions and admin burden is acceptable collateral damage for fiscal sustainability, or whether that harm alone defeats conditionality regardless of efficiency. Third, for each crux name one concrete case both sides agree is relevant -- like comparing UK Universal Credit sanction regimes to earlier JSA rules during a recession, or looking at what happened when a specific state suspended work requirements during an economic shock. For each case, write Side A's prediction (e.g., suspension reduces labor-force attachment without improving welfare) and Side B's prediction (e.g., suspension cuts hardship with minimal long-term employment loss) in advance. Make the disagreements sharp enough that settling the empirical crux about recessions would actually force one side to update. Don't ask for bibliographies or citation lists -- just reason from what's known, and keep it in the weeds rather than generic "values" talk.
