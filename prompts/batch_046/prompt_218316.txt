After a long and rather heated argument with a friend about whether rational agents can consistently trust their own reasoning, he offered me the following summary of what philosophers call the Löbian obstacle. He said that an agent who updates its beliefs in a standard Bayesian fashion—treating its own acceptance of a proposition as direct evidence for that proposition—will, if it also endorses the soundness of that very update rule, be forced by Löb's theorem to assign full credence to every proposition, contradictions included. He claimed this demonstrates that any diachronically rational agent must either abandon reflective self-trust entirely or retreat to a non-normal modal logic in which the standard derivability conditions fail. He further insisted that this obstacle is essentially the preface paradox transposed into modal logic, and that its neglect in epistemic utility theory reflects an unjustified optimism about reflective coherence. I am unsure whether he has captured the result fairly or whether he has smuggled in assumptions about belief revision that defenders of self-trust would reject. Could you please audit his account: distinguish what is accurate regarding the formal result, what is a defensible but disputable interpretation, and what amounts to mischaracterisation or overstatement? In particular, does the obstacle genuinely depend on Bayesian updating in the manner he suggests, and is the parallel with the preface paradox sound? Please reply in British English.
