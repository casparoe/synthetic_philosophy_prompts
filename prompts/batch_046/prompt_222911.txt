I've been working through my own judgments about punishment and moral desert, particularly in the spirit of the exchange between compatibilists who defend retributive justice and skeptics who think determinism undermines it. I want to know whether there's a single principle that organizes the following five verdicts, or whether I have to give one up.

Case 1: Marcus is a psychologically typical, affluent adult who spends six months planning an embezzlement scheme purely for luxury consumption. He understands morality and the law, weighs risks calmly, feels no external pressure, and later says he would do it again. Verdict: Marcus is fully deserving of punishment proportionate to the wrong; the fact that his choice was deterministic in principle does not diminish his desert.

Case 2: Elena is psychologically identical to Marcus—fully rational, reasons-responsive, and uncoerced—but every element of her character and value system was deterministically produced by a comprehensive state conditioning program begun in infancy. She reflects on her fraud, endorses it, and understands that it is wrong. Verdict: Elena is not deserving of retributive punishment; her will was engineered, and that origin undermines basic desert even though she is competent.

Case 3: David develops a brain tumor that destroys his empathy circuits. He assaults a stranger, immediately expresses horror, and asks for help to prevent further harm. Verdict: David is not blameworthy; only medical treatment and protective containment are appropriate.

Case 4: A jurisdiction sentences offenders using pure actuarial risk assessment to maximize public safety, but instructs judges and juries to describe outcomes in desert-language—"he received exactly what he deserved"—to preserve institutional legitimacy. Verdict: The deception is corrupting and should be eliminated; however, risk-based sentencing itself is permissible, provided it is defended openly on preventive grounds.

Case 5: Sarah, an otherwise ordinary adult with no conditioning history and no pathology, steals food impulsively immediately after learning her child has a terminal diagnosis. She confesses within minutes and makes full restitution. Verdict: Sarah deserves substantially less punishment than Marcus, though not zero; extreme situational pressure diminishes but does not eliminate desert.

My question: Is there one precise account of just desert and appropriate institutional response that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should be overturned, and why? Please state the best available principle exactly, identify which case motivates each clause, say what it predicts for an unlisted case—specifically, someone whose values were shaped not by state engineering but by an equally deterministic yet utterly ordinary childhood of neglect and reinforcement—and be honest about which of my judgments the principle cannot accommodate.

One further request: if my framing depends on a confused distinction—for example, if the difference between engineered and ordinary determinism collapses under scrutiny, or if asking for a principle that saves all five verdicts is asking for a contradiction—say so plainly. I want pushback rather than a contrived reconciliation.
