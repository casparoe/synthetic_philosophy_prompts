As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I have been trying to model weakness of will using hyperbolic discounting and have arrived at an argument that seems to prove too much. (1) Genuine akrasia requires that one and the same agent judges action X best all-things-considered and yet intentionally performs Y instead. (2) Hyperbolic discounting makes an agent's preferences time-indexed: the present-self weights near rewards far more heavily than the future-self weights distant ones, so at t1 the ordering ranks X above Y while at t2 it reverses. (3) On this model there is never a single agent with contradictory preferences—only a sequence of internally consistent time-slice agents. (4) If no enduring subject spans the sequence, then blaming someone for yielding is conceptually incoherent, like blaming Tuesday for abandoning Monday's schedule. (5) Yet we routinely and coherently blame people for weakness of will, and hyperbolic discounting is empirically robust, so the reasoning forces us either to reject the model or to declare blame irrational—both of which seem unacceptable. Which step hides the equivocation or scope shift, what is the precise error, and what does repairing it cost? Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
