You are grading a final-year examination in philosophy of decision theory. The question set was as follows:

"Set out the coherence argument for expected utility maximisation, explaining both its probabilistic strand and its preferential strand. Then consider an agent who is not fully certain of her own utility function: she assigns non-trivial credence to the hypothesis that she values outcome A above B, and also non-trivial credence to the converse ordering. Does the standard coherence argument still apply to her? If so, explain precisely how the argument is reconstructed at the second order. If not, explain what this reveals about the limits of the coherence justification."

A student submitted the answer below. Read it carefully.

"The coherence argument has two parts. First, the Dutch-book theorems show that if an agent's degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, a bookmaker can construct a set of bets that guarantee her a loss, whatever the world turns out to be like. Rationality therefore requires probabilistic coherence. Second, there is a preferential analogue: if an agent's choices violate the axioms of expected utility theory—transitivity, completeness, or the independence axiom—she can be turned into a money-pump. A sequence of trades can be arranged that leaves her with strictly less of everything she values. Hence rationality requires that she maximise expected utility relative to her credences and her utility assignments.

Now suppose the agent has second-order uncertainty about her preferences. She does not possess a single utility function but rather a probability distribution over possible utility functions. In this situation the coherence argument applies unchanged. The agent should compute the expected utility of each act by taking a weighted average over the candidate utility functions, using her second-order credences as weights. This produces a single, well-defined ordering over acts. Should she deviate—for instance, by preferring act X to act Y despite the weighted average favouring Y—she is vulnerable to a second-order money-pump. A bookmaker who knows her distribution over utility functions can offer her a sequence of preference-revelation bets that extract value, precisely mirroring the first-order case.

Consequently, second-order preference uncertainty does not weaken the argument; it merely shifts its application upward. The agent is still required to maximise expected utility, except that the expectation is now taken jointly over states of the world and over her possible preference orderings. The coherence justification therefore survives intact, with only a minor increase in computational complexity."
