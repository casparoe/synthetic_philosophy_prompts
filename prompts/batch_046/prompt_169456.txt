In a debate about whether the United States ought to accept the jurisdiction of an international criminal tribunal, an interlocutor ended the exchange by saying, “We are the city upon a hill—our constitutional order is already the highest moral standard, so submitting to foreign judges is both redundant and degrading.” Unpack that slogan. Separate the precise claims it could be expressing: a descriptive thesis that American institutions are uniquely successful; a normative exemplar thesis that the U.S. should serve as a visible model for other nations; a thesis of moral exemption, holding that exceptional status releases the U.S. from obligations binding ordinary states; a teleological thesis that American history is directed toward an exemplary endpoint; and a stronger philosophical thesis that American political goods are incommensurable with international legal norms, so that comparison, ranking, or external judgment is conceptually inappropriate rather than merely unwelcome. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false; identify which readings are being conflated to produce the conclusion that international jurisdiction is insulting; specify which single reading actually supports that conclusion, which ones leave it entirely open, and which ones commit the speaker to implausible further positions—such as that no treaty cooperation is ever permissible, or that ratified obligations are void whenever they conflict with domestic constitutional interpretation. Finally, say what work the phrase “city upon a hill” is actually doing in this specific argumentative context: is it functioning as evidence, as a rhetorical closure device, or as a hidden premise about national sovereignty?
