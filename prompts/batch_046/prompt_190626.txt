I am a philosopher serving on the allocation board of a municipal endowment distributing a fixed annual budget between wellness and arts-access initiatives. We are weighing three options. First, a specialized rehabilitation program that would yield large, measurable quality-of-life gains for a small cohort of beneficiaries who are already relatively advantaged. Second, a broad mental-health outreach initiative that would deliver smaller but meaningful benefits to a large population facing severe deprivation. Third—advanced by a colleague who rejects comparing outcomes across different kinds of goods—an equal per-capita funding model that splits resources identically. Because the rehabilitation program depends on economies of scale, this restructuring eliminates its efficacy for the advantaged cohort without freeing additional resources for the disadvantaged cohort; the latter receives only what the broad outreach program already provides. The result is a pure reduction in the well-being of the better-off with no corresponding improvement for the worse-off.

My colleague defends the equal-split model not by asserting that equality is intrinsically valuable, but by claiming that because rehabilitation gains and mental-health gains are fundamentally different in kind, any attempt to weigh them against one another lacks epistemic justification; procedural equality—equal funding per recipient—is therefore the only defensible standard. Several board members find this persuasive; others insist that justice requires directing resources where need is greatest, even if that produces pronounced disparities in outcomes. I am struck by the intuition that the equal-split proposal is not merely inefficient but unjust in a specific way—yet I struggle to articulate whether my intuition tracks a genuine structural defect in the view that favors the disadvantaged, or whether it reveals an independent commitment to equality that such a view correctly rejects.

Could you help me map the philosophical architecture of this dilemma precisely? In particular, is there a principled account of distributive justice that assigns greater moral importance to benefits for those who are worst off, without that account collapsing into a pure demand for equal treatment? And does my colleague's argument from the incommensurability of goods expose a real vulnerability in that account—perhaps by undermining the very comparisons on which it depends—or does it merely restate a well-known objection about harming some without helping others? Please distinguish between considerations that would be decisive against the view that favors the worst off—such as a demonstration that it cannot respect the distinctness of individual lives, or that it becomes immune to counterexample by restricting its domain—and those that are merely awkward adjustments. Also indicate which criticisms defenders of that view are prepared to concede and which they reject, and whether shielding the view from this scenario by limiting it to cases where goods are comparable renders it unfalsifiable in a damaging way.
