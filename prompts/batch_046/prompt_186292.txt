I just spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether Gödel's first incompleteness theorem actually undermines computational theories of mind, and I realized that neither of us could say anything precise about what the theorem requires beyond "a sufficiently strong formal system." She kept insisting that every philosophical invocation of Gödel outside mathematics commits a category error, while I kept feeling there must be some structural analog—not in brains, but in institutional or normative systems—where the conditions for Gödelian limitation are actually met rather than merely metaphorical. I want a short research proposal, suitable for a six-month fellowship or a term-paper prospectus, that takes this seriously without falling into the usual hand-waving about "minds transcending algorithms."

The proposal should specify exactly one tractable question: not whether Gödel applies to ethics or physics in general, but whether there are normative frameworks—say, constitutional interpretation regimes or formal clinical-ethics decision protocols—that jointly satisfy consistency, recursive axiomatizability, and sufficient expressive power, such that Gödelian incompleteness becomes more than an analogy. It needs to explain why existing answers leave this open: philosophers of mind treat the theorem as irrelevant to cognition because minds are not formal systems; mathematicians treat non-mathematical applications as category mistakes; and neither camp has done the conceptual work of specifying which institutional or linguistic practices actually instantiate the premises of the theorem. The proposed approach should be philosophical rather than empirical: map the formal conditions onto a single, well-defined non-mathematical domain and identify precisely where the analogy holds and where it collapses—whether at expressiveness, consistency, or recursive enumerability. A useful result would be a clear taxonomy of the breakdown points and an assessment of what that reveals about the limits of formalization in that domain; failure would be demonstrating that the conditions are never jointly satisfiable outside mathematics, but even that negative result should clarify whether the Lucas-Penrose-style arguments depend on a false formal parallel. The scope must be realistic for a semester or half-year project: one domain, one formal framework, no attempt to resolve the philosophy of mind or produce empirical data.
