I'm working through a variant of doomsday reasoning with a study partner in our philosophy of science seminar, and we've constructed what looks like a reductio of the Self-Sampling Assumption that applies far beyond human extinction. Here is the chain we've laid out, with each step seeming individually defensible:

(1) By the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA), an observer should reason as though randomly sampled from all observers in their appropriate reference class.
(2) Define a reference class R not biologically but conceptually: all minds, across time and substrate, that genuinely understand and deploy a specific theoretical framework — call it Framework M (the Madhyamaka analysis of emptiness as absence of intrinsic existence).
(3) If Framework M survives in long-lived civilizations, post-biological minds, or widely distributed artificial reasoning systems, then the cardinality of R is astronomically large, with the overwhelming majority lying in the distant future.
(4) I am presently an R-member: I understand Framework M well enough to reason with it. By (1), I should therefore assign near-zero credence to being among the first fraction of a percent of R.
(5) Yet empirically I am clearly among the earliest R-members — the framework is roughly two millennia old but currently inhabited by only a tiny population of specialists. This is a severe Bayesian surprise unless |R| is in fact small, which requires that Framework M dies out, remains permanently marginal, or is never adopted by non-human reasoners.
(6) Generalize: for any conceptual framework F that I currently inhabit (Bayesian epistemology, modal logic, quantum field theory), the same reasoning applies. SSA predicts that each is near extinction or permanent obscurity, which is manifestly incompatible with their present vitality.
(7) The only escape is to restrict SSA to biological humanity, denying that conceptual lineages can serve as reference classes.
(8) But the original doomsday argument selects the biological class precisely because we lack epistemic access to non-biological observers, not because biology enjoys any metaphysically privileged status. There is no non-arbitrary principle that permits biological reference classes while forbidding conceptual ones.
(9) Therefore either SSA proves that nearly every framework I use is about to vanish (absurd), or the restriction in (7) undermines the original doomsday argument by revealing that its reference class was arbitrarily chosen.
(10) Hence the argument proves far too much.

We are stuck on where to break the chain. I want to keep (1) intact if possible, and I also want to preserve the idea that reference-class selection is genuinely problematic rather than trivial. Should I reject (2) and deny that conceptual frameworks can constitute legitimate reference classes? If so, why did that look like a natural extension of SSA, and what price does that pay for defending the biological class without special pleading? Or should I reject (6) and block the generalization — and if so, by what principled distinction? Or is the flaw in (8), requiring me to defend a metaphysically loaded boundary between biological and conceptual lineages? Take a definite position: name the specific step you think fails, explain precisely why it seemed plausible, and price the alternatives — what each rejection costs for someone who wants to retain both SSA and a non-arbitrary doomsday argument. I want a committed answer, not a survey of options; defend your diagnosis with reasons, and note where your own reply is weakest.
