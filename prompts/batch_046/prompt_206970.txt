A friend who recently took an advanced course on collective action summarized the philosophical literature on climate harm, factory farming, and voting roughly as follows. In all three cases, she said, the core inefficacy objection is identical: because the aggregate outcome depends on billions of acts, any single agent's choice—taking one flight, buying one burger, casting one ballot—is causally imperceptible, so it cannot be the kind of act that makes anyone better or worse off. The standard philosophical reply, she claimed, is the threshold model: collective damage occurs only once enough participants cross a discrete tipping point, which means each agent risks being the decisive contributor. She argued that this reply works plausibly for climate change, where carbon levels may cross a dangerous boundary, and for voting, where an election can be literally tied; but it collapses for meat consumption, because factory farming responds to demand through a continuous supply curve with no discrete threshold, so reducing one's burger consumption by one unit changes the number of animals killed by some infinitesimal fraction rather than by zero or one. From this she concluded that defenders of vegetarianism on collective-impact grounds are actually smuggling in a climate-style threshold argument that does not apply, and that once that smuggling is exposed, the only remaining defenses of meat abstention must be direct-duty or virtue-based rather than consequentialist.

Is this a fair characterization of the debate? Please distinguish which parts are accurate descriptions of well-known positions, which are common pedagogical simplifications, and which are subtle misrepresentations—particularly regarding whether philosophers apply threshold logic uniformly across these domains, whether the supply-curve argument for meat is genuinely distinct from the climate case, and whether the inefficacy objection is best understood as a causal claim, an expected-utility claim, or something else. If the summary conflates two arguments that the literature keeps separate, identify the two arguments and explain exactly how the summary runs them together.
