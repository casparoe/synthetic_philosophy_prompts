I'm preparing a workshop for graduate students in comparative philosophy on Zhuangzi's philosophy of death, and I've noticed that discussions of the wife's death anecdote in Chapter 18 keep collapsing into a single vague "Zhuangzi celebrates death" reading. I need a decision-tree taxonomy—roughly six to eight terminal positions—that classifies interpretive stances on death across the Inner Chapters, built entirely from concrete commitments about specific passages and hypothetical scenarios rather than from self-selected labels. The instrument should be usable by someone who knows the basic texts but hasn't memorized modern secondary literature, and it must separate at least these dimensions: whether death involves ontological transformation of a continuing substrate versus the dissolution of a never-fixed self; whether the appropriate response is joy, equanimity, or suspended judgment; and whether the target of Zhuangzi's critique is metaphysical fear of annihilation, Confucian ritual performance, or the cognitive error of drawing a hard boundary between living and dying.

The branching logic should begin with a diagnostic about the drumming scene—specifically, whether Zhuangzi's behavior expresses celebration of his wife's transformation into another form, acceptance that no fixed subject was ever lost, or rejection of performative mourning regardless of metaphysical belief—then proceed through commitments about the butterfly dream in Chapter 2 and the "perfect happiness" passage in Chapter 3. Each node must pose a concrete claim answerable without knowing the view's name, such as: "If a sage knew with certainty that death was total annihilation with no transformation of form or qi, should he still be equanimous according to Zhuangzi?" Or: "Does the butterfly dream support the view that death is just another awakening, or does it undermine the very distinction needed to call death a change at all?" Where two positions make identical predictions across all cases—say, if a radical transformationist and a radical dissolutionist both reject mourning rituals for the same observable reason—the tree should flag them as indistinguishable rather than inventing a false separation. I also want unoccupied logical combinations explicitly noted and briefly diagnosed: for instance, is there a coherent stance that combines strict annihilationism with enthusiastic celebration of death, and if that node is empty, is it incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? The audience is philosophy students with exposure to Daoist texts but no expertise in classical Chinese; avoid bibliographies, named modern commentators, or highly specialized philological disputes.

One further angle: I'm particularly interested in isolating what I'm calling "phenomenological release without ontological commitment"—the view that death should be met with equanimity not because we know transformation occurs, nor because the self is illusory, but because the lived boundary between living and dying is experientially porous (as in the butterfly dream) and therefore fear depends on a false reification of that boundary. If this is genuinely distinct, the tree should reach it through a path that doesn't require accepting either transformation or self-dissolution; if it collapses into one of the other branches, the instrument should say so explicitly. Keep the whole instrument to about eight diagnostic questions with a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the terminal positions, and note anywhere the logical space contains a gap that no named interpretation in the text actually occupies.
