Question: Supererogation is standardly described as action that goes beyond the call of duty. One prominent model holds that supererogatory acts are supported by genuine moral reasons—reasons strong enough to make the act clearly good—yet not so strong that omitting it makes the agent blameworthy. Contrast this reasons-without-blame position with two rivals: (a) the reductionist view that supererogation is merely an illusion produced by an implausibly demanding moral theory that misclassifies ordinary required acts as heroic, and (b) the Kantian view that apparently supererogatory acts are best understood as expressions of imperfect duties—obligations that admit of latitude in when and how they are fulfilled, but obligations nonetheless. Then consider this objection, especially in institutional contexts where individual costs are negligible but aggregate omission is catastrophic: whenever an act benefits others greatly at minimal cost to the agent, failure to perform it is clearly blameworthy; therefore, in all low-cost, high-benefit cases there can be no supererogation, and the reasons-without-blame model collapses because it must either treat these cases as duties or implausibly withhold blame.

Student answer:

The reasons-without-blame view holds that we have strong moral reasons to do supererogatory acts, but no corresponding duty, so omission is not blameworthy. This differs from the reductionist view, which denies that there is any special category: either an act is required or it is not, and what looks supererogatory is just required by a correct but stricter theory than we usually admit. It also differs from the Kantian imperfect-duty account, because on that view helping others is still a duty—we just have freedom about which opportunities we take—whereas the reasons-without-blame model denies there is any duty to help at all, only a reason.

Regarding the objection: if the cost is minimal and the benefit large, then anyone who omits the act deserves blame. So in these cases the act is actually required, not supererogatory. This undermines the model, because the model says there is a reason but no blame for omission. But if the reason is very strong and the cost is near zero, then withholding blame is implausible—think of failing to press a button that saves lives at no risk to yourself. So the model faces a dilemma. Either it concedes that low-cost, high-benefit cases generate duties, which means supererogation disappears precisely where it should be easiest; or it keeps saying there is no blame, which conflicts with ordinary moral judgment. Therefore the objection succeeds in showing that the reasons-without-blame model cannot consistently maintain its central claim across all cases.
