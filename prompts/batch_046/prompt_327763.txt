Here is an argument about graded sentience that I keep returning to, and now I find it suspicious because the conclusion seems to make ordinary moral life impossible. (1) Sentience is not binary but continuous: a human, an insect, and a thermostat that regulates temperature via negative-feedback loops differ only in the complexity of their information integration, not in kind. (2) If sentience is continuous, moral status should be continuous with it: the more complex the integration, the greater the weight of interests, but there is no metaphysically privileged cutoff at which status jumps discontinuously from zero to positive. (3) To take this seriously, assign each being a real-valued moral weight w in (0,1], where w = 1 corresponds to full human moral status and any entity with minimal information integration receives some w > 0, however small. (4) Rational moral comparison requires integrating these weights over all affected beings; otherwise we are not honoring the gradation but merely smuggling a hidden binary threshold back in under the guise of “negligible.” (5) Because populations of low-w beings are astronomically large—consider the insects disturbed by a single step, or the bacteria affected by a common antibiotic—the integrated moral weight of these populations in ordinary actions routinely exceeds the weight of the few high-w beings involved. (6) So if we take the gradation seriously and aggregate consistently, almost every ordinary human action (walking, eating, using medicine) is morally impermissible, since it harms an aggregate whose total moral weight dwarfs any benefit to high-w beings. (7) That conclusion is absurd. (8) Therefore sentience must be binary after all, and (1) is false.

But I want to hold onto (1) and (3) if I can; I would rather reject (4) or (5) than deny that consciousness comes in degrees. Where does the argument actually go wrong? Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, does giving up (4) force me to abandon the idea that moral reasoning can be quantitative at all, or is there a principled way to block the aggregation in (5) without reintroducing the very binary cutoff that (2) was meant to reject?
