Below is an examination question I administered in my upper-level seminar on Kant’s practical philosophy, together with one student’s submitted response. The exercise asks students to reason from the core commitments of Kantian ethics without consulting secondary literature.

Question: Kant’s well-known discussion of lying to the murderer at the door—developed in his essay on the supposed right to lie from benevolence—defends an unconditional prohibition on falsehood, even when an innocent life is at stake. For the purposes of this exercise, assume the standard interpretation: the maxim of lying to save a life involves a contradiction in conception, because it undermines the very practice of truthful assertion upon which its own efficacy depends.

Consider now the following thesis advanced by a hypothetical interlocutor: ‘Kant’s argument succeeds only against direct falsehoods. If I avoid an explicit lie—remaining silent, or answering a different question than the one put to me—I do not violate the categorical imperative. The maxim “I will protect the innocent through strategic silence or misdirection, without uttering a direct falsehood” can be universalized without contradiction in conception; at most, it might generate a contradiction in will by eroding social trust. Moreover, such misdirection respects the Formula of Humanity better than lying does, since it does not actively implant false beliefs in the listener’s rational faculties but leaves him to draw his own inferences. Finally, Kant’s perfect duty of veracity is purely negative: it forbids active deception but does not require that one volunteer truthful information when directly questioned.’

Write a critical evaluation of this thesis (approximately 500 words), addressing the following precisely:
(a) Is the proposed maxim genuinely universalizable without contradiction in conception? Examine carefully whether strategic silence or misdirection relies on conditions that would be undermined by its universal adoption, and identify whether any contradiction that arises is in conception or in will.
(b) How does the Formula of Humanity apply? Does strategic misdirection treat the aggressor merely as a means, or does it respect his rational agency in a manner that direct falsehood does not?
(c) Is Kant’s duty of veracity purely negative, or does it entail a positive obligation to disclose? In particular, if one is directly asked about the location of the hidden person, does silence constitute a violation equivalent to lying?

Please support your reasoning with reference to the logical structure of the categorical imperative and the distinction between perfect and imperfect duties, but do not appeal to contemporary commentators.

Student answer:

The proposed maxim—‘I will avoid direct falsehood while protecting the innocent through strategic silence or misdirection’—can indeed be universalized without contradiction in conception. Unlike direct lying, which destroys the institution of truthful assertion, strategic silence merely allows each agent to decide what to reveal. If everyone adopted this maxim, communication would remain possible; people would simply learn to ask more precise questions. Thus there is no contradiction in conception.

The contradiction, if any, arises in the will: universal strategic misdirection would erode the general trust necessary for cooperative social life, and since rational agents necessarily will such cooperation, they cannot consistently will the maxim’s universalization. This is where the Formula of Universal Law operates.

As for the Formula of Humanity, strategic misdirection actually respects the murderer’s rational agency more than lying does. Lying directly manipulates the listener’s belief-forming faculties by supplying a false premise. By contrast, silence or answering a different question leaves the murderer to draw his own inferences; we do not treat him as a mere means to our end of saving the friend, but rather allow him to exercise his own reason, however flawed its results.

Finally, Kant’s unconditional duty of veracity entails not merely avoiding falsehood but actively providing truthful information when asked. If one is directly questioned about the friend’s location, remaining silent violates the perfect duty of veracity just as much as lying, because it withholds the truth that the questioner is entitled to receive. Therefore, one must answer directly and truthfully, even if this leads to harm.
