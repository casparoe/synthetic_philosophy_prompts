In philosophical discussions of Dennett's brain-transplant thought experiment — the scenario in which a brain is extracted and placed in a new body, raising the problem of self-location — defenders of the brain-based answer frequently invoke an analogy between the brain and an aircraft pilot, and between the body and the airframe. The claim is that just as a pilot retains her identity when moved to a different plane, so the subject of experience remains with the brain regardless of bodily transplantation. Provide an explicit correspondence table that maps pilot, aircraft, navigation instruments, flight plan, air-traffic authority, and maintenance crew onto their proposed analogues in the brain-transplant case. Then audit the analogy: identify which features of piloting depend on a functional separation between commander and vehicle that embodied cognition denies; examine whether the analogy quietly imports a Cartesian picture of the self as a detachable controller that sits in tension with Dennett's own characterization of the self as a distributed center of narrative gravity rather than a localized substance; assess whether the conclusion — that personal location tracks the brain — survives once we recognize that a pilot's competence is largely externalized in instruments and protocols that have no parallel in neural tissue alone; and, if the pilot-aircraft model collapses, propose whether an alternative analogy — such as a distributed operating system whose processes are redundantly hosted across server nodes — better captures Dennett's view without smuggling in the disanalogous assumption of a singular detachable commander.
