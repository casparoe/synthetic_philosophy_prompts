I work as a software engineer building recommendation and preference-learning systems, with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been trying to think through the conceptual foundations of how we should extract user values from behavioral data—partly out of concern for how similar methods might be used in AI alignment. Over the past few months I have settled on the following five commitments, and I want an audit of whether they actually hang together.

(1) Inverse reinforcement learning provides the correct formal framework for preference inference: given a trajectory of observed actions, there exists a reward function (unique up to scaling and potential shaping) that best rationalizes the behavior, and recovering this function is what it means to learn what the agent wants.

(2) I also accept the standard behavioral-economics result that human choice is deeply frame-dependent: the same individual can prefer option A over B in one presentation context and B over A in another, with no change in information or stakes, which suggests that revealed preferences are not stable across contexts.

(3) For any system that acts on a user’s behalf, the normatively relevant preferences are not these frame-dependent revealed ones, but rather the preferences the user would endorse upon calm reflection in a framing-neutral environment.

(4) At the same time, I hold that if two framing contexts produce genuinely contradictory orderings and there is no principled, non-arbitrary reason to privilege one frame, then there is simply no further fact of the matter about which option the user “really” prefers.

(5) I think an aligned AI should maximize expected reward relative to the inferred reward function, and that this is compatible with (3) provided we restrict the behavioral data used for inference to contexts that approximate reflective endorsement rather than impulsive choice.

My question is whether (1) through (5) form a jointly tenable package. Specifically: does (2) undermine the uniqueness and stability assumptions built into (1)? Does (4) render (3) unattainable, since there may be no reflective preference to recover when frames conflict? Does (5) implicitly commit me to treating preferences as stable in a way that (2) and (4) explicitly deny? I also want you to flag any tensions that are only apparent—pairs that look incompatible but dissolve once the commitments are properly interpreted—versus genuine conflicts that require giving something up. Please rank the real tensions by severity, identify the least costly revision that restores consistency, and be explicit about which single commitment is doing the most damage to the coherence of the set if I refuse to revise it.
