So I'm retired from the physics dept after about thirty years, and lately I've been reading philosophy of science in the evenings—mostly because I miss arguing about what knowledge actually is. There's a small group that meets at the community center, mostly other retired scientists and a couple grad students, and last Tuesday Maria and Dave got stuck on peer disagreement. I figured I'd ask here since I couldn't referee them properly.

Maria started with what she called conciliationism: if you and someone who is your epistemic peer—same smarts, same access to info, no obvious bias—disagree about something, you both have to revise your confidence downward, probably meeting in the middle. She said this is especially true in science, where two seperate labs might get different measurements and neither can spot the error. Dave pushed back with steadfastness. He talked about his old analog climate sensors—he spent years with them, knows how they drift in humidity, how a particular scratch on one dial affects readings. If a peer in another lab gets a different number, Dave might stick with his result not out of arrogance, but because he has a kind of embodied, tacit familiarity with thier own apparatus that the other person literally can't share. Maria replied that if Dave really has extra evidence—this private instrument knowledge—then they're not true peers anymore, and conciliationism was never meant for that case. Dave said peerhood should be about general competence and honesty, not about having identical evidence streams; otherwise you'd never have peer disagreement in science at all, since every researcher works with different notebooks and weather conditions. Then Maria changed the ground: okay, but what about disagreement over a methodological principle, not raw data? Say, whether you should always use flat priors in climate reconstruction models. Here Dave doesn't have special apparatus knowledge. Dave admitted that for pure principles conciliation seems stronger, but then he asked whether any principle is ever really pure—our intuitions about priors come from practice too, so Dave might still have a kind of practical wisdom that functions like evidence. Maria said now he's just redefining "evidence" to save steadfastness.

Could you adjudicate this move by move? Tell me who's right on Maria's initial claim, Dave's apparatus point, Maria's peerhood reply, Dave's definition of peerhood, and that principle twist at the end. Also say what both are missing that makes the whole argument feel circular—don't just list books or say "it depends on the literature," I want to know where the reasoning actually breaks or holds. Thanks!
