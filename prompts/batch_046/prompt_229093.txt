I'm prepping a talk for next month's medical ethics seminar at my university's Centre for Philosophy and I'm genuinely a bit worried about the Q&A. I'm defending this idea I cooked up: transformative experiences don't merely block first-person knowledge of outcomes—the usual Paul line—but they dissolve the preference framework that made the pre-choice self a legitimate normative authority over the decision. For genuinely transformative choices—parenthood, irreversible gender-affirming surgery, religious conversion—the pre-transformative self has no special standing to consent on behalf of the post-transformative self, and the moral remainder that usually justifies autonomy-based consent doesn't carry across that epistemic rupture. So standard informed consent protocols are structurally misapplied to these cases, not just epistemically limited.

The room will hold about twelve people: mainly clinical ethicists who design hospital consent protocols, a couple analytic philosophers of autonomy who love relational autonomy literature, and one decision theorist who thinks I'm conflating epistemology with normativity. They're practical, not metaphysicians; they care about protecting vulnerable patients and they really don't want to hear that informed consent is "broken." I was chatting about this with a colleague in the pub and she said the hidden danger is negative transformations—trauma, dementia—where my view might accidentally justify preventing harm because the pre-trauma self "knows best." Can you act as an expert philosopher of medicine and decision theory? First, briefly list the hidden assumptions this audience carries into the room. Include the one objection I'm secretly hoping nobody asks, and please think carefully step by step.
