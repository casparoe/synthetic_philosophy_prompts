I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've kept noticing the same structural pattern in local conventions that I can't place with the vocabulary I have. In the U.S., failing to tip a restaurant server is frequently treated as a moral wrong, not merely a social misstep, even though there is no legal requirement and the worker receives a base wage. In some Northern European cities, cutting in line at a bus stop draws immediate public correction despite the fact that the physical harm is negligible. Jaywalking in parts of Germany is reprimanded as disrespectful even when traffic risk is minimal, whereas in other countries the identical act is ignored entirely. What interests me is not the variation itself, but a specific feature these cases share: the norm is arbitrary in content, it carries moral weight that exceeds any direct harm it prevents, and its binding force seems to depend on what the person knows and whether they are seen as part of the local group, rather than on consent or consequence. A tourist who tips inadequately out of genuine ignorance is usually excused; the same tourist who knowingly refuses is condemned. A local resident who cuts a line in a clear emergency is often forgiven; an outsider who does the same is seen as violating a duty. Meanwhile, other equally arbitrary local differences—driving on the left, using metric measurements—are treated as purely practical or conventional, with no comparable moral charge.

I've observed a micro-scale version of this in technology offices, where an unwritten ordering emerges around a shared coffee station: people implicitly line up by arrival time, and using the machine out of turn because you are in a rush triggers stronger moral disapproval than simply failing to recognize the pattern. Yet an equally unwritten preference about desk organization in the same office generates no comparable obligation. So the phenomenon is not merely unwritten rules or local etiquette. It is a subset of conventions that are defended in a circular way—people say that is just how it is done here without pointing to harm—that bind knowledgeable insiders and aware outsiders, that excuse the genuinely ignorant, and that are not reducible to instrumental reasoning.

Is there an established philosophical concept that captures exactly this cluster? I am not looking for a bare label. I want to understand what precisely belongs inside the boundary and what falls outside. For example, does the concept cover norms that have some instrumental benefit but also acquire this membership-sensitive moral overlay? Does it apply when ignorance is never treated as an excuse? How should we distinguish it from legal obligation on one side and from pure etiquette on the other? And if no precise concept exists, I would welcome a coined term together with clear definitional conditions and a few boundary cases that show where the edges lie.
