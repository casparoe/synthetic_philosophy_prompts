Write a blank-verse poem of exactly twenty-four lines, arranged in three octets, on the distinction between cranes and skyhooks in evolutionary explanation, with greedy reductionism as the hidden antagonist. In the first octet, build a biological adaptation—say, the vertebrate eye—step by step from simpler precursors, using only the vocabulary of incremental selection and inherited variation; let each line add a structural element so the form itself acts like a crane. In the second octet, translate that same history into the vocabulary of pure physics and chemistry—atoms, thermodynamics, gene frequencies—deliberately stripping away every functional and intentional term; this should feel like an explanation that consumes its own subject. In the third octet, restore the eye as a genuinely seeing organ, but now show that its functional description survives only because it is anchored in the genealogy of the first octet; the recovery should feel earned, not smuggled in. Do not name Dennett or any philosopher; someone who knows the debate should recognize the architecture, and someone who does not should still read it as a coherent poem about explanation and loss. Begin with the poem itself, with no preface or commentary.
