I'm a retired marine biologist—spent fourty years tagging sharks in the Pacific—and lately I've been sitting in on philosophy of science seminars at the local university. I'm getting tired of hearing my younger collegues talk about "the Scientific Revolution" like it was one thunderclap that hit in 1543 or 1687, with the Middle Ages and Renaissance just being the dark and transitional waiting rooms. So I want you to argue, seriously, that the whole trio—Middle Ages, Renaissance, Scientific Revolution—is mostly a 19th-century museum-labeling scheme that we should stop treating as real historical joints.

But don't just recycle the usual Kuhn talking points or remind me that Copernicus was religious. Instead, build your case around what I'd call "distributed calibration": the idea that scientific credibility doesn't flip like a switch but accumulates through small, local adjustments in instrument trust, peer-review norms, and error tolerance that spread unevenly across centuries and disciplines. Use that framing to show why carving history at 1500 and 1600 is about as sensible as declaring that "the Climate Shift" happened on a specific Tuesday in 1952. Make the argument that these periods are later inventions, and do it while respecting the actual messiness of archival evidence—without asking me to read some obscure Italian micro-historian I can't pronounce.
