Here is the chain:

(1) For Strawson, an agent is morally responsible for an act just in case reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, indignation) directed at the agent for that act are appropriate.
(2) The appropriateness of a reactive attitude requires that its target be a unitary quality of will—an integrated psychological source capable of being affected by the attitude in a non-instrumental way.
(3) In cases of radical diachronic psychological discontinuity (complete replacement of psychological traits, memories, and values, with only biological continuity preserved), the later individual is not the same unitary quality of will as the earlier agent.
(4) Despite this, ordinary moral practice treats the later individual as the proper target of reactive attitudes for the earlier agent's acts (e.g., we hold the transformed or amnesiac person responsible, not merely the past stage).
(5) Therefore, either reactive attitudes in these cases are systematically inappropriate, which contradicts Strawson's practice-based criterion of appropriateness, or the unitary-source requirement in (2) is false.
(6) If (2) is false, then reactive attitudes need not target an integrated psychological source at all; they could be appropriate toward any entity that exhibits the relevant behavioral patterns, including corporations, artificial systems, or causally extended processes lacking any unified will.
(7) But extending responsibility in this way is absurd—it dissolves the boundary between moral agents and non-agents that Strawson's framework was designed to preserve.
(8) Hence Strawson's view either condemns ordinary practices of holding people responsible across psychological change or collapses into an indiscriminate ascription of responsibility.
