I am preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for policy graduate students who have never encountered Amartya Sen's entitlement analysis. The aim is to establish that major famines are more accurately understood as collapses of entitlement relations than as natural food shortages, drawing on the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of 1845–1852, and the Great Leap Forward crisis of 1959–1961. I need a teaching plan rather than a lecture script: lay out the exact dependency order in which the concepts must be introduced—beginning with the direct/exchange entitlement distinction—so that the "political crime" framing emerges as an analytical conclusion rather than a moralistic assertion. Specify the one carrying case study that should run through the session: it must be an instance where aggregate per-capita food availability did not fall, or even rose, during the mortality peak, and it must be structurally different enough from the other two cases that the trio reveals a pattern rather than an anecdote. Map the predictable misconceptions—especially the belief that Sen rejects all agronomic or climatic causation, and the slide from "political failure" to "intentional genocide"—indicating the precise point in the sequence where each should be interrupted and corrected. Provide one closing diagnostic question that tests whether a student grasps the entitlement mechanism rather than merely endorsing anti-government sentiment. Finally, identify one significant component of Sen's broader capability framework that should be explicitly excluded at this level and named as out of scope.
