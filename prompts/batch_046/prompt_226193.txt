I'm constructing a taxonomy for a workshop on moral worth that avoids the standard Kantian-versus-Humean shorthand. I want a decision tree built from three binary commitments that sort views by what they actually require of an agent, not by the labels philosophers wear.

The branching questions are:

1. Must the agent's operative motivating reason be identical to the objective right-making reason for the act to have full moral worth? (Not merely compatible—literally the same reason.)
2. If the answer to 1 is yes, does the agent need occurrent conscious access to that reason at the time of action, or is stable dispositional tracking sufficient?
3. Independently of 1 and 2, does the action retain its moral worth only if the agent is counterfactually sensitive—i.e., they would not have performed the same act in a nearby world where that moral reason was absent and only a non-moral motive (habit, self-interest, social pressure) remained?

Present this as a tree: each internal node is one question with yes/no branches; each leaf carries the three-answer combination. For each occupied leaf, name a position or philosopher's view if one genuinely fits, marking it "unnamed" if none does; then in one sentence state its most awkward commitment—something that makes it hard to defend in ordinary moral judgment. For any empty combination, briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and say why. Finally, flag any two positions that share all three answers but still differ on something important for moral worth, so the tree's limits are visible.

Keep the tree focused on the logical space first, then map views onto it; do not ask for bibliographies or reading lists. The audience is philosophers who already know the basic terrain but want to see where their own intuitions land.
