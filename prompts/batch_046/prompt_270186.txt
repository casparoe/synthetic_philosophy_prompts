I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of mind and moral psychology, and next Thursday I am presenting a chapter of my thesis to our departmental research seminar. The position I defend is a reconceptualisation of Strawson’s reactive attitudes—specifically resentment, gratitude, and indignation—not as binary emotional responses triggered by perceived ill will, but as graded “normative calibration signals” embedded within what I term a “tight-loose institutional architecture”. I argue that the participant stance is not an all-or-nothing switch between the objective and the reactive attitudes, as Strawson is often read, but a continuously variable posture whose intensity is calibrated to the predictability demands of particular social domains. Drawing on predictive-processing frameworks only analogically, I claim that holding an agent morally responsible is best understood as assigning them a functional node-status within a social forecasting system: reactive attitudes are deployed when treating the target as a locus of correction improves the system’s predictive accuracy regarding future behaviour. Consequently, determinism does not undermine moral responsibility, because the legitimacy of these attitudes depends not on contracausal freedom but on their instrumental efficacy within institutionally structured expectation-sets. The audience will include two senior compatibilists who defend orthodox Strawsonian accounts, one philosopher of mind who works on predictive coding and is sceptical of normative analogies, a hard determinist who rejects reactive accounts altogether, a legal philosopher interested in institutional design, and three graduate students in ethics.
