Consider the asymmetry embedded in both forensic psychiatry and autonomous-systems liability law: when a human acts harmfully during an automatistic or dissociative state—behavior that is stereotyped, environmentally triggered, and lacking in contextual flexibility—courts typically treat the act as non-voluntary and excuse the agent; yet when an artificial system causes equivalent harm through an equally rigid, pre-programmed response chain, regulators and courts treat it not as an excused agent but as a defective product, imposing liability on the manufacturer or operator rather than recognizing any absence of volition in the system itself. At the same time, both fields treat flexible, context-sensitive adaptation as the hallmark of genuine agency or competence, though neither offers an operational boundary between mechanical optimization and the kind of flexibility that warrants moral or legal standing.

What philosophical commitments about free will, agency, and responsibility does this institutional double standard presuppose? Must it rest on a form of biological or phenomenal dualism—holding that only conscious organisms can lack volition—or can it be reconstructed within a functionalist framework that distinguishes degrees of control without invoking consciousness? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and examine whether that same framework could consistently classify deeply habituated or addictive human behavior as sphexish without eroding the legal and moral distinctions on which criminal excuse doctrine depends. If, instead, we adopted a uniform view that treats all stereotyped behavior—whether in human automatism or in machine execution—as equally non-volitional, what changes would be required in either liability rules for autonomous systems or in the criteria for legal excuse?
