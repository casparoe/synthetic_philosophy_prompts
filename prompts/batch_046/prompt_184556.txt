Write a deadpan parody of a corporate "Interpretive Stance Licensing Agreement," formatted as a numbered legal-compliance document of approximately 600 words, for a hypothetical predictive-analytics vendor that offers clients three licensed interpretive frameworks: bottom-up physical simulation, functional design specification, and belief-desire attribution (the intentional framework). The voice should be consistently dry, risk-averse corporate counsel—complete with sections on scope of license, warranty disclaimers, indemnification, liability caps, and dispute resolution—without ever breaking register. Each clause should quietly encode a substantive philosophical position regarding real patterns and stance-dependent ontology: for example, whether a predictive pattern qualifies as "real" only if it survives translation across all three frameworks or if stance-relative compression is sufficient for warranty coverage; whether licensing the design framework commits the user to the existence of an actual designer; whether predictive success using the intentional framework binds the licensee to realism about propositional attitudes; and what remedies apply when a pattern is robust at the design or intentional level but dissolves under physical-stance simulation (a "stance-conflict event"). The satire should derive from mapping genuine debates in philosophy of mind onto bureaucratic risk-management language, so that a specialist recognizes the underlying arguments while a non-specialist can still parse the contract. Do not name Dennett, cite specific works, include bibliographies, or use verbatim quotations from philosophical texts. Maintain internal consistency: treat all three frameworks as interchangeable commercial products, yet let the fine print reveal that their ontological and predictive commitments diverge in ways the vendor refuses to guarantee. Include at least one clause addressing user obligations during a stance-conflict event, and conclude with a severability clause that implicitly raises the question of whether any single stance can survive the failure of the others.
