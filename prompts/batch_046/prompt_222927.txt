I have been reflecting on a structural feature that recurs in debates about whether the content of a person's thoughts is fixed partly by features of their environment rather than solely by what is in their head. The familiar thought-experiment involves two planets that are physically identical except for an undetectable chemical difference in the clear liquid inhabitants call 'water'. I want to isolate a different kind of variation. Imagine two worlds in which the liquid itself is chemically identical everywhere, but the social mechanisms that determine authoritative classification differ. On one world, a single central body decides whether borderline cases — melted snow, synthetic replicas, heavily diluted solutions — count as genuine instances, and ordinary speakers defer to it uniformly. On the other, authority is distributed among regional boards with slightly different criteria, and speakers shift which board they treat as decisive according to practical context. Now consider an individual who forms her concept in the first environment and is then placed in the second. Intuitively, what her thoughts are about seems to shift, not because the substance around her has changed, nor because her internal psychology is different, but because the institutional framework that fixes correct application has fragmented. Is there an established name for this precise configuration — content-variation driven by divergence in the social architecture of expertise rather than by divergence in natural kind? If not, I would like it distinguished carefully: how it relates to the standard twin-planet scenario, to purely internalist pictures, and to broad social theories of meaning, together with the boundary conditions that would prevent it from collapsing into one of those neighbours.
