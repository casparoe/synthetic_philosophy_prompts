As a novelist constructing a multigenerational narrative about inherited moral consequence, I have become preoccupied with karma not as devotional doctrine but as a rigorous theory of diachronic moral desert—an account of how blame and merit accrue, transform, and eventually discharge across the arc of a life, or across lives, without requiring the continuous presence of a judging consciousness. Most philosophical treatments I encounter either reduce karma to a causal law of action and reaction or treat it as an exotic variant of Western retributivism. I believe both miss something conceptually distinctive: karma appears to presuppose a narrative economy of moral status in which an agent’s present conditions are readable as the accumulated expression of past desert, yet this very readability creates tension with our ordinary commitments to personal identity over time and to the role of circumstance in shaping character. Before I commit my manuscript’s moral architecture to one interpretation, I want to understand which conceptual problems in this space are genuinely upstream of the others.

Specifically, I am trying to prioritize among several open lines of inquiry: first, whether karma can maintain coherence without a metaphysically persistent self that links actions to their fruits, or whether it collapses into a form of impersonal moral accounting; second, whether its account of desert survives contact with modern debates about moral luck—whether the conditions of one’s birth, inherited social structures, and the random contingencies of temperament can be cleanly separated from what is genuinely earned; third, whether karma’s implicit theory of moral progress requires a teleological endpoint, and if so, whether that endpoint functions as a secular ideal of perfected character or as a metaphysical release from narrative obligation altogether; and fourth, how karma’s treatment of intentionality compares to legal doctrines that distinguish attempt from completion regardless of external outcome. Rather than a survey, I want a defended agenda: for someone with limited formal philosophical training but a serious investment in narrative coherence—the equivalent of a single sustained research project—which two or three of these questions should come first?

Please defend the ordering with explicit reasoning about dependency—does resolving the personal-identity problem unlock the moral-luck problem, or are they largely orthogonal?—about tractability—can they be addressed through conceptual analysis alone, or do they depend on empirical claims about cognition, culture, or neuroscience?—and about practical weight—if one answer changed, which parts of a secular ethics of desert would actually have to be rebuilt? I am less interested in the list itself than in the criteria that make one question worth years of attention and another a secondary elaboration.
