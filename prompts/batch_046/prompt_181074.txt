Specify the hard desiderata that an adequate ethical theory of agricultural pest control must satisfy. I am not asking for a survey of positions, but for the specification sheet: the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must preserve, and the empirical or normative boundaries it cannot cross. In particular, include: (i) the range of interventions it must cover—lethal chemical application, biological control, habitat modification, and sub-lethal or reproductive-suppressive measures that reduce fitness across generations; (ii) the conceptual distinctions it must maintain, especially between direct harm to individual sentient organisms and the disruption of ecological functional roles, and between immediate killing and indirect population collapse; (iii) the empirical constraints, such as what is known about nociception and behavioral complexity in target insect taxa and about ecosystem dependency networks that replacement strategies must respect; (iv) the normative non-negotiables—e.g., that the theory must not collapse pest ethics into generic environmental ethics, since it needs to capture the specific moral relation between human agricultural agents and pest populations; and (v) an assessment of which single desideratum does the most damage to the dominant utilitarian-aggregation and rights-extension approaches, together with a judgment about which requirements are genuinely mandatory and which are framing artifacts of one camp. Note whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, so that the list itself reveals a tension in the problem space. You may include a brief table scoring the main contenders—utilitarian, rights-based, virtue-ecological, and contractualist or agricultural-duty frameworks—against the list, but keep the analytical weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, not on the scoring.
