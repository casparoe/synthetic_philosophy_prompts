As a journalist covering historical sociology and the philosophy of science, I keep encountering a particular framing move in debates over the Great Divergence. The contrastive question “why Britain and not China?” is posed as though “Britain” and “China” were stable, bounded entities with consistent internal properties across the centuries preceding industrialization. In practice, this means treating highly heterogeneous regional economies—the Yangzi delta versus the northern frontier, or England’s agrarian south versus its industrial north—as expressions of a single national trajectory, then asking which unified trajectory possessed the correct conditions for takeoff. The suppression of internal geographic and institutional variation is not merely anachronism; it actively constructs comparable macro-units so that divergence can be explained by differences between them rather than within them. Does this specific pattern—stabilizing fluid, regionally varied entities into uniform comparative units for divergence explanations—have an established name in historiography or philosophy of history? If not, how should it be precisely defined, and how does it differ from ordinary presentism and from standard critiques of the comparative method itself?
