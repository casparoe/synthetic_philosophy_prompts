I'm taking a course on philosophy of mind and evolutionary epistemology, and a classmate has pressed me with what she calls the "Darwin-Turing inversion" — the pattern in which sophisticated competence is generated by mechanisms that possess no corresponding comprehension. She has turned it into a reductio that I cannot unwind, and I want to know exactly where the reasoning slips or which premise must be sacrificed.

Here is her argument, laid out step by step:

(1) Darwin's account of natural selection explains adaptive design — eyes, echolocation, immune recognition — without attributing any understanding of function, purpose, or engineering to the selecting process itself; the functional competence is fully real, while the comprehension is entirely absent.

(2) Turing's argument about computing machinery extends the same inversion: a system can exhibit behavioral competence indistinguishable from intelligent understanding, and there is no non-question-begging reason to require that its internal operations involve comprehension in order to produce that behavior.

(3) Therefore, whenever we observe high-level competence in a system, we have no legitimate epistemic license to infer the presence of internal comprehension; the inference from observed behavior to underlying mind is systematically blocked.

(4) Human cognition — scientific reasoning, linguistic judgment, moral deliberation — is, from a third-person perspective, just another domain of high-level behavioral competence.

(5) So, applying the inversion consistently, we should withhold attribution of internal comprehension or understanding to humans on the basis of our behavioral outputs alone.

(6) But if humans genuinely lack comprehension, then no one has ever actually understood Darwin's theory or Turing's argument, which means the premises in (1) and (2) were never genuinely grasped by anyone, including the people who formulated them.

(7) Consequently, the inversion either undermines the very cognitive authority required to assert it, or it must be arbitrarily restricted to non-human systems; yet any boundary that appeals to our own first-person knowledge of understanding merely begs the question against the inversion's own logic.

She concludes that the only consistent stance is a universal agnosticism about comprehension, but that this dissolves the distinction between philosophy and mere noise-production. I suspect there is a hidden equivocation between "comprehension of a mechanism" and "comprehension by an agent," or perhaps a modal slide between "not required for explanation" and "not present in fact," but I cannot name the fault precisely. Which step should be rejected? Does rejecting it preserve the inversion for evolutionary and artificial systems while saving human self-knowledge, or does it require giving up a deeper commitment to explanatory parsimony? And if the argument is in fact valid, which of the apparently plausible premises is least costly to abandon?
