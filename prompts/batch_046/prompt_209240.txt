I am preparing remarks for a philosophy of probability seminar in which we are examining whether Bertrand's paradox genuinely undermines the principle of indifference, or whether it instead exposes a hidden ambiguity in how we specify the state space of an experiment. I want you to argue for the following position: the paradox is not a failure of indifference but a demonstration that uniform probability assignments must be indexed to the automorphism group of the physical generating process, not to arbitrary geometric parameterizations of the outcome space. Call this the process-relative symmetry constraint. On your view, the three classic constructions—selecting two points uniformly on the circumference, selecting a radius uniformly and then a point uniformly along it, and selecting a midpoint uniformly within the inscribed circle—are not alternative descriptions of one experiment but descriptions of three experiments with different causal structures; applying the principle of indifference within each parameterization is correct, and the apparent contradiction arises only from the illegitimate assumption that they share a single state space. Defend this against the charge that it collapses into a covert frequentism or a physicalist reduction of epistemic probability, and explain why treating parameterization as part of the state description rather than as a neutral coordinate choice avoids the paradox without abandoning the normative force of indifference. Be specific about how the radial-distance and chord-inscription methods encode different physical symmetries, and address whether your resolution generalizes to other geometric paradoxes of uniform measure. Please conclude by stating how confident you are in the argument and identifying the specific claim—whether about causal structure, symmetry groups, or the distinction from frequentism—about which you are least certain.
