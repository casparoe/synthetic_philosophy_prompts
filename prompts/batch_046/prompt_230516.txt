As a retired natural scientist who has recently turned to philosophy, I have been observing a debate in my discussion group on whether brain size is morally relevant. Two fellow members—call them A and B—exchanged the following arguments, and I should welcome a point-by-point adjudication.

A opened by claiming that brain size is plainly irrelevant to moral status: a human with severe microcephaly retains full rights, whereas a sperm whale, with a brain far larger, does not possess personhood; hence size is a category error, and only capacities such as rationality or sentience carry weight.

B replied that A treats brain size as raw cranial volume, which nobody defends. The serious claim is that structural scale—once corrected for neuronal density and connectivity—functions as an unavoidable epistemic proxy for precisely those capacities A values, since direct access to another being's consciousness is rarely available. To reject all structural proxies, B said, would paralyse comparative ethics.

A then contended that, even as a proxy, brain size is actively misleading. Corvids achieve sophisticated cognition with brains a fraction of primate size owing to high pallial neuron density; elephants possess enormous brains governed by different allometric rules. Relying on size, even in weighted form, unfairly favours large-bodied mammals and obscures functional equivalence across taxa.

B conceded the imperfection but introduced a hypothetical: imagine a being whose brain is twice human volume solely because of enlarged glial bodies and longer axonal tracts, with no increase in neuron number or synaptic connectivity. Its moral claim should not rise, B argued, precisely because volume tracks nothing functionally new. This demonstrates, B said, that size is not irrelevant but conditional—relevant only insofar as it correlates with architecturally meaningful complexity, such as expanded prefrontal integration or richer temporal modelling.

A countered that B has now abandoned the original thesis. If size matters only when it tracks architecture, then architecture does all the moral work and size is redundant. Furthermore, A observed, B's framework appears to permit graded moral status: if structural complexity comes in degrees, must our obligations scale proportionally? That seems implausible—moral status is binary, not a scalar function of cortical organisation.

B responded that binary status need not exhaust moral consideration; obligations of care or representation might vary with the range and depth of a being's interests, which structural features help reveal, without rendering anyone's basic rights quantitative.
