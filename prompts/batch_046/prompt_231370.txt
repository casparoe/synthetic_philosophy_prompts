Erwin Schrödinger, in his later philosophical reflections, embraced a Vedantic-inspired monism according to which individual minds are not genuinely separate substances but rather transient modalities of a single, continuous consciousness—a view he connected to the problem of personal identity across biological generations and to his skepticism about technological or mnemonic forms of personal immortality. Consider a contemporary scenario in which distributed quantum-entangled neural interfaces allow multiple human subjects to share synchronized subjective states across spatial separation, producing what researchers term inter-subjective coherence without localization to any one brain. How do you think Schrödinger would have interpreted such a phenomenon? Would he have taken it as empirical confirmation that the illusion of separate selves can be technologically dissolved, thereby revealing the underlying Vedantic unity? Or would he have insisted that this is merely a simulated fusion—an engineered mimicry of oneness that leaves the fundamental singularity of consciousness untouched and perhaps even obscures it? In addressing this, discuss whether he would have regarded the erasure of individual narrative boundaries in such networks as spiritually significant, and explain how his resistance to the preservation of personal identity through memory or data transfer might inform his reaction to claims that these interfaces achieve a kind of collective immortality.
