I am not an academic — I work as a rapid-assessment officer for an international food-security NGO — but for several years I have been thinking through Amartya Sen's argument that famines are rarely caused by a simple absence of food and are more often the result of collapsed "entitlements" to it: lost wages, broken trade links, failed transfers, or direct production failures. I want to turn that framework into a reusable field procedure I can apply when I am sent into a crisis zone with incomplete records, no library access, and about forty-eight hours to advise whether we should treat the crisis primarily as a natural disaster requiring emergency supply, or as a politically induced entitlement collapse requiring diplomatic pressure and institutional repair rather than only truckloads of grain.

Please construct a practical decision procedure — an ordered checklist or set of observable tests — that an assessment officer without formal economics training can run under time pressure. It should be grounded in Sen's distinction between food availability decline and entitlement failure, including the observable signatures of direct, trade-based, labor-based, and transfer entitlement collapses, translated into indicators I can check in market prices, labor and wage records, export and import flows, and administrative distribution logs, with explicit instructions for what to do when records are missing, contradictory, or deliberately obscured.

The procedure must flag every point where judgment cannot be eliminated — especially when climate stress and government policy both contribute — and it must include a "misfire warning" section describing the cases where the checklist will wrongly blame nature or wrongly blame politics. It should also state clearly what the procedure cannot settle and where an officer must personally sign off.

Apply the procedure to three historical cases: the Bengal famine of 1943, the Great Irish Famine of the 1840s, and the food crisis during China's Great Leap Forward. For each, walk through the steps, state the likely classification, and highlight any uncomfortable result — in particular, identify which of the three forces the procedure to classify the crisis as politically induced despite a prominent natural or climatic trigger, and explain why that result follows from the logic of the checklist rather than from hindsight.

Finally, include a test for a scenario I encounter in modern fieldwork that does not fit the classic models cleanly: a region where national grain reserves are physically adequate, climate and harvest conditions are within normal ranges, but food access is blocked by a layered system of private security contractors controlling transport corridors together with digital-ration verification that systematically excludes one ethnic group from state transfer programs. The procedure should tell me whether this is an entitlement collapse, and if so, whether the primary failure lies with the state, the market, or the contracted intermediary layer. If the procedure cannot settle this, say so explicitly and mark the exact judgment call where an officer must decide without the checklist's cover.

Keep the deliverable fully self-contained: no reading lists, no requests for citations or further sources, and no references I would need to verify externally. I need something I can laminate and take into the field.
