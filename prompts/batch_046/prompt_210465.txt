I've been trying to fix my own position on presentism in historiography, and I'm unsure whether these commitments form a coherent package or are pulling in incompatible directions. Here is roughly where I've landed:

(1) Historians should evaluate past actors and institutions by the normative and epistemic standards that were actually available to them in their historical context, not by whether they anticipated our current moral or political norms. Strong presentism, on this view, corrupts historical understanding.

(2) At the same time, I think that describing and explaining systemic injustices — chattel slavery, colonial extraction, institutionalized gender exclusion — requires deploying structural-analytical categories (for example, racial capitalism, biopolitical administration, gendered labor extraction) that reveal the logic of harm, even when those precise categories were not available to contemporaries.

(3) I also hold that explaining past action frequently requires rational reconstruction: attributing to agents practical distinctions, reasoning steps, or implicit commitments that they never explicitly articulated, provided those steps were genuinely available within their context.

(4) But I also maintain that applying contemporary analytical categories to past societies as if they were native classifications — without direct evidence that they map onto actors' self-understandings or practical distinctions — is a serious methodological distortion.

(5) Finally, I think that writing history for present audiences unavoidably requires some conceptual translation, and that an absolute refusal to translate produces antiquarianism rather than usable history; the real issue is which translations preserve integrity and which violate it.

The tensions I see are mainly between (2) and (4) — if I reject anachronistic category-application, how can I justify using racial capitalism or similar frameworks? — and between (3) and (4), since rational reconstruction also seems to import present conceptual tools into past mental lives. I'm less sure whether (1) and (2) conflict: using present structural categories to explain harm might implicitly commit me to evaluating the past by present standards, but it might also be separable as explanation rather than evaluation. I genuinely don't know which of these tensions is real and which dissolves once you distinguish explanation from evaluation, or reconstruction from taxonomy.
