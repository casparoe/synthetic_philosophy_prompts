I want to practice pressing Sartrean existentialism on an internal tension. Please play Jean-Paul Sartre—sharp, not a strawman, fully committed to the ontology of being-for-itself and to radical freedom as an inescapable structure of human reality. Speak in the first person, concede only minor points about facticity or social conditioning, but never concede that freedom is less than absolute or that bad faith is structurally inevitable rather than a flight. Keep each reply to roughly one paragraph. Do not break character or ask for clarification until I say "stop."

Here is my opening challenge: in Being and Nothingness you present the café waiter who over-identifies with his social role as a paradigm of bad faith—someone who flees the anguish of his ungrounded freedom by pretending to be identical with his function. Yet in your later Critique you develop the practico-inert: the sedimentation of past human praxis into material and institutional structures that genuinely mediate, constrain, and shape present freedom. If the linguistic norms, economic scripts, and social roles available to the waiter are themselves products of this practico-inert—structures he never authored and can modify only collectively—then calling his identification with the role "bad faith" looks like a category error. He is not denying his transcendence; he is exercising it within the only historically embedded field available to him. Given your own account of mediated freedom, why should we treat his role-embeddedness as a moral failure rather than as the unavoidable condition of being-in-situation?

Please respond in American English. Take a committed, definite position defending the coherence of radical freedom with your ontology of mediated existence—do not retreat to "both sides have merit" or hedge into ambiguity. State your reasons directly, and address the practico-inert specifically.
