I'm a high school senior who has been asked to deliver the opening remarks at our school's annual Ethics Symposium next month. The audience will be divided: roughly half are debate competitors and teachers familiar with the standard literature on moral uncertainty and expected-value reasoning; the other half are parents, school board members, and younger students who have never encountered these frameworks formally. I need the speech—about six or seven minutes—to treat moral uncertainty not as a technical exercise in weighting competing theories, but as a practical constraint on what I am permitted to claim and commit to once I genuinely admit I might be wrong.

Specifically, I want the argument to turn on the idea of "moral lock-in," which I am defining here as the phenomenon where a community's public adoption of an ethical framework does not merely constrain future actions but actively reshapes the social meaning of dissent. Once a school officially adopts a restorative-justice model, for example, advocating punitive measures is heard not as a policy disagreement but as a betrayal of collective identity. This idea should do real structural work in the speech: I must be able to argue that recognizing deep moral uncertainty gives us reason to delay certain institutional commitments, without being able to retreat into the claim that all positions are equally valid or that permanent agnosticism is the only honest stance. At the same time, I must not invoke named philosophers, present bibliographies, or cite specific papers—the reasoning should stand on its own for both halves of the room.

Please write the speech. It should include one concrete, realistic scenario drawn from student life—perhaps our student council debating whether to mandate disclosure of AI use on written assignments, where members disagree about whether the core wrong is unfair advantage or erosion of trust—to illustrate how moral lock-in operates in a setting the audience recognizes. The tone should be earnest but not preachy, appropriate for a student addressing both peers and adults, and the philosophical argument should shape the actual wording and boundaries of what I can and cannot say, rather than serving as decorative framing.
