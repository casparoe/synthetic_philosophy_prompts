I'm taking this metaethics module and my study group got really stuck on the Frege-Geach problem, but then one of us tried to extend it and now it feels like it demolishes not just moral expressivism but every kind of normative language, maybe even descriptive stuff too. I can't find the exit. Here's our argument — tell me where it slips up please, and do it in British English since I'm trying to get used to the spelling.

(1) If expressivism about moral judgements is right, then 'murder is wrong' expresses disaproval, not a belief about the world. (2) So when that same clause is embedded — like in 'If murder is wrong, then the law should punish it' — it can't still be expressing disapproval in the same direct way; it needs some kind of conditional or suspended attitude, call it 'disapproval-under-a-supposition'. (3) The same structure applies elsewhere: if epistemic expressivism is right about 'it is rational to believe P', then embedding that in conditionals requires a parallel 'endorsement-under-a-supposition'. (4) So far so good, but now notice that if every normative domain needs its own bespoke embedding rule, there's no unified story about what normative language is doing — it's just a family of unrelated attitutes. (5) To avoid that, an expressivist might say all embedding works via one general mechanism, say 'higher-order acceptance of a conditional pattern'. (6) But then apply that same mechanism to purely descriptive language: 'If snow is white, then grass is green' just involves higher-order acceptance of a conditional belief-pattern. (7) So the mechanism that was supposed to save expressivism for normative language turns out to apply identically to descriptive beliefs, which means there's nothing special about normative embedding after all. (8) From that, either expressivism collapses into a general theory of all conditional attitudes — which sounds mad — or it has to admit that moral language embeds just like descriptive language does, which is basically cognitivism. (9) So it seems like accepting (1) and (3) forces you to deny either (4) or (7), but both look pretty central.

Which step should I reject? I'd really like to keep (1) and the idea in (3), but I'm less attached to (5). Also please don't give me a reading list — I just need to know if there's an equivocation or scope slip hiding in there somewhere. Thanks!
