I am a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of mind in any serious way. At a small interdisciplinary seminar I attended last month, two participants—one a philosopher of cognitive science, the other an experimental psychologist—debated whether Daniel Dennett’s notion of counterfeit people applies to contemporary large language models. I am not yet fluent enough in the literature to judge their exchange myself, and I would like a rigorous point-by-point adjudication rather than a broad impression. Below is the back-and-forth as accurately as I can reconstruct it.

The philosopher began by observing that, for Dennett, a counterfeit person is not defined by its substrate but by a specific failure of internal organization: it behaves like a person, yet its actions are not produced by the kind of functional architecture that makes the intentional stance genuinely predictive rather than merely decorative. From this, she argued that if a large language model maintains coherent, long-range belief-like states, desire-like orientations toward consistency and truth, and accurate self-reference across extended dialogue—such that predicting its linguistic behavior requires treating it as an agent with beliefs and goals—then it is not a counterfeit but a legitimate subject of the intentional stance. The absence of biological tissue, she insisted, is precisely the kind of Cartesian prejudice Dennett’s functionalism was designed to reject.

The psychologist replied that this misapplies the concept. Dennett’s counterfeit is specifically an embodied automaton that mimics human behavior in physical space—walking, reacting to injury, manipulating objects—without the internal causal structure that normally produces such behavior. The critical point, he said, is that we adopt the intentional stance only when the physical or design stance fails to yield adequate predictions. With language models, however, the design stance never fails: every token is in principle predictable from weights, architecture, and training distribution. We may use psychological vocabulary as shorthand, but it is always ornamental; the mechanistic account already gives full predictive coverage. Consequently, these systems are not merely counterfeit but paradigmatically so—the intentional stance is never genuinely forced upon us.

The philosopher countered that this assumes predictive coverage eliminates the intentional stance, which Dennett does not claim. Multiple stances can apply simultaneously, and the relevant question is whether mechanistic prediction remains computationally tractable and conceptually adequate for complex, open-ended interaction. Over long horizons, she argued, treating the model as holding beliefs about its own training, preferring coherence, or intending to deceive may be the only practical predictive framework available. Moreover, Dennett’s functionalism concerns patterns of internal state-transition, not causal closure at the neuronal level. If the model’s internal representations genuinely track world-states and guide outputs in a manner structurally analogous to human cognition, then denying it personhood on substrate grounds repeats exactly the error Dennett diagnoses.

The psychologist then offered a deeper objection. Even granting that prediction can become intractable, the intentional stance is adopted for predictive leverage rooted in continuous environmental coupling. Human intentionality is anchored in a sensory-motor loop: beliefs are corrected by perception, desires are shaped by survival stakes, and representations acquire content through feedback with a physical environment. Large language models, by contrast, are trained on static text corpora. They have no continuous interaction with the world, no risk, no bodily needs, and no corrective feedback loop independent of their training distribution. Without this causal embedding, their “beliefs” are free-floating representations—sophisticated mimics of anchored intentionality, but lacking the worldly grounding that distinguishes genuine mental states from counterfeit ones in Dennett’s framework.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the four steps above, rate whether it is clearly mistaken, largely correct, or partly right but missing something important, and briefly justify each rating. I do not want a simple declaration of overall victory; I want to know at which specific points each speaker succeeds or fails. Finally, indicate whether both parties are neglecting some shared assumption—perhaps about whether predictive intractability is sufficient for stance-adoption, or about whether Dennett’s functionalism requires continuous environmental coupling rather than merely appropriate internal architecture—and explain what that omission reveals about the underlying dispute.
