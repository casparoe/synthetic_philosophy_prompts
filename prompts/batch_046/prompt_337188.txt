Read William Golding's Lord of the Flies through the debate between Christopher Browning's ordinary-men thesis—situation, peer pressure, and incremental role commitment explain perpetrator behavior—and Daniel Goldhagen's willing-executioners thesis—that deep cultural or characterological eliminationist ideology drives violence. Focus on the progression from the first pig-killing to Simon's death and Piggy's murder: do the boys act as ordinary children swept into violence by group dynamics and the absence of institutional constraint, or do they reveal a pre-cultural willingness to eliminate the other? Because Golding's subjects are pre-adolescent, without bureaucratic scaffolding or the specific antisemitic culture Goldhagen emphasizes, use the novel to probe a niche but crucial gap in both accounts—whether situational explanations scale downward to immature moral agents lacking fully formed character, and whether an eliminationist mindset can exist prior to the cultural indoctrination Goldhagen insists upon. Which scenes best support each reading, where does the novel resist being mapped onto either adult model, and what does Golding's choice of child perpetrators add to the philosophical debate that historical case studies alone cannot establish?
