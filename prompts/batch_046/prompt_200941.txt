In recent debates about the Black Death and the so-called Year Without a Summer following Tambora, historians have begun to treat Yersinia pestis and stratospheric sulphate aerosols not merely as background conditions but as something closer to protagonists. One analogy used to defend this shift compares integrating them into narrative history to widening the cast of a theatrical production: just as a director who treats the poison in the ear, the graveyard mud, and the pirates altering the prince’s voyage as mere scenery misses how the plot actually turns, so a historian who treats microbes and volcanic winters as passive backdrops misses the causal architecture of the past. The comparison is meant to licence a polyphonic historiography — one in which human societies and non-human forces share explanatory weight — without collapsing into the determinism that makes culture and politics merely epiphenomenal.

Audit this theatrical analogy precisely. Set out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the playwright, the human actors, the scenery-turned-character, the audience, and the script? Then locate the fractures: where does the analogy smuggle in intentionality, moral responsibility, or narrative coherence that microbial and atmospheric processes plainly lack? Distinguish the disanalogies that are merely decorative from those that threaten the historiographical conclusion — that is, does recognising the plague’s logistics actually require granting it something like agency, or can the work be done with a vocabulary of conditions and contingent conjunctions? Assess whether the conclusion survives once the theatrical framing is dismantled, and propose whether a different structural comparison — perhaps drawn from music, law, or architecture — might preserve the insight without anthropomorphising nature.
