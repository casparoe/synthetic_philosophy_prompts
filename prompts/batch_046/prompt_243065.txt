The debate over whether consciousness requires a Cartesian theater—some spatiotemporally localized stage where representations are presented to a unified subject—versus a multiple drafts architecture, in which competing representations are distributed across time and neural substrate with no central audience, has persisted in the philosophy of mind since its initial articulation. Proponents of the theater emphasize the phenomenological unity of a single present moment, the immediacy of first-person report, and the apparent fact that there is something it is like for an experience to occur at a specific now, arguing that distributed revision without a final presentation site leaves the very subject of experience unexplained. Defenders of the drafts model reply that no anatomically plausible center exists, that temporal smearing and retrospective reconstruction are empirically well-documented, and that the sense of a unified observer is itself a narrative artifact generated by post-hoc editorial processes rather than evidence for a central viewer. Despite these exchanges, neither side appears to shift: theater advocates treat distributed processing as irrelevant to the need for a subject, while draft theorists treat the demand for a subject as a residue of discredited metaphysics. I suspect the stall reflects a hidden divergence in explanatory targets—one side asking how cognition produces a coherent narrative for action and report, the other asking what conditions make an experience genuinely mine in a single moment—but I am unsure whether this diagnosis is correct, whether the disagreement is instead verbal over the term 'subject,' or whether an unresolved empirical question about the temporal dynamics of reportability and retrospective coherence is actually doing the work. Diagnose the structure of this impasse using what each position actually says about representation, temporal integration, and the conditions for first-person authority. Is there a formal or empirical result—perhaps concerning the binding window for retrospective coherence or the neural dynamics of report-generation—that could resolve it, or does progress require a conceptual distinction that separates the architecture of cognition from the logic of ownership? And if neither path is available, does the persistence of the disagreement indicate that the original question is ill-posed, perhaps because it forces a false dichotomy between localized presentation and distributed construction?
