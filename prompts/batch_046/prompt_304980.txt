I'm giving the retirement toast for my mentor, Dr. Elena Voss, who taught phenomenology for thirty years and tagged monarchs on weekends in the Sierra foothills. Half the room are grad students who sat through her seminars on embodied cognition; the other half are family who mostly know she loved butterflies and hated platitudes. I want the toast built around Zhuangzi's butterfly dream, but not as decoration—structurally. She always argued that people misread it as Cartesian doubt, when really the text is about the joy of not knowing whether you're the observer or the observed, and that the Chinese word for transformation (hua) is the same one used for chemical change, so identity is more like a reaction than a container. That idea should shape what I can and can't say: I can't claim she's watching over us, or that she lives on through her students, or that she turned into something eternal, because she was explicit that she found all that false and a bit embarrasing. Write it so the philosophers in the room recognize the argument and the family feels genuinely comforted, not lectured. Keep it to about five mintues. Start with the toast itself—no preface, no "here's what I'm going to say."
