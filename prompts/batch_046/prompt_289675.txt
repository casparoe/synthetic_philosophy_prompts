Consider the standard scoring rules used in protected-area management: success is measured by trophic completeness, habitat connectivity, and native-species persistence, never by the net experiential welfare of the animals inside the system. Managers will reject reducing predator numbers to lower prey suffering, reject culling overabundant herbivores even when starvation mortality is visibly high, and reject shrinking a protected boundary even if the smaller range would reduce the total number of individuals born into suffering—yet they will often justify excluding invasive species on the grounds that those species disrupt “natural” suffering dynamics, without explaining why those dynamics are valuable. Reconstruct the implicit theory of value that makes this combination coherent. Does the practice strictly presuppose that ecosystems or species have intrinsic worth, or only that individual welfare is incommensurable with ecological structure? Can it be reconciled with a view that wild-animal suffering is real but administratively intractable? And if the underlying framework were replaced by one that treats each sentient life as carrying direct moral weight—without denying ecological facts—what specific management rules would have to change, and which parts of the practice could remain intact?
