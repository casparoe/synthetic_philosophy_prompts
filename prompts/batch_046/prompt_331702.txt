Design a diagnostic decision tree that sorts positions in the debate between methodological individualism and methodological holism in the social sciences. Build the tree from three binary distinctions presented below, treating them as logically prior to any named school or author.

First distinction—Ontological reducibility: Are macro-social properties (institutional norms, market equilibria, cultural frames, collective intentionality) fully reducible to properties of individuals plus their pairwise relations, or do they possess a form of ontological autonomy that is not captured by aggregation?

Second distinction—Explanatory reversibility: May macro-level variables enter genuine causal explanations of micro-level behavior (downward explanation) without being reducible to summaries of micro-level mechanisms, or is all legitimate social-scientific explanation ultimately unidirectional, running from individuals upward?

Third distinction—Compositional asymmetry: In explaining a collective outcome, is it permissible to cite macro-structural conditions (e.g., legal frameworks, resource distributions, network topologies) without decomposing them into individual intentions, provided one does not claim those conditions are causally efficacious independently of the individuals who instantiate them?

The full logical space yields eight combinations (2 × 2 × 2). Map the following representative positions into this space: classical Weberian methodological individualism; analytical sociology of the Hedström variety; Durkheimian structural functionalism; and critical realism in the Bhaskar tradition. Then identify any unoccupied cells. For each empty cell, diagnose whether the combination is incoherent (the commitments contradict), unattractive (coherent but defensible only at high theoretical cost), or unexplored (logically available but lacking prominent advocates).

Next, apply the tree to three concrete explanatory targets: (1) the rapid, geographically dispersed adoption of a drought-resistant crop variety that spread faster than individual-to-individual diffusion models predict; (2) the persistence of large racial wealth disparities decades after the removal of formal legal barriers to accumulation; and (3) a systemic financial crisis that emerged from individually rational risk-management strategies. For each target, state which branch of the tree provides the most natural explanation, and show why an adjacent branch—one that differs on exactly one distinction—would be forced either to dismiss the macro-pattern as an aggregation artifact or to invoke downward causation that violates its own commitments.

The instrument should classify respondents by their answers to the distinctions and to the concrete cases, not by which label they endorse. Do not provide bibliographies, author biographies, or citation lists.
