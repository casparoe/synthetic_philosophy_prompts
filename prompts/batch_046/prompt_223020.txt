I've been drafting an essay for a seminar on the moral economy of time, and I'd like your comments on the conceptual architecture rather than the prose. The core claim is that the Protestant work ethic, the institutionalized 35-hour week, and the Japanese phenomenon of karoshi are not simply points on a spectrum of labor intensity, but three distinct ontologies of leisure—each defining what counts as rightful rest by the negative space it leaves around work.

The first section reconstructs Weber's argument not as a celebration of industriousness but as a theological anxiety about wasted time, where leisure is morally dangerous precisely because it is unobservable: one cannot demonstrate election through rest. I then contrast this with the post-war French 35-hour week, which I treat not as a welfare concession but as an administrative invention of recoverable time—time that belongs to the worker but must be accounted for in aggregate, turning leisure into a quantifiable counter-weight to production.

The twist I want to introduce—this is where I'm least sure—is to read karoshi (death from overwork) through this lens not as excessive labor but as a collapse of the boundary that makes leisure legible. In Japanese office culture, the empty desk—remaining at one's post with no tasks—functions as a kind of negative labor: presence without productivity, sustained by a collective refusal to authorize departure. I want to argue that karoshi reveals a hidden premise in both Weber and the 35-hour framework: that leisure requires a socially recognized off switch. Without it, the right to leisure becomes meaningless because rest cannot be performed or witnessed. So the essay asks: is there a coherent right to leisure that does not depend on a prior institutional grammar of absence?

I'd appreciate comments on whether this mapping holds—particularly whether I'm mischaracterizing the 35-hour week as an administrative ontology rather than a labor victory, and whether the comparison between Weberian unobservable grace and Japanese unobservable departure is illuminating or merely analogical. If the framework feels forced, which part should I drop?
