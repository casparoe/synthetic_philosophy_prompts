I'm giving the opening remarks at the retirement dinner of Margaret Chen, who spent thirty-five years in regional welfare administration, rising to direct a large workfare and benefits-integrity office. The room is split: on one side are her colleagues — policy analysts, frontline assessors, and a few academic social-policy specialists who know the literature on conditionality and fraud detection inside out; on the other are her family, neighbours, and former school friends who have never filed a claim and for whom terms like 'sanction' and 'undeserving poor' are largely abstract.

I want a speech of about four minutes that does not merely decorate the occasion with a philosophical reference, but is actually structured by a conceptual distinction I want you to introduce: the difference between 'moral bookkeeping' and 'actuarial bookkeeping' in welfare regimes. By moral bookkeeping I mean the institutional practice of maintaining a visible, ritualised ledger of blame and innocence — the demand that claimants repeatedly perform their lack of culpability so that the state can display that benefits go only to the undeserving-no-more. By actuarial bookkeeping I mean the purely risk-management logic of preventing fraud and error, which needs no public theatre of moral worth. The speech should use this distinction to navigate what can and cannot be said in honouring Margaret: it must avoid both the family-friendly but false narrative that she was simply 'helping people' and the expert-only but cruel narrative that she spent her career policing the undeserving poor. Instead, it should argue — gently, for the family, precisely, for the colleagues — that her career was lived inside a system that systematically conflated these two ledgers, and that the real harm of the 'undeserving poor' rhetoric is not stigma alone but the corruption of administrative reason itself.

Please write in British English, keep it suitable for a spoken address, and include a brief concluding note in which you state how confident you are in the framing and identify the single point where you are least sure — specifically, whether the distinction between moral and actuarial bookkeeping holds up under historical scrutiny of British welfare reform since the 1980s, and where the speech takes liberties with that history for rhetorical clarity.
