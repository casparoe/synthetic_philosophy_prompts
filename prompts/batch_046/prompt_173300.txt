I'm drafting an essay on whether virtual harms are real in persistent virtual worlds, centered on a conceptual distinction I'm calling "projective commitment structures." The core claim is that virtual objects have a "thin" ontology — they exist only as functional nodes within a rule-governed system — but certain virtual harms can still be "thick" because they destroy long-term, normatively structured achievements that are not reducible to real-world property loss or mere psychological disappointment.

The essay develops this through a specific case: in a persistent multiplayer world with no real-money trading, a player spends hundreds of hours crafting a unique procedural instrument that other players rely on for collaborative performances. Another player exploits a known glitch to corrupt the instrument's data irreversibly. No physical damage occurs, no contract is broken, and while emotional distress may follow, that is not the primary harm being analyzed. I argue that the harm is genuinely virtual yet real: it annihilates a structured capability — the instrument's functional role in an emergent social practice — that existed within the world's normative framework. I contrast this with "avatar-mediated harm," where the virtual event is only a conduit for real-world injury.

The essay then addresses two objections. First, that I am smuggling in real-world value by treating the player's time-investment as a proxy for labor-value. Second, that if virtual objects are only functional nodes, destroying one is no different from capturing a chess piece — it is just a permitted state-change within rules. I reply that chess captures occur within competitive norms that anticipate loss, whereas this corruption violates the cooperative norms that made the instrument's existence meaningful, making it closer to vandalism than to gameplay.

Please comment on these notes and the line of argument. In particular, I want feedback on whether the distinction between "thin ontology" and "thick harm" holds up, whether "projective commitment structures" does genuine philosophical work or merely relabels familiar notions of social practice, and whether my reply to the chess analogy succeeds. Please separate what is settled or widely agreed upon in the philosophy of virtual worlds from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any contested position — including my own — as if it were the consensus view.
