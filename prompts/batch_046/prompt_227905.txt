A friend and I have been arguing at length about whether Rawls's separateness of persons objection genuinely undermines utilitarianism, and she keeps returning to an analogy I have not been able to dismiss cleanly: she says that aggregating welfare across individuals is like the way a central bank treats separate vault safes—each safe holds assets, but the institution's only legitimate concern is total reserves across the vault, not which safe contains what. If gold can be moved from safe A to safe B without loss, no wrong is done to either safe, and complaining that safe A was "depleted" is just mistaking a bookkeeping entry for a moral harm. From this she concludes that insisting on the "separateness" of persons makes the same mistake: welfare is the gold, persons are the safes, society is the vault, and redistribution is merely internal reallocation within one moral economy, not a harm to anyone. Please audit this analogy with precision. First, lay out the explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the safe, the gold, the vault, the bank's ledger, the act of moving assets, and the claim of wrong done. Then identify where the mapping breaks: can experienced well-being be "moved" like gold, or is it necessarily destroyed in one location and recreated in another? Do safes have interests or standing in a way that vaults do not, and does the analogy silently import an "ownership model of well-being"—that a person owns her utility the way a safe owns its gold—that is itself contested or question-begging? Distinguish the disanalogies that actually matter for her conclusion—that separateness is an administrative fiction rather than a moral constraint—from those that are merely decorative. Say whether a better analogy (perhaps one involving irreducibly first-person experience, or one comparing persons to branches rather than containers) would do the work more honestly, and whether her conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Finally, please state clearly what would change your mind: which argument or consideration—such as a defensible model of interpersonal fungibility, or a concrete case in which burden-shifting clearly differs from asset-shifting—would lead you to a different verdict about whether the analogy carries her conclusion, and identify where your own reasoning about its strength is most likely to fail.
