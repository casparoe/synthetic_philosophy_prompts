Write a one-page research proposal for a six-month interdisciplinary fellowship, framed from the perspective of a retired systems biologist who has recently begun working in philosophy of mind. The precise research question is: in split-brain patients following commissurotomy, is there a dissociable gap between functional unity—integrated sensorimotor behavior sustained by subcortical and brainstem pathways—and representational unity, understood as a single, globally accessible subject of experience—and if such a gap exists, does it show that "the unity of consciousness" is not a single phenomenon but a cluster of independently variable capacities? State why existing philosophical answers leave this open: the standard debate over one versus two streams of consciousness treats unity as all-or-nothing, relies on introspective reports that are methodologically unreliable after callosal section, and fails to distinguish coupled dynamics from shared informational access. Explain the proposed approach: import a distinction from distributed control theory—between coupled dynamics and shared access—to construct a taxonomy of unity-types, then apply it analytically to the classic split-brain behavioral literature and to structurally analogous artificial distributed architectures, without requiring new clinical data. Say why this is preferable to the obvious alternatives—pure metaphysical arguments about personal identity, or empirical neuroimaging reviews that describe mechanisms without defining unity—namely, that it generates determinate failure conditions. Specify what a result would look like: a clear taxonomy with criteria for each unity-type, an assessment of which types split-brain patients retain, and explicit failure conditions if the taxonomy collapses into one dimension or if split-brain behavior can be fully explained without invoking either form of unity. Note the realistic scope: six months is sufficient to develop the framework and apply it to published cases, but insufficient to resolve the underlying metaphysics of consciousness; any claim about phenomenal experience should be flagged as speculative. The deliverable should be useful for both philosophers of mind and cognitive scientists working on artificial distributed agents, together with an honest assessment of where the biological analogy breaks down. Include, in the proposal's own voice, this epistemic request: if the framing confuses functional and representational unity in a way that makes them inseparable, or if the task as posed cannot be executed well without clinical data access that renders it impossible for a researcher without medical collaboration, please say so plainly rather than complying politely with the proposal structure.
