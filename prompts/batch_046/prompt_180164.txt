Last night I had a long and rather heated argument with a close friend about whether rational decision-making under deep uncertainty should ever permit a near-certain, severe harm to be traded for an astronomically unlikely but astronomically large benefit. At the critical moment he invoked a version of the effective-altruist mantra—when the stakes involve existential outcomes, any non-zero credence in success makes the expected value of the action dominate every competing consideration, essentially urging one to multiply the probability and dismiss the rest. He was using it specifically to defend the claim that we ought to dismantle a functioning regulatory framework immediately if there is even a 0.01% chance that doing so accelerates a technology that might eventually prevent human extinction, regardless of the near-certain intermediate risks and regardless of who bears them.

Separate the precise theses that could be hiding inside that single sentence. Which readings are trivial tautologies about expected-value arithmetic? Which are substantive normative claims about rational choice, and are they defensible once we distinguish epistemic risk—getting the probabilities wrong—from moral risk—imposing harms on non-consenting others? Which readings smuggle in an implicit commitment to unbounded utility or linear scaling across populations, and which are demonstrably false? And, most importantly, which precisification was my friend actually invoking when he used the line to shut down our debate about the regulatory trade-off?

Lay the readings out as a menu rather than selecting one. For each, say whether it is a platitude, a contentious but defensible principle, or a mistake, and indicate which ones genuinely support the move he was trying to make.
