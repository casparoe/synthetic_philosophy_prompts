I’m a PhD student in philosophy of mind running a workshop for about 14 upper-level undergrads, 90 minutes. I want an in-class activity or game that makes them actually feel the paradox of fiction—not just talk about it. Specifically, I want them to run into the problem that you can know something is made-up and still have a real emotional reaction, but the rules should force them to try to keep those two things in seperate boxes and see where the boxes leak.

Here’s the twist I want built in: instead of treating the emotional response as just a quarantined belief, I want the activity to hinge on counterfactual proximity—like, the intensity of the affect tracks how close the fictional situation is to something that could actually happen in our world, not whether we think it’s real. So participants should construct short fictional vignettes, assign emotional weights, and then vote or score each other, but the payoff depends on them being consistent about both the epistemic status (fake) and the affective weight (real grief, fear, etc.). The rules need to create a situation where consistency collapses when the counterfactual distance gets either too small or too large.

Design the rules, any roles or cards, materials (just paper and pens, maybe index cards), a scoring scheme with no real money but real tension, a 90-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps the collapse moments onto the philosophical structure. Be frank about which feature—probably the actual phenomenology of judging modal proximity—this can’t reproduce and I’ll have to supply in discussion. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first run—like people resolving the tension by just downgrading the emotion or pretending they don’t care—and how to handle it without just lecturing them.

No citations, no reading lists, and don’t make them name-drop philosophers. Let them discover the structure. Sorry for the messy framing, I’m writing this for a dataset and there might be a typo or two.
