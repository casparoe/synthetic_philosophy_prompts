I'm prepping a session for my philosophy of economics grad course and I'm stuck on how to make Aumann-style agreement results feel live rather than like a logic puzzle they've already solved. I want my students to wrestle with the no-trade literature—especially the idea that rational agents with a common prior can't agree to disagree—but I need them to see where the formalism actually touches dirt.

Could you write an analysis that takes the January 2021 GameStop squeeze and runs it through that lens? Not the finance mechanics, I don't care about gamma exposre or whatever—I'm interested in the epistemic structure. Treat the retail crowd, the institutional shorts, and the market makers as distinct agents with different belief-formation mechanisms. Did they share a common prior about GME's value, or was the prior itself being manufatured in real time through the price movement and the social media loop? There's this idea I ran across—maybe in some recent social epistemology, I can't place the author—that in speculative bubbles the market doesn't just reveal disagreement but actively produces it. I want that idea injected into the analysis seriously, not as a throwaway.

The deliverable should be a disciplined case study: map the players, map what counts as evidence (the stock price? the options flow? the meme discourse?), say exactly where Aumann's conditions are violated and wether that saves the theorem or makes it irrelevant, and flag where the theory just goes silent. Don't let it collapse into 'markets are irrational'—I want them to see wether the squeeze is an anomaly for the no-trade result or actually a confirmation once you model the prior correctly. And please don't ask them for citations or a reading list—this is a closed-book exercise and I want them reasoning from the concept, not hunting references.
