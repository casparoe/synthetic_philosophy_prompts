I am a doctoral researcher in the philosophy of cultural heritage, currently advising a national statutory conservation body. I need a reusable decision procedure that translates the metaphysical criteria emerging from the Ship of Theseus debate—specifically the conditions under which an object persists through the replacement or dispersal of its parts—into an operational protocol that a non-philosopher heritage compliance officer can apply when classifying reconstructed monuments for legal protection status.

The officer makes these determinations approximately twice a month, often with dossiers that will be scrutinised in judicial review, and typically has no more than forty minutes per case. The procedure must therefore function without reference to specialist literature or external consultation, must distinguish mechanical steps from irreducible judgement calls, and must be honest about where it will misfire. Present it as an ordered sequence of tests—effectively a flowchart in prose—with explicit failure conditions at each stage and clear marking of the points where weighting rather than derivation is required.

Conceptually, the procedure must preserve and separately weigh three distinctions that heritage practice usually collapses: (1) material continuity (spatiotemporal persistence of original substance); (2) formal or architectural continuity (preservation of design geometry and relational structure among parts); and (3) indexical commemorative continuity (whether the object still stands in the appropriate causal and symbolic relation to the specific historical event it marks). I also want you to inject a further criterion—call it teleological inversion—applicable when a reconstruction employs substantial original material and retains the original form, yet now serves a commemorative or political purpose diametrically opposed to that of the original monument. The procedure must specify how much weight this inversion should carry relative to the other three criteria, and must flag precisely where that weighting is a normative judgement rather than a consequence of the identity conditions themselves.

Please apply the procedure in full to three cases: (a) a medieval civic bridge destroyed in conflict and rebuilt using approximately seventy per cent original stone arranged in the original arch geometry, now used for commemorative rituals that invert its original civic function; (b) a monument from which every original fragment has been recovered and reassembled with modern structural binding, but situated ten metres from its original footprint in a redesigned landscape; and (c) a site containing no surviving original material, yet treated for centuries by continuous local practice as being numerically identical to the destroyed original. One of these applications should yield a result that a sympathetic heritage officer would find unwelcome—identify which one, and explain what the procedure cannot do to prevent that outcome.

Finally, state clearly what this protocol is unable to settle: which disputes it merely relocates rather than resolves, what kinds of expertise it still tacitly requires despite being designed for generalists, and under what conditions it should be suspended in favour of direct legislative or community decision. Please write the answer in British English.
