I work as a senior software engineer and have recently been asked to chair the architecture review board at my company, a mid-sized firm that builds logistics-coordination software. Every quarter we evaluate competing system-redesign proposals, and I have noticed a recurring pattern: sometimes two teams disagree about implementation details but clearly share the same underlying goals and success criteria, while at other times they appear to talk past each other—using words like scalability or robustness with incompatible operational definitions, and treating entirely different past systems as the canonical exemplars of good engineering. I encountered Kuhn’s ideas about paradigm shifts and incommensurability in a popular essay, and because I have no formal training in philosophy of science I need this translated into a practical, reusable decision procedure that a non-specialist can actually run in a meeting without appealing to authority.

Please construct an ordered decision procedure—framed as a flowchart in words, with explicit questions, sufficient-answer criteria, and clear stopping conditions—that our board can apply to any disputed proposal pair. The procedure should distinguish normal puzzle-solving disagreements (soluble within a shared framework of values, metrics, and reference implementations) from genuine incommensurability, and it should test for three layers that I have begun to sketch but do not fully understand: lexical divergence (shared vocabulary with different operational definitions), exemplar divergence (different canonical implementations treated as authoritative), and metric divergence (success measured along dimensions that cannot be jointly optimized). For each layer, specify what evidence counts as sufficient, where the procedure terminates with a recommendation to proceed via standard technical arbitration, and where it must hand the decision to leadership because the conflict is one of values rather than engineering facts. Be explicit about where the procedure is likely to misfire—especially when one team is genuinely conservative and the other genuinely innovative but they still share a framework, or when superficial vocabulary differences mask deep agreement. Then run the procedure on three cases: (A) replacing our central relational database with an event-sourced architecture, where proponents cite large-scale event-log systems as the gold-standard exemplar and opponents cite our own legacy transaction system; (B) abandoning conventional unit-testing in favor of exhaustive formal verification, where the sides disagree on whether correctness means empirical fault-rate reduction or mathematical proof coverage; and (C) a migration to a service-mesh API gateway that I personally favor, but where the procedure classifies the dispute as incommensurable and recommends escalation, which I find frustrating because I believe it is merely a hard technical puzzle. In that third case, report the exact output of the procedure and explain why I should still respect its verdict even if I disagree.

In your response, please separate what is well-established in Kuhn’s original account from what derives from my invented three-layer model or from contested interpretations of incommensurability—particularly regarding whether metric divergence is a necessary feature of paradigm clashes or only a common symptom. Do not present a disputed philosophical position as if it were the consensus view among people who study this topic.
