I'm presenting a paper at a philosophy-psychology joint seminar defending a specific form of rational epistemic akrasia. My thesis is that an agent can rationally maintain a first-order belief p despite her second-order judgment that the evidence does not support p, provided she possesses reliable third-order evidence that her second-order assessment is systematically corrupted—specifically, by an overcorrection bias that arises in peer disagreement. I argue this avoids both bootstrapping and the charge of mere stubbornness, because the agent is not privileging her first-order reasoning but discounting a distorted meta-level evaluation. The audience is roughly half mainstream analytic epistemologists steeped in the peer-disagreement and higher-order-evidence literature, a quarter decision theorists who will treat belief as credence and worry about Dutch-book incoherence, and a quarter philosophers of mind who work on self-trust and calibration bias. They are broadly skeptical of "special pleading" for rational irrationality and will press hard on whether my view collapses into standard conciliationism or into an unprincipled refusal to update.

Prepare my Q&A defense sheet: list the objections I will actually face from this audience, ordered from most likely and damaging to least, phrased in the vocabulary each subgroup would use (epistemologists, decision theorists, philosophers of mind). For each objection, give a concise reply I can deliver in under two minutes, mark the weakest point in that reply, and note what I should concede versus hold firm. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks—likely from the decision-theoretic side—and sketch how to survive it if it comes. Keep the sheet focused on the argument structure rather than bibliographic references; I will not have access to texts during the session.
