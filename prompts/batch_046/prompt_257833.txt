You have been asked to deliver the opening address at a university symposium marking the founding of its ethics program, held in a lecture hall named for Benjamin Franklin. The audience is deliberately split: on one side sit historians of philosophy and scholars of early American thought who know Franklin’s Autobiography in detail—who recall that his scheme for moral perfection listed thirteen virtues tracked with black spots in a small book divided by days of the week, that he ordered them by ease of acquisition rather than by metaphysical importance (placing temperance first because it aided the rest), that he eventually admitted he found himself far fuller of faults than he had imagined, and that his thirteenth virtue—humility, modeled on Jesus and Socrates—collapsed into a kind of performative pride that made the scheme self-defeating; on the other side sit benefactors, alumni, and students who know Franklin mainly as the archetype of American self-improvement and practical virtue. The organizers want an eight-minute speech in which Franklin’s actual project does structural work, not decorative work. This means you must not present him as a success story of quantifiable self-mastery, nor can you invoke a transcendent moral standard to excuse his failure, since his list was explicitly pragmatic and non-metaphysical. You must explain, for the non-specialists, why his method of counting failures in a personal ledger is philosophically different from modern algorithmic self-tracking, and you must conclude with a precise claim about whether moral perfection can be pursued at all without a prior, complete specification of what perfection consists in—a tension Franklin lived but never resolved. Write the address so that it respects the experts without turning into a lecture, and lands for the general audience without falsifying Franklin’s own skeptical conclusions about his scheme.
