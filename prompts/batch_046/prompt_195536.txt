I was discussing care ethics with a friend over coffee yesterday — not the standard Gilligan/Noddings intro material, but whether attentiveness can be outsourced to sensors without losing its moral character. I'm writing a short story for a workshop and want it to actually wrestle with the concept instead of just showing a kind nurse. The setting: in 2047, the Lyon-7 district runs eldercare through a Proximity Ledger. Residents earn 'care credits' when apartment sensors detect biometrc distress spikes nearby and they physically respond; they lose credits for ignoring signals. The system anonymizes the data but geolocates it, so you know someone on your floor is suffering but not who until you arrive. The protagonist, Mara, is a care auditor who reviews edge cases. She notices her mother — three floors up — has been emitting sub-threshold distress patterns for weeks, filtered out because they don't match the 'acute crisis' alogrithm. Mara can manually override and spend her own credits to investigate, but a false override will penalize her and train the system to distrust her future judgments. I want the story to ask whether this quantified responsiveness is still 'care' in the relational sense — is attentiveness moral when it's tracked and scored? — and whether Mara's obligation to her mother differs structurally from her obligation to strangers on the ledger. Dont make it a simple morality tale; let the ending stay ambiguous about whether the ledger corrupts care or just exposes how fragile it always was. Keep it around 800 words.
