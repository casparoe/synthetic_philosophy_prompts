I was arguing with a friend about Locke's Some Thoughts Concerning Educaiton, and we ended up with this argument that seems to prove Locke's whole program collapses into pure conditioning with no space left for reason. Here are the steps we wrote out:

(1) Locke is a strict empiricist — no innate ideas, no innate practical principles, the mind starts as a blank slate.
(2) In the educaiton treatise he treats virtue and vice as nothing more than names for actions that happen to be linked, through repetition and praise, to pleasure and pain.
(3) So if moral education works at all, it works only by manipulating assocaitions until the child feels pleasure at what we call good behavior.
(4) But Locke also insists that the end of education is rational autonomy: the child should eventually govern himself by reason rather than by fear or desire.
(5) If every moral motivation is ultimately an associative pleasure/pain link, then even the adult's "rational" decision is just the stronger association winning out.
(6) That means Locke's ideal of self-governance by reason is an illusion on his own premises — we can train well-behaved people, but never genuinely rational moral agents.
(7) So Locke's theory proves too much: it eliminates the very rational autonomy it claims to cultivate.

We both want to hold onto (1), and I'd rather keep (4) than (5) if I have to choose, but I'm not sure where the real damage is. Is the mistake in (2), treating Locke's associationism as exhausting moral motivation? Or in (5), sliding from "associations influence choice" to "reason is nothing but association"? Or am I smuggling in an Aristotelian hexis where Locke only has mechanical habit, and there's actually a hidden quantifier shift between how habits work in children versus adults? Tell me which step to reject and what giving it up costs Locke's broader picture.
