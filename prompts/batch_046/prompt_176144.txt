I hold the view that the moral weight we should assign to harming a video-game character depends not on the character's simulated psychological depth, representational fidelity, or narrative prominence, but on whether the game structures the harm within what I will call a "narrative economy of irreversible consequence" — a design framework in which damaging or killing an NPC permanently alters questlines, world states, or other characters' behavior in ways the player cannot undo through reload, quicksave, or respawn, and in which the game explicitly registers the loss through mourning dialogue, changed environments, or permanently locked content. By contrast, characters in games built around loop-based resets, roguelike runs, or sandbox respawns are structurally positioned as disposable, and harming them generates at most a tactical error, not a genuine moral wrong. What would have to be true for this view to be wrong? I want an honest map of its exposure: which observations about player psychology, which design counterexamples (for instance, highly responsive procedural characters in fully resettable worlds), and which philosophical arguments about moral status or agency should count as decisive defeaters versus merely awkward adjustments; what defenders of this position should themselves concede as genuinely damaging; whether the view makes distinctive predictions that rival accounts — such as those grounded purely in emotional attachment or in the character's internal representational complexity — do not; and whether it risks being qualified away, say by retreating to "it applies only when the player actually feels regret," into something unfalsifiable, and if so whether that insulation is a defect for a view of this kind.
