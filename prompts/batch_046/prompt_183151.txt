In philosophy of mind and action, the standard analogy for self-deception treats it as interpersonal deception folded inward: one cognitive subsystem plays the con artist, another plays the mark, with the former suppressing evidence the latter would otherwise accept. This mapping is routinely used to motivate the paradox—if the deceiver knows the truth, the deceived must remain ignorant within the same mind, which seems to require a partitioned psychology and makes self-deception conceptually unstable. I want an explicit audit: give the correspondence table—what plays the role of the con artist, the mark, the hidden evidence, the payoff, and the exposure—then isolate the disanalogies that matter for the possibility debate, such as shared doxastic ownership, the absence of an external audience, and the lack of a clear success condition. Does the skeptical conclusion—that self-deception is impossible, or at least incoherent—survive once these breaks are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the argument all along? If the analogy collapses, can a model of motivated reasoning without representational partition preserve the phenomena without importing the paradox?
