I run a philosophy workshop for advanced undergraduates and graduate students—about 18 participants, one 90-minute session in a seminar room with movable chairs and only paper materials available. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel, rather than merely discuss, the tension in the omnipotence paradox when it is framed not as a physical challenge but as a problem of self-binding constitutional authority. The specific idea I want them to encounter is that an agent with genuinely unlimited power to establish rules should be able to establish a rule that permanently restricts its own future power; yet if it succeeds in binding itself, its power was never truly unlimited, and if it fails, there was a hidden limit all along. I want the rules and roles of the activity itself to generate this circularity before anyone names it.

Please specify: the exact roles (including one or more hidden-information positions), the round structure, any materials distributed, and a scoring or recognition scheme that uses social status rather than real money so that participants have a genuine incentive to defend or challenge the sovereign’s moves. The debrief should map specific moments in the play onto parts of the logical structure—the initial grant of unlimited rule-making authority, the attempt to issue an irrevocable self-restriction, the failure or success of that act, and the distinction between a task being logically impossible and an agent being genuinely limited. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, such as participants collapsing the paradox into a simple contradiction and missing the constitutional-analogy layer, and how to handle that without turning the session into a lecture. Finally, be frank about which feature of omnipotence the activity cannot reproduce—for instance, the theological context of essential properties versus contingent powers—and therefore must be supplied by me in the closing ten minutes.
