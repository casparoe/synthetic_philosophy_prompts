As a retired molecular biologist who has recently begun studying philosophy of mind, I am drafting an essay that argues functionalism’s thesis of multiple realizability ought to be reconceptualized through the biological phenomenon of degeneracy—the well-documented capacity of structurally distinct lower-level elements (genetic sequences, ion channels, metabolic pathways) to yield the same functional outcome. Rather than treating mental states as analogous to software running on different hardware, I propose that mental kinds are better understood as degenerate biological traits: genuinely multiply realizable, yet constrained by evolutionary and physical substrate conditions that pure logical functionalism tends to ignore. In my sketch, I suggest that this “degenerate functionalist” picture might resolve the tension between mental causation and the causal closure of physics, because mental properties could then be construed as causally efficacious higher-order patterns instantiated through diverse mechanisms, rather than as epiphenomenal summaries of those mechanisms. I present this not as an established view but as a tentative analogy drawn from my former field.

I would welcome your comments on the coherence of this analogy—particularly whether genetic degeneracy genuinely maps onto mental-state realization or whether the comparison breaks down at critical points—on whether my proposed resolution of the exclusion problem succeeds or merely relabels it, and on whether this position collapses into a form of non-reductive physicalism that abandons what is distinctive about functionalism. Please distinguish clearly between what is settled in philosophy of mind and neuroscience, and what remains genuinely contested among specialists; do not present any disputed interpretation—such as the status of mental causation, the adequacy of functional explanation, or the implications of biological degeneracy for metaphysics—as if it were the consensus view.
