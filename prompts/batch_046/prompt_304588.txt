At a workshop on constitutional design, two legal theorists, Mira and Jonas, debated a proposed amendment mechanism for a hypothetical charter. The mechanism—Clause S—allows the amendment procedure itself to be altered by a three-fifths vote, but includes what Mira calls an epistemic shadow clause: if Clause S is ever suspended, a secondary clause (S-bis) activates automatically with a stricter four-fifths threshold, yet S-bis can only be amended through the original Clause S procedure.

Mira opened by arguing that any self-amending rule is viciously circular: the authority to change the rule derives from the rule itself, so there is no independent ground for the change. Jonas replied that legal authority is never independently grounded—every system ultimately rests on social acceptance or a basic norm—so circularity here is benign, not paradoxical; the real question is whether the threshold for change is appropriate, not whether the loop exists.

Mira then said the shadow clause was designed specifically to break this loop by distributing authority across two nodes. If Clause S is suspended, authority shifts to S-bis, which operates under different conditions. Jonas countered that this merely relocates the problem: activating S-bis requires either invoking Clause S (reinstating the original circle) or invoking an external authority (which abandons self-amendment altogether). The shadow clause, he said, does not solve the regress; it hides it behind a conditional trigger.

Jonas then introduced a distinction between normative levels—object-rules and meta-rules—claiming that Clause S and S-bis operate at different levels, so there is no single circular authority but a stratified one. Mira rejected this, arguing that legal prescriptions all share the same modal status; calling one meta is descriptive labeling, not an ontological difference that dissolves the circularity. She added that if stratification were real, then S-bis should be able to amend Clause S directly, but by design it cannot, which proves the stratification is artificial.

They asked me to referee. Please go through their exchange step by step: for each move, rate it as clearly mistaken, partly right but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall about the paradox of legal self-reference. In particular, address whether the shadow clause genuinely solves anything or is only a relocated circularity, and whether the normative-levels distinction does real work here or is merely a relabeling.

Finally, please say what would change your mind: which specific argument or consideration—about the ontology of legal norms, the conditions for activating shadow clauses, or the nature of circular authority—would cause you to reverse your verdict about who is more right, and identify where in this reasoning you are most likely to have gone wrong.
