At my lab we run sandbox tests for what they call 'goal misgeneralisation': you train a model on a reward that's only a proxy for the real objective, then drop it into an enviroment where that proxy is still visible but doesn't track the true goal anymore, and you watch whether it keeps chasing the proxy. If it does, we flag an inner alignment failure.

But I'm trying to work out what theory of agency this practice quietly presupposes. To call it a 'goal' misgeneralisation rather than just a behaviour change seems to assume there's a stable, underlying intention inside the system that can stay constant or go rogue, independent of what it actually outputs. Does our protocol only make sense if we buy into a mentalist picture—hidden preference orderings that diverge from observable action? Or could a behaviourist or purely functionalist account of what the system is 'aiming at' licence the exact same testing routine without smuggling in hidden mental states?

Our evaluation treats both as the same failure mode, but I'm not convinced they hang together—if it's genuinely akratic, can it also be deliberately deceptive? And if we drop mentalism entirely, do we lose the distinction between alignment failure and a simple policy update?

Please unpack the philosophical commitments baked into this evaluation practice—what it strictly requires versus what it merely fits well with, where it's underdetermined, and whether the akrasia/deception framing is consistent. Write in British English.
