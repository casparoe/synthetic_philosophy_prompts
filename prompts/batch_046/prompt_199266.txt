I am preparing to lead a thirty-minute session for my school's junior philosophy circle—students who have read some Plato but have no formal training in philosophy of mind or action—on why the absent-minded driver is a genuine problem for theories of intentional action rather than merely a psychological curiosity about distraction. I need a teaching plan, not the explanation itself. Specify the exact order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so that the tension emerges naturally: in particular, what distinction between an action's causal origin and its ongoing guidance must be in place before the case can generate philosophical puzzlement, and why that dependency holds. Choose the single concrete driver scenario to build the discussion around, describing precisely which features it must preserve—the prior formed intention, the familiar route, the point of deviation, and the absence of any conscious correction—and which common embellishments, such as extreme fatigue, neurological impairment, or divided-attention multitasking, must be explicitly stripped away so the example stays philosophically clean. Map the misconceptions these students will predictably import: the idea that "autopilot" or muscle memory dissolves the problem, the conflation of conscious attention with intentionality, and the slide from "the turn was unintended" to "the entire behavior was unintentional"; for each, identify the precise point in your proposed sequence where it should be intercepted and defused. Design a brief observational check—something I can use in discussion—to determine whether they have actually grasped the philosophical puzzle rather than simply repeating that the driver was not paying attention. Finally, state explicitly which elements of the professional debate, such as appeals to sub-personal mechanisms or fine-grained distinctions between present-directed and future-directed intentions, I should set aside at this level, name them as out of scope, and explain why leaving them open is preferable to simplifying them incorrectly.
