I am delivering the keynote address at a memorial symposium being held next month for my grandmother, who died two weeks ago after a long illness. She was both a novelist and a lecturer in philosophy of art, and the audience will be divided: roughly half are her former students and colleagues in aesthetics, and the other half are family and friends who knew her only through her novels and through our family life. I want the paradox of fiction to serve as the structural spine of the address, not as ornament. She held, and taught, that emotional responses to fictional characters are genuine but depend on a specific cognitive posture—something like an actively maintained suspension of belief—whereas grief for a real person operates without that suspension and therefore belongs to a categorically different kind of response. I need to honor that distinction precisely: the speech must not collapse her death into narrative, must not suggest she is watching over us from somewhere, and must not treat our grief as if it were merely another reaction to a well-crafted story. At the same time, I want to introduce, gently but clearly, a tension she never resolved in her own work. If fictional emotion requires that kind of suspended commitment, and if one of fiction’s values is that it exercises our moral emotions in a safe, reversible register, then real grief—which refuses any suspension—might share far less with fictional sorrow than her account implied. That threatens the idea that fiction genuinely trains us for real loss. I want this tension to shape what I can and cannot claim about her legacy, about the novels she left behind, and about what we are doing together in this room. The address should run about twelve minutes, be formally composed but not lecture-like in tone, and should be fully intelligible to the family without simplifying away the philosophical stakes for her students. Please write it.
