I've been revisiting Fernand Braudel's tripartite division of historical time—geographical time, social time, and the time of events—as I prepare to close a semester-long reading group on whether structural explanations in history amount to determinism with better public relations. Rather than another round of prose arguments, I'd like to end with a poem, specifically a sestina, that renders the tension structurally rather than resolving it. The six rotating end-words should remain at the geographical scale—ridge, current, salt, border, winter, grain—so that their cyclical return across stanzas mimics the near-immobile recurrence of climate and terrain in the longue durée. The interior lines of each stanza should descend to concrete episodes: one harvest failure, one treaty negotiation, the death of a particular ruler. Then the envoi should gather all six structural words into a single image of individual decision—someone choosing to cross a border, or abandon a valley—where the choice is visibly free yet visibly shaped by every recurring constraint that has circled through the poem. It should be accurate enough that someone familiar with Braudel recognizes the three levels without feeling they've received a textbook summary in verse; the form itself should do the philosophical work, leaving the tension between agency and structure alive in the final lines.
