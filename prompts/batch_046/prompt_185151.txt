I'm working through a conceptual problem at the intersection of epiphenomenalism and first-person judgment, and I'd like a careful explanation of where the tension lies. The standard paradox of phenomenal judgment goes roughly like this: if phenomenal properties are genuinely epiphenomenal—causally inert with respect to both behavior and other mental states—then our judgments about what it is like to experience them are not caused by those experiences themselves. Instead, such judgments must be driven entirely by underlying physical-functional processes. But if the causal chain running into a phenomenal judgment never touches the phenomenal property it purports to be about, it seems hard to see why that judgment should be reliably about the phenomenal property rather than about its physical correlate, or why we should take ourselves to have any epistemic access to it at all.

To push this further, consider an augmented scenario that I think sharpens the problem rather than dissolving it. Suppose we grant that agents possess what we might call non-integrative phenomenal handles: direct conceptual references to phenomenal properties that are structurally isolated from our physical-functional conceptual framework. These handles are not reducible to descriptions of neural or functional states, and they do not enter into inferential relations with our physical-theoretic beliefs. Even so, because the phenomenal properties themselves remain causally inert, these handles are not triggered or sustained by the properties they denote; they are, at best, conceptually autonomous tags that happen to be deployed in judgment. The puzzle is not merely causal but architectural: we have a conceptual apparatus that points at something outside the causal-physical economy, yet that apparatus is itself embedded within and sustained by that very economy.

Please explain the core conceptual issue here. In particular, I want to understand whether this kind of structural isolation between phenomenal handles and physical-functional concepts resolves the paradox of phenomenal judgment—by giving phenomenal judgments an independent, non-physical conceptual pathway—or whether it deepens the paradox by leaving those judgments both causally orphaned and conceptually quarantined from the rest of our belief system. How should we think about the relationship between causal exclusion and conceptual autonomy in this case?
