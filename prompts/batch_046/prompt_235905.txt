I'm working through Karl Jaspers's four-part taxonomy of guilt—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical—trying to extend it rigorously to nations across generations, and I keep running into the same tension: the analogies that preserve Jaspers's insistence on personal attribution seem to deny any literal collective guilt, while the analogies that allow collective guilt seem to collapse into unfair punishment of people who never chose their membership. To clarify my own view, I'd like you to rank the following five conceptual frameworks for collective intergenerational responsibility on a scale of 1 to 10 according to how well they accommodate both the phenomenology of inherited national shame and the normative constraint that literal guilt requires some form of agency or identification. The frameworks are: (1) Jaspers's direct analogical extension, in which political guilt attaches to citizens qua citizens regardless of direct action but emotional-moral guilt does not; (2) the 'corporate continuity' model, treating the state as a persistent legal person whose obligations survive generations but whose emotional capacities do not; (3) a hybrid I'm tentatively calling 'distributed diachronic agency,' where a collective is literally guilty to the extent that present members knowingly maintain and benefit from unjust structures initiated by predecessors, with the guilt scaling by the degree of present identification with those structures; (4) the 'pure symbolic repair' view, holding that all talk of national guilt is metaphorical rhetoric aimed at restorative justice and carries no literal moral weight; and (5) the 'statistical aggregation' approach, denying collective guilt but allowing that if a sufficient proportion of a past generation was criminally culpable, the present inherits a functional social stigma that operates like guilt without being it. For each score, explain in one sentence the specific tension—either with Jaspers's original distinction or with the intuition that inherited structures generate genuine obligations—that fixes its rank.

Finally, tell me whether any single framework can justify a formal state apology for acts committed before any living citizen was born without collapsing into either empty metaphor or collective punishment, and if not, which two frameworks you'd combine, in what proportion, and how you'd resolve the conflict between their core premises when they pull in opposite directions on a concrete case—say, a present-day government paying reparations funded by taxes on citizens who themselves belong to a historically oppressed subgroup within the same nation.
