Exam question: Many economists argue that honoring sunk costs is irrational because rational choice should depend only on prospective costs and benefits. Some philosophers, however, distinguish “instrumental” rationality—which selects means for given ends—from “archival” rationality, which treats past commitments as part of one’s practical identity and therefore as constraining present choice regardless of future payoff. Is honoring sunk costs always irrational, or can archival rationality justify it in certain cases? Defend your answer.

Student answer: A sunk cost is a past expenditure that cannot be recovered, so the sunk-cost fallacy occurs when people allow these unrecoverable losses to influence present decisions. Standard decision theory correctly holds that, under purely instrumental rationality, only future outcomes matter. Archival rationality, by contrast, is essentially a psychological bias: it is the emotional reluctance to “waste” prior effort, which decision theorists have demonstrated is a cognitive error. Because all non-instrumental reasoning about past costs is merely this bias, archival rationality never offers a genuine justification. Therefore honoring sunk costs is always irrational. Even when a commitment feels central to one’s identity, that feeling is not a rational reason; as classical utilitarians maintain, rationality requires maximizing expected future utility. Hence there are no exceptions.
