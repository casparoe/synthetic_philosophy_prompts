I'm trying to work through a conceptual tension in Nozick's entitlement theory that standard classroom treatments of the Wilt Chamberlain argument usually leave untouched. The canonical version is straightforward: start with a just distribution D1, allow voluntary exchanges—imagine payments flowing to a skilled athlete for his performances—and arrive at D2, which remains just by entitlement even though it violates every patterned principle of distributive justice. Most readers take this to establish that liberty necessarily disrupts patterns. But I want to examine a more elusive issue that we might call historical shadowing: the possibility that a chain of entirely legitimate acquisitions and transfers can yield a macro-distribution structurally isomorphic to an unjust one, not because any link in the chain violated rights, but because the initial baseline and the rules governing exchange jointly constrain the state-space so that the final holdings reproduce, without any coercion, the very concentrations that a patterned theory would reject. Does Nozick's framework contain any internal criterion for distinguishing a distribution that genuinely tracks an unjust dependency relation running through its history from one that merely mimics such a pattern—because free choices have overlaid a just narrative onto it? In particular, I want you to explain whether the Chamberlain case proves that liberty is incompatible with pattern-preservation, or whether it only demonstrates that liberty permits pattern-mimicry, leaving open whether hidden structural dependencies in the sequence of holdings should still concern a defender of entitlement.
