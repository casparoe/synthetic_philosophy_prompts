I've been arguing with my friends about tranquilism — specifically the hard version where wellbeing is literally only the absence of craving and suffering, with positive pleasure or achievement counting for nothing. I'm also taking a course on well-being theories and the standard examples feel kind of stale, so I want to design something fresher for my philosophy discussion group at the local library (about 8 people, mostly grad students plus a couple outsiders who wander in). I have about 45 minutes. The niche twist I want to inject is framing everything around "epistemic craving" — the drive to understand and know things — since that's not the usual suffering-example people use, and it should make the structural claim feel sharper.

Here's the activity: split the group into "designers" and "auditors." Designers propose social institutions — schools, museums, research collectives — that they think reduce suffering. Auditors score each proposal using three cards: eliminates craving, creates pleasure-but-spawns-new-craving, or achieves pure absence. The hidden mechanic is that any institution satisfying curiosity also generates open-ended desire for more knowledge, so designers keep accidentally building "craving-machines" while thinking they're helping. No real money, just point tracking and a board. They should run into the trap themselves before anyone names tranquilism. I need clear rules and roles, the score/payoff scheme, a debrief that maps each round onto the philosophical claim, and an honest note on what the game can't reproduce — probably the actual subjective phenomenology of being completely free of craving. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. And please say how confident you are in this design, and specifically call out the part you're least sure about — my hunch is that the epistemic-craving framing might clarify the view for some people but just muddy the water for others.
