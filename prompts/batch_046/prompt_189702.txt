I am a doctoral student in formal decision theory working on symmetric reasoning and correlation without communication, and I recently heard a fellow seminar participant summarize Douglas Hofstadter’s Platonia dilemma and the associated Scientific American “Luring Lottery” experiment in a way that I want to check before using it in my literature review. He described superrationality as the thesis that symmetrically situated agents with common knowledge of identical reasoning capacities ought to apply the same decision procedure, thereby producing perfectly correlated actions without explicit coordination. Applied to Platonia—where twenty people each independently decide whether to send a telegram, with exactly one sender receiving a billion dollars—he said this requires each agent to randomize with probability 1/20, not via equilibrium reasoning but because any deterministic choice would violate the symmetry of the reasoning process itself. He further claimed that standard game-theoretic analysis is unable to recover this result, since Nash equilibrium allegedly forces each player into a deterministic pure strategy rather than the symmetric mixed strategy. Turning to the Luring Lottery, in which readers submitted postcards specifying a number of entries with a prize of one million dollars divided by total submissions, he said Scientific American explicitly designed the contest as an empirical test of superrational coordination: they expected rational participants to roll simulated dice with sides equal to the expected number of respondents, but the actual flood of submissions—including astronomically large values—demonstrated that real-world agents fail the requisite symmetry conditions, thereby confirming that superrationality is descriptively false and normatively unstable in large populations. Before I adopt this framing—particularly in light of recent work extending Hofstadter-style reasoning to continuous-action analogs and formalizing symmetry conditions via modal conditions on algorithmic identity—I need an accuracy audit. Which elements correctly capture Hofstadter’s argument and the formal structure of these games? Where does the summary conflate superrational coordination with equilibrium selection, misrepresent the magazine’s purpose, or overstate what the contest results establish? And does his normative conclusion—that empirical failure of coordination undermines superrationality—follow, or does it rest on an equivocation between descriptive failure and normative inadequacy?
