I am preparing the interview script for a department colloquium Q&A with a philosopher who has developed a contextualist resolution to Fitch's paradox of knowability. Rather than accepting omniscience or abandoning the principle that every truth is in principle knowable, she argues that the knowledge operator shifts its epistemic standards between the premise and the derivation: in ordinary contexts the standards are moderate enough that the knowability claim holds, but in the reflective contexts required for applying closure under conjunction—specifically, when one applies the principle to a conjunction of the form p ∧ ¬Kp—the standards become maximally stringent, so that the embedded knowledge ascriptions in the derived omniscience claim fail in those very contexts, thereby blocking the contradiction without denying factivity outright.

Please draft eight to ten questions that go well beyond asking her to summarize this framework. I want at least one question pressing whether factivity can survive these contextual shifts without generating second-order paradoxes about knowing that one does not know; one that targets the precise derivation step where closure over conjunction interacts with the modal possibility operator, asking whether her contextualist machinery applies to the embedded knowledge claims inside the conjunction or only to the outer assertion of knowability; and one that asks whether her view entails that there are self-referential truths about our own ignorance that are themselves subject to the same contextual variability, together with a follow-up anticipating her likely reply that second-order ignorance is evaluated at a different contextual level. Finally, include a concluding question asking what conceptual or phenomenological evidence could motivate preferring her contextualist strategy over a more austere restrictionist approach that simply limits the scope of the knowability principle to non-self-referential or atomic truths. The questions should anticipate objections familiar from the epistemic logic literature and be appropriate for an audience of graduate students and faculty in epistemology.
