I'm directing a modest three-year fellowship program—two postdoctoral researchers, no empirical laboratory, strictly conceptual work—anchored in Strawson's reactive-attitudes framework rather than in libertarian or compatibilist metaphysics of the will. The fellows will have backgrounds in moral psychology and some familiarity with social philosophy, but not deep training in modal logic or philosophy of physics. Before I finalize the call for proposals, I need a defended prioritization: which two or three open conceptual lines within or extending this framework actually deserve these limited resources, and in what order?

I'm especially interested in how that prioritization handles what strikes me as a neglected structural tension—between the interpersonal dyadic model Strawson originally emphasized, in which one agent resents another, and the increasingly prominent institutional or distributed cases, where reactive attitudes are mediated by organizational roles, algorithmic decision-procedures, or collective entities that do not fit neatly into the participant-stance versus observer-stance binary. Some recent discussions suggest that if we take the reactive-attitudes account seriously, we may need either to expand the taxonomy of reactive attitudes beyond resentment and gratitude to include something like structural disapprobation, or else to concede that large-scale institutional blame operates through an entirely different normative mechanism. Which of these directions—taxonomic expansion, institutional mechanism separation, or a different open question altogether—is most worth pursuing?

Please do not merely list unresolved issues. I want the reasoning: for each line you prioritize, explain why it is upstream of other work in the area, how tractable it is for philosophers working without heavy empirical collaboration, and what kind of conceptual result over the next three years would either elevate or diminish its importance relative to the others. Be explicit about the criteria you are using—whether dependency on other questions, practical urgency for legal or organizational ethics, or the risk of conceptual stagnation if ignored—and defend any trade-offs, such as choosing a highly tractable but narrowly focused taxonomic question over a more ambitious but riskier institutional reconceptualization.
