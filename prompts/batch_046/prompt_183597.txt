I am preparing an essay for my A-level philosophy course on theories of well-being. The debate sparked by Nozick's experience-machine thought experiment—whether it defeats hedonism—appears to me to have stalled: defenders of hedonism reply that we value real contact with the world, while opponents treat that reply as conceding that well-being requires more than pleasure. I suspect the disagreement is not really about pleasure at all, but I am unsure whether it is substantive, verbal, or a case of two camps answering different questions under one heading.

To test this, consider a modified scenario: an "adaptive-preference machine" that not only supplies pleasant experiences, but continuously updates your values so that you always favour the state you are in, removing any feeling of alienation. If this variant still seems deficient for well-being, does that reveal that the original dispute was never genuinely about hedonism versus reality-contact, but rather about whether autonomy or authenticity is an independent constituent of well-being?

Please diagnose the disagreement: are the sides divided over facts about human psychology, over standards of what counts as a counterexample, or over the very meaning of "well-being"? Is there a hidden empirical question—perhaps about adaptive preference formation—that could resolve it, or is the impasse due to unargued intuitions? And what distinction, experiment, or reformulation would count as genuine progress rather than a restatement? Please respond in British English.
