I'm convening a ninety-minute reading group for philosophy postgraduates who have taken introductory ethics but are not specialists in moral epistemology. I want them to understand moral uncertainty not as ordinary empirical doubt about outcomes, but as structural uncertainty about which normative framework governs a given choice — and specifically how this creates "moral risk", the danger that acting on one's best-guess theory might constitute a grave wrong if a different framework is actually correct.

Plan the session's explanatory arc rather than delivering the explanation itself. What idea must be in place before "structural uncertainty" can be distinguished from simple ignorance of facts? Identify the single carrying scenario: it should present an agent facing a serious but not sensational choice where both consequentialist and deontological assessments are genuinely available, and where the agent has no credible way to resolve which framework applies. Explain why the familiar "trolley problem with unknown probabilities" fails to carry this particular idea, and what your scenario must include and exclude to work instead. Map the misconceptions in the order they will surface — I anticipate they will first reduce moral uncertainty to risk-assessment with moral preferences, then conclude it licenses inaction, then assume that averaging across theories is the rational response — and specify exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off. Provide one question I can ask at the end that distinguishes those who understand the structure from those who have merely memorised the phrase "moral risk". State clearly what should be openly refused at this level — for instance, formal decision-theoretic machinery or specific meta-ethical commitments — and say so explicitly. Use British English throughout. Finish with a line reading Verdict: followed by the name or brief description of your carrying scenario and the first misconception to be defused.
