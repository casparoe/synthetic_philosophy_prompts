Write a dissertation prospectus proposal (approximately 4,000 words, one semester) on the following precisely framed question: Can Aumann's agreement theorem be generalized to agents whose evidential structures are not embedded in a shared state space—specifically, when agent A's sigma-algebra and agent B's are incommensurable in that neither is a sub-algebra of the other and no canonical joint space exists? Rather than rehearsing the familiar critiques that common priors are descriptively unrealistic or normatively coercive, identify the structural gap: standard treatments assume disagreement occurs only within a common ontology, partitioned differently by private information. Your proposal should ask whether a minimal common refinement can be constructed that respects both agents' evidential constraints without imposing an external ontology, and whether convergence of posterior beliefs can still be proved under that refinement. State the question so that it has a determinate answer—either a qualified generalized theorem holds for a well-defined class of incommensurable structures, or a concrete counterexample demonstrates irreducible divergence. Explain your approach: formalize the conditions for a minimal refinement, specify the measure-theoretic machinery you will use, identify the class of finite-partition cases you will analyze first, and say why this formal route is preferable to the obvious philosophical alternative of arguing that shared ontology is a precondition for rational disagreement. Say exactly what a successful result looks like (the theorem and its scope conditions), what failure looks like (the counterexample and the feature that blocks refinement), and how you will distinguish a technical obstacle from a conceptual one. Give an honest assessment of scope: what formal prerequisites you assume, where the proof is likely to stall, and what restriction—such as limiting the analysis to finite partitions or to binary belief states—would make the project feasible within one semester. Do not request a bibliography or citation review; refer to positions, arguments, and results by name only, and note that citations will be filled in separately. Finally, take a committed position: argue whether you expect the project to yield a qualified positive result or a negative counterexample, defend that expectation with one specific structural reason, and say what consideration would change your mind.
