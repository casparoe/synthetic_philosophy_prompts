As a retired physicist who has lately taken up philosophy of science, I have become preoccupied with how institutional authority operates across disciplinary boundaries. Consider the episode of March 2024, when the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy voted not to ratify the Anthropocene Working Group’s proposal for a formal geological epoch. The AWG had spent over a decade identifying a Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point at Crawford Lake, Ontario, anchoring the boundary to 1952 through the initial rise of plutonium-239 and other radionuclides from thermonuclear testing, coincident with globally synchronous signals—black carbon, nitrogen isotope shifts, and synthetic materials. The SQS rejected this. I ask you to analyze the episode not as a dispute over raw data, but through a conceptual distinction I am trying to formulate: the difference between chronostratigraphic authority, the power to declare a synchronous physical boundary in sedimentary rock according to stratigraphic rules, and epochal-historical authority, the power to designate a period defined by its causal significance for human civilization and planetary systems. Apply the distinction with discipline: what exactly was the SQS exercising when it rejected the proposal—was it denying the physical evidence, denying that the evidence warranted geological taxonomy, or denying that geological taxonomy was the appropriate framework altogether? Then turn the analysis back on the framework itself: does the Crawford Lake proposal reveal that “Anthropocene” is a category mistake, an attempt to submit a historical-moral judgment to the procedural rules of stratigraphy? Or does the rejection show that chronostratigraphic authority is illegitimately narrow, excluding forms of planetary change that do not leave a neat 1952 line in lake mud? I want no bibliography and no citation of sources; I want a careful two-directional mapping—what the distinction illuminates about the vote, and what the vote exposes about the limits of the distinction.
