Consider the following argument from Hume's essay Of Money. Hume writes that money is not properly one of the subjects of commerce, but only the instrument that men have agreed upon to facilitate the exchange of commodities. It is none of the wheels of trade: it is the oil that renders the motion of the wheels more smooth and easy. He then considers a single kingdom in isolation and claims that the greater or less quantity of gold and silver is in itself of no consequence, because prices depend on the proportion between commodities and money; yet he also maintains that if a nation suddenly acquires a large stock of money, its goods will initially be cheap relative to neighbors, exports will surge, and the surplus specie will flow out until the balance is restored.

I'm trying to understand whether Hume's oil metaphor is consistent with his specie-flow mechanism. Or can the two be reconciled by reading the oil as precisely the medium whose viscosity or pressure regulates the differential that drives flow? Also, consider whether Hume's distinction between wheels and oil still holds for a purely digital or fiat currency that has no physical substance or weight: if the lubricant itself has no material presence, is it still correct to say it is not one of the wheels?
