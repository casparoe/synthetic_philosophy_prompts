I'm drafting this historical novel about a city-state that keeps tearing itself apart through public vote, and I was telling my friend about the real Athenian messes—Mytilene, the Sicilly expedition, even that Socrates trial—when she asked if there's an actual concept for the pattern. Here's what I keep seeing: the assembly gets whipped up by a speaker, votes for something extreme and cruel, then in a calmer session they partly walk it back—sending the second ship, blaming the generals, maybe regretting thier hemlock decision—but they never fix the conditions that made the first vote happen. The reversal feels humane, yet it's structurally useless because the same emotional machinery is sitting there for the next crisis.

So I'm wondering if philosophers or historians have a specific name for that loop—this emotional half-life of collective judgment that decays between meetings without actually correcting the failure mode? I know there's talk about demagogery and democratic collapse, but I'm after the concept for the reversal dynamics themselves: how they differ from ordinary policy correction, and whether they apply the same way across Mytilene, Sicily, and the Socrates case. If there's no established term, could you coin one and map its edges? Like, does it cover total reversals too, or only partial ones, and how do we separate it from plain hypocrisy? I need the carving precise for my characters, not just a bare label.
