I'm currently in a seminar on AI ethics and value alignment, and I'm trying to understand a design practice I've encountered in discussions of low-impact artificial intelligence. The practice works as follows: to avoid unspecified side effects, engineers construct a counterfactual baseline in which the agent takes a null action at every timestep, then measure how much the agent's actual behavior reduces the set of states reachable from the current state over some fixed horizon. The agent is penalized in proportion to this reduction in reachability. The claim is that this captures "impact" without anyone having to specify a full utility function or value ordering—only a state-space representation and a baseline of inaction are required.

This puzzles me philosophically. What commitments about value, causation, and agency does this institutional design choice actually presuppose? Does it implicitly treat the preservation of future option-space as intrinsically valuable, independent of what those options contain? Does it privilege the "do nothing" counterfactual as normatively neutral in a way that depends on a particular theory of moral responsibility or causal contribution? And is the practice genuinely independent of value specification, or do the choices of reachability metric, state-space granularity, and temporal horizon already encode substantive judgments about what counts as harm? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory that makes this engineering framework intelligible, assess whether its parts are consistent with each other, and say what would have to change in the design if we abandoned the idea that impact can be cleanly separated from a full specification of value.
