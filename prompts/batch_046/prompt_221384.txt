I run a grad philosophy seminar, 14 people, 90 minutes, on agregation. Design a classroom activity that gets them to feel the torture-vs-dust-specks tension without naming it up front. The rules: split into pairs, each pair gets a deck of cards with harm scenarios. They build an exchange-rate ladder — round 1 vote whether to prevent one hour of severe torture or save 10 people from an imperceptible dust speck in the eye lasting one second; round 2 multiply the dust-speck count by 100; round 3 by 1000; they write the running total on the board and vote each time. They keep going through round 6, not knowing the endpoint, then in round 7 you reveal the number is 3^^^3 and ask them to vote on that specific comparison. Include a timeline (20 min for ladder-building, 30 min for the forced-choice votes, 30 min debrief), clear rules for when pairs split and have to justify their vote to the room, and materials (just cards and whiteboard). The debrief should map each round onto the aggregationist move, identify exactly where in the sequence people usually rebel, and be frank that the activity can't reproduce the actual phenomenology of torture — it only reproduces the arithmetic, so the facilitator has to supply the qualitative gap themselves. Also tell me what's gonna go wrong: probably someone invokes quality-over-quantity in round 3 and tries to halt the ladder, or people retroactively change their earlier votes once they see 3^^^3. Keep it active, no lecture first, and don't mention Parfit or any classic authors until after they've cast the final vote. Make sure the endpoint feels like a discovery, not a gotcha.
