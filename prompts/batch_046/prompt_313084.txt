People always compare gradual mind uploding to the ship of Theseus — swap out neurons for silicon chips one at a time and you're still you, just like replacing planks keeps it the same ship. The idea is that since there's no single moment where you get destroyed, consciousness just glides over to the new subtrate without a gap. But I think this analogy is doing way more work than it should. Can you lay out the mapping precisely — what's the ship, what's the plank, what's the blueprint? — and then show where it cracks. I think one hidden import is that ships have an external design plan that survives part replacement, but brains don't have a blueprint outside the active tissue itself. There's also the hybrid middle stage: for months you'd have biological and silicon tissue running together with diffrent clock speeds and noise profiles, so the system isn't just "wood turning to steel" but actually changes its dynamics in real time. Plus there's the issue of whether phenominal continuity needs substrate-level causal continuity or just functional equivalence. Given all that, does the conclusion — that gradual replacement preserves personal identity and there's no hard line where "you" disappear — survive once the disanalogies are on the table? Or is there a better analogy, maybe like slowly translating a poem word by word into another language?
