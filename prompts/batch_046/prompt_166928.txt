I am an undergraduate student in my university's philosophy of law and distributive justice seminar, and next week I am presenting a paper that defends a restricted version of Nozick's entitlement theory against the standard Wilt Chamberlain objection. My thesis is that the Wilt Chamberlain argument only undermines patterned principles of justice if we assume that initial holdings are separately valid in a strong, atomistic sense. I argue that when initial holdings include socially constructed positional goods—such as licensed professional credentials or zoning entitlements whose value depends on collective recognition—the chain of voluntary transfers that produces Chamberlain's income inherits a pattern-dependency from the initial allocation. Consequently, liberty-preserving transfers can reproduce distributive patterns, and the Wilt Chamberlain example fails to show that patterns and liberty are necessarily incompatible; rather, it reveals that the compatibility claim depends on an implausibly thin account of initial acquisition. I am presenting this to our graduate seminar, which consists of two committed Rawlsians who defend the difference principle, one democratic egalitarian focused on relational equality, a libertarian who accepts Nozick's framework without restriction, and several agnostic first-year students. Please prepare me for the objections I will actually face in this room: list them in the order they are likely to arise and by how damaging they would be to my position, phrase each objection as that particular interlocutor would put it, indicate what I should concede immediately, what I must hold firm on, provide a concise reply suitable for a two-minute defense, and note where each reply is weakest. In particular, include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—the one that targets my distinction between atomistic and socially constructed holdings by pointing out that labor markets themselves are positional goods, which would collapse my restriction into a general pattern principle. Please write the response in American English.
