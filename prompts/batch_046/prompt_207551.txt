One of them, Marcus, said it's basically useless — he brought up Weinberg's old complaint that philosophy never helps physicists do physics, and argued that philosophers always show up after scientists have already figured things out, just to rename discoveries. The other student, Priya, pushed back with what she called the "instrument calibration dependency" problem: she said every measuring device embeds hidden philosophical commitments about what counts as a cause versus a correlation, and scientists can't interpret their own readings without those commitments being in place first. Marcus said that's just semantics — if the machine gives you teh correct number, you don't need a philosopher to explain why it works. Priya said "correct" already assumes a philosophy of reference and error, and that operational definitions only hold up because philosophers mapped out the logical structure behind them. Marcus said even if that's true, it doesn't help anyone build a better spectrometer or discover a new compound. Priya said building equipment isn't the only metric — knowing what the equipment is measuring is part of science too, and without interpretation you just have curves, not knowledge. Marcus said scientists solve interpretation by testing predictions, not by reading metaphysics. Priya said predictions themselves depend on what you count as evidence, which is a philosophical choice that happens before the data ever arrive.
