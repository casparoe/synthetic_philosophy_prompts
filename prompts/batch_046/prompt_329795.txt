I have recently joined a working group drafting a fifty-year institutional resilience programme, and a disagreement between two senior colleagues has stalled our framework document. I report their exchange below without taking sides.

R opened by arguing that the classical cyclical accounts—Polybius on constitutional anacyclosis, Ibn Khaldun on the decay of ’asabiyya, Spengler’s seasonal morphology of cultures, and Toynbee’s challenge-and-response cycles—remain structurally sound. He claimed modern bureaucracies display classic winter-phase symptoms: ossified procedures, declining civic trust, and the substitution of procedural legitimacy for substantive solidarity. He added that digital archives do not break the cycle but accelerate decay through algorithmic lock-in and epistemic closure, so the dynamics persist in a new register.

S replied that treating societies as organisms with predetermined life cycles is a category error. She noted that cumulative scientific knowledge, globally networked governance, and predictive modelling represent genuinely linear capacities that break the feedback loops behind historical collapse. Past cycles, she said, were driven mainly by exogenous shocks—epidemic, invasion, climate shift—that modern institutions can now anticipate and mitigate, so assuming inevitable decay is unwarranted.

R countered that S’s confidence is itself a cyclical symptom: late-stage societies consistently overestimate their own exceptionalism. He proposed reconceptualising Ibn Khaldun’s ’asabiyya not as tribal loyalty but as network trust density, arguing this density degrades across generations regardless of technological mediation. Hence predictive models trained on cyclical historical data are unreliable precisely because they embed an assumption of continuity where discontinuity is due.

S responded that R’s reconceptualisation is speculative and unfairly shifts the burden of proof. Without a principled mechanism showing why digital mediation cannot sustain trust density, she said, the cyclical view collapses into an unfalsifiable narrative. Planning should therefore proceed on adaptive linear growth, with cyclical models treated only as cautionary tales rather than structural forecasts.

R finished by observing that predictive modelling itself depends on datasets drawn almost entirely from cyclical regimes; relying on linear extrapolation thus risks the failure mode Toynbee identified—societies neglecting creative response because they assume perpetual progress.

Please adjudicate the exchange point by point: say where each speaker is correct, where they miss something important, and whether any claim should be treated as settled. Separate clearly what is well established from what remains genuinely contested among scholars of long-range historical dynamics, and do not present any disputed view as consensus. Present your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion. Write in British English.
