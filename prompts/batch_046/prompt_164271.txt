In a workshop discussion on moral psychology, two philosophers debated whether the situationist critique undermines Aristotelian virtue ethics. The first argued that situationist experiments demonstrate weak cross-situational behavioral consistency, which directly refutes virtue ethics because that tradition requires robust global character traits—dispositions that reliably manifest across diverse contexts. The second replied that this objection assumes a behaviorist model of personality foreign to Aristotelianism: virtue ethics demands not uniform behavior across situations but domain-specific practical excellences governed by phronesis, so that someone might exhibit physical courage while lacking bureaucratic moral courage without contradiction, since practical wisdom determines how virtues are activated in particular domains. The first countered that this defense risks unfalsifiability—any observed behavioral variance can be explained away by invoking hidden contextual parameters or localized trait absence—thereby stripping virtue ethics of predictive content. The second responded that the situationist critique commits a category mistake: it measures consistency through correlation coefficients of observable actions, whereas virtue ethics locates consistency at the level of practical reasoning structures, not behavioral outputs; therefore the empirical data, however accurate, targets the wrong object.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the four positions from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and identify what, if anything, both interlocutors overlook—especially whether phronesis can genuinely integrate local virtues without collapsing into an ad hoc theoretical rescue.
