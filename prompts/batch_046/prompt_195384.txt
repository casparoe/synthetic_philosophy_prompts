Please help me understand a passage from Thomas Huxley’s 1874 essay “On the Hypothesis that Animals are Automata, and its History.” In the relevant section, Huxley proposes that consciousness may stand to bodily action as a steam whistle stands to a locomotive: the whistle sounds only because the engine operates, yet removing or altering the whistle does not affect the engine’s motion in the slightest. He suggests that mental states might be precisely such by-products—vividly present, perhaps, but causally inert with respect to the neural machinery that produces behavior.

What I find difficult is how this analogy bears on what philosophers later called the paradox of phenomenal judgment. If my awareness that I am experiencing red is itself generated entirely by neural mechanisms that never receive causal input from the experience of red—since that experience, on this view, is an epiphenomenon—then my judgment that “I am experiencing red” appears to be about something that played no part in causing it. The judgment is produced wholly by the “engine,” yet its content attributes properties to the “whistle.” Does Huxley’s locomotive analogy therefore commit him to the stronger claim that phenomenal judgments are systematically misdirected? That is, in forming such a judgment I implicitly take my consciousness to be part of the causal chain leading to the judgment, whereas the analogy denies exactly that. Or can the analogy accommodate a form of non-causal self-monitoring—perhaps analogous to an acoustic sensor on the locomotive that registers the whistle’s presence without the whistle driving the sensor—so that the judgment can be veridical about consciousness even though consciousness never enters the causal chain?

I am especially puzzled by a recursive feature that the passage seems to invite but does not resolve. The judgment “consciousness is causally inert” is itself, on Huxley’s view, caused without any contribution from consciousness. Its own causal history thus exemplifies the very inertness it asserts. Is this recursion a hidden contradiction—suggesting that the epiphenomenalist cannot consistently affirm her own view—or does it actually reinforce the analogy, showing that a mechanistic system can generate correct descriptions of its own by-products? Please help me understand whether the passage supports the self-stultifying reading, in which we are necessarily wrong about the causal role of our own awareness, or whether it leaves logical space for a consistent epiphenomenalist account of self-knowledge.
