I have been trying to settle my own view on universals by collecting verdicts on concrete cases, and I find the pattern both suggestive and internally strained. Here are my judgments.

First, consider two copper spheres manufactured independently in separate laboratories with no causal contact. I take it that there is literally one and the same structural universal—copper-sphericity—present in both; they do not merely resemble each other, but share a form. Second, imagine a unique violet shade synthesized in a single dye molecule that is then destroyed, never reproduced or observed elsewhere. Even in this permanent isolation, I think a universal—this specific violet-quality—exists, uninstantiated but real, capable in principle of being shared. Third, take the relation of being taller than that holds between two particular people. I treat this as a single universal with two ends, not as two matching particular relations.

Fourth, and here is the difficulty, imagine two people who suffer injuries producing phenomenally identical headaches—same intensity, location, and qualitative character, verified by perfect neural correspondence. Despite this exact match, I am inclined to deny that they share a universal headache-quality; instead, there seem to be only two particular headache-instances that happen to coincide. So I hold that physical form is shared across spheres, yet phenomenal form is not shared across minds. Fifth, a green leaf is not-red. I do not think non-redness is a positive universal instantiated by the leaf; it is merely the absence of redness. Sixth, consider a gerrymandered property defined disjunctively: being spherical, or cubic, or a prime number exceeding 10^20, or situated in the northern hemisphere on a Tuesday. I judge that no universal corresponds to this list. Seventh, the mathematical property of being prime is instantiated by 7 and by 11. I regard primeness as a genuine universal, existing in some sense independently of its particular numerical instances.

Can you state a principle, or a competing pair of principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible? For each candidate, identify the clause that handles each case, predict its verdict on a case I have not mentioned—specifically, whether two different drinkers with identical neural profiles share a universal taste-quality of a particular wine—and state honestly which of my seven judgments it cannot accommodate without contortion. In particular, address the tension between the shared copper-form and the unshared headache-form: does preserving both verdicts require an ad hoc distinction, or does a cleaner principle force me to abandon one?
