I serve on the Municipal Integration and Education Policy Board for a large, diverse school district. By Friday we must circulate a formal written opinion recommending which of three frameworks the district should adopt for governing visible religious and cultural expression among public-school teachers and staff.

Option A is a strict neutrality model, modeled on French laïcité: all visible religious and cultural symbols are prohibited for staff during instructional hours, on the grounds that the classroom must present a uniform institutional voice and avoid any appearance of state endorsement.

Option B is our current plural-accommodation framework, closer to Anglo-American multicultural practice: staff may wear or display symbols of personal identity, provided they do not coerce students or dominate the pedagogical environment, and the curriculum actively incorporates diverse cultural frameworks.

Option C is a hybrid proposed by our staff analysts, which they term “asymmetric hermeneutic neutrality.” Under this model, staff must maintain a “blank institutional voice”—no visible personal symbols—while the curriculum is required to include mandatory “translation modules” in which minority cultural epistemologies are presented not as heritage material but as live, competing frameworks for understanding the subject matter. Proponents argue this protects a shared public reason without erasing difference; critics argue it suppresses teacher identity while instrumentalizing student culture, producing what they call a “double asymmetry” in which minority students bear a one-way hermeneutic burden and teachers forfeit expressive autonomy.

The board is split. Some members see Option C as an unstable compromise that combines the worst features of assimilation and multiculturalism; others see it as the only resolution that avoids both assimilationist erasure and the fragmentation of common educational purpose. Please draft the board’s decision memo: recommend one option—A, B, or C—provide the reasoning with an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, and specify a concrete implementation course with a clear timeline. The memo should address whether the philosophical tension between assimilation and multicultural recognition is best resolved at the level of staff expression, curriculum design, or both, and should be written as a document the board could act on directly.

Verdict: [A, B, or C]
