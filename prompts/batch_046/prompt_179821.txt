I am a junior in my high school’s philosophy seminar, and we have been working through the classic exchange between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett on whether dreaming counts as a genuine experience. From what I understand, Malcolm argues that we can never apply the concept of an experience to the sleeping state itself, since there is no criterion—no possible observation or first-person check—that could verify whether a dream is occurring while the sleeper is still unconscious; all we ever have is a report made after waking. Dennett, for his part, seems to push this even further with his cassette theory: the brain does not undergo any experiential process during REM sleep at all, but rather stores a kind of narrative script that gets loaded into consciousness only at the moment of awakening, like playing back a prerecorded tape. If both of these positions are correct—if Malcolm shows that we lack any logical basis for attributing experience to sleep, and Dennett shows that whatever we call a dream is literally constructed only upon waking—then it seems to me that there is no coherent place left for “dream experience” in either the sleeping or the waking state.

Yet in my biology elective, we recently discussed a framework called “hypnagogic experiential indexing,” which claims to map real-time phenomenological signatures during REM sleep using combined EEG and subjective-report calibration. The idea seems to be that certain neural patterns can be indexed to specific experiential contents before the subject even wakes. But if Malcolm’s verificationist constraint rules out any ascription of experience during unconsciousness, and Dennett’s model treats the waking report as the sole site where any dream-content comes into being, wouldn’t this kind of indexing be conceptually impossible? Wouldn’t it be trying to measure an experience that, according to both philosophers, either never happens or hasn’t happened yet? I am trying to write my seminar paper on whether neuroscience should abandon talk of “REM phenomenology” altogether, but I worry I am conflating the philosophical arguments.

Could you help me see precisely where the confusion lies—particularly whether Dennett’s cassette theory really denies that anything experiential occurs during sleep, or whether Malcolm’s argument is meant to apply to the logical status of the waking report rather than to deny any neural or phenomenal presence in the sleeping brain itself? I want to understand whether these two philosophers actually agree in ruling out dream-experience, or if I have misread them into a false alliance that makes the neuroscience seem incoherent.
