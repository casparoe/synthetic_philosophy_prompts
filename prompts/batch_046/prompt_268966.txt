I'm auditing a graduate seminar on intertemporal choice and recently overheard a sharp disagreement between two doctoral students, Mira and Jonas, about a variant of the money-pump argument that does not involve classic intransitive preferences. The case they were debating concerns an agent—call her Elena—who currently strictly prefers gamble A (a certain $50) over gamble B (a 50/50 shot at $120 or nothing). Elena also knows that if she takes B and loses, she will undergo a specific cognitive update—say, learning that her risk model was miscalibrated—that will cause her to strictly prefer A over B in that subsequent state, whereas if she takes B and wins her preferences remain unchanged. Jonas argued that Elena should be free to take B now and then revert to A after a loss, because each local preference ordering is rational given her information at the time; the shift is essentially Bayesian updating applied to values, not diachronic irrationality. Mira objected that this dynamic creates an exploitable cycle: an adversary can induce Elena to enter gamble B at a price just below her initial reservation price, then—after a loss triggers her update—induce her to exit B and revert to A at a price just above her revised reservation price for B, extracting surplus from the round-trip while Elena never enjoys a net gain. Jonas replied that this is not a genuine money pump, since Elena, anticipating the entire sequence, will refuse the initial entry once she computes the expected value of the full path, including her anticipated reversal; the vulnerability only arises if she suffers from a framing failure or myopic evaluation, not from the preference dynamics themselves. Mira then countered that even if Elena refuses the explicit package deal, she can still be pumped through a series of seemingly independent, locally attractive micro-transactions—each justified by her current beliefs—without ever assessing the global trajectory, which is precisely the diachronic failure the argument is meant to expose.

They turned to me to referee, but I genuinely could not determine who was tracking the correct distinction. Could you adjudicate their exchange point by point? Specifically, please explain whether Mira has shown that anticipated epistemic updating generates a structurally exploitable path even in the absence of static intransitivity, or whether Jonas is correct that global foresight dissolves the vulnerability. In addition, identify what, if anything, both parties are missing—perhaps regarding the role of precommitment devices, the boundary between preference change and belief change, or whether diachronic rationality ought to be evaluated over outcomes rather than over decision nodes.
