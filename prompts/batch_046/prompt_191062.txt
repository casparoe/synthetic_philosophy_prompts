I've been trying to settle my view on how we can know anything about the distant past through source criticism, running from Herodotus and Thucydides to contemporary practice, and I want an audit of whether the following commitments are jointly tenable. (1) I accept Thucydides's rejection of mythological explanation and his demand that claims about the distant past rest either on eyewitness testimony or on rigorous inference from physical remains, and I regard Herodotus's recording of unverifiable oral traditions as epistemically admirable humility that does not, by itself, constitute knowledge. (2) I hold that when textual and archaeological evidence conflict, material traces should take default priority, on the grounds that they are less susceptible to intentional falsification. (3) At the same time, I think we can legitimately recover specific intentions of past agents—for example, that a particular ruler commissioned a monument to legitimize a dynastic transition—through contextual interpretation of material culture alone, even in the absence of any corroborating texts. (4) Conversely, I maintain that if an event is attested solely by a single, uncorroborated textual tradition, we should suspend judgment about whether it occurred, rather than accept it provisionally. (5) Finally, I believe modern historiography should treat every textual account as a rhetorical artifact shaped by its present audience, and I take Herodotus's preservation of variant traditions without forced synthesis to be a model of the intellectual humility this requires. Is this package consistent? Specifically, is there a hidden tension between giving material culture default authority over texts in (2) and reconstructing intentions from material culture alone in (3), given that the latter seems as interpretation-dependent as textual criticism? Is there a parallel tension between allowing single-source interpretation of intentions from artifacts in (3) while demanding suspension of belief for single-source textual reports of events in (4)? Does (1)'s distinction between Thucydidean rigor and Herodotean humility help resolve these, or does it deepen them? And is the apparent conflict between treating texts as rhetorical performances (5) and praising Herodotus's non-adjudicating preservation of variants only superficial? Please audit these commitments, ordering tensions by severity, specifying for each what has to be modified or abandoned and at what cost. Separate what is settled in philosophy of history and historiography from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed position as if it were the consensus.
