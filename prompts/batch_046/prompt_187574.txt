Consider a cooperative housing association that governs shared spaces through a charter containing three provisions. First, a neutrality clause: the association may never evaluate the intrinsic merit of any resident's lifestyle, values, or projects; all preferences enter the decision process as equally legitimate inputs. Second, a Pareto clause: if every affected resident prefers outcome X to outcome Y, the association must choose X. Third, a minimal liberty clause: each resident holds sovereign authority over a defined personal domain—say, the books they read in private, the food they prepare in their own kitchen, or the art displayed inside their unit—meaning no collective decision can override that domain for paternalistic reasons.

The charter also includes an operational rule not found in standard liberal frameworks: residents must frame objections to others' activities within shared spaces using only the vocabulary of subjective welfare impact ("this causes me measurable discomfort") rather than objective evaluation ("this practice is degrading"). The association does not verify sincerity; it treats well-formed welfare claims as valid inputs regardless of underlying motivation, effectively requiring residents to translate value judgments into preference-language before they can enter aggregation.

Apply this to a concrete conflict structured like Sen's liberal paradox. Resident A wishes to read a particular controversial work in the common library; Resident B wishes that A not do so. Each resident also holds a symmetric preference about the other's parallel activity in the same space, and each is indifferent or mildly negative about their own choice if it prevents the other. Because the common library falls outside any resident's minimal-liberty domain, the charter activates the Pareto and neutrality clauses. Depending on how preference intensities are registered, the association either restricts A but not B, restricts both, or cycles among assignments—while still honoring minimal liberty inside private units and never endorsing a conception of the good.

What philosophical commitments does this institutional design presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value, liberty, and preference aggregation that makes the charter coherent. Does it strictly require that preferences are interpersonally comparable, or can it operate with an ordinal or purely procedural metric? Is it compatible with a view on which liberty is a positive, enabling condition rather than a negative constraint, or with a perfectionist view that ranks conceptions of the good but disguises them behind welfare-framing? Distinguish what the charter strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates—particularly regarding the "welfare framing" rule and its implicit theory of value pluralism. Finally, if the underlying liberal view were replaced by an openly perfectionist framework, or if minimal liberty were interpreted as a claim about effective opportunity rather than non-interference, what specific provisions of the charter would have to be abandoned or rewritten, and would the preference-translation mechanism survive?
