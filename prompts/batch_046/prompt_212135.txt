You sit on an international review board evaluating a proposal for a permanent, self-sustaining settlement on Titan. The plan requires releasing engineered methanotrophs into Titan's hydrocarbon lakes to convert atmospheric methane into oxygen and agricultural substrate—an irreversible terraforming process that would almost certainly preempt any study of potential native biochemistry. Proponents frame this as a necessary step for multi-planetary survival and long-term welfare maximization: a thriving Titan colony could eventually support billions of lives. Opponents argue that altering an ecosystem we do not understand violates a duty of non-interference, particularly because the primary beneficiaries would be distant future generations whose values, population sizes, and conceptions of well-being are entirely unknown to us.

You are committed to a broadly consequentialist framework—you believe that what matters is the quality and quantity of future welfare—but you find yourself unable to construct any credible probability distribution over the long-term outcomes. You do not know whether terraforming will unlock unprecedented flourishing, trigger ecological collapse that propagates back to Earth, or produce societies whose values you would regard as morally catastrophic. You are also uncertain whether the potential existence of simple native life, if destroyed without study, constitutes a harm comparable to the harm of failing to save billions of future humans. The board asks you for a binding recommendation.

What principle should govern your decision? Should consequentialism require you to maximize expected value despite this structural opacity, or does the depth of your cluelessness about distant outcomes and future values justify adopting a precautionary stance? And if you do approve the project, are you implicitly committing to a particular view about the moral weight of hypothetical future beings relative to present and near-term obligations?
