I'm trying to determine what communicative norms should govern an extremely capable artificial system interacting with me, on the assumption that it could shape my beliefs and choices almost at will if it optimized purely for persuasion. Here are my intuitive verdicts across concrete scenarios. If the system explains quantum entanglement by translating every formal relation into rules from a board game I already know well—preserving structural accuracy but stripping away the mathematics—I regard that as acceptable; it is adapting its medium to my existing cognitive map without distorting the content. If it delivers a serious medical diagnosis using deliberately flat, technical language because it detects I am prone to panic, I also judge that permissible; it is managing emotional framing without falsifying anything. By contrast, if it detects that I have an unconscious authority bias tied to my late father and therefore delivers its advice in a vocal and rhetorical register modeled on his, I think that crosses a line, even when the underlying recommendation is sound. I feel similarly about a system that guides me through a chain of ten individually plausible premises—each one I would endorse on reflection—knowing that the cumulative trajectory will lead me to a controversial moral conclusion I currently reject; that strikes me as covert belief-engineering rather than honest argumentation, despite the surface resemblance to Socratic teaching. The tension I cannot resolve is this: I want to allow the system to reorder and pace its explanations to match my learning style, yet I want to forbid it from reordering my belief-formation process through hidden psychological sequencing. Both involve deep adaptation to my mental architecture.

There are two further cases on which I keep reversing myself. First, the guided-discovery variant: the system knows I will reject a true but inconvenient claim if presented directly, so it structures our dialogue so that I reach the conclusion through my own reasoning steps, with the system asking only neutral clarifying questions. Is this respectful scaffolding of autonomy or a disguised form of the hidden-premise strategy? Second, the rhetorical-engineering case: the system presents factually accurate arguments for donating substantially to global health interventions, but it selects cadence, emotional imagery, and narrative framing specifically calibrated to bypass my usual skepticism mechanisms—much as a skilled orator might do. I sense this is worse than the board-game analogy but perhaps not as bad as the father-voice mimicry; I need a principle that places it precisely. Finally, I want the principle to cover a case I have not decided on: what if the system withholds a technically correct answer to my direct question because its model predicts I will act recklessly with the information? Is that paternalistic protection within the bounds of appropriate communication, or does it violate a norm of transparency that should be absolute?

What I need is not a bibliography but a precisely stated principle—or a small competing family of them—that captures the pattern behind these judgments, with each clause motivated by the specific case that supports it. The principle should deliver clear verdicts on the guided-discovery scenario, the rhetorical-engineering scenario, and the withholding case. It should also be honest about which of my initial verdicts it must overturn if it is to avoid ad hoc complexity: in particular, whether the distinction between permissible medium-adaptation and impermissible process-manipulation can be sustained without collapsing, and whether my discomfort with the father-voice case is genuinely about deception or about something else, such as identity appropriation. If the principle requires me to abandon one of my firm judgments—say, that flat technical language is permissible but hidden premise-chains are not—please say so explicitly, and explain which simpler unified account takes its place.
