I've been working through Douglas Hofstadter's 1997 study Le Ton beau de Marot and the cluster of translations it gathers around Clément Marot's short Renaissance poem addressed to a sick lady—commonly known by its opening, Ma mignonne—and I've arrived at a position I want to stress-test, which I call the modal-filter thesis. The claim is this: when a translator subjects the poem to strict formal constraints—preserving its original rhyme architecture, syllable count, and rhetorical scaffolding in English—the translations that survive that filter reveal the poem's essential modal structure. Features that vary freely across admissible renderings are contingent ornament; features that must remain fixed are the poem's necessary expressive commitments. From this I draw a stronger conclusion: a rendering that abandons these formal constraints, say by producing unrhymed free verse, does not merely offer an alternative interpretation but fails epistemically, because it removes the very filter that distinguishes necessary content from accidental form. In other words, formal fidelity is not one virtue among many but the defining condition of having translated the poem rather than replaced it with a paraphrase.

What would have to be true for this to be wrong? I want an honest map of the thesis's exposure, not just standard objections to formalism in translation. Are there actual renderings of Marot's poem—perhaps among those discussed in the literature surrounding Hofstadter's book—that preserve every formal constraint yet diverge radically on the consoling gesture or emotional register of the original, which would suggest that form underdetermines meaning? Conversely, are there loose, free-verse translations that seem to capture precisely the modal attitude and social act of consolation despite discarding rhyme and meter? I also want to know whether the thesis risks unfalsifiability: if a defender can always claim that any successful loose translation "really" preserved a hidden formal structure, then the view may have insulated itself into emptiness, and I want an assessment of whether that insulation is a problem for a claim of this kind. Please distinguish defeaters that would be fatal from those that would merely be awkward, say what a committed defender of the modal-filter thesis would themselves concede as counting against them, and rank the vulnerabilities by how decisively they would move someone holding this view.

Conclude with a single line beginning Verdict: that summarizes whether the modal-filter thesis is well-insulated against counterexample and whether that insulation, if present, undermines its value as a theory of poetic translation.
