I am writing a feature on mind and identity for a science and ideas magazine. Douglas Hofstadter has proposed that the self is essentially a pattern that need not be confined to one brain and can persist, in part, within others who internalise its structures. Consider now a hypothetical technology—direct bidirectional cortical linking between two brains—that allows continuous sharing of memories, reasoning pathways and affective states, so that mental patterns genuinely circulate rather than merely being observed. Given John Locke's theory that personal identity consists in the continuity of consciousness and memory, how would he likely judge whether one "I" could be distributed across such a coupled pair? Would he treat them as a single person, as two persons with intertwined but distinct consciousnesses, or as a case that breaks his framework? Please respond in British English, grounding your answer in Locke's philosophical commitments without invoking neuroscientific findings unavailable in his era.
