I'm helping my cousin organize this weird municipal workshop and she keeps asking me to check if her jury's rulings are actually consistent. So far they've said yes to letting citizens take a quick civics exam to get double voting weight on constitutional amendments, but hard no to letting property owners vote more on tax bills. They also approved giving climate scientists extra seats specifically on the carbon board, but rejected letting doctors sit with extra weight on school boards. Then they liked random selection for the neighborhood zoning council, but said that's absurd for federal budgets. And they shot down letting only seniors vote on pension reform, yet said it's totally fine to require an actual engineering license to sit on the bridge-safety panel.

Now there's this new proposal for "epistemic vouchers" — you can hand your vote on one specific issue over to an acredited expert, but you can reclaim it anytime, so it's never a permanent surrender. The pilot case is a regional water-rights dispute that mixes heavy hydrological modeling with indiginous land-use practices that don't map onto standard credentials. My cousin wants to know: if the jury wants to stay true to themselves, should they approve the voucher system here? Which of thier old rulings is the closest analog — the carbon-board experts, the civics-weighted voters, or something else? And if thier past decisions genuinely don't force one answer, what's the hidden principle they'd be locking in with each choice? She also wants one further case that would actually split the two rules they might be running, though I'm not totally sure what that'd look like.
