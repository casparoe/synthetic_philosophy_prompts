I spent much of last evening in a long and rather exhausting argument with a friend about George Eliot's religious views, and the discussion has left me unable to settle a precise disagreement I witnessed earlier between two members of my philosophy of literature reading group. They were debating whether Eliot's well-documented rejection of orthodox Christianity — her refusal of personal immortality, divine intervention, and petitionary prayer — amounts to a complete elimination of theological commitment or merely to its relocation into her ethics of sympathy and moral determinism. I would like you to adjudicate their exchange move by move, rating each step from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification, saying who is right about which specific point, identifying what both miss, and indicating who is more right overall. I am not asking for bibliographic references or a reading list; I want conceptual adjudication of the arguments as actually made.

Here is the exchange as I heard it. (1) The first speaker, drawing on Middlemarch, argued that Eliot's secularism is total: she replaces God with interdependent human community and with the natural law of moral consequence, leaving no residue of worship or transcendence. Sympathy, on this view, is simply refined evolutionary social bonding, and her deterministic belief that character is formed by heredity and environment rules out any notion of unearned grace. (2) The second speaker replied that this ignores the functional architecture of sympathy in Adam Bede and Middlemarch: it operates precisely like Christian agape, arriving unbidden to transform both giver and receiver, and Eliot's essays describe human interdependence with the emotional vocabulary of reverence and duty that once belonged to religious worship. She has therefore not eliminated theology but secularized it, redirecting devotional energy toward the human collective. (3) The first speaker answered that this confuses available metaphor with underlying metaphysics: Eliot uses religious language because it is culturally inherited, but her deterministic framework leaves no room for the uncaused, transformative intervention that grace requires. If sympathy is causally determined by education and environment, then exhorting people to expand it is incoherent — like urging water to flow uphill — and only makes sense if she secretly presupposes a normative order independent of natural causation. (4) The second speaker said this proves her point: Eliot's determinism should undermine moral exhortation, yet she relentlessly urges readers to widen their sympathy. The only reconciliation is that she presupposes a moral law with the authority of divine command, an order that demands obedience regardless of causal predisposition. Thus her secular humanism is philosophically unstable; it requires the theological backbone it claims to have removed.

Please examine each of these four moves in turn. For each, say whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right but flawed, or clearly right, with brief reasoning. Then assess who wins on the central issue, whether the disagreement exposes a genuine and unresolved tension in Eliot's thought that neither speaker resolves, and whether there is a coherent third position — one that preserves both her determinism and her moral urgency without smuggling in theology — that both are missing.
