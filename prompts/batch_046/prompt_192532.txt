I'm preparing a position paper for an advanced philosophy of mind seminar, and after a long argument with a friend about whether phenomenal properties can survive physicalist scrutiny without collapsing into incoherence, I want an internal consistency check before I present. Here is where I have landed. First, I reject the Chase-Sanborn argument that qualia are incoherent, yet I accept their framing premise that any genuinely phenomenal property must be individuated independently of functional or causal-relational features, since otherwise the hard problem dissolves into an easy one. Second, I hold that phenomenal concepts are demonstrative in a strong, non-descriptive sense: they pick out qualitative character directly without describing it, and this direct reference is what makes physical descriptions seem perpetually incomplete. Third, I endorse a restricted form of representationalism about experience: the intentional content of every conscious state is fully representational and physically explicable, but I insist there remains a thin, non-representational qualitative residue that accompanies this content and is not itself about anything. Fourth, I deny that the zombie argument establishes anything metaphysically, because conceivability mediated by phenomenal concepts is not a reliable guide to possibility; but fifth, I maintain that inverted-spectrum scenarios are genuinely conceivable in a way that reveals real structural features of phenomenal space, specifically that chromatic and affective relations are not fixed by physical or functional relations alone. I also want to hold, sixth, that if this residual qualitative character is real, then it must be purely intrinsic, yet I simultaneously think that purely intrinsic properties cannot be individuated without some relational frame, which seems to push me back toward the incoherence I wanted to avoid.

Please audit these commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look like conflicts that dissolve on closer inspection. For each real tension, rank it by severity, identify the cheapest revision that restores consistency, and say what that revision costs me in explanatory power or intuitive plausibility. In particular, I am unsure whether my acceptance of the Chase-Sanborn framing premise undermines my rejection of their conclusion, and whether my dismissal of zombie conceivability is compatible with my claim that inverted-spectrum conceivability reveals something substantive about phenomenal structure. Do not treat every pairing as problematic; I need an audit that discriminates between deep conflicts and surface friction. Finally, tell me which single commitment, if abandoned, would clean up the largest number of tensions with the least damage to the overall view, and whether giving it up forces me into a position I have explicitly rejected elsewhere.
