At a philosophy discussion group, two members—Juno and Elias—debated a modified version of Parfit's hitchhiker case. The scenario they considered was this: a traveler needs to cross a dangerous valley, and a guide offers to lead her across at no charge, but only if she makes a sincere promise to pay $100 once they reach the other side. Once safely across, she has no further use for the guide and no enforcement mechanism compels payment, so paying appears locally irrational. However, before departure she can take a one-time, irreversible "binding vow" pill that chemically induces an overwhelming psychological compulsion to honor any promise she makes. The catch is that the pill does not discriminate: for the next year, she will feel compelled to honor even trivial promises—such as "I'll call you back" to casual acquaintances—costing her time, social flexibility, and in some cases tangible opportunities. The guide can observe whether she has taken the pill, but cannot observe her mental state after arrival.

Juno opened by arguing that taking the pill is clearly rational. The value of crossing the valley exceeds the expected aggregate cost of honoring trivial promises over the following year. Once across, paying the $100 is psychologically unavoidable, which makes her original promise credible to the guide and secures the ride. She concluded that this is precisely Parfit's point: it can be rational to cause oneself to perform an act that will be locally irrational when the time comes.

Elias replied that this misses what is actually interesting about the original hitchhiker problem. In Parfit's version, the irrationality is unavoidable at the moment of decision because one's disposition has already been fixed; the rational choice concerns whether to have that disposition in the first place, but the later act is genuinely against one's interests with no spillover. In Juno's pill scenario, by contrast, the mechanism is an artificial tool chosen in advance, and the "irrational" act of paying is only one instance among many generated by the same device. If the spillover costs of honoring trivial promises over a year exceed the benefit of the ride, then installing the mechanism is globally irrational, and the case does not demonstrate that rationality requires irrationality—it merely shows that a faulty commitment device can have net negative value.

Juno countered that Elias draws a false distinction. Even in the original hitchhiker case, the decision to be a "promiser" is made ex ante; the pill only makes the psychological mechanism explicit and chemically guaranteed. The core structure is identical: one rationally chooses to become the kind of agent who will later do something that is, considered in isolation, contrary to her interests. The spillover promises are not an external malfunction but the price of credibility; if being credible requires being consistently reliable, then those "false positives" are part of the package being purchased.

Elias responded that Juno has smuggled in a normative premise about the value of character. Prudential rationality concerns advancing one's own interests, not cultivating an abstract trait like "reliability." If honoring a trivial promise causes her to miss a job interview or damage a useful relationship, then that particular act is locally irrational, and multiplying such acts through the pill makes the overall choice imprudent. He added that Juno cannot rescue the case by claiming the spillover builds "social capital," because that is an empirical bet that may fail, and rationality should depend on expected outcomes, not on the hope that being compulsively honest will eventually pay off.

Juno then argued that Elias treats the spillover costs as pure losses, but they should be internalized into the value of being trustworthy. A person who honors only weighty promises and breaks trivial ones is not fully credible; the guide's willingness to help depends on observing a general disposition, not a selectively applied one. Therefore, the "false positives" are not bugs but features: they constitute the very credibility that makes the ex-ante promise work. Without them, the pill would not convince the guide, and the ride would not be offered.

Elias concluded that this proves too much. If any local cost can be rebranded as a "feature" of a valuable global trait, then the concept of local irrationality collapses, and Parfit's puzzle dissolves into triviality. The interesting claim was that a specific, isolated act can be rational to perform despite being locally against one's interests because of its connection to a prior rational choice. Once that connection is expanded into a year-long pattern of compelled behavior, we are no longer adjudicating a single rational-irrational pair; we are weighing one large bundle of costs against one large benefit, which is ordinary instrumental reasoning, not rational irrationality.
