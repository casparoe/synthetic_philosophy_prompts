I'm writing this for my philosophy discussion group at school; we've been working through the idea of supererogation—actions that are morally good but not strictly required, like volunteering for a dangerous rescue when no duty compels you. I want to apply this to Frodo Baggins's decision at the Council of Elrond in The Lord of the Rings, where he volunteers to carry the Ring to Mount Doom. Most readings treat this as the textbook case of supererogation: a small hobbit taking on a burden that no one can demand of him. But I think the text resists that framing in at least two ways that make the case philosophically interesting rather than just illustrative.

First, there's the problem of role and vocation: once Frodo accepts the quest, the narrative seems to treat the journey not as an ongoing voluntary excess but as a kind of assigned destiny—Gandalf treats him as chosen for the burden, Galadriel addresses him as an instrument of a larger design, and the entire providential structure of Middle-earth suggests he was meant for this suffering. If supererogation requires that the agent could have refused without moral failing, does Frodo's choice collapse into a hidden form of obligation? Second, there's the failure at the Crack of Doom: Frodo ultimately claims the Ring and fails to destroy it, and the quest succeeds only through Gollum's intervention. This raises what I want to call the problem of incomplete supererogation—can an action still count as going beyond duty if the agent ultimately fails to accomplish the good they risked everything for, leaving only the suffering and moral cost without the corresponding achievement? I want a reading that takes both Tolkien's narrative logic and the philosophical structure of supererogation seriously.

Specifically, I'm asking whether the work ultimately vindicates the concept of supererogation or undermines it: does Tolkien dramatize that going beyond duty is noble but ultimately insufficient without grace or fate? Or does the text show that what looks like supererogation is actually a concealed duty once one understands one's place in a larger moral order? I'd like an analysis that doesn't reduce the novel to a philosophical example, but also doesn't distort the concept of supererogation to make it fit the plot. In particular, I want to know where the honest critic has to admit that the philosophical framework misses something about Frodo's experience—perhaps the way suffering transforms the agent regardless of success—and what, if anything, the story adds to the debate about whether moral heroes are admirable or merely self-destructive.
