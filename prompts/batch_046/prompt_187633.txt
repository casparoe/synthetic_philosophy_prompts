Write a polished dialogue between two equally competent philosophers—Dr. Mara Chen and Dr. Jonas Reed—presenting opposing interpretations of Quine's naturalism with respect to moral values. Set the scene at a small, invitation-only workshop on ontological commitment and normative discourse, where both speakers have just heard a paper arguing that Quine's criterion of being the value of a bound variable should apply uniformly across domains. Chen should defend a stringent Quinean anti-realism about moral properties: she should argue that moral predicates, unlike mathematical or physical ones, fail the indispensability test because they do not figure in the explanatory machinery of our best empirical theories, and that Quine's Deweyan pragmatism allows us to retain moral talk as a socially useful coordination device without granting it ontological weight. Reed should oppose her by contending that Quine's holism blurs the boundary Chen wants to draw—specifically, that moral judgments about harm, fairness, and obligation are so deeply interwoven with our social-scientific explanations of cooperation and conflict that excising them would damage the predictive and explanatory coherence of our overall web of belief.

To keep the exchange genuinely dialectical rather than parallel monologues, require that they directly engage each other's points across at least three rounds: Chen should concede that Quine's rejection of the analytic-synthetic distinction makes moral beliefs revisable in principle, but insist that this revisability differs in kind from empirical revision; Reed should concede that indispensability alone does not entail mind-independent reality, yet press Chen on whether her exclusion of moral values commits her to an implausibly sharp fact-value separation that Quine's own naturalism was meant to dissolve. Crucially, have them probe a niche conceptual tension: whether Quine's disquotational ascent—his strategy for treating propositional attitudes without reifying mental entities—can be analogously applied to normative commitments, and whether doing so would either collapse moral discourse into mere behavioral prediction or inadvertently commit Quine to an ontology of "normative states" he explicitly sought to avoid. The dialogue should be formal in tone, avoid strawman representations, and conclude without either speaker having clearly capitulated.
