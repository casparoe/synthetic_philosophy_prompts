Following a long argument with a friend over Keynes's philosophy of belief, I am struggling to resolve what appears to be a stark contradiction. She observed that in A Treatise on Probability Keynes treats many probabilities as non-numerical and orders them only by logical relations and weight of argument, and that in his 1937 essay he describes long-term economic expectations as fundamentally uncertain, admitting no statistical calculus. From this she inferred that Keynes must have held rational action to be possible only under measurable risk, relegating uncertainty to the realm of pure intuition or animal spirits; consequently, she argued, his own speculative investment conduct was irrational by the very epistemic standards he advanced. I countered by invoking the principle of limited variety, which seems to permit inductive reasoning without precise frequencies, but she retorted that this principle smuggles in the frequentist assumptions Keynes officially rejected. Please reply in exactly five numbered points, each no more than two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: clarify whether the principle of limited variety requires a hidden frequentist basis; explain how Keynes distinguishes reasoning under true uncertainty from both risk-calculation and blind intuition; identify the false presupposition in my friend's claim that non-measurable probabilities preclude rational conduct; show how Keynes's investment behaviour actually illustrates the operation of weight of argument; and state whether the tension is a genuine defect in his epistemology or merely a misreading that conflates his Treatise logic with his macroeconomic rhetoric.
