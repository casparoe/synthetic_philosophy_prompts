I serve on our city's Public Memory and Civic Ethics Commission, and we are divided over a proposal that has forced me to think about the problem of dirty hands in politics, not in the familiar executive form but in the institutional and commemorative register. A private foundation has applied to install a bronze relief in the central plaza honoring Elias Vorn, the industrialist who in the 1920s designed and funded both the municipal waterworks and the free public library that remain in use. Recently reviewed municipal archives confirm that Vorn personally ordered the demolition of a working-class settlement — without compensation or relocation assistance — to clear the reservoir site. The foundation insists the relief celebrate civic infrastructure alone and carry no reference to the eviction; the descendants' association demands rejection, arguing that any institutional endorsement sanitizes coercion and makes our commission morally complicit. The mayor's office urges approval with only a minimal plaque, contending that democratic governance requires shared public symbols and that rejecting every figure with a morally ambiguous record amounts to a different kind of dirty-handed act: imposing presentist purity at the cost of civic cohesion and historical complexity.

Our charter requires the commission to issue a concrete recommendation — approval, approval with a mandatory curatorial condition, or rejection coupled with an alternative commemorative proposal — rather than abstaining, and we must circulate our written opinion to the city council within ten days. I am drafting the memo for our Thursday meeting. It should recommend one of these courses explicitly, include a realistic timeline for implementation or revision, and treat the strongest opposing argument with intellectual honesty rather than dismissing it as bad faith. If the recommendation is approval with conditions, the memo must engage the descendants' claim that symbolic endorsement is not neutral historical judgment but an active form of institutional complicity; if the recommendation is rejection, it must engage the mayor's claim that democratic bodies sometimes must tolerate moral ambiguity in public memory to avoid the greater harm of civic fragmentation. The reasoning should not appeal to a non-existent legal ordinance governing commemorative art, but should work through whether a body like ours — possessing symbolic authority without executive power — can avoid getting its hands dirty regardless of which option it chooses, and what that implies for the decision we ought to make.
