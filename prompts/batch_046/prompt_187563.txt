I work as a software engineer without any formal training in philosophy, and I keep running into a conceptual tension in how technology firms treat collective work that I cannot resolve with the vocabulary I have.

Consider the institutional pattern that is standard in large software organisations and open-source foundations. In agile ceremonies—sprint retrospectives, post-mortems, and blameless reviews—the team is addressed as a single subject: official reports state that “we underestimated the integration risk,” shared ownership of failure is affirmed, and corrective actions are sometimes framed as collective commitments. At the same time, the organisation’s legal, human-resources, and engineering-accountability frameworks decompose every output into individually attributable units. Performance reviews rank engineers separately; version-control logs assign each commit to a named author; contributor licence agreements require individual signatories to certify provenance line by line; and post-incident documentation ultimately assigns named owners to each remediation task, even when the feature in question was designed in a collaborative workshop and refined through pair programming.

The two frameworks are not treated as temporary exceptions to one another; they operate continuously and are defended by management as complementary. Yet they appear to presuppose opposite answers to a basic question: is the development team a genuine agent capable of shared intention and joint responsibility, or is it merely an aggregate of individuals whose intentions happen to overlap?

What theory of collective intentionality and joint action does this pattern presuppose? Does it require a non-reductionist view—that groups can literally share intentions and bear responsibility in ways that are not reducible to the states of their members—or can a reductionist or summative account explain the collective language of the retrospective without attributing real agency to the team? Finally, indicate which concrete institutional features—the retrospective format, the individual commit attribution, the named remediation owners, or the contributor agreements—would need to change if the underlying theory were replaced by its principal rival.
