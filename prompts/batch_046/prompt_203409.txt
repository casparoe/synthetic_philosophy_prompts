I run a philosophy workshop on comparative metaphysics, about 12 grad students, one 80-minute session. Design an in-class activty that makes them feel Jain anekantavada — not the watered-down "everyone has a point" take, but the actual logical pressure of syadvada and saptabhangi. They should discover that holding contradictory descriptions together isn't just humility but a structured constraint on assertion.

Set up a tribunal game: the group gets four expert reports about the same fictional artifact (a "layered relic" from an invented site called Nayapura) that describe it with genuinely incompatible properties — physical, mental, temporal, relational. They have to write a collective verdict, but every sentence must use one of the seven modal qualifers (syat, etc.). Roles: four naya advocates, one syad referee who docks points for unqualified speech, one scribe. Hidden scoring: they win only if their final report keeps all four descriptions without collapsing them into one true/false judgment.

Give rules, paper/card materials, a 20-40-20 timeline, and the debrief mapping moments to the Jain distinction between epistemic limitation and ontological multi-sidedness. Be frank: what part of the view — that reality itself literally contains contradictory properties — the activty can't reproduce and you'll have to lecture on. Also say what'll go wrong predictably the first time (probably students try to "solve" the contradiction). No tech needed.
