I'm a clinical ethics consultant who sits on a regional hospital network's behavioral-policy review board, and I evaluate proposed patient-facing interventions roughly twice a month—usually under a fifteen-minute time constraint, with a non-specialist administrator present, and with the understanding that my written recommendation will be filed but I am not the final signatory. I want a reusable decision procedure, translated from the theoretical literature on paternalism and nudging but stripped of jargon, that lets me classify whether a proposed clinical "choice architecture" remains within permissible libertarian paternalism or crosses into impermissible hard paternalism in a healthcare context. The procedure must take the form of an ordered checklist or flowchart in words, with explicit stop points, and it must flag exactly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedural rules rather than genuine deductions.

The conceptual work should center on three synthesized pressure points that standard checklists tend to collapse: the reversibility threshold—whether a patient can undo the default without navigating bureaucratic friction; the transparency asymmetry—whether the patient knows they are being guided but not how the framing is calibrated; and the vulnerability coefficient—whether the patient's clinical state, such as acute pain, cognitive impairment, or emotional crisis, makes the nudge exploitative rather than merely guiding. Apply the finished procedure to three hypothetical proposals: (a) an opt-out seasonal flu vaccination protocol for elderly inpatients where refusal requires speaking to a charge nurse; (b) a default enrollment in a palliative-care information session that sends a personalized message framing non-attendance as "choosing to proceed without support"; and (c) one case where the procedure yields an answer I will actively dislike—specifically, a well-designed, highly effective intervention that the checklist still rejects because of the vulnerability coefficient. For each case, show precisely where in the sequence the decision lands and which pressure point does the decisive work. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot determine—such as long-term erosion of trust or population-level autonomy effects—and describe the misfires it is likely to produce when used by reviewers with different baseline intuitions about patient autonomy.
