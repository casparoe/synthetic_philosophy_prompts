I am drafting a short philosophical essay that takes up the well-known comparison between severely torturing one individual and distributing a barely perceptible harm—say, a single dust speck in the eye—across an enormous population, with the aggregate of those tiny harms apparently exceeding the suffering of the one tortured victim. Rather than rehearsing standard arguments about linear utility aggregation or lexical priorities for the worst suffering, I want to defend a more specific structural claim: that our resistance to aggregation in this case tracks not the total quantity of discomfort but a feature of how harm is situated within moral experience. I propose that harm operates in moral reasoning as what I will call a located burden, by which I mean that for a harm to carry its full moral weight it must be attributable to a specific locus of experience in a way that permits narrative recognition by the affected subject. The torture victim can integrate her suffering into a coherent autobiographical narrative of injury, whereas each recipient of a dust speck experiences only a momentary irritation that never crystallizes into a recognized harm, both because it is drowned out by the background noise of ordinary experience and because no social mechanism exists to collect and acknowledge these micro-injuries as a shared burden. From this I derive a principle: aggregation is morally permissible only when the aggregated harms can be represented back to the affected subjects as a collective injury rather than as an isolated statistical residue. This explains why we reject the dust-speck scenario without committing to a lexical threshold against all large-scale suffering, and it yields a counterintuitive result: if we could design an institutional mechanism that made each recipient aware that their dust speck was part of a deliberate global distribution, and if that awareness allowed them to recognize themselves as co-victims of a single distributed harm, then our moral intuition might shift toward accepting the aggregation. The essay then considers an objection that this view collapses into subjectivism, making harm depend on recognition rather than on objective physical states, which would be problematic for unconscious or non-human subjects. I respond by distinguishing between the metaphysical reality of harm and its moral salience, arguing that the latter is partly constituted by recognitional conditions without denying that physical damage can occur in their absence. I also examine whether the framework applies to temporal rather than spatial distributions, asking whether a single person experiencing one dust speck per day for eighty years accumulates a harm comparable to torture, and I argue that temporal continuity within one life provides a kind of implicit narrative integration that spatial dispersion across lives lacks, which is why chronic low-grade irritation can feel genuinely burdensome in a way that distributed specks do not. I would appreciate your comments on the coherence of the distinction between located burden and mere statistical harm, on whether the institutional-awareness counterfactual actually undermines rather than supports my position, and on whether I have overlooked an obvious objection from discussions of collective harm or distributive justice.
