In discussions of Orwell's politics of language, people in my seminar keep reaching for an analogy: a degraded political vocabulary is like a rusted hand-tool — it fails to perform its proper function, and repeated clumsy use deforms the user's grip. They deploy it to license a specific reformist conclusion: that stripping away euphemism and dead metaphor is both necessary and sufficient for recovering clear political judgment, because bad thinking is mainly a mechanical failure of the instrument rather than a strategic choice by speakers or a feature of institutions. I want to test this against Orwell's own construction of Newspeak, which is not sloppy English but a deliberately restricted code with a closed vocabulary and engineered grammar — closer to a programming language with limited operators than to a dull blade. Audit the rusted-tool analogy under this framing: give the explicit correspondence table, identify where the mapping breaks and which breaks matter for the reformist conclusion, expose the causal and normative assumptions the analogy smuggles in about the relation between vocabulary and belief, state whether the conclusion that linguistic hygiene is sufficient survives, and suggest whether an analogy to architecture, law, or code does better. Answer in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
