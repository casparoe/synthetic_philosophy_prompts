I am a retired natural scientist who has recently begun studying philosophy, and I have come to hold a sharpened version of Moritz Schlick's claim that life's meaning resides not in purpose or lasting achievement but in playful, self-forgetting absorption—those episodes in which an activity becomes its own end. Specifically, I maintain that scientific work contributes to a meaningful life only when it takes this autotelic form; that the instrumental, career-driven research that dominates contemporary laboratories is not merely less meaningful but actively undermines meaning; and that because these episodes are non-cumulative and leave no residue, a life abundant in them but devoid of enduring legacy is preferable to one of sustained instrumental labor that yields lasting results but few such moments. What would count against this view? I ask for decisive objections—whether ethical (concerning commitment and sacrifice), psychological (whether such absorption presupposes prior instrumental structure), or philosophical (whether the view collapses into hedonism or renders progress unintelligible)—distinguished from merely awkward ones, ranked by strength, together with an honest assessment of whether defenders can qualify the position to escape them without rendering it empty.
