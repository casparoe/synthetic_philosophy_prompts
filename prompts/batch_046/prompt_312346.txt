Analyse the 1977 Skokie march controversy — in which the National Socialist Party of America sought to demonstrate through a Chicago suburb with a substantial population of Holocaust survivors, prompting the American Civil Liberties Union to defend their permit while local authorities and residents sought to prohibit the assembly — as a case study in Karl Popper’s paradox of tolerance. Rather than offering a generic exposition, map the paradox precisely onto the principal actors: the organisers, the survivors and residents who opposed the march, the ACLU representatives who insisted upon its legal permissibility, and the municipal officials who initially denied the permit. In your analysis, introduce a distinction between doctrinal tolerance (the willingness to allow the articulation of an intolerant ideology) and performative tolerance (the willingness to permit an act that is not merely expressive but is itself a spatial and symbolic re-enactment of the harm the ideology advocates). Examine whether Popper’s warning — that unlimited tolerance must eventually destroy itself — provides any determinate guidance in cases where the intolerant act is not confined to propositional speech but takes the form of an embodied intrusion into a community that the doctrine explicitly targets for elimination. Assess whether the Skokie episode vindicates Popper’s claim that a tolerant society retains the right to suppress intolerant movements, or whether it instead exposes a structural ambiguity in his distinction between speech and action that makes the paradox more difficult to apply in practice than his formulation suggests. Finally, determine whether the case should be read as a confirmation of the paradox, as an anomaly that the framework fails to accommodate, or as an occasion that forces a refinement of the concept of tolerance itself.
