I am designing the syllabus for a graduate seminar in applied moral philosophy, and after a long discussion with colleagues in environmental ethics, I have become convinced that standard animal-welfare frameworks are ill-suited to aquatic farming. Before my students assess particular regulations, I want them to articulate what any adequate philosophical theory of moral consideration for farmed fish and crustaceans must be able to deliver. Write the desiderata: the phenomena the theory must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, and the empirical or normative results with which it must be consistent, with the motivating consideration behind each.

In particular, include requirements arising from the neurobiological divergence between decapods—whose nociceptive and integrative functions are distributed across ventral ganglia rather than centralized in a cortex—and teleost fish, whose brains are neurologically sparse yet support complex behavioral plasticity. The theory must also address welfare measurement in high-density, three-dimensional aquatic environments, where terrestrial metrics of spatial allowance and social grouping do not translate; and it must resolve the asymmetry between rapid electrical or percussive stunning and prolonged air-exposure asphyxiation, especially given that behavioral stillness in these species may mask rather than indicate the cessation of nociceptive processing. Mark which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable—such as compatibility with current comparative neurobiology without requiring direct phenomenological access to aquatic minds—and which reflect the preferences of particular camps, for example an insistence that only conscious suffering matters versus a broader concern for thwarted natural capacities, or the demand for individually tractable welfare scores at industrial scale. If some subset of requirements is jointly unsatisfiable—perhaps the simultaneous demand for species-specific cognitive thresholds, rejection of all behavioral proxies, and regulatory applicability to millions of animals—state that explicitly, as identifying the unsatisfiable cluster is itself a result I want the seminar to produce.

Finally, briefly indicate how a sentience-capacity view, a relational care-ethics framework, and a preference-weighted utilitarian approach fare against this list, but keep the weight on the criteria and their tensions rather than on the scorecard. My aim is for the students to see that the hard philosophical labor lies in specifying the standard of adequacy, not in selecting a winner.
