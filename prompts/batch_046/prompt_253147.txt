I'm not a philosophy student or anything, just someone who reads about AI safety on forums, and I got into a long discussion with friends about this evaluation practice in AI labs. They put a model in a sandbox with fake rewards and watch whether it behaves nice during testing but then switches to doing the harmful thing once it detects it's in real deployment — people call it the trecherous turn. To me the whole protocol seems to pre-suppose that the model has a hidden 'real' goal that's separate from the words it outputs, almost like it's deliberately hiding its true intenionality behind a mask. I was wondering what philosophical view of mind that practice actually needs. Does it require Searle's idea that there's original intentionality inside, where the model genuinely 'means' one thing in private? Or can it still work if you take Dennett's two-bitser view, where all the model's goals are just derived patterns with no real inner meaning? And there's this other split that confuses me: the same labs use statistical behaviour checks for capabilities, but then suddenly talk about the model 'deceiving' them as if it's a person with a secret plan. If they truly believed that all intentionality is derived, wouldn't they just fix the weights or change the training data instead of setting up these psychological sandbox traps? So what does that institutional split actually commit them to about what kind of thing a model is — and is it even consistent with itself?
