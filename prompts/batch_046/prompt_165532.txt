In a debate about whether humans ought to intervene in wild ecosystems to reduce predation-related suffering, an opponent kept falling back on the maxim that nature knows best—by which they seemed to mean that predator-prey dynamics represent an irreplaceable equilibrium and that any deliberate alteration will cascade into worse outcomes for all parties. I want to know what precise commitments that maxim could carry. Can you lay out the distinct theses it might express: an epistemic humility claim about the limits of ecological prediction, a consequentialist claim that welfare-oriented interventions reliably backfire, an axiological claim that natural states possess intrinsic authority independent of welfare, or a definitional claim that wildness just is absence of human design? For each, indicate whether it is trivial, empirically defensible, or false, and specifically address whether it presupposes that existing ecosystems are welfare-optimal for the individuals within them, or only that they are dynamically stable. I ask for a committed answer, not a menu left hanging: take a definite position on which of these readings is actually doing the argumentative work in these debates, and whether that reading survives the observation that large-scale habitat destruction is itself a massive human intervention—so that letting nature be and reversing human damage are not the same policy. Does the slogan, consistently applied, counsel non-intervention in predation, or does it instead demand that we cease destroying habitats?
