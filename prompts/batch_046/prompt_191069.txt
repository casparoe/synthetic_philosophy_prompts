I have been discussing the justification of punishment with colleagues who work in applied ethics, and we have noticed that the standard philosophical examples — the innocent scapegoat, the negligent factory owner — tend to make the divergence between retributivism and consequentialism look cruder than it actually is in real institutional practice. To push past that, I would like you to construct a thought experiment that isolates a more subtle point of tension.

Please design a single, realistic paragraph describing a scenario within a professional institution — for instance, a hospital licensing board, a university research-integrity committee, or a regulatory tribunal — in which both theories agree that some formal sanction is warranted, yet the retributivist demands a substantially harsher outcome than the consequentialist recommends. The retributivist severity must not stem from an obvious 'eye for an eye' rule; rather, it should emerge from a genuinely defensible commitment to proportionality between culpability and punishment, particularly where the wrongdoer has exploited a specific fiduciary role and acted with epistemic recklessness rather than mere negligence. Meanwhile, the consequentialist's relative leniency must flow from an optimal general rule that properly weighs rehabilitation, specific deterrence, and the preservation of institutional trust — not from a lazy or self-defeating policy. After presenting the scenario, walk through the verdict each theory delivers and why, pinpoint the feature of the case that does the work (for example, high culpability combined with genuinely plausible rehabilitation), and explain whether the divergence collapses if that feature is removed. Finally, indicate how a defender of the harsher, retributivist position would respond to the objection that they are disregarding socially beneficial outcomes. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to particular philosophers by name, and avoid any exotic or science-fictional stipulations.
