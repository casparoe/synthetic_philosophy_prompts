I am revising the syllabus for my upper-division seminar on responsibility and moral luck, and I have noticed that students invariably revert to the standard vehicular examples—drunk driving that either does or does not result in a crash—when asked to engineer their own cases. To push them past these familiar templates, I want an original thought experiment that introduces what I am tentatively calling "workflow constitutive luck": a form of moral luck that arises not from brute causal fortune, but from the interaction between an agent's fixed, non-culpable psychological disposition and a contingent organizational sequencing feature that determines whether that disposition manifests in actual harm.

Please construct a realistic, one-paragraph scenario set in a hospital or clinical research workplace—no science fiction, no exotic physics, no perfect predictors. The case should feature two agents, A and B, who share an identical negligent mental state: for instance, both suffer the same cognitive overload and consequently fail to perform an identical cross-check on a dosage or protocol parameter. The only difference must be an institutional structural contingency—such as the temporal ordering of an automated verification protocol relative to a shift handover, or the sequencing of two independent audit trails—so that Agent A's omission reaches the patient or trial subject, while Agent B's is intercepted before harm occurs. The scenario must be engineered so that a theorist who holds that moral blame tracks outcomes assigns greater blame to A than to B, whereas a theorist who holds that blame tracks only the quality of the will assigns them precisely equal blame. Crucially, the divergence in outcomes must flow entirely from that organizational sequencing feature, not from any difference in choice, knowledge, effort, or awareness; if that feature is standardized or removed, the outcomes for A and B must collapse into perfect sameness.

After presenting the scenario, please walk through the verdict each theoretical position delivers and explain why, then identify precisely which institutional feature is doing the philosophical work. You should also explain what happens to the case if that feature is eliminated, and how an advocate of the mental-state-only view would attack the scenario as question-begging or as smuggling outcome-dependence into the description of the agents' mental states. Please write your response in polished American English, avoid all bibliographic references or citations to specific texts, and do not include reading lists or suggestions for further literature, as I intend to use this directly for an in-class design exercise.
