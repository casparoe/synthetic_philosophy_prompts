You are a senior moral philosopher with particular expertise in Bernard Williams’s work on practical identity, the integrity objection to utilitarianism, and the problem of having one thought too many. I am a university student in an advanced course on moral psychology and the ethics of character. Before you answer, please take a moment to brainstorm the structure of Williams’s arguments—especially the role of ground projects, agent-centered permissions, and the corruption of practical reasoning—then think step by step and carefully.

Here are my reflective verdicts on several concrete cases. I want to know whether they cohere under a single precise principle, or whether consistency forces me to abandon one.

Case 1. George, a chemist deeply opposed to chemical-weapons research, is offered a lucrative position in that field. His family would suffer severely if he declines. My verdict: he should refuse. The refusal is not justified by a prior weighing of social harms against salary; rather, taking the job would violate his ground project and make him an agent of something he fundamentally rejects.

Case 2. Jim is captured with nineteen others; his captor offers to kill just one if Jim agrees, sparing the rest. If Jim refuses, all twenty may die. My verdict: Jim should not kill the one. Even if nineteen lives hang in the balance, performing the killing transforms him into the kind of agent who can do that for numbers, and that corruption of integrity is not canceled by the body count.

Case 3. A husband saves his drowning wife instead of a stranger in equal peril. My verdict: he should save her, but if his reasoning includes the explicit utilitarian calculation that this choice maximizes welfare weighted by relationship, then he has introduced one thought too many. The act remains right, but that specific justification corrupts the moral psychology that should govern it.

Case 4. A physician whose ground project is healing is asked to participate in a legal, pain-free euthanasia program for consenting terminal patients. Aggregate suffering would clearly fall. My verdict: she should refuse. Participating would fracture her practical identity by making her an agent of intentional killing in a domain where her ground project is preservation and cure.

Case 5. A climate archivist has spent three decades preserving primary records of a discredited regime. A donor offers complete digitization funding on the condition that she append a false historical framing she believes seriously distorts understanding. Acceptance means widespread access; refusal means physical decay within a decade and likely permanent loss. My gut says she should refuse, preserving the integrity of her curatorial project—yet I feel genuine unease, because unlike George, her refusal destroys the very objects her project exists to protect. At the same time, if she accepts and tells herself that the access justifies the distortion, she seems to exhibit exactly the corrupted reasoning Williams warns against.

My central question: Is there one formal principle of integrity—specifying the conditions under which an agent may or must protect her ground project against utility-maximizing demands, and specifying when practical reasoning becomes corrupted—that delivers verdicts 1 through 5 without ad hoc adjustments? State the principle precisely, map each clause onto the case that motivates it, and say what it predicts for an unlisted variant: a surgeon whose ground project is saving lives is asked to perform a non-therapeutic amputation on a healthy prisoner in order to harvest organs that would save five others.

If no single principle can accommodate all five verdicts cleanly, tell me honestly which one I should overturn and why. In particular, address whether case 5 genuinely belongs with cases 1–4, or whether my unease signals that integrity here conflicts with a different value—such as stewardship of collective goods or fiduciary duty to future scholars—that Williams’s framework does not fully capture.

Finally, assess briefly whether your proposed principle can be coherently extended to collective agents—for example, a research collective with a shared ground project of open science—without losing its determinacy.
