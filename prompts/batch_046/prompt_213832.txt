I'm preparing the opening remarks for a workshop titled "Comparative Memory After Political Violence," co-hosted by our university's history department and a visiting German research center on historical reconciliation. The audience is deliberately mixed: on one side are scholars of Germany's Vergangenheitsbewältigung, Japanese postwar commemorative practice, Turkish state archival policy, and American Southern memorial culture; on the other are local students, city officials, and community members from our town, which recently removed a Confederate monument and is now debating what should take its place. I want the remarks—about ten minutes—to do real conceptual work rather than serve as a polite welcome. Specifically, I want them structured around a framework I've been developing that distinguishes four grammars of public remembrance rather than treating "working through the past" as a universal procedure. In the confessional-archival grammar that characterizes much German practice, the state is positioned as perpetrator and public institutions as courtrooms of pedagogy; in the liturgical grammar visible in much Japanese postwar practice, remembrance operates through seasonal repetition and disciplined silence that resists assigning stable collective perpetrator status; in the statist-archival grammar of official Turkish memory, records are preserved selectively to maintain national continuity and manage rupture rather than to enact it; and in the American South, remembrance is currently split between a genealogical grammar of inherited family honor and an emerging civic-archival grammar that treats landscape as evidentiary. The philosophical problem I want the speech to stage is whether comparison across these grammars is possible without imposing an unjust universal standard—whether asking our town to "learn from Germany" already mistranslates our own conflict into foreign terms. The framework should actually constrain what can be said: if the grammars are genuinely incommensurable, then exporting a monument-removal model is conceptually confused; if they are comparable, then our local debate must acknowledge that "replacing a statue" means something structurally different in each grammar. Please write the remarks so that they land for the scholars—showing that I am not flattening differences—while remaining fully intelligible to the local audience, who are not theorists but are living inside one of these grammars right now. The speech should not ask anyone to apologize on behalf of ancestors, should not treat German success as an exportable blueprint, and should make clear that the conceptual framework, not decorative rhetoric, determines which proposals for our town's memorial space are coherent and which are category mistakes.
