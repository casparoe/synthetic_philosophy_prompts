I retired from field biology a couple years ago and lately I've been dipping into philosophy, though I keep getting sidetracked by how actual instutions treat work and rest. Take Japan: they have this legal category karoshi, death by overwork, and families can claim compensation but usually only if the deceased was clocking something like 80 to 100 hours of overtime per month. So there's an implicit line where work stops being hard and starts being lethal, and the law only steps in after you've crossed it. Then you have France with that 35-hour week law, which doesn't just say don't overwork for health reasons but seems to treat leisure time as a kind of social right that the state protects by capping labor. And then in a lot of American discourse shaped by the Protestant ethic, being unemployed carries a moral stain—like having a job is part of being a decent person, not just an economic fact. So we have one system that punishes extreme work post-mortem, one that distributes work-time as a collective good, and one that moralizes employment itself. What philosophy of human value or moral agency are these practices together presupposing? Do they all quietly assume work is a virtue and leisure is only recovery time, or is there a hidden rights-based view underneath? And are they realy consistent with each other, or do they rest on incompatible ideas about what a person owes society versus what society owes them?

If we replaced whatever implicit theory is doing the work here with a straightforward welfare account—where work is only instrumentally valuable and leisure is just another form of wellbeing—what exactly would have to change in these three institutional setups?
