I've been trying to pin down what variety of free will is actually worth wanting, and I have constructed an argument that seems to show that none of the familiar positions can deliver it. Here are the steps. (1) If free will is worth wanting, it must include what I will call source-authorship: not merely that my actions flow from my present character or reasons, but that I am the ultimate origin of the psychological sources—my deepest values, my reflective preferences—that produce those actions. (2) If those deepest values are themselves the product of upbringing, genetics, social conditioning, or any prior causal chain that I did not choose, then I am not their source; I merely inherit them, and any action flowing from them traces back to forces outside me. (3) So genuine source-authorship requires that I have authored my deepest values through an act of my own will. (4) But any act of willing one set of values over another must itself be guided by prior values, reasons, or criteria; if it is not guided by anything, then it is arbitrary noise rather than an exercise of authorship. (5) Therefore the values that guide the choice of my deepest values must also be ones that I have authored. (6) This launches a regress: either the chain terminates in values I did not choose, which breaks source-authorship at the base, or it continues infinitely, which no finite being can complete. (7) Hence no finite being can satisfy the condition of source-authorship. (8) The compatibilist attempts to halt the regress by saying that identification with, or wholehearted endorsement of, unchosen values is sufficient for freedom, but this abandons source-authorship and substitutes mere internal coherence, which was never the feature that made free will worth wanting in the first place. (9) The libertarian attempts to halt the regress by positing an uncaused, spontaneous act of will, yet an act that is uncaused by prior psychological states is not something the agent authors; it is a random occurrence that happens within her, leaving her no more in control than she is over a sudden sneeze. (10) Therefore both standard responses fail, and the only consistent position is that free will worth wanting requires a form of infinite self-creation that is impossible for any actual human being. I find this conclusion intolerable, and I am inclined to preserve both (1) and (8), since surrendering source-authorship feels like accepting a hollowed-out compatibilism that does not capture what I care about, yet I also reject (10). Where exactly does the argument slip? Is there a hidden equivocation between being the source of an action and being the source of every psychological precursor? Or is the flaw in the assumption that authorship must be transitive across levels? Please identify the step I should reject, explain why it looked plausible, and tell me what kind of freedom—if any—I am left with once I pay that price.
