I am a museum registrar, and a recent provenance audit—revealing contested chains of custody, forced sales, and unresolved restitution claims—has made the normative logic of ownership urgently personal to me. I work fluently with the vocabulary of registry practice: accession and deaccession, chain of title, custodial transfer, voluntary donation or purchase, forced alienation, and rectification of record. Could you restate Robert Nozick's entitlement theory of justice—including just acquisition, just transfer, and rectification of injustice, together with the Wilt Chamberlain-style argument that patterned distributive principles are undermined by voluntary exchange—entirely within that provenance vocabulary? Treat each principle and the counterexample as analogues to how objects enter a collection, move between owners through documented transactions, and are redistributed according to curatorial preference rather than chain of custody. Then provide a rigorous residue report: which normative claims translate cleanly into registry logic, which must be distorted to fit (for example, framing a voluntary exchange between collectors as disrupting a "pattern" of collection distribution), and which elements of Nozick's argument have no counterpart in how museums actually adjudicate title? I want to know whether the residue indicates that provenance practice is missing a conceptual resource, or whether Nozick's resistance to end-state patterns is already embedded in registrars' insistence that ownership follows documented history rather than desired outcomes.
