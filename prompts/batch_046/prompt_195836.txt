Next month I'm giving a 20-min paper at an interdisciplinary conf on political rhetoric. My thesis: "Western values" is coherent, but not as the grocery-list people use (democracy, free speech, secularism, capitalism). I think it's actually a shared institutional reflex — a habit of resolving value conflicts through procedural abstration, delegating authority to rules that don't specify outcomes, rather than through substantive hierarchy or kin-based obligation. You see it in common law reasoning, constitutional review, and even bureacratic ethics codes across the societies usually labeled Western, but it doesn't entail individualism or market ideology the way politicians claim. The audience is a critical theorist convinced the phrase is pure Orientalist branding, a comparative historian who knows Confucian legalist and Islamic administrative traditions inside out, an analytic philosopher of liberal legitimacy, and a foundation program officer whose org literally funds "Western values" initiatives. I need the objections in the order they'll actually come, phrased the way each of them would put it, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my own position that I should defend rather than hope they miss. Include the objection I'm praying nobody asks. Make it a prep sheet, not a polished defense — I want to know where my reply sounds softest, especially against the historian. Don't give me a bibliography; I won't have time to read anything new before the talk.
