As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of mind systematically, I am preparing a single ninety-minute session for a monthly salon of fellow retirees—mostly engineers and experimental scientists with no training in formal logic or philosophy of mind. I want them to understand Douglas Hofstadter’s Gödelian analogy in Gödel, Escher, Bach: specifically, the move from formal self-reference via Gödel numbering to the claim that self-aware minds are structured analogously as strange loops. I do not want the lecture itself; I want a teaching plan. Please construct the explanatory arc with the following elements.

First, the dependency order. What must be firmly in place before the audience can even grasp what formal self-reference is, and in particular, how should I introduce the distinction between token-reflexivity (a symbol occurring inside the very formula it denotes) and type-reflexivity (a system containing descriptions of its own rules) so that the second does not collapse into the first? State the sequence precisely and justify each dependency.

Second, the carrying example. I have in mind using a physical feedback interferometer—an apparatus that measures the path of its own laser beam—to model how a system can represent its own operation without requiring an external observer. Assess whether this example should carry the session, or whether it should be replaced; specify exactly what properties the example must possess (e.g., that representation remains internal and syntactic rather than semantic) and what properties it must lack (e.g., any implication that consciousness is literally an optical measurement). Defend your choice definitively.

Third, the misconception map. My audience will likely bring three errors: that self-reference necessarily involves an infinite homuncular regress; that Gödel’s incompleteness theorems apply directly to human reasoning; and that mental representation requires something like literal numerical encoding of syntax. Identify at which stage in your proposed sequence each misconception typically arises, and specify exactly where and how it should be headed off—without disrupting the flow.

Fourth, the check for understanding. Provide one precise question I can ask at the end that distinguishes someone who grasps the analogy as a structural parallel from someone who has merely followed the narrative; explain what a correct answer sounds like and what a wrong answer reveals.

Fifth, what to leave out. Identify one technical element—perhaps the full construction of Gödel numbering or the distinction between first- and second-order logic—that I should explicitly refuse to explain, state that refusal clearly to the audience, and explain why omitting it does not damage the analogy.

Please take a committed, definite position throughout: do not present a menu of options. Argue specifically that the analogy should be taught as metaphorically illuminating rather than literally descriptive of neural processes, and defend your choices about sequence, example, and omission with reasons rather than qualifications.
