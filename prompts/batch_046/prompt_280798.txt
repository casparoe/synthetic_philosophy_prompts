Modern hospice and palliative-care protocols routinely screen patients for “existential distress”—a felt loss of coherence, purpose, or significance in one’s life—and treat elevated scores as clinical symptoms warranting intervention, typically through psychotherapeutic techniques aimed at restoring subjective meaning, while remaining officially neutral on whether life possesses objective purpose or value. At the same time, these protocols treat physical pain and physiological harm as objective conditions to be relieved regardless of a patient’s philosophical outlook. What account of meaning in life does this institutional pattern presuppose? Must it assume that meaning is fundamentally a subjective psychological good—something like experienced coherence or purpose—rather than an objective relation between a life and genuinely valuable ends? Can an absurdist framework, which denies that life has objective meaning but also denies that this absence constitutes a harm, justify the same screening practices and therapeutic aims? Or does the practice implicitly commit to a hybrid view in which subjective meaning functions as a component of welfare while objective meaning is bracketed as metaphysically unsettled? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value and meaning embedded in the protocol, distinguishing what it strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and assess whether it is philosophically consistent with the medical system’s parallel insistence that physical suffering is an objective harm. Finally, specify what a palliative-care framework built on a thoroughly objective account of meaning, or on a consistently absurdist account, would have to change: which screening criteria, therapeutic goals, or claims of institutional neutrality would become incoherent, and which elements of current practice could survive unchanged?
