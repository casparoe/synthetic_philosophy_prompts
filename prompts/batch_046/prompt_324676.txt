I was arguing with my roommate about Arendt's report on the Eichmann trial last night — thrilling social life — and I ended up staking out a position that feels stronger than the usual pop-psych take. I'm saying the banality of evil isn't mainly a claim that Eichmann was personally dull or lacked opinions; it's a structural claim that modern bureaucratic organization can decouple moral agency from the visible consequences of one's actions so thoroughly that even a person with intense ideological commitments behaves with the same cliché-ridden, unthinking detachment Arendt described. The banality is in the architecture, not the soul, and it doesn't depend on thier being no monsters inside the system.

So what would actually count against this structural version? I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward: historical evidence about Eichmann's private motives that breaks the decoupling story, conceptual arguement that moral distance can't carry the explanatory weight I'm giving it, or comparative cases of bureaucrats in identical structures who clearly retained full moral awareness and acted differently. Which ones would a defender of this view have to concede are genuinely damaging, and which could they absorb just by refining the thesis? Also be straight with me — has this version been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that actually a problem for a claim that's meant to tell us something real about how organizations produce harm?
