Following a lengthy debate with a close friend about whether logical positivism can sustain any normative ethics at all, I have been trying to think through what Moritz Schlick—whose writings on the meaning of life center on the distinction between purposive labor and non-instrumental play—would make of a phenomenon that did not exist in his lifetime: the algorithmic optimization of leisure itself. Specifically, I am interested in how he might respond to contemporary engagement architectures that use predictive modeling to extend and intensify recreational activities, not by coercion but by dynamically calibrating rewards so that play never reaches a natural endpoint. Would Schlick regard this as a corruption of the pure, self-contained play that he takes to be the source of life's meaning, or might his anti-metaphysical commitments lead him to deny that there is any philosophically relevant difference between organically concluded play and algorithmically prolonged play, reducing the matter to observable behavioral patterns?

I want a careful reconstruction of his likely position that does not simply import modern vocabulary onto his texts, but genuinely extends his framework—particularly his claim that meaning arises when activity is pursued for its own sake rather than as a means—to this case. Please treat the strongest objection seriously: my friend argues that Schlick's verificationist theory of meaning strips him of the resources to distinguish genuine from mediated play at all, since both are empirically indistinguishable in their subjective reports. How might Schlick reply, and does that reply succeed?
