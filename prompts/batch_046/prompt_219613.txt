Construct a decision-tree taxonomy of historiographical stances on whether the labels “Middle Ages,” “Renaissance,” and “Scientific Revolution” denote real, bounded historical periods or are later retrospective constructions. Each internal node must be a concrete, answerable claim about a specific historical feature—such as whether the continuity of universities across 1400 undermines the idea of a Renaissance rupture, whether the diffusion of the printing press produced a qualitative discontinuity in knowledge transmission, whether the mathematization of nature between roughly 1600 and 1700 was continuous with medieval natural philosophy, or whether the fall of Constantinople in 1453 caused a discontinuity in European intellectual life—rather than a question about which school of historiography one belongs to. The tree should be built first from the logical space of yes/no answers to these feature-claims, and only then should named or unnamed positions be placed at the leaves.

The tree must include at least these four leaves: (1) a strong discontinuity view that treats all three periods as real epochs with essential, non-overlapping characteristics; (2) a continuous-change view that rejects all three as misleading reifications of gradual development; (3) a selective-realist view that accepts the reality of exactly one or two of the periods while rejecting the remainder, with an explicit justification for the asymmetry; (4) a functionalist view that holds all three are useful fictions regardless of whether they map onto ontologically distinct eras. For each leaf, provide a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment.

You must also identify at least two logically coherent but apparently unoccupied combinations in the tree—branches where the answers to the feature-claims are jointly consistent but seem to be defended by no one—and briefly diagnose why the space is empty (whether because the combination is incoherent on closer inspection, unattractive, or simply unexplored). Finally, flag any pair of named positions that your tree cannot genuinely separate on the basis of the diagnostic questions, explaining whether the failure is due to shared commitments on every feature-claim or merely to verbal overlap in labeling.

The taxonomy should be designed for an audience that knows basic European history but does not need specialized historiographical training; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not invoke obscure theories unknown to standard training data. Arrange widely discussed markers—such as the recovery of classical texts, the institutionalization of experiment, and the persistence of scholastic methods—into combinations that go beyond the standard textbook triad of Whig, revisionist, and social-constructivist narratives.
