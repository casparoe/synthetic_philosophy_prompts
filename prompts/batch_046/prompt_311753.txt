Question: Consider a standard six-stage centipede game with alternating moves and monotonically increasing payoffs. (a) Reconstruct the backward-induction argument precisely, identifying the epistemic condition required for each backward step. (b) A recent forum proposal replaces common knowledge of rationality with an independent graded-belief hierarchy: each player assigns probabilities p_i < 1 to the opponent being rational, with no shared prior required. Does this dissolve the backward-induction paradox? In particular, does any profile with p_i > 0 preserve first-move defection, and does the graded framework eliminate the infinite belief regress or merely relocate it?

Student answer:
"(a) Backward induction begins at the final decision node, where the active player compares taking a large payoff now with allowing the opponent to take an even larger payoff on the next move. Because taking is strictly dominant at that terminal node, rationality requires it. Working backward, each player anticipates being taken at the subsequent node, so taking is optimal at every prior node. This collapses to immediate defection by player A at the first node. The argument requires common knowledge of rationality—each player knows the other is rational, knows that she knows, and so on—though some commentators have argued that mere mutual belief is sufficient.

(b) The graded-belief proposal initially seems to break the induction: if I assign only 70% probability to my opponent being rational, there is a 30% chance she will cooperate, which might make passing attractive. But this intuition fails. As long as every p_i is strictly positive, conditioning on the rational branch still yields defection at every node, and the expected payoff of passing remains strictly below that of taking once you weight by the rational path. Hence backward induction survives for all p_i > 0; the paradox is not dissolved, only masked by probabilistic noise. Furthermore, graded belief does not stop the regress—it pushes it into the calibration of the priors over p_i themselves. This is structurally identical to the chain-store paradox, where entry is deterred precisely when beliefs about beliefs align without error. So the framework relocates the paradox from rationality to belief calibration, leaving the core problem untouched."
