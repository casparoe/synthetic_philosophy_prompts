Consider the widespread institutional practice—particularly in post-colonial and post-authoritarian democracies—of a present government issuing a formal, often parliamentary, apology for historical injustices committed by prior regimes (such as forced assimilation of indigenous populations, state-sanctioned slavery, or colonial expropriation), while maintaining two parallel commitments: that the state is a continuous legal personality inheriting treaty obligations and sovereign debts from its predecessors, and that the apology is purely expressive, generating no actionable legal liability or reparative obligations to descendants, typically on the grounds that moral responsibility expires with the direct perpetrators or that distributive justice should be assessed only by present conditions.

What philosophical commitments about collective moral agency, intergenerational responsibility, and the purpose of apology does this specific configuration presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory that renders it coherent to treat the state as identical across time for contractual and legal continuity but discontinuous for culpability, and that separates an act of apology from any obligation of corrective justice. Is the practice strictly committed to one particular account—perhaps a symbolic or performative theory of apology combined with a discontinuity thesis about collective moral agency—or is it philosophically underdetermined, equally compatible with several rival theories of collective responsibility? And if the underlying framework were replaced by a structural-injustice account that treats historical wrongs as generating present distributive obligations regardless of individual culpability, which elements of current practice—the legal doctrine of state continuity, the budgetary separation of apology from reparation, or the rhetorical framing of regret—would necessarily have to change?
