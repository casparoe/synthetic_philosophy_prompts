Compare the American and European normative attitudes toward economic inequality, treating each as a coherent philosophical framework rather than a mere cluster of policies. Specifically, examine whether the American framework's reliance on procedural fairness and ex-ante equality of opportunity can consistently resist ex-post redistribution without implicitly committing to a metaphysically thick account of moral desert that the framework itself cannot defend; and examine whether the European framework's appeal to social solidarity and status equality can avoid collapsing into outcome-leveling that undermines the conditions for the very trust and cooperation it presupposes. Identify the precise conceptual juncture—likely in competing accounts of what renders an inequality undeserved—where the two frameworks cease to be empirically reconcilable and become genuinely incommensurable. To ground the comparison, apply both frameworks to a single institutional proposal: a universal capital grant at adulthood funded entirely by a progressive inheritance tax. Show exactly where the disagreement over this proposal is substantive rather than factual, and argue which attitude is ultimately more defensible given that incommensurability. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; rely on conceptual analysis and the constructed case alone.
