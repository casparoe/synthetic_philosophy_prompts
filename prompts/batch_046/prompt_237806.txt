I am a novelist currently drafting a narrative that turns on the problem of intergenerational commitment, and in my research I keep encountering the analogy between constitutional precommitment and Ulysses binding himself to the mast. The comparison is routinely invoked to defend rigid constitutional entrenchment: just as Ulysses, knowing his future self would be irrationally seduced by the Sirens, rationally restrained his own freedom, so a founding generation rationally binds its successors to prevent democratic backsliding. I want to audit this analogy with some care—not as a jurist, but as someone interested in whether the narrative logic holds once its parts are mapped precisely onto political reality.

Specifically, please lay out the explicit correspondence table: in the constitutional context, what plays the role of Ulysses, the mast, the ropes, the Sirens, the crew, and the wax in the sailors’ ears? Then identify the points of breakdown that matter for the normative conclusion. For instance, in the Odyssey the bound agent and the future seduced agent are the same continuous self, whereas in constitutional law the binders and the bound are different generations—a disanalogy that introduces the dead-hand problem rather than resolving it. But I also want you to examine a less discussed mapping: what happens if we treat the crew not as neutral enforcers but as interested interpreters with their own institutional incentives, and what if the wax represents not judicial review but the deliberate suppression of democratic voice? Does the analogy quietly smuggle in an assumption that the founding generation possesses superior rationality, and if so, does that assumption survive once the mapping is made fully explicit?

Finally, tell me whether the analogy genuinely supports the conclusion that constitutional rigidity is a form of rational self-binding, or whether it collapses into a different picture—perhaps one of intergenerational paternalism or strategic delegation to a third party—once the disanalogies are on the table. If the Ulysses story fails as a justification, is there a better literary or conceptual analogy for constitutional precommitment, or should analogical reasoning in this domain be abandoned altogether?
