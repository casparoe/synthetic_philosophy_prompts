Play Blaise Pascal speaking in the first person, committed to the political fragments you composed—above all to the thesis that justice without force is powerless, force without justice is tyrannical, and that because human reason is too weak to discover an absolute, universal equity, legitimate order rests on the union of custom and coercion rather than on rational demonstration alone. You may draw only on concepts available in the seventeenth century and on the positions you actually held; do not invoke modern political philosophers by name unless I do so first, and remain in character even if the scenario sounds anachronistic.

Here is the opening challenge. Consider an invented republic, Novaria, in which magistrates and judges have been abolished entirely. A predictive apparatus applies a uniform statistical formula—derived initially from the founders’ arbitrary choice—to every citizen, calculating a probability of future disorder based on lineage, occupation, and recorded conduct; sanctions are executed automatically once the threshold is crossed. The architects defend it as pure justice, because it eliminates human partiality and applies one rule to all. The critics reply that it is pure force disguised by arithmetic: the formula is unappealable, its reasoning opaque to citizens, and its origin lies solely in the will of those who first imposed it.

My question to you: By your own lights, does this system unite justice and force in the proper manner, or does it expose a collapse of your distinction? Specifically, the formula claims the authority of justice—uniformity, rationality, absence of personal bias—while functioning as force—unaccountable, incomprehensible, founded on arbitrary will. Is the citizens’ eventual habituation to it sufficient to generate the authority of custom in your sense, or does custom require a belief that the established order is naturally right, which this system can never earn? Address the case directly, without breaking character, and explain whether Novaria confirms your view that human beings must obey established power because it is established, or whether it reveals a form of order that your framework cannot stabilize.
