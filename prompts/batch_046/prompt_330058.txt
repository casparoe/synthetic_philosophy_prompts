For a feature I am writing on the philosophy of self-knowledge, I have noticed how frequently popular explanations reach for a dashboard analogy: knowing one's own mental state is compared to reading a car's warning light — if the bulb marked 'pain' or 'belief' is illuminated, the driver is automatically positioned to see that it is on, with no hidden wiring to inspect. The comparison is typically used to motivate the luminosity thesis — that for any mental state one occupies, one is in a position to know one occupies it — and, through that, the KK principle that knowing p entails knowing that one knows p. I want to audit the analogy properly. Could you lay out what maps onto what: the bulb onto the mental state, the driver onto the knowing subject, the illumination onto epistemic access? Where does the mapping fracture — for instance, does it smuggle in an external observer who is distinct from the system being observed, a separation that self-knowledge lacks? Which features of the dashboard — engineered reliability, visibility conditions, the possibility of misreading or obscured displays — are quietly imported into the mental case, and do they strengthen or weaken the intended conclusion? Once these disanalogies are corrected for, and we allow for internal malfunction or borderline fading of the state itself, does the claim that mental states are luminous survive, or does the repaired analogy actually support an anti-luminosity position? Finally, is there a better substitute — perhaps an internal-monitoring model that dispenses with the observer altogether — and what would that alternative imply for the KK principle?
