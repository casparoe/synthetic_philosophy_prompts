A colleague recently summarized for me an argument circulating in discussions of AI safety about whether low-impact agents can be defined without first solving value alignment. According to her summary, every side-effect measure works by comparing the actual world trajectory to a counterfactual baseline in which the agent never acted, but because the world contains astronomically many changed variables—local temperature shifts, microbial population dynamics, long-range economic indicators—any comparison requires deciding which differences matter and by how much. Those weightings, she claims, are just value judgments in disguise, so specifying a side-effect penalty necessarily smuggles in a full utility ordering over world states. She adds that attempts to escape this with value-neutral metrics, such as total physical energy displaced or raw information-theoretic divergence from the baseline, either collapse back into implicit valuations or permit catastrophically destructive behaviors that happen to score well—for instance, slowly redistributing all matter within a region without changing its net energy budget. From this she concludes that low-impact AI is conceptually impossible: instructing an agent to avoid side effects is identical to instructing it to maximize an unstated utility function, so impact minimization and value alignment are not separate research problems but the same problem under different names.

I suspect she is overstating the case, but I cannot tell where the distortion lies. Could you audit this summary? Specifically: which parts fairly represent the debate over counterfactual impact measures and option-preservation strategies? Where does she conflate structural or variable-specific constraints with implicit utility maximization, or mischaracterize the role of baseline selection? Would a defender of low-impact design agree that all metrics eventually reduce to value judgments, or is there a principled distinction—perhaps involving reachability constraints, empowerment bounds, or restricted domains of comparison—that avoids requiring a complete specification of value? And finally, even if her characterization of the metrics is corrected, does her conclusion that low-impact AI is essentially value alignment in disguise actually follow?
