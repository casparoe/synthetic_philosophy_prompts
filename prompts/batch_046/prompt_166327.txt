hey — putting together notes for a short essay about presentism in historiography, but i'm coming at it from writing novels so it's probably a bit of a muddle.

the gist: historians talk about escaping presentism, but they never fully do, and maybe thats okay. i split it into three kinds — epistemic presentism (using concepts like "state legitimacy" or "market liquidity" that past people wouldn't recognise), normative presentism (judging them for not sharing our morals, basically), and then something i'm calling "aesthetic presentism." thats when you shape the past like a narrative — tragedy, irony, redemption arcs. since i write fiction i do this all the time, and i think historians do it too without admitting it. describing the irish famine as a "tragedy" already smuggles in a story-structure.

my claim is that epistemic presentism is unavoidable and not actually bad, normative presentism ought to be bracketed, and aesthetic presentism is the sneaky one that distorts most. but i'm not sure the three-way split holds water, or whether "aesthetic presentism" is a real term i half-remembered from grad school. also i might be conflating how novelists think about plot with how historians should think about causation — is that just projection?

could you comment on these notes? i'm particulary interested in whether my defence of epistemic presentism sounds like a cop-out, and whether i'm wrong to think narrative shape is a hidden problem in serious historiography. also, is there an error in my reasoning, not just my spelling?
