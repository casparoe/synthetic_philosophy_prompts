I've noticed a recurring argumentative move in debates about synthetic organisms and artificial systems: an entity is denied membership in a natural kind—say, "this is not a horse"—because of some essential origin difference, which blocks obligations designed for that kind; yet in the same breath its functional and observable similarity to members of that kind is used to justify applying exactly those practical protocols. The essentialist denial and the functional inclusion never interfere with each other, allowing the speaker to have it both ways. Is there an established philosophical name for this stable oscillation between an essential exclusion and a practical assimilation—something more specific than mere equivocation or a motte-and-bailey? And if one maps it onto the classical Chinese discussion of whether a white horse is a horse, does it correspond to the School of Names' distinction between sameness and similarity, or is it a different failure mode altogether?
