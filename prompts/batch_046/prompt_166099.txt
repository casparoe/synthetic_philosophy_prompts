I tried to extend it to actual science and now I'm stuck in a loop that feels like it proves science is either unjustified or totally arbitrary. here's the chain:

(1) Carroll's tortoise shows that if you apply modus ponens you need an extra premise that says you're allowed to apply it.
(2) In science we don't just use logic, we also use bridge principles like "the derivative of position is velocity" or "this equation describes that system" — these are like hidden inference licenses.
(3) So a derivation like predicting a satellite orbit actually has tons of hidden premises: logical rules plus bridge principles for every step.
(4) By Carroll's logic each hidden premise needs its own license, which needs another, so there's an infinite regress.
(5) That means no multi-step scientific derivation can ever be fully justified.
(6) But I still think my old colleagues were justified in calculating those orbits.
(7) So maybe hidden premises don't need individual licenses after all.
(8) But if they don't need licenses, then someone could slip in a fake bridge principle — say, "gravity stops at 200 miles up" — and the derivation would still count as justified.
(9) So either science is never justified, or false bridge principles are on equal footing with true ones.
(10) Both feel wrong, but I can't see teh exit.

Which step do I reject? I want to keep (6) and probably (2) since bridge principles seem real. What's teh cost of giving up whichever one is the weak link?
