Next month I present a workshop paper arguing that collective intentionality is not always constituted by shared intentions in the Bratman sense—mutual responsiveness, commitment to a joint activity, and commitment to mutual support—but can instead emerge from what I call reciprocal accountability loops: dynamic structures in which participants track each other's commitments to a jointly maintained normative frame without representing a shared plan. I take free-improvisation jazz quartets and distributed municipal governance protocols as my central examples, and I claim that these loops are sufficient for joint action and can even ground limited forms of collective responsibility. The audience consists of three philosophers of action who are committed defenders of Bratman's shared cooperative activity account, two cognitive scientists who treat collective intentionality as summed individual intentions with common knowledge, and one legal philosopher whose work centers on joint liability in corporate decision-making. Give me the objections in the order they are likely to arise, phrased as each faction would actually phrase them: the Bratmanians first, then the cognitive scientists, then the legal philosopher. For each objection, indicate what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest. Identify the weakest point in my own position—the gap between showing that accountability loops exist and showing that they can carry normative weight equivalent to shared intention—and advise me how to defend it rather than hoping the audience misses it. Finally, include the objection I am most hoping nobody asks—specifically the legal philosopher's challenge that if no individual holds the full intention, my loops cannot ground joint liability under existing corporate law doctrines—and provide a concise reply I could deliver in about two minutes, with an explicit note on exactly where that reply is soft.
