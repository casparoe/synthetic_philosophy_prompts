I have recently become personally involved in navigating long-term care decisions for my mother, whose advancing dementia has placed our family in sustained tension between institutional autonomy protocols and the collective normative expectations of our close-knit religious community. This experience prompted me to sketch an essay applying the liberal-communitarian debate to elder care, but with a conceptual move I am not sure holds together.

The essay argues that both liberal autonomy-based models—prior directives, substituted judgment, procedural consent—and standard communitarian critiques—the patient as bearer of tradition, family authority as expressive of shared values—presuppose what I call a representational model of the self. On this model, the patient either represents her own prior will or represents her community’s normative identity, and the ethical task is to determine which representation is legitimate. I propose replacing this with a model of performative embeddedness, adapted loosely from practice-based accounts but modified for institutional care: in progressive dementia, the self is not a static entity to be represented but is continuously constituted through practical negotiation among caregivers, family members, and institutional protocols. The ethical task, then, is not to identify who correctly represents the patient’s interests, but to maintain the conditions for this practical constitution without collapsing into either pure procedural autonomy or uncritical deference to tradition.

I have three specific concerns. First, is the distinction between representational and performative embeddedness genuinely different from familiar relational-autonomy theories, or is it merely a rebranding? Second, does the model risk obscuring real conflicts of interest—e.g., when family members benefit materially from care decisions—by treating all negotiation as constitutive rather than strategic? Third, is there an implicit normative commitment to preserving the patient’s social presence at the expense of her prior expressed wishes that I have not adequately defended?

Please comment on these points and on whether the essay’s central claim is conceptually coherent and interesting. In your response, state how confident you are in each of your judgments, and explicitly single out the point about which you are least certain.
