I'm applying for a one-semester research fellowship to write a 30-page conceptual paper on Aquinas's conditions for licit resistance to tyranny. My half-formed idea is that scholars treat the conditions in De Regno and the Summa as a loose moral checklist—tyranny by usurpation or exercise, harm to the common good, absence of a superior authority to appeal to, and prohibition on purely private initiative—without asking whether they form a single, integrated theory of authority failure. I want to ask whether these conditions are conceptually interdependent in a way that makes the final killing an act of distributed public judgment rather than an exception to the private-killing prohibition. Specifically, does Aquinas require that the community's shared practical reason recognize the tyrant's failure before any individual may act, and does the absence of a magistrate not merely license private action but constitute the political community itself as the temporary locus of authority? If so, the standard "checklist" reading misses that the conditions are not safeguards around an individual decision but prerequisites for the act to count as public authority at all. I will spend month one mapping the conditions in both texts without assuming they are identical; month two comparing them with Aquinas's broader account of practical reason, law, and the common good; month three constructing the distributed-judgment interpretation and testing it against hard cases, such as a technically legitimate ruler who governs for private gain where corrupt magistrates still exist; month four drafting the argument; and months five and six writing the deliverable and identifying precise failure conditions. A useful result would be a reconstruction showing that the conditions resolve ambiguities—why some usurpers may be killed and others not, and why the presence of a negligent magistrate blocks action—without importing modern democratic theory anachronistically. The project fails if the texts clearly separate the conditions with no unifying logic, or if the interpretation requires distorting Aquinas's account of private judgment. Please explain what would change your mind—particularly which textual feature or counter-argument from the standard checklist reading would most undermine the distributed-judgment view—and indicate where the reasoning is most likely to fail, likely in the move from "no superior authority" to "the community itself becomes authority."
