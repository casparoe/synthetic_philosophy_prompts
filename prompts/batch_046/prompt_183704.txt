I'm trying to isolate a reasoning pattern that sits near the sunk-cost fallacy without being identical to it. In the cases I have in mind, someone defends persisting with a failing course of action not by arguing that past investments will be recovered, but by claiming that those investments have constituted them as a particular kind of agent: abandoning the path now would fracture the narrative connecting their past and present selves, or turn years of effort into an existential contradiction rather than merely lost resources. For example, a scholar might continue a moribund research program not because she expects success, but because quitting would render her prior work something like a wasted life rather than part of a longer arc; similarly, an artist might refuse a career change not out of optimism, but because the sunk years of training have made her a specific kind of person, and leaving feels closer to self-annihilation than to rational recalculation. The reasoning is not utility-maximizing, yet it also does not seem reducible to plain irrationality if diachronic coherence or narrative identity carries independent weight. Does this specific move have an established name, distinct both from the economic sunk-cost fallacy and from generic virtues of commitment? If so, how does it relate to theories of diachronic rationality or narrative identity; if not, what precise boundaries distinguish it from those neighboring concepts?
