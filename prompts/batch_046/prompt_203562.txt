For decades, the dispute between defenders of original intentionality and those who accept only derived intentionality has circulated through philosophy of mind and rationalist-adjacent forums without resolving: Searle’s claim that syntax is not sufficient for semantics, and that any machine intentionality must be borrowed from conscious designers, versus Dennett’s reply that we are ourselves two-bitser systems whose intentionality derives from evolutionary and cultural selection processes that are mechanistic all the way down. The standard moves are all catalogued—the Chinese Room thought experiment, the evolutionary counter, the distinction between intrinsic and derived meaning, the charge that one side begs the question by treating consciousness as primitive. Yet the debate feels stalled in a way that suggests the participants are not engaging on the same axis.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not primarily about whether silicon can think, nor about the ontology of mental content, but about the direction of explanatory priority: whether intentionality is a primitive that explains the organization of physical systems, or whether it is a predictive heuristic we project onto systems whose behavior we could in principle describe non-intentionally. Under this reading, the two sides may be answering different questions—one about what must be present in the world for a symbol to mean something, the other about which explanations we are licensed to use—while treating them as competing answers to the same one.

But I want a sharper diagnosis. Is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or a case of bedrock intuition that neither side can argue for? Are there hidden empirical commitments—perhaps about the neural correlates of understanding, or about informational closure in biological cognition—that either side implicitly relies on but does not defend? And does the emergence of modern large-scale artificial systems, in which intentionality might be derived not directly from human designers but through nested layers of optimization—what we might call a derivation stack—break the old binary? If so, does that introduce a genuinely new crux, or does it merely multiply the levels without changing the underlying structure?

Please do not survey the interpretations or rehearse the familiar arguments. Instead, diagnose the stall: identify what kind of disagreement this actually is, using what each side actually says about explanation, syntax, and semantics; specify whether a hidden empirical or formal question is doing the work; and tell me honestly whether anything—a conceptual distinction, a result in cognitive science, a formal result about representational closure, or a change of framing—could actually move it. If your diagnosis implies that progress should not be expected, say so and explain why.
