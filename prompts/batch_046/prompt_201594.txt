I'm trying to figure out where my reasoning on escalation breaks down, but every fix seems to break something else. Here's the chain:

1. In the dollar auction, if you've already bid 90 cents and you're behind at 95, rational choice says bid the dollar — quitting guarantees losing 90, bidding gives you a shot.
2. So escalation is rational whenever you've got irrecoverable investment and any path to recovery.
3. That applies everywhere: years in a dead-end PhD, capital in a bad startup, even epistemic investment — once you've staked your reputation on a theory in public, quitting means eating that reputational cost for nothing.
4. Therefore rational agents should always escalate: finish the PhD, double the investment, defend the theory harder.
5. But we clearly think "throwing good money after bad" is a failure, and changing your mind when evidence shifts is a virtue, not a failure of nerve.
6. So the reasoning in (1)-(2) must be wrong.
7. But if escalation reasoning is wrong, then in the auction there's no reason to keep bidding once you're behind — which means there was never a reason to enter.
8. So rational agents should avoid all competitive commitments with potential sunk costs: never start long projects, never take public stances, never enter relationships that might fail.
9. That can't be right either; it makes rational life impossible.

I definatly want to keep (5) and maybe salvage (1) for actual auctions. Is the mistake in (3) — that PhD years and reputation aren't actually "sunk" in the same strategic structure? Or is there a hidden modal slide between (2) and (4)? Where exactly does it go wrong, and what does fixing it cost me?
