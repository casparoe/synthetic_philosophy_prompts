I've come around to this view that performance legitimacy — prosperity and order justifying authoritarian rule, like in China or Singapore — is actually a lot more robust than liberal critics want to admit. Not just GDP growth, but that rapid development requires suppressing the kind of distributive confict that paralyses democracies, and citizens genuinely internalize that trade-off. But I'm also playing with this niche twist: what if the legitimacy doesn't come from actual results but from a belief in 'developmental exceptionalism' — that the regime has unique epistemic access to long-term planning — and that belief gets maintained by controlling the narrative of results, not the results themselves? Anyway, I want to know what would actually count against the core view. Not moral objections, but concrete defeaters: what if prosperity stalls, does legitimacy just collapse or shift to nationalism? What if the 'order' is actually suppressing feedback loops that correct bad policy, so it's borrowed-time stablity? Which of these would defenders of performance legitimacy concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss? Rank them fatal to awkward. And be honest — has the view been qualified so much (every failure is temporary, every success is structural) that it's basically unfalsifiable? If so, is that a problem for a view like this?
