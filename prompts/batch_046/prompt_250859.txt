At a meetup for people who work in tech and read philosophy casually, two other attendees—Jordan and Riley—got into a sustained argument about metaphysical grounding that I couldn't resolve, and I'd appreciate a careful adjudication. I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, so I may be missing distinctions that are obvious to specialists; please treat my framing as approximate.

Jordan started by proposing that we should understand fundamentality on the model of a dependency tree in software architecture. Fundamental entities, he said, are like root nodes with no parents: they don't depend on anything else, and everything else ultimately resolves to them. From this he argued that social or institutional facts—say, the fact that a particular corporation exists—are fully grounded in physical and psychological facts about people and objects. Because they inherit all their being from that base, he said, they are ontologically derivative and therefore less real in a meaningful sense: if the physical base were gone, they would vanish, whereas the base wouldn't vanish if they did.

Riley replied that this confuses grounding with a kind of ontological demotion. She used the analogy of compiled binary code: a running program is fully derived from source code, but it is not less real—it executes, consumes resources, and causes effects. Derivation, she said, is a structural relation, not a value judgment. But then she introduced a more specific challenge: in modern distributed version control, there is often no single root commit. Every node can be reconstructed from peer nodes, and the history is a mesh rather than a tree. She argued that asking for the fundamental layer of reality assumes a centralized, hierarchical architecture that may not apply. If reality is more like a distributed system, there might be no bare substrate—only relations all the way down.

Jordan countered that even distributed systems rely on base protocols—TCP/IP, physical hardware—that don't depend on the applications running above them. He said this proves there must still be a fundamentum, even if we haven't mapped it precisely. He also claimed that Riley's binary-code analogy actually supports his point: the binary runs, yes, but its entire causal profile is fixed by the source, just as institutional facts are fixed by physical ones.

Riley then pushed on what she called the bare substrate problem. In physics, she said, quantum fields seem to have properties only relationally—there may be no node with no properties. This is like a version-control repository with no initial commit: you can't have a root that is pure potentiality without content. Therefore, Jordan's picture of root nodes with intrinsic natures is not just empirically unconfirmed but conceptually incoherent, because any candidate fundamental entity already carries relational structure that requires explanation.

Jordan answered that this only shows our current theories haven't isolated the root, not that there is none. He compared it to debugging a large codebase: just because you haven't found the entry point doesn't mean the program has no entry point.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: rate the strength of Jordan's dependency-tree analogy, Riley's binary-code reply, her distributed-system challenge, Jordan's base-protocol counter, Riley's bare-substrate objection, and Jordan's final debugging analogy. Say who is right about what specific issue, where both are missing something, and who is closer to being right overall. Please also state how confident you are in your adjudication overall, and explicitly identify the single point where you are least sure.
