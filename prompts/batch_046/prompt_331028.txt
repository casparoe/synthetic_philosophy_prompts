I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of cognitive science, and I have been developing a framework that extends the standard Clark-Chalmers debate into what I call institutional substrate cognition: situations in which a research collective's distributed record-keeping system—featuring automated consistency-checking protocols, version-controlled hypothesis registries, and governance rules that determine which entries count as endorsed—functions not merely as an external consulting tool but as a constitutive part of the cognitive architecture through which the group holds, revises, and draws inferences about beliefs concerning complex dynamical phenomena, such as nonlinear climate feedback loops. I want to avoid both generic appeals to the extended mind literature and empirically unverifiable claims about specific laboratory practices, so I am asking you to evaluate a carefully circumscribed conceptual scenario rather than to survey the empirical literature.

Please provide your actual credence that at least one present-day climate-science collaboration genuinely instantiates belief-holding partly through such an institutional substrate. Decompose your answer into four terms: (i) the probability that substrate-neutral functionalism about belief is correct, so that non-neural architectures can realize belief states; (ii) the probability that the parity principle applies to multi-agent systems governed by shared protocols, rather than being restricted to individual organism-environment couplings; (iii) the probability that existing collaboratives actually meet the functional conditions of direct integration, automatic accessibility, and endorsement without further inferential mediation—rather than treating the database as a mere reference source; and (iv) any further factor you deem relevant, such as the necessity of collective intentionality or the possibility of explaining the phenomenon through distributed individual cognition alone. Give a rough number and one line of justification for each term, combine them into an overall credence, and then identify which term you are least confident in and what conceptual argument or organizational study would most substantially shift it.
