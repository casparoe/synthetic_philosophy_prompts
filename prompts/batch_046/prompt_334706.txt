I am preparing the opening session of a graduate seminar on scientific realism and have grown dissatisfied with standard pedagogical treatments of verisimilitude, which tend to recycle familiar comparisons without isolating the precise structural feature that drives disagreement. To remedy this, I want you to engineer an entirely hypothetical thought experiment—requiring no historical citation, empirical literature review, or bibliographic apparatus—involving two false but empirically equivalent theories about a domain characterized by what I shall call fragmented measurement spaces, where predictive scales do not commute under transformation, and by a ghost parameter that one theory treats as physically instantiated while the other treats as a pure coordinate artifact.

The design specifications are strict: both theories must be false yet closer to the truth along orthogonal dimensions—one retaining true structural relations among observed entities, the other retaining true existential commitments regarding unobserved ones; a structural realist, a truthlikeness realist committed to a content-based measure, and an anti-realist who rejects verisimilitude as incoherent must each deliver a distinct verdict on which theory is closer to the truth, or whether comparison is even coherent; and the divergence must depend specifically on the non-commensurability of the measurement framework rather than on predictive accuracy or ontological parsimony. Once the scenario is set out, identify the feature that makes the three verdicts split, explain what happens to the comparison if that non-commensurability is removed, and articulate the strongest attack available to the defender of the view that fares worst—specifically, an argument that the comparison illicitly presupposes a metric across incommensurable spaces. Deliver your response as exactly five numbered points, each consisting of no more than two sentences, with no introductory or concluding remarks.
