As a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy in a more systematic way, I am serving as an occasional grader for an introductory course in early modern metaphysics. I would like you to evaluate the following examination response rigorously and without regard to my own interpretation.

The question presented to the student was this: In Descartes' Second Meditation, the cogito is commonly rendered as I think, therefore I am. What precisely does this argument establish about the thinker? Does it prove that I am a persisting mental substance whose essence is thought, or only something more modest? Furthermore, how should we understand Descartes' immediate turn to the wax example: does it reinforce the cogito's conclusion, or does it shift the ground of certain knowledge from the intellect's grasp of itself to its grasp of extended bodies?

The student answered as follows. The cogito is best understood not as a syllogism but as an immediate intuition. Whenever I am aware of my own mental activity—whether doubting, understanding, or willing—I cannot coherently deny that I exist in that very moment. From this, Descartes establishes that he is a thing that thinks, but he has not yet shown that thought constitutes his full metaphysical essence or that he persists as the same self over time; those stronger conclusions depend on arguments he develops only after proving God's existence and establishing the real distinction between mind and body in Meditation VI. Turning to the wax, Descartes demonstrates that even the properties of a physical object—its flexibility, extension, and mutability—are grasped more clearly by the intellect than by the senses. This reinforces the priority of the cogito: just as I apprehend my own existence through pure intellect, so too I apprehend the wax's true nature through the same faculty. Consequently, both examples together show that all certain knowledge, whether of mental or material objects, depends on the intellect's direct inspection of its own contents.

Please assign a letter grade and justify it with specific, point-by-point comments. In your assessment, I am especially interested in whether the student correctly distinguishes the thin, indexical self-reference of the cogito—functioning somewhat like an observational pointer that registers existence without disclosing internal structure—from the thicker substantial reading that attributes an essential nature to the self. I also want to know whether the student commits any subtle conflations between the epistemic priorities of Meditation II and the metaphysical conclusions reached only in later meditations, and whether the inference from the wax example to a general claim about knowledge of material objects actually follows.
