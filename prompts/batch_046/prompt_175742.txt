Write a satirical report of approximately 750 words, composed in the deadpan administrative prose of a fictional 18th-century French royal commission, evaluating the "happiness outcomes" of a philosophical salon according to the framework Émilie du Châtelet develops in her Discours sur le bonheur (c. 1746). Address the report to a fictional Minister of Enlightenment. Apply du Châtelet's specific conceptual distinctions precisely: that happiness is not mere pleasure but requires both rational study and managed illusion; that physical happiness (health, agreeable sensations) and moral happiness (freedom from prejudice, self-knowledge) are distinct but interdependent; that ambition and the desire for reputation are the greatest obstacles; that love and friendship are necessary conditions yet dangerous if they become ungoverned passions; and that freedom from social prejudice is a precondition rather than a consequence. The satire should target modern wellness auditing and happiness quantification: invent bureaucratic metrics derived from her criteria—such as an "Illusion-to-Study Ratio," a "Passion Stability Index," a "Glory-Aversion Score," and a "Prejudice-Clearance Certificate"—and present them in tables and footnotes. The narrator must remain fully inside du Châtelet's rationalist, anti-utilitarian framework; do not allow modern psychological language, self-help tropes, or compatibilist concessions to enter the voice. Include at least one dissenting footnote from a skeptical clerk who argues, in terms consistent with du Châtelet's own concerns, that the measurement of happiness necessarily corrupts it. The piece should be readable to someone who does not know du Châtelet's work, but a specialist should recognize that the comedy depends on an accurate grasp of her argument rather than a caricature. Do not request bibliographies, citations, or further reading; do not quote her texts verbatim—paraphrase all positions in the bureaucratic idiom.
