I'm taking an advanced seminar on the metaphysics of political ideology, and our central question is whether "left" and "right" name a genuine conceptual distinction or merely an accidental historical clustering of policy preferences. My professor insists that if the distinction is real, it must be anchored in shared metaphysical commitments about the person or society rather than in contingent alliances, so I'm constructing a diagnostic decision tree for our final discussion that sorts positions by the conceptual commitments that actually hold them together—not by which policies someone endorses.

The tree should begin with whether one believes any single axis can capture ideological coherence at all. From that root, I want three successive branching questions: first, whether ideological alignment requires a shared metaphysics of the self—specifically, an atomistic conception of persons as prior to their social relations versus a relational conception in which identity is partly constituted by communal roles and inherited practices; second, whether the left-right split tracks primarily attitudes toward tradition and change, or rather different answers to whether unearned social advantages ought to be actively neutralized by institutional design versus preserved as legitimate inheritances; and third, whether either side can maintain internal consistency without smuggling in contradictory commitments—such as combining strict liberal neutrality about the good life with perfectionist state aims, or combining relational metaphysics with a defense of radically unregulated market allocation. Each leaf should name an actual position (for example, classical liberalism, social democracy, conservative communitarianism, revolutionary socialism) or be marked "unnamed/unstable" for combinations that seem conceptually available but that nobody consistently occupies—such as a view that pairs relational metaphysics with aggressive preservation of unearned advantage, or atomistic metaphysics with the complete institutional erasure of traditional practices. Every leaf must include one sentence stating its most awkward commitment: the bullet its defenders must bite. I also want you to flag any pair of leaves that this particular tree cannot genuinely separate, and to say whether the overlap is conceptual or just a limitation of the branching logic.

Please phrase every node as a concrete claim or hypothetical case that a student could answer without already knowing which ideological label it leads to—for instance, asking whether a society that eliminated all inherited cultural practices but retained market allocation intact would count as more just than our own, rather than asking "are you a liberal?" The instrument should be usable without any bibliography or citation list, since I'm designing it to test my classmates' intuitions in a classroom exercise, not to survey the literature. Keep the tree compact enough to serve as a handout—no more than about twelve leaves—but make sure the diagnostic questions are genuinely divisive rather than rhetorical.
