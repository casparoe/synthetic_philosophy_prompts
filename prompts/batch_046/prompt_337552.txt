Last week we got onto the Sleeping Beauty problem, which I’d never heard of, and one of the students summed it up for me in an email. He wrote: "Beauty is put to sleep Sunday and a fair coin is flipped. If heads she’s woken once on Monday; if tails she’s woken Monday and Tuesday with her Monday memory erased. Every time she wakes she’s interveiwed and asked for her creedence that the coin landed heads. The halfers stay at 1/2, saying she recieved no new information since she knew she’d be interviewed either way. The thirders update to 1/3, counting three equally likely centered worlds—Monday-heads, Monday-tails, and Tuesday-tails. But the real lesson people miss is that this isn’t just about counting wakings. It proves standard Bayesian conditioning collapses for any belief indexed to the self—what philosophers call de se belief—because Bayes requires you to already know which world you’re in, and in self-locating cases you never have that index. So rational belief needs a separate centered-probability machinery that actually replaces Bayes for first-person reasoning. That’s why the halfers win, but not for the usual no-new-evidence reason: they win because they implicitly refuse to apply Bayes to self-located credences, which is exactly what the paradox forces us to do. Meanwhile the thirder answer only looks right if you smuggle in the self-indication assumption from doomsday arguments, which has nothing to do with Sleeping Beauty."
