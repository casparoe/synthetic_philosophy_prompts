I am about to defend a curatorial thesis before the acquisition and exhibition board of a major modern-art museum. The proposal is to restructure a retrospective of Harlem Renaissance visual art not around artist, chronology, or medium, but around what I call propaganda targets: grouping works by the specific social falsehoods they were designed to combat—about Black intelligence, sexuality, labor, or historical memory. I take W. E. B. Du Bois's 1926 essay Criteria of Negro Art as literal authorization: he argues that all art is propaganda and must be, and I claim that the formalist framing now dominant in museums—aesthetic autonomy, medium-specificity, art for art's sake—actually obscures the intentional work these artists were performing. To honor the works on their own terms, I argue, the gallery must organize itself around those propagandistic structures rather than suppress them.

The audience is mostly senior curators and art historians steeped in modernist formalism. They believe that political framing reduces visual complexity to sociology; they worry that a message-based layout will feel like an illustrated textbook; and they have institutional commitments to universalist aesthetic value. Two board members are scholars of African American culture who are not hostile to politicized readings but are skeptical of treating Du Bois as a blanket authorization: they fear it flattens the Renaissance's internal diversity into a single didactic program and ignores artists who explicitly resisted propaganda.
