I serve on the advisory committee for our university Museum of Science and History, which is finalizing designs for a permanent gallery tentatively titled "Celestial Order." The curators propose displaying Johannes Kepler's manuscripts on planetary motion—including material bearing on his three laws—alongside artifacts from his 1601 treatise on the more certain foundations of astrology: specifically, original horoscope charts he prepared for patrons and family members, together with his annotations distinguishing geometrically defined "aspects" (angular correlations he likened to harmonic intervals and tidal influence) from the deterministic "houses" and natal predictions he explicitly rejected. The committee is divided. The director and several historians argue that omitting these materials misrepresents the historical unity of celestial mechanics and correlational astrology in Kepler's own practice, and that honest institutional presentation requires showing how a figure now canonized for empirical law also pursued what he considered a reformable, physically grounded predictive art. Conversely, a coalition of science faculty and a major donor representative insists that placing horoscope charts in shared gallery space with the Astronomia Nova grants them undeserved epistemic authority, blurs the boundary between empirical science and rejected pseudoscience, and violates the museum's duty to avoid implicit legitimation of predictive astrology. Our charter obliges us to issue a formal written recommendation—approve the pairing with binding framing conditions, reject the astrological display, or segregate it into a separately interpreted contextual wing—before the board votes at the end of next month.

Because I am currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on philosophy and history of science, I want to frame this not as a public-relations dispute but as an institutional-philosophy problem about whether an organization can display a historically embedded but epistemically superseded research program without endorsing it. Please draft the committee's decision memo: it should state a concrete, actionable recommendation with an implementation timeline, provide reasoning that addresses the curators' historical-fidelity argument and the faculty's demarcation concern with equal seriousness, and devote explicit, substantive space to the strongest objection on the side you reject—especially the charge that institutional neutrality collapses once pseudoscientific artifacts are granted the same visual and spatial authority as empirically validated laws. The memo should not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should rely only on conceptual reasoning and institutional judgment that a body without access to external sources could evaluate.
