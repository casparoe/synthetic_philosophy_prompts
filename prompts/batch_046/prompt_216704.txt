In my course on Buddhist ethics and its relation to Western moral philosophy, we have been examining whether the tradition’s emphasis on cetanā (intention) and the classification of kamma as kusala or akusala can be reconstructed as a form of rule-consequentialism, or whether it remains incommensurable with outcome-based reasoning. I would like to apply this framework to Ursula K. Le Guin’s short story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas. The narrative presents a city of perfect happiness sustained by the abject suffering of a single child confined in a basement, and it contrasts the majority who rationalize this arrangement with the minority who silently depart. My question is whether a Buddhist ethical reading—particularly one that invokes upāya (skillful means) and the bodhisattva ideal—can make sense of walking away as a morally significant act rather than a mere escape from complicity, or whether the story instead dramatizes the charge that intention-focused ethics collapses into quietism when confronted with institutionalized harm. I am especially interested in whether the story’s refusal to show whether the departure actually liberates the child exposes a genuine tension between mental purification and effective intervention, and whether Le Guin thereby offers something that the philosophical debate between Buddhist ethics and consequentialism tends to overlook—namely, the moral psychology of witnessing suffering without the capacity to end it. A reading that treats both the narrative structure and the ethical concepts seriously, and that is willing to say where the text resists a Buddhist interpretation, would be very helpful.
