I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of logic and philosophy of mind. I am convening a 90-minute workshop for eighteen graduate students in philosophy and cognitive science. I need a participatory simulation—with explicit rules, assigned roles, physical materials, and a collective non-monetary payoff structure—that makes the participants feel, rather than merely understand, what it is to “jump out of the system” (JOOTSING) through the specific lens of the MU-puzzle.

The MU-puzzle, as it should function here, involves a formal derivation system with four transformation rules applied to strings over the alphabet {M, I, U}: (1) if a string ending in I is present, a U may be appended to produce xIU; (2) if Mx is present, Mxx may be produced; (3) any occurrence of III may be replaced by U; (4) any occurrence of UU may be dropped. The initial axiom is MI. The target string MU is provably unreachable by any sequence of these internal rules alone; the resolution requires stepping outside the formal language to observe an invariant—specifically, the parity of the number of I’s—that no internal derivation can alter.

I want the activity to include two roles. Derivers sit at a “system table” and may manipulate only physical tokens according to the four rules; they begin with the token sequence M-I and have the explicit collective objective of producing M-U. They are not told in advance that this is impossible within the rules, and they should exhaust the internal combinatorial space through genuine collective trial before any external resolution occurs. Trackers sit outside and record every derivation step on a shared display board, using a symbolically alien notational system (e.g., geometric shapes isomorphic to the letters M, I, and U). The Trackers begin under a strict non-interference protocol: they may observe and transcribe, but they may not speak to the Derivers, alter tokens, or suggest moves.

Here is the specific, somewhat non-standard constraint that should distinguish this design from a generic logic exercise: there must be a hidden “reflection mechanism” embedded in the Tracker protocol. After every third derivation step, the Trackers must transcribe the current internal state onto the display. The transcription is structurally isomorphic to the Derivers’ token sequence but symbolically opaque to them. Crucially, if any Deriver—without any prompting from the facilitator—spontaneously notices that the shape-sequence on the board encodes their own internal derivation state, and articulates this correspondence aloud using both notational systems in a single utterance that explicitly references the isomorphism, then that utterance itself counts as a legally appendable token within the Derivers’ system. In other words, the escape hatch is not handed to the group; it is generated only when an inside agent treats the outside tracking apparatus as representational data within the inside system.

The rules, materials (physical letter-tokens, display board with shape-stencils, timer, sealed “solution envelope”), role assignments, and timeline should be arranged so that the Derivers experience genuine collective frustration, coordination failure, and the sense of being trapped in an exhaustive but fruitless search space before anyone invokes the meta-rule. The payoff should be collective rather than competitive: the group unlocks the sealed envelope only if they either reach M-U or explicitly invoke the reflection mechanism and justify it by citing the isomorphism; if they invoke the mechanism without recognizing the correspondence, or if they break an internal derivation rule, they forfeit the envelope.
