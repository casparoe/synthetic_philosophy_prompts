I'm preparing a paper for my moral psychology seminar on whether moral worth requires acting for the right reasons, and I've constructed a chain of reasoning that now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to evacuate moral worth from almost every ordinary agent. The argument runs as follows. (1) If an action has moral worth, then the agent performed it for the right reasons—that is, the considerations that objectively justify the action were among the agent's actual motivating reasons. (2) For a consideration to be among an agent's motivating reasons, the agent must have been able to access it in conscious deliberation at the time of acting; otherwise it functions only as a background causal influence, not as a reason for which she acts. (3) So moral worth requires that the agent consciously accessed the justifying moral consideration during the action. (4) But consciously accessing a consideration as justifying requires that the agent hold a second-order representation of her own motivation—she must see herself as being moved by a valid reason, not merely be moved. (5) Therefore, moral worth requires not only being motivated by the right consideration, but explicitly representing one's own motivation as being governed by a valid reason. (6) Now consider actions done from habit, emotional reflex, or instinctive sympathy: the agent responds to situational cues without forming any second-order representation about why those cues matter. (7) Hence habitual kindness, instinctive rescue, and spontaneous compassion all lack moral worth. (8) Moreover, under cognitive load or time pressure agents rarely form such second-order representations, so nearly all urgent moral actions also come up empty. (9) But it is plainly implausible that almost every moral action performed by ordinary people is morally worthless. (10) Therefore (1) is false, and acting for the right reasons is not necessary for moral worth.

If I am forced to choose, I would rather preserve (1) than accept (9), but I want to understand exactly what I am buying. Which step should I reject, why did it look defensible, and what does abandoning it cost for the broader claim that moral worth depends on acting for the right reasons? Please lay out the diagnosis clearly, price the alternatives, and say how confident you are in your assessment—singling out the specific point about which you are least sure.
