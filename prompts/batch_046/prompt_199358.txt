Here is a short essay proposing that the standard binary between finks and masks should be expanded to include a third category. Please read it and then comment.

The literature on dispositions distinguishes finks—interfering conditions that remove or disable an underlying disposition precisely when it would manifest—from masks—external conditions that block the manifestation without altering the disposition itself. This binary, however, overlooks a distinct form of interference. Consider a compound that has a disposition to dissolve in water, but that also contains a reactive substrate which, upon water contact, undergoes rapid polymerization that seals the compound's surface. The polymerization does not merely mask dissolution like a protective shell, since the chemical affinity for water remains present and active in the interior. Nor does it fink away solubility, because the underlying chemical property is unchanged; rather, it redirects the causal pathway activated by water contact into a different manifestation—surface sealing rather than dissolution. Call this a dynamic mask. Unlike a static mask, which leaves the original disposition intact but dormant, a dynamic mask couples the triggering condition to an alternative disposition-manifestation pair, so that the original pathway is not simply blocked but displaced. This creates a problem for identifying a "canonical" disposition: if we claim the compound is "really" soluble but dynamically masked, we appear to privilege one causal chain arbitrarily. But if we treat solubility and polymerization as equally real, co-present dispositions, then the fink/mask framework—which presupposes a single true disposition being either protected or revealed—loses its metaphysical point. A tri-partite taxonomy of finks, static masks, and dynamic masks might better capture these interactions, but only if we can specify non-circularly which pathway counts as canonical for the object.

Please comment on whether the distinction between static and dynamic masks holds up, on whether the argument about canonical identification succeeds or instead collapses into a pluralist view of dispositions, and on what adjustments—conceptual or taxonomic—would make the proposed framework more defensible.
