The dispute over Bertrand's paradox has persisted for well over a century: three standard constructions—the random chord by endpoint selection, the random chord by radial distance, and the random chord by stick-drop—yield probabilities of one-half, one-third, and one-fourth respectively, and defenders of each continue to accuse the others of smuggling in an unjustified parameterization. Despite extensive formal work on invariant measures and symmetry groups, the literature feels circular; the same objections about hidden variables are met with the same replies, and no empirical finding could evidently settle which symmetry is correct. My working hypothesis is that this is not a mathematical disagreement at all, but a structural one: one side treats the principle of indifference as a normative rule governing rational belief given ignorance, while the other treats it as a descriptive claim about the physical mechanism that generates the chord, and the two camps are therefore not addressing the same question despite using identical vocabulary. Diagnose the stall with reference to what each position actually requires—for example, whether the stick-drop resolution appeals to a causal process that the endpoint construction deliberately abstracts away from—and determine whether the disagreement is verbal, about standards of symmetry, about hidden empirical assumptions regarding physical randomness, or about irreducible bedrock intuitions. Finally, say what would genuinely count as progress here: a formal distinction that dissolves the conflict, a demonstration that one framing is ill-posed, or an argument that the question should be abandoned in favor of a different formulation of uniform distribution.
