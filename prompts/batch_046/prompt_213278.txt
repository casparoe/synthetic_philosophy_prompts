hi, i'm grading essays for a phil module on normative ethics and i'm a bit stuck on this one. the exam question was: 'Critics often claim deontology is paradoxical because it forbids harming one innocent person to save several, yet it also insists we have a duty to promote good outcomes. Some recent framing treats this not as a simple contradiction but as a problem of distributed agency — where no single agent can prevent harm without violating a constraint, but collectively they could. Explain whether this counts as a genuine paradox or merely a tension, and discuss how a deontologist might respond using double effect and agent-relative permissions. In particular, assess whether double effect resolves the tension or just relocates it into the agent's intention, and say whether distributed cases undermine the agent-relative defence.'

here's the student answer (around 450 words):

'The so-called paradox of deontology is really just a tension between two levels of moral reasoning. At the individual level we have the agent-relative prohibition on treating people as mere means, which comes straight out of Kant's Formula of Humanity. At the general level we have the imperfect duty of beneficence. These do not contradict because the prohibition only bites when harm is intended either as an end or as the means to some further end; if it is merely foreseen as a side-effect then it is not covered by the prohibition. This is exactly what the doctrine of double effect, as developed by Aquinas and later refined by Foot and Quinn, allows us to say.

In distributed cases the same logic applies. Suppose ten people each hold a switch and only if all ten pull together is one person killed but nine saved; if each pulls alone nothing happens. Any individual who refuses can claim that they are not causing the harm — the harm is a collective result that requires everyone's participation. So the agent-relative defence survives: no single agent uses anyone as a means, because no single agent produces the outcome.

But there is a hidden problem. Double effect requires not just that the harm be unintended, but that the agent's intention be directed solely at the good effect. In distributed omissions, however, each agent knows that their refusal, together with the others', will produce the death of the nine. This is not quite collective intention, but it is close enough to make the individualist defence look shaky. Some philosophers, notably Scanlon, have argued that this kind of collective knowledge breaks the individualist structure of deontology because it introduces a form of shared agency that contractualism cannot accomodate. I think this misreads Scanlon, since Scanlon's contractualism actually permits collective obligations when they are justifiable to each person. So the distributed case does not break the defence; it just shows that deontology needs a supplementary theory of collective responsibility.

Therefore the tension is relocated rather than solved. Double effect protects the individual agent, but it leaves the group-level outcome unexplained. The paradox remains, but only at the collective level, not for any particular agent.'
