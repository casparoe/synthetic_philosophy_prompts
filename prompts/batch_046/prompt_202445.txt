In the philosophy of consciousness literature, and particularly in discussions that bridge cognitive neuroscience and phenomenology, there has long been a stalled exchange about whether Global Workspace Theory explains phenomenal consciousness or merely explains access consciousness. The separatists grant that the global broadcasting of information—its availability to memory, report, and action-guidance—accounts for what cognitive scientists call conscious access, but they maintain that phenomenal consciousness, understood as the intrinsic subjective quality of experience, remains unexplained by any functional architecture. They appeal to conceptual possibilities in which information is globally broadcast yet lacks any accompanying feel, or to empirical cases such as blindsight and masked priming, to argue that access and phenomenology can be dissociated. The integrationists reply that these appeals either confuse partial access with full broadcasting, or they invoke an unnecessary extra layer: once information is genuinely integrated and made available across the workspace, there is simply nothing left for phenomenal consciousness to be. They treat reports of phenomenology without access, or access without phenomenology, as either misdescriptions or as indications that the information was never truly in the workspace to begin with.

What is puzzling about the deadlock is that neither side seems to reject any specific experimental finding. Both accept the basic dynamics of working memory, attentional gating, and the neural signatures associated with reportability; both acknowledge that subjects can sincerely deny awareness of stimuli that nonetheless influence behavior. The disagreement appears to lie not in the data but in what each side takes an adequate explanation to require. The separatist demands an account of why broadcasting is accompanied by subjective experience at all; the integrationist denies that such a demand is coherent, treating phenomenology as identical to, or constituted by, the functional properties of global availability. I have begun to suspect that the two sides are answering different questions—one metaphysical, one functional—under the single heading the nature of conscious experience, and that the term phenomenal consciousness is doing ambiguous work. Yet I am uncertain whether this is the right diagnosis, or whether there is instead a hidden empirical disagreement about whether dissociation cases are genuinely possible, or about whether phenomenal reports are trustworthy indicators of a distinct property.

Could you diagnose the structure of this disagreement? Is it a verbal dispute about the word phenomenal, a substantive but intractable metaphysical clash, or a case in which two distinct research programs—phenomenology and cognitive architecture—have been forced into false opposition? And if your diagnosis is that the sides are asking different questions, what conceptual distinction or empirical result could actually advance the discussion—something that would allow progress rather than mere restatement? I am less interested in a survey of positions than in an analysis of why the debate has not moved, supported by what each side actually says, together with an honest judgment about whether anything should be expected to resolve it.
