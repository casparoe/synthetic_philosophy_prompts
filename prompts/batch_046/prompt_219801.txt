After a long argument last night with a friend who defends relational conceptions of personhood, I am stuck on a chain she sketched that seems to prove far too much: that any particular wrong eventually wrongs everyone, and that specific moral accountability dissolves into an undifferentiated global harm. I have reconstructed the argument with numbered steps, but I cannot locate the exact slide. Here it is.

(1) On an Ubuntu-style relational view, being a person is not an intrinsic property but a status constituted by reciprocal recognition within a specific web of social relations.

(2) If B's standing as a person is partly constituted by B's relation to A, then damaging A's capacity for recognition does not merely harm A; it damages the relational fabric that partly makes B the person B is.

(3) Therefore, wronging A is also wronging B, not only causally but constitutively: the harm reaches B through the very structure of B's identity.

(4) Recognition relations are never isolated dyads. They are mediated through shared language, institutions, and practices that link B to C, C to D, and so on, with no natural cutoff.

(5) So the constitutive harm done to B propagates outward through the network: B is diminished, which weakens B's relations to others, and so forth.

(6) In contemporary conditions—global markets, digital communication, shared ecological dependencies—the network of mediated recognition extends, for practical purposes, without boundary.

(7) Hence wronging anyone eventually wrongs everyone whose personhood is woven into the same web.

(8) But moral wronging requires specificity: a particular victim, a particular duty-bearer, and a particular breach. If every harm is a harm to all, then no act is a specific wrong to anyone in particular; particular obligations dissolve into an undifferentiated duty of global benevolence.

(9) So the relational view must either abandon the specificity of moral accountability or arbitrarily restrict the "constitutive community" to a local boundary—thereby reintroducing the very insider-outsider distinction it was meant to eliminate.

My friend is committed to keeping (1) and (4), and I am inclined to keep (3) if I can. I am tempted to reject (6) by insisting that constitutive relations are locally bounded even if causal connections are global, but I worry that this just postpones the problem rather than solving it. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for a relational conception of personhood? If the argument is valid and the cost must be paid at the premises, I want to know which premise is least expensive to give up and whether doing so saves the core idea or hollows it out.
