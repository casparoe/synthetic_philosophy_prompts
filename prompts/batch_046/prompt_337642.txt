I was at a panel last week where philosophers and AI researchers kept reaching for the same comparison when they talked about Descartes' brain in a vat: they said it's basically the same predicament as a large language model trained purely on synthetic text with no sensors, no body, no direct causal contact with the world — just tokens in, tokens out, wondering if there's anything "outside" the training distribution. The analogie gets used to argue that skepticism isn't just a human problem, it's really an engineering problem about grounding, and that once you solve grounding for AI you've kind of dissolved the philosophical version too. I'm not sure I buy it, tbh.

Can you map the correspondance out explicitly — what plays the role of the vat, the neuroscientist feeding stimulli, the subject's beliefs about trees and tables, the "external world" that might not exist, and the "solution" if there is one? Then tell me where the mapping actually breaks: is the AI's lack of access epistemic, metaphysical, or just practical? Does the analogy quietly import the idea that consciousness requires embodiment, or can you run it with a fully disembodied LLM? And most importantly, does the analogy actually help dissolve Cartesian skepticism, or was it carrying the conclusion all along — that is, if the AI case is solved by adding sensors, does that mean human skepticism is solved by trusting our senses, or does it miss the point of the skeptical argument entirely? Give me the table first, then the breaks, then a verdict on whether "grounding solves skepticism" survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and say what a better analogy would be if there is one.
