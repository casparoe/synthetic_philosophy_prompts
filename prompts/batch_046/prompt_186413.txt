In her account of happiness, Émilie du Châtelet treats stable conditions—freedom from prejudice, regular study, bodily health, and financial security—as necessary foundations, yet she also insists that intense episodic states, particularly passionate love and moments of intellectual discovery, are what make life genuinely worth living. At the same time, she holds that reason must govern the passions, not suppress them, and that study requires a tranquil, sustained attention that passionate absorption tends to disrupt. Explain whether this combination produces a conceptual tension or merely a practical difficulty in scheduling one's life. Specifically, analyze whether her claim that reason should govern passion can be interpreted as a form of directed amplification—channeling intensity without diminishing it—such that study and passion reinforce rather than compete with one another, or whether governing necessarily entails a cooling restraint that drains the very intensity required for episodic happiness to contribute to the whole. If the tension is real and not merely practical, identify the least costly revision to her framework: weakening the claim that episodic passion is essential, reconceiving structural conditions as sufficient on their own, or redefining rational governance so that it no longer threatens affective intensity? Defend your interpretation of what her view actually commits her to.
