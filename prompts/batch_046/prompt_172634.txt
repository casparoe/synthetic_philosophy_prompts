Look, since I retired from biology I've been trying to read into wireheading and reward tampering in philospophy texts, and I keep hitting the same wall. There's this argument where one side says wireheading is the death blow to hedonism: if pleasure is the highest good, then direct brain stimulation ought to be the best possible life, but it's obviously a disaster, so pleasure can't be the good. The other side pushes back saying wireheading isn't real pleasure at all — it's just a broken tracking mechanism, like feeding false data into a thermostat. The reward signal gets decoupled from the actual evolutionary goal, so what's happening is noise, not genuine pleasure.

Now both sides seem to agree on the basic neuroscience — reward circuits evolved to point toward survival and reproduction. But then they split over whether 'pleasure' needs that tracking relation built in. I think the stall happens because they're answering different questions under one word: one about normative value, one about the causal origin of mental states. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a hidden empirical question about whether decoupled reward feels subjectively identical? And if I'm right that it's mostly verbal or conceptual, is there any distinction or framework that could actually end the impasse, or should we expect it to stay stuck?

Also, I keep coming back to supernormal stimuli — you know, the ethology experiments where birds will sit on giant fake eggs and ignore their own. That seems like the exact same decoupling, just in the wild. Am I forcing a biological analogy onto a philosophical problem, or does it actually help seperate the tracking question from the value question?
