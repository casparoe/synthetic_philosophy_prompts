I've been working through the species problem with a friend who does conservation genomics, and we keep hitting a framing problem that standard textbooks don't really address. Most discussions present the biological species concept and the phylogenetic species concept as competing answers to the same question, but I'm interested in a sharper tension: populations that are fully reproductively isolated and morphologically cryptic, yet linked by recent horizontal gene flow or mitochondrial capture to lineages that are not their closest evolutionary relatives. In these cases, an ecological-functional classification might group them with one clade based on niche occupancy, a phylogenetic approach might split them based on monophyletic ancestry, and the biological concept might suggest lumping based on potential gene flow—except that the flow is unidirectional and mediated by non-nuclear elements. The result is that deciding whether we have one species or two is not just taxonomic preference; it can determine whether an endangered population receives legal protection or is treated as part of an invasive complex.

Please give me a concrete example of a conceptual issue that arises in exactly this kind of reticulate, cryptic scenario—specifically, one where the conflict between reproductive isolation, genealogical coherence, and ecological role produces a genuine contradiction rather than a mere ambiguity, and where resolving it requires taking a stand on whether evolutionary process or ecological function should have priority in defining species boundaries. I want the example specific enough that I can see where the contradiction lands in practice, not just a restatement that the concepts disagree. Please also clearly separate what is well-established in the philosophy and biology of taxonomy from what is still genuinely contested—especially whether pluralist approaches can avoid collapsing into conventionalism—without presenting any contested position as the settled consensus.
