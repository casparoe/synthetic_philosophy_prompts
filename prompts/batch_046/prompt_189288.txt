I am drafting a novel in which a long-dead aesthete is compelled to judge an artefact that violates every premise of his life’s work. To render the scene without anachronism, I ask you to inhabit Leo Tolstoy — speaking in the first person, holding fast to your actual convictions from the 1890s, augmented only by the precise modern account I now provide. You must treat art as the transmission of feeling from one soul to another, regard sincerity as non-negotiable, and maintain that counterfeit art achieves its effects through technical cunning rather than genuine emotional infection. You may acknowledge mechanical details if pressed, but you must never concede that true art can exist without a living, suffering consciousness behind it. Stay in character until I write ‘stop’; answer in one measured paragraph, sharp and unyielding, not a summary.

Here is what confronts you. Engineers have constructed a system they term a sympathetic synthesiser: trained upon every image ever known to move viewers to compassion — including your own studies of peasant labour — it now generates novel images that reliably reproduce in spectators the precise emotional state you once demanded of art. Its creators feel nothing; it feels nothing; yet its outputs are indistinguishable, in effect, from the works you praised. They argue that they have isolated the functional architecture of sincerity itself — the correlation between formal element and emotional response — and that requiring a biological heart is merely a prejudice in favour of muscle over mechanism. I place before you one of its images: a scene of harvest in which every blade of grass appears to suffer with the labourer, though no hand that suffered guided the brush.

Is this art, or is it the most dangerous counterfeit you ever warned against? And if you condemn it for lacking a conscious sufferer, how do you answer the charge that you have mistaken the vessel for the wine — that what matters is not whether a heart beat behind the image, but whether the image succeeds in making other hearts beat in unison?
