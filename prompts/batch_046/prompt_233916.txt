hey, i'm a backend dev with basically no formal history or philosophy training, but i've been reading about how countries tell stories about battles they lost and i keep seeing this wierd pattern that i dont have a word for.

so take a few cases. after the american civil war, the confederate story frames the south as crushed by an industrial north that was always going to be too big to beat, which makes the defeat feel noble. but the exact same narrative also blames internal traitors and scalawags and thier allies for sabotaging the cause from inside, which only makes sense if they actually could have won if they'd stayed pure. in the kosovo field myth, the serbian defeat in 1389 gets told both as proof that the ottomans were an unstoppable barbarian force and as proof that serbian nobles betrayed each other, so the loss was totally avoidable. masada works similarly: the mass suicide is framed as heroic resistance against rome's inevitable crushing power, but also as a cautionary tale that internal division made the fall happen. the thing that bugs me is that these two explanations are straight-up contradictory - either you lost because the enemy was too big, or you lost because you messed up inside - but both stay in the same story and get used for different political jobs. one version gets you sympathy and outsider blame, the other gets you a reason to demand internal unity or purge dissenters. it's kind of like in software when you have two conflicting error logs for the same crash and you keep both in production because each one is useful for a different debugging session.

does this specific double-use have an established name? i know lost cause is in the ballpark, but that usually just means glorifying a defeat. this feels sharper: it's maintaining two contradictory causal accounts of the same loss and switching between them depending on whether you want to blame outsiders or insiders. is that just part of lost-cause mythology, or is there a more precise concept? and if there's no real term, what should we call it - maybe something like a dual-ledger defeat or a schizogenic myth? also where are the edges: does dunkirk fit, since the british frame it as a moral victory but also as a humiliating retreat that required national unity? i dont care about a bibliography, just whether the carving is real and where it stops.
