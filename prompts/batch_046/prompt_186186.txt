I'm a retired oceanographer — thirty years on research vessels, mostly studying deep-water currents — and I only started reading philosophy seriously after I stopped working, usually while fixing up an old boat in the yard. I'm giving the opening remarks at a workshop we're holding for Dr. Elena Vasquez, my old mentor, who just turned eighty and technically retired but still comes into the lab every morning like nothing happened. The audience is split: half are her former grad students who know her taxonomy work on obscure deep-sea organisms inside out, the other half are family members and university admin people who wouldn't know a nematode from a noodle. I want the talk to be about ten minutes, and I want it built around Van Fraassen's Reflection principle — the idea that a rational person's current beliefs ought to line up with what they expect their future self to believe — but applied to how we talk about a scientific legacy that's still unfolding. I want this to actually constrain what I say, not just decorate it: for instance, if I praise her for constantly revising her classifications when new data came in, that needs to connect to reflection in a real structural way, and I should probably avoid framing her as someone who "never wavered" since that'd sit weird with the principle. I'm also lately obsessed with this image from statistical mechanics — I might be misremembering the details — where a system at any moment has to be consistent with the equilibrium it's eventually going to reach, and I'd like to use that as a loose metaphor for evaluating a career at eighty that isn't finished yet. Don't quote anyone verbatim, I don't want a bibliography or reading list, and please keep it casual since I'll be reading it aloud. Oh, and I tend to misspell recieve, so don't make it sound too polished.
