For my philosophy of religion seminar, we are examining Ayer's verificationist claim that assertions about post-mortem survival are not false but meaningless, since they cannot be empirically verified. I am trying to apply this framework to a problem that only became philosophically serious after his death: suppose future neurotechnology achieves a complete structural emulation of a human brain's informational patterns, producing a substrate-independent continuation of mental life. Would Ayer have treated such an emulation as a meaningful, empirically checkable form of personal survival—because the patterns could in principle be observed and causally interacted with—or would he have insisted that the cessation of the original biological organism still ends personal identity, leaving the emulation as merely a duplicate? I am especially interested in whether his distinction between logical and empirical possibility would allow him to reject an immaterial "soul" while accepting, or still rejecting, a technologically mediated continuation.
