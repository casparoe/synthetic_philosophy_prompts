I'm a doctoral student in ancient historiography and philosophy of history, and I'm working through an essay on the epistemic boundary between legendary narrative and historical record. I want to compare three canonical cases—the Trojan War, the Arthurian cycle, and the Exodus narrative—to ask whether there is a defensible threshold at which a legend becomes history, or whether the distinction collapses into a graded spectrum of evidentiary confidence.

The controversy I have in mind opposes what I call the material correlation thesis to the genre autonomy thesis. On the material correlation thesis, once independent archaeological, toponymic, and linguistic data align with the spatial and temporal setting of a narrative—Bronze Age stratification in northwestern Anatolia, high-status post-Roman military material culture in Britain, or Late Bronze Age demographic shifts in the Levant—the narrative should be treated as historically anchored, even if its supernatural or anachronistic elements are clearly embellishments. The genre autonomy thesis, by contrast, insists that without near-contemporary documentary attestation and a demonstrable separation of reportorial kernel from mythic accretion, correlation remains epistemically inert: the same material traces could just as easily underwrite a later ideological projection onto an empty past.

My specific worry is that both positions smuggle in unstated assumptions about the asymmetry between negative and positive archaeological evidence—whether the absence of direct proof for Arthur or the Exodus functions differently from the presence of indirect proof for Troy. To sharpen this, I'm introducing a distinction between narrative fossilization, in which a story hardens around a real event but loses its historical plasticity, and historical sedimentation, in which successive generations deposit interpretive layers onto a documented occurrence. I need to know whether these are genuinely different processes or merely two descriptions of the same hermeneutic circle.

Write an essay weighing these two theses against each other through the three case studies. Reach and defend a specific conclusion: either that there is a coherent, non-arbitrary criterion for upgrading a legend to history (and specify what it is), or that the distinction is irreducibly methodological and should be replaced by a graded evidentiary framework. Do not end non-committally—take a side and justify it, and address explicitly how your conclusion handles the asymmetry between negative and positive archaeological evidence in at least one of the three cases.
