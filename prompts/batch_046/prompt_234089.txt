I'm taking a graduate seminar on the philosophy of artificial intelligence, and in our discussion of value alignment my classmates and I kept returning to a specific design pattern in reward modeling. The institutional practice—common across several major labs—is to present human annotators with pairwise comparisons of model outputs, record which option is preferred, and then treat the resulting dataset as noisy samples from an underlying reward function that inverse reinforcement learning can recover. Crucially, these ratings are typically pooled across annotators with different cultural backgrounds and normative commitments, then collapsed into a single scalar reward model, as though there were one true preference ordering obscured by noise rather than genuinely conflicting evaluative frameworks.

What philosophical commitments about preference, rationality, and aggregation does this practice presuppose? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory: does it require a revealed-preference view on which behavior transparently tracks stable internal utilities, and does it silently rule out constructivist or strategic accounts of evaluation? Does the aggregation step presuppose that interpersonal differences in judgment are epistemic errors around a shared target, or could it be justified on non-cognitivist or democratic grounds? Finally, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say what concrete changes—say, maintaining separate reward models for different normative communities or abandoning the unitary reward assumption altogether—would be necessitated if we replaced its implicit philosophy of mind and value with one that treats human feedback as irreducibly plural, context-dependent, and sometimes deliberately strategic.
