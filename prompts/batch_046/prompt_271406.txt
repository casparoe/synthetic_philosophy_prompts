In Switzerland, constitutional amendments require both a popular majority and a cantonal majority, while ordinary federal legislation is parliamentary unless citizens trigger an optional referendum; California allows voters to enact or repeal statutes and constitutional provisions by ballot proposition, including technically intricate measures on energy-market restructuring, criminal sentencing, and bond financing; and the United Kingdom held a 2016 referendum presenting voters with a binary choice on EU membership without specifying departure terms. Meanwhile, the same legal cultures treat jury deliberation and judicial statutory interpretation as competencies that must not be subjected to popular vote. What philosophical commitments about democratic legitimacy, epistemic authority, and the division of cognitive labor does this institutional pattern presuppose? Does it require the view that direct authorization is intrinsically valuable even when voters lack technical mastery, or can it be sustained by a sharp distinction between value judgments (properly democratic) and instrumental implementation (properly expert)? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the design strictly requires from what it merely tolerates, and assess whether it is consistent with the legal principle that negligence liability turns on objective standards of reasonable care rather than on popular opinion about risk.
