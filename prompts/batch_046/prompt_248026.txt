I’m putting together a workshop for about 18 people — mix of philosophy grad students and seniors — one 90-minute session on collective memory vs. the historical record. I want an activity where they actually feel the squeeze when a community’s living memory and the archive flat-out contradict each other, not just discuss it in the abstract. The idea should hit them through the rules and the hidden twist before anyone in the room names the concept out loud.

Please design a full runnable game around a fictional case I’m calling the "Port Marrow Winter Lock" of 1974. The oral tradition among descendants says roughly forty families survived by pooling resources, led by a woman named Maris; the municipal archives (print these as document packets) show hierarchical rationing for only twelve families, with Maris not present until after the crisis ended. Add a hidden reveal halfway through: a discovered letter shows the original archivist was politically motivated to erase Maris. Split the group into Memory Keepers (6 people), Archive Committee (6 people), and a Town Board (6 people). The Board must produce a single 100-word commemorative statement and vote on one funding choice — either an oral-history project or a digitization grant — but they only have one token to spend. Give the Memory Keepers a "mnemonic veto" card they can play once: if used, the Board must include the community version in the memorial but loses credibility points with outside funders. Give the Archive Committee an "archival veto" with the reverse trade-off — I accidentally called both "mnemonic veto" above, so keep them distinct in the rules. Use a symbolic payoff that actually matters in the room: for instance, the Board’s final decision determines whether everyone gets a 10-minute cookie break or has to stay in for an extra writing task. Keep it paper-only, no tech.

Then give me the debrief: map each moment (the veto play, the budget vote, the letter reveal) onto the philosophical tension between collective memory and historical record. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — probably the Board writes a vague statement that offends both sides — and how to handle that without just lecturing. Be frank about what feature of the idea the game can’t reproduce, like the real emotional weight of ancestral memory, and tell me exactly what I’ll need to supply myself (maybe a brief testimony or a short reading). No bibliography needed, just the design with rules, materials, payoff scheme, and an honest failure forecast.
