As a doctoral student working at the intersection of democratic theory and the history of early modern political philosophy, I have noticed a recurring argumentative structure in contemporary debates over expanding the franchise—specifically in disputes about lowering the voting age and extending local electoral rights to long-term non-citizen residents. In each case, one side treats the proposed expansion as an additive application of an unchanged democratic principle: the collective subject that exercises sovereignty remains the same kind of entity, and the change merely distributes membership in that subject to additional individuals who satisfy the relevant criteria. The other side treats the identical expansion as constitutionally transformative: including sixteen-year-olds shifts the intertemporal horizon of collective decisions in a way that redefines what self-rule means for that body, while including resident non-citizens shifts the boundary between civic membership and territorial presence, thereby altering not just the size but the very nature of the demos. The disagreement therefore seems to concern not merely who ought to be included, but whether including them changes the authority being exercised at all.

This pattern feels adjacent to Spinoza’s distinction in the Tractatus Politicus between democracy as one form of imperium and the natural power of the multitude, since for Spinoza the power of the multitude is not an aggregate of individual powers but a relational capacity constituted by the actual agreements and exclusions among those included. Yet I am uncertain whether democratic theory already possesses an established term for this specific move—the disagreement over whether democratic expansion is compositional (adding members to a fixed collective subject) or constitutional (reconstituting the collective subject itself). The nearest concepts I can identify are the boundary problem of the demos, debates over the all-affected principle, and perhaps analogues of the non-identity problem applied to collective agents, but none of these seem to capture precisely the formal structure I have described: two parties agreeing on the factual change in membership while disagreeing on whether that change leaves the nature of democratic authority intact.

Is there an established name for this distinction? If so, how does it relate to Spinoza’s separation of the form of state from the potentia multitudinis, and how is it distinguished from mere equivocation over the term “the people”? If nothing established fits well, I would welcome a coined term together with a precise definition and clear boundary cases that separate it from both the standard boundary problem and from Spinoza’s own framework. I will not be satisfied with a bare label; I need the carving—what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie, particularly with respect to cases where the membership change is large but allegedly leaves the collective identity untouched.
