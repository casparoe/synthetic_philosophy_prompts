I'm a philosophy professor delivering tomorrow's opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop, "Objects and Institutions: The Limits of Material Inference." The audience is split between field archaeologists—who reconstruct ritual economies and social structures from burial assemblages, settlement layouts, and ceramic wear patterns—and philosophers of science who regard such reconstructions as underdetermined projections of modern categories onto mute objects. There will also be graduate students and funding partners with no specialist background. I need a formal, polished speech—one tight paragraph, no more than 275 words—that makes the philosophical problem do real structural work rather than serving as decoration. Frame the discussion around the underdetermination of belief-reconstruction from material remains, but avoid both naive realism and blanket skepticism. To do this, introduce and apply a specific, under-discussed conceptual distinction: "exclusionary inference," in which a spatial arrangement or artifact yields knowledge of an institutional constraint by physically preventing alternatives (what the layout makes impossible), versus "direct ascription," in which we treat an object as a transparent symbol for a particular belief or practice. Use the distinction to show the archaeologists that their most defensible claims operate in the first mode and therefore carry genuine philosophical weight, and to show the philosophers that their skepticism is properly aimed at the second mode rather than at material inference itself. The tone must respect both groups—no condescension, no lecture—and must remain accessible to the non-specialists present. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to specific texts; the goal is to open the day's conversation, not to survey the literature.
