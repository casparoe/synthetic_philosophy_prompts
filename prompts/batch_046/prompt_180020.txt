I have come to take seriously a strong metaphysical version of the Ubuntu-inspired relational account of personhood: not only that moral status is socially constituted, but that the persistence conditions for human persons are constituted by sustained webs of reciprocal recognition rather than by psychological continuity or bodily continuity alone. On this view, the same individual might survive radical psychological discontinuity — for instance, total amnesia — provided the community continues to recognise and address them as the same relational node; conversely, an individual who retains full psychological continuity yet undergoes complete and irreversible social rupture — permanent exile coupled with the erasure of all communal records and relationships — might cease to be that numerically identical person, since there is no underlying inner substrate preserving identity independently of these relations. The view seems to capture something important about social death that individualist theories overlook, yet I am unsure how exposed it really is.

What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I want an honest audit of its vulnerabilities, sorted by how decisive they would be. Which empirical cases — perhaps involving individuals raised in extreme isolation, or patients with neurodegenerative conditions who are no longer recognised by their communities — and which conceptual arguments — for example, analogies with the persistence of institutions or the conditions for group agency — would genuinely undermine it? I am particularly concerned with whether the thesis can be qualified into unfalsifiability: if defenders can always invoke a hidden relational thread, or claim that the isolated subject was never fully a person to begin with, does that insulation become a defect rather than a strength? Please distinguish between objections that a committed relational theorist should concede and those they would reasonably dismiss, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and assess whether any development — empirical, formal, or conceptual — could settle the dispute, or whether the disagreement ultimately rests on bedrock intuitions about what a person fundamentally is.
