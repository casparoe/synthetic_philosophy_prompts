I have recently begun rethinking my career priorities after recognizing how much of my professional effort is driven by status competition rather than by genuine need, and Keynes's vision of the good life—particularly his distinction between satiable absolute needs and insatiable relative needs—has become personally urgent. Among the open interpretive and practical questions surrounding that vision, I am trying to decide which deserve priority: how to operationalize the need-distinction in an attention economy where relative wants appear algorithmically amplified; whether his expectation that material abundance would automatically yield leisure fails mainly because positional goods expand to absorb productivity gains; what institutional mechanisms could prevent a post-scarcity society from collapsing back into competitive status-seeking; and how his aesthetic ideal of treating life itself as an art form interacts with contemporary philosophical theories of well-being. Rather than a survey, I want a defended prioritization. For each question you rank highly, explain why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers rather than economists or policymakers, and what evidence over the next few years would cause you to reorder the list. In particular, if Keynes was wrong that solving the economic problem automatically resolves the problem of living well, does that error undermine his entire framework or merely call for a supplementary theory of social comparison and institutional design?
