I spent three decades in experimental condensed-matter physics before turning, in retirement, to philosophy of science and epistemology. Over the past year I have been tracking the judgments of a philosopher colleague, Dr. Lena Vasquez, on how rational agents ought to calibrate confidence when their first-person reasoning conflicts with statistical base rates about their domain. Her rulings on four cases have been consistent, but they appear to underdetermine the underlying principle.

First, she considered a mathematician who derived a result using an unconventional proof strategy. The base rate for subtle errors in such proofs in that subfield is approximately 35 percent. The mathematician had independently verified each logical step three times. Vasquez ruled that the mathematician should maintain high confidence and treat the inside view as dominant, on the grounds that formal verification is transparent to the reasoner.

Second, she considered a clinical psychologist predicting therapeutic success for a particular patient on the basis of deep case knowledge. The modality's success rate in comparable cases is only about 40 percent. Vasquez ruled that the psychologist should substantially moderate her confidence and weight the outside view heavily, because the causal mechanisms governing individual therapeutic outcomes are largely opaque to introspection.

Third, she considered a historian predicting an imminent coup in a specific country based on granular knowledge of local factional dynamics. The historical base rate for correct coup predictions by outside experts in that region is roughly 25 percent. Vasquez ruled that the historian should adopt epistemic modesty: she should not discard her inside view, but she should treat it as one weighted factor among several, rather than as decisive.

Fourth, she considered a chess grandmaster evaluating a complex endgame position. The base rate for grandmaster misjudgment in endgames of comparable complexity is about 18 percent. Vasquez ruled that the grandmaster should trust her evaluation but apply a calibrated downward adjustment, since pattern recognition is largely reliable though not infallible.

These four rulings are jointly compatible with at least two general principles. One holds that the inside view dominates precisely to the extent that the reasoning process is formally checkable or perceptually direct, with the outside view supplying only a background constraint. The other holds that rational confidence should always begin with the base-rate prior and then update by the strength of inside-view evidence, with the four rulings reflecting different magnitudes of permissible update.

Now consider a new case. I have recently developed an interpretation of quantum measurement—specifically, an information-theoretic framework that resolves both Wigner's-friend scenarios and delayed-choice anomalies by treating measurement outcomes as correlation constraints rather than as collapse events. The base rate for non-mainstream interpretations proposed by researchers outside the core quantum-foundations community being eventually abandoned or shown equivalent to standard formulations is approximately 85 percent. I have verified the formal mathematics twice and discussed it informally with two colleagues, who found no errors. At the same time, I recognize that my long-standing metaphysical commitment to information realism predates the technical construction and may be functioning as a motivated reasoning filter.

Predict Vasquez's verdict: should I maintain high confidence, apply heavy epistemic modesty and defer largely to the consensus, or adopt a calibrated middle position? Identify which of the four precedents is the closest analog. Then state whether her precedents genuinely underdetermine this case, and if so, articulate the principle I would be adopting with each of the two main choices. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case that would best distinguish the transparency-based principle from the universal Bayesian-updating principle.
