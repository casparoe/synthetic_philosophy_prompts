After a long argument with a friend last night about Milton Friedman’s defense of unrealistic assumptions in economic modeling — what people call the F-twist — I want to press on the analogy we both kept reaching for. My friend insisted that a model built on false premises is like a subway map: it deliberately distorts distances, omits surface streets, and misrepresents the actual geometry of the city, yet nobody demands a topographically faithful map because the only thing that matters is whether it gets the commuter to the right station. He used this to license a relaxed attitude toward idealization: if predictions are accurate, the falsity of assumptions is as harmless as a subway map’s scale distortion, so Friedman is right that realism is a red herring.

How far does the subway-map analogy actually hold? Give me an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the territory, the map-maker, the commuter, the deliberate distortion, the omitted surface detail, and the successful arrival? Then identify where the mapping fractures: does an economic model serve a single predetermined destination like a station, or must it also support counterfactual interventions — pulling a policy lever whose effects we have not observed — that a static map never addresses? Does the analogy quietly assume that the model-user already knows the underlying terrain and only needs orientation, whereas policymakers often rely on the model precisely because they lack direct access to causal mechanisms? Separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine Friedman’s specific defense from those that are philosophically harmless. Does the relaxed conclusion — that unrealistic assumptions are benign so long as predictions hold — survive once we notice that a subway map’s distortions are engineered for users with fixed goals, while economic models are frequently used to discover both mechanisms and goals?

Also, say whether the analogy smuggles in a controversial instrumentalist view of scientific representation — that a model’s only job is predictive guidance rather than explanatory understanding — and whether defending the analogy therefore requires defending that view independently. Finally, is there a more adequate analogy that captures both the predictive success Friedman prizes and the mechanistic explanation he dismisses — perhaps a flight simulator rather than a map — and what would adopting that substitute do to the argument?
