I am writing an essay for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics and ethics of privacy, and I have become stuck on what seems to me a decisive objection to treating privacy as an independent moral category. In some recent literature there is a structural account—occasionally referred to as causal seclusion theory—which defines privacy not as a normative relation but as a physical-causal condition: a state is private with respect to some feature if that feature is insulated from external causal interaction, regardless of whether any observer exists or intends to observe. Under this definition, a boulder resting in an unvisited subterranean cavern possesses perfect privacy regarding its internal mineral structure, since no external agent can causally interact with that structure without physically penetrating the rock.

Yet we do not attribute privacy rights to boulders, and we do not think it morally wrong to drill into the stone and expose its interior. From this I am inclined to draw a stronger conclusion: privacy has no independent normative weight whatsoever. The reason we protect human informational boundaries is entirely derivative of autonomy interests, and the boulder case demonstrates that without an autonomous subject, structural seclusion is morally empty. So it seems that whenever we invoke privacy, we are really just invoking autonomy in a different vocabulary.

Is this reduction of privacy to autonomy sound? Or is there a principled reason to maintain that privacy retains intrinsic value for persons—even, perhaps, value that does not collapse into self-determination—despite the fact that structurally identical conditions of causal insulation apply indiscriminately to inanimate objects? I want to be sure I am not missing a distinction that would block the inference from rocks to persons.
