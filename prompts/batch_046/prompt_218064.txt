Consider a proposed municipal budgeting mechanism in which residents are sorted into "inquiry circles" and must frame spending priorities as empirically testable hypotheses about community welfare rather than as expressions of preference or group identity. Each proposal enters a structured challenge phase in which other residents, aided by municipal data analysts, attempt to identify disconfirming evidence or unintended consequences; proponents are required to revise their proposals in response to any successful challenge before the proposal can advance. Final adoption requires that the revised hypothesis has survived the available tests to the satisfaction of the circle, not a simple majority vote. The rules explicitly forbid appeals to tradition, unexamined moral intuition, or the inherent authority of elected representatives as justifications.

What philosophical commitments about democracy, inquiry, and value does this institutional design presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it treat democratic legitimacy as deriving primarily from the rigor of collective problem-solving rather than from the aggregation of pre-political preferences? Must it assume that public welfare is an experimentally determinable property rather than a plural or irreducibly contested concept? Is the mechanism strictly Deweyan—treating democracy as continuous cooperative inquiry with no fixed ends—or could it also be justified under a Schumpeterian competitive-elite model, a Rawlsian framework of public reason, or an agonistic pluralist view that treats conflict as constitutive rather than resolvable? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, identify where it is philosophically underdetermined, and specify what structural features would have to change if its underlying commitments were replaced by a view that treats democratic decision as primarily a mechanism for registering incommensurable values rather than for testing hypotheses about a common good.
