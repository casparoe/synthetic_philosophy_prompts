I have been tracking a recurring interpretive deadlock that shows up in legal interpretation, in expository mathematical writing, and in any text that employs explicit framing devices—preambles, meta-remarks, or embedded definitions—to govern how its explicit content should be read. The pattern is this. Two careful readers agree on every literal sentence and on the framing instructions that specify whether to read literally, analogically, or conditionally. They also agree on the explicit claims being advanced. Yet they diverge sharply on what the text is really doing—one might say its deepest point concerns the limits of formalization, the other that it concerns the inevitability of formalization—and neither can locate the disagreement in any particular sentence or framing error. What makes the deadlock especially strange is a second-order move I keep observing: when pressed on their reading of the deepest layer, a reader will sometimes reclassify a piece of the explicit text as belonging to the framing layer, or vice versa, not because they have changed their view of what the words say, but because they need to preserve the coherence of their deeper reading. Thus the boundary between framing instructions, explicit content, and implicit significance becomes unstable under pressure from disagreement about the third layer, even though everyone agrees the words themselves have not changed.

Is there an established concept for this specific configuration—where agreement holds on both the literal text and its decoding rules, yet a contested implicit thesis causes the very distinction between frame and content to become negotiable? I am not asking about ordinary ambiguity, about disputes over framing rules in the first place, or about disagreements over authorial intent resolvable by biographical evidence. I want to know whether philosophers of language, hermeneutic theorists, or the literature on layered communication—such as Hofstadter's distinction in Gödel, Escher, Bach among frame messages, outer messages, and inner messages—have named and characterized exactly this kind of boundary instability. In particular, does the phenomenon have edges that clearly separate it from simple equivocation, from strategic retreat to framing claims, and from cases where ambiguity genuinely resides in the explicit layer? And if no precise term exists, what would be the right way to define its conditions—especially the feature that the frame-content boundary shifts only when the deepest message layer is contested?
