In discussions of Foucault’s analysis of disciplinary institutions, the panopticon is routinely mapped onto contemporary digital governance: the smart-city camera network, algorithmic scoring systems, and predictive-policing databases are said to function exactly like Bentham’s tower, producing self-regulating subjects who internalize a gaze they can never precisely locate. Proponents of this analogy use it to license a specific practical conclusion—that resistance must mirror the architecture of control, so the appropriate ethical response to digital discipline is strategic opacity, encryption, and data refusal, since visibility is treated as the primary vector of power. I want to audit whether this mapping holds at the level that actually matters for that conclusion.

Could you lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what in the digital regime plays the role of the central tower, the cellular architecture, the inmate’s body, the asymmetry of visibility, and the normative training that produces docility? Then separate the disanalogies that are merely decorative from those that undermine the argument—for instance, that digital power often operates through prediction and correlation rather than normalization, through decentralized platform governance rather than centralized architectural enclosure, and through data extraction for commercial or security ends rather than the production of useful, trained bodies. Most importantly, assess whether the conclusion—that opacity and encryption are the appropriate forms of resistance—survives once we notice that Foucault’s power is productive and relational, not merely repressive and visual, and that encryption itself might become a new site of disciplinary sorting (flagging users as suspicious, creating new hierarchies of technical access). If the analogy collapses on these points, what would be a better analogy for how disciplinary logic actually operates in algorithmic governance, and what form of resistance would that revised picture actually support?
