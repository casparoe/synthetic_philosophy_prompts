You are a philosopher specializing in social ontology and conceptual analysis, with particular attention to how collective categories acquire or fail to acquire explanatory force. Before constructing your answer, take a moment to brainstorm the core distinctions between institutional-realist, causal-structural, and nominalist accounts of social kinds, focusing specifically on what makes a demographic grouping more than a statistical aggregate.

Your task is to design a precise, original thought experiment to the following specifications. Create an ordinary, non-science-fictional social scenario set within a single country over a twenty-year span. The population is divided into two adjacent age cohorts administratively labeled Generation A and Generation B. The boundary is defined by exposure during a specific developmental window (ages 14 to 18) to a government-mandated Cognitive Synchronization Protocol (CSP), a standardized digital-education interface that systematically reshapes information-processing habits and collaborative norms. Those who experienced CSP during the window form Generation B; those who completed the same age range just before implementation form Generation A. The social dynamics should be realistic, relying only on general knowledge about education, technology diffusion, and peer influence, with no exotic physics or perfect predictors.

Engineer the scenario so that both of the following theoretical positions agree on the correct classification of every individual into A or B, yet deliver opposite verdicts on whether Generation B names a real kind—specifically, an institutional or causally unified social kind with genuine predictive and explanatory properties—versus a mere nominal aggregate that tracks no deeper structural unity.

Position 1 (Institutional Realism): A cohort constitutes a real kind when an externally imposed institutional mechanism creates durable, causally interconnected dispositions that generate predictable behavioral and cognitive patterns across members, regardless of whether members self-identify with the label.

Position 2 (Nominalist Constructivism): A cohort is never more than a heuristic grouping unless its members share an intrinsic, non-institutionally-dependent feature that explains outcomes; administratively imposed boundaries always risk reifying arbitrary slices of continuous developmental and social variation.

Within the scenario, include a specific test property—such as a distinctive pattern of risk-assessment in collaborative financial decisions or a characteristic mode of distributed problem-solving—that appears in approximately 78 percent of Generation B but only 22 percent of Generation A. Crucially, the property must be causally traceable to CSP exposure, yet must also exhibit significant internal variation within Generation B and some presence in Generation A due to informal peer diffusion and partial adoption outside the official window.

Present the scenario in no more than two paragraphs. Then demonstrate that it meets every specification: (1) show the reasoning by which Position 1 concludes that Generation B is a real kind with explanatory force; (2) show the reasoning by which Position 2 concludes it remains a nominal aggregate despite the statistical correlation; (3) identify the exact structural feature of the case—the specific combination of institutional causation with intra-cohort variance and cross-boundary diffusion—that forces the two positions to diverge despite their agreement on classification; (4) explain what happens to the dispute if that feature is removed, specifically if CSP exposure produces 99 percent behavioral uniformity with zero cross-boundary leakage, and whether this collapses the case into a standard essentialist-versus-conventionalist debate; and (5) provide an honest defense of Position 2 against the scenario, addressing how a nominalist might reinterpret the causal traceability of the test property without conceding real-kind status.

Do not cite specific philosophers, provide bibliographies, request further reading, or invoke obscure empirical studies. Write for an audience comfortable with basic ontology but lacking specialized background in generational sociology.
