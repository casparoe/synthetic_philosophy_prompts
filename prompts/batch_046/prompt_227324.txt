Here's an argument I sketched about intuition pumps, and I now find it suspicious because it seems to prove that philosophical reasoning collapses into self-defeat. (1) An intuition pump works by isolating a single conceptual variable and stripping away the surrounding context that normally regulates its application. (2) If removing that context systematically shifts the judgments we make, then the pump is not testing our ordinary concept but substituting an artifactual concept produced by the framing itself. (3) Empirical work on framing effects shows that moral and modal judgments shift reliably when background details are removed or reordered. (4) So in every intuition pump, the concept being tested is the artifactual one, not the ordinary concept we care about. (5) Therefore, no verdict delivered by an intuition pump provides evidence for claims about our ordinary concepts. (6) But core philosophical theses—about personal identity, knowledge, or moral responsibility—are largely defended by appealing to such verdicts. (7) Hence those theses lack evidential support. (8) The reasoning in (2)-(4), however, itself depends on comparing our intuitive reactions across framed and unframed cases—exactly the kind of pump-dependent comparison it condemns. (9) So either intuition pumps are evidentially useless everywhere, including here, which makes (7) unsupported, or (4) is false and isolation does not always substitute concepts. Where does the slide happen? I would prefer to keep (1) and (3) and reject the move that generates the self-defeat, but I want to know exactly what I'm paying—whether I must deny that framing ever creates artifactual concepts, or only that it always does, and whether that saves the philosophical use of pumps or merely weakens the skeptical conclusion.
