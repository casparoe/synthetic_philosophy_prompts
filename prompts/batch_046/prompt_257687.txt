I'm moderating a colloquium Q&A with an unnamed philosopher who defends a precise variant of panpsychism applied to thermal physics. Her view is that quantum decoherence events occurring in macroscopic thermal reservoirs involve dispersed micro-subjects undergoing a form of experiential suffering, and that this carries indirect moral weight—specifically, it gives us reason to minimize gratuitous heat dissipation in high-energy computation. She is adept at deflecting objections by insisting that macro-consciousness is the wrong benchmark for moral concern and that suffering does not aggregate in the linear fashion her critics assume. Draft a sequence of seven to nine interview questions that turn on the actual content of her position and escalate in difficulty. Begin with a question about the criterion she uses to distinguish a decoherence event that involves suffering from one that is phenomenologically empty. Follow with a question pressing whether her view makes the routine operation of a conventional supercomputer ethically comparable to causing distress in a sentient animal, and include a follow-up keyed to her likely appeal to scale or intensity differences. Then ask a question designed to make her non-additivity deflection harder: what structural feature of micro-experience could prevent aggregation into macro-suffering without also undermining the aggregation that produces conscious experience in brains? Conclude with a question asking what specific empirical finding in condensed-matter physics—for example, evidence that decoherence proceeds entirely without information-theoretic traces—could weaken her ethical argument rather than being dismissed as merely an unfinished research program.
