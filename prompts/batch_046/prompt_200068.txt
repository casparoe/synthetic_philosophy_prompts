You are a senior urban philosopher and former ethics advisor to municipal zoning boards, with specific expertise in translating normative political theory into operational decision tools for non-philosophers. Before constructing the deliverable, take a moment to brainstorm the central tension you must preserve: the philosophical idea commonly called the right to the city—understood as residents’ claim to participate in and benefit from urban space rather than merely to hold property title within it—versus the practical need for a board to distinguish legitimate structural-exclusion claims from preference-based opposition (NIMBYism). Identify the three distinctions that must survive simplification: use-value impacts versus exchange-value impacts; participatory standing based on affected interest versus standing based solely on geographic proximity; and temporal equity, meaning whether accommodating growth shifts burdens onto future or marginalized residents rather than distributing them fairly.

Your task is to design a reusable, six-step decision procedure for a five-member municipal zoning appeals board that meets twice monthly and must complete written findings within forty-five minutes per case. Board members are experienced planners and community representatives, not philosophers, and the chair must sign off on every recommendation. The procedure must guide them in deciding whether to uphold or reject an appeal opposing a proposed housing development, specifically testing whether the opposition expresses a valid right-to-the-city claim or an illegitimate exclusionary preference. Structure it as an ordered checklist with decision nodes. For each step, specify: the exact question to ask; what counts as sufficient factual evidence; whether the determination is factual or normative; where the procedure terminates in a clear recommendation; and where it must halt and hand the decision to the chair because the conflict is irreducibly one of values rather than verifiable conditions.

The procedure must include:
- A flowchart-in-words with no more than six ordered steps, including at least one hard stop that yields a clear rejection of the appeal (standing or substance fails) and at least one explicit hand-off point where the facts are settled but competing values require the chair’s judgment.
- For each step, a brief “test sentence” that a board member without philosophical training can apply directly to testimony and planning documents.
- Explicit flags marking cases the procedure cannot settle—especially where use-value harm and exchange-value protection are inextricably intertwined in the same witness statement—and a brief explanation of why simplification fails there.
- Three worked applications: (A) an appeal by long-term renters opposing a market-rate tower because it will displace local vendors and reduce transit access for low-income workers; (B) an appeal by homeowners opposing an affordable-housing complex, citing shadow studies and “neighborhood character,” with documented evidence that the homeowners’ association excluded non-owner residents from its deliberations; and (C) a case where the opposition has genuine participatory standing and documented use-value harm, yet the procedure directs the board to reject the appeal because blocking the development would concentrate displacement risk onto unhoused populations elsewhere in the city. For case C, explain precisely why the procedure produces that counter-intuitive recommendation and what the board must formally acknowledge if it chooses to overrule it.
- A concluding section listing what the procedure is not designed to do—for instance, it does not calculate optimal density, it does not adjudicate conflicts between municipal and regional housing obligations, and it does not replace democratic deliberation with algorithmic judgment.

Write in formal American English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific texts or authors. If you invoke any conceptual framework, refer to it in general terms only. Present your brief brainstorming first, then the procedure, then the three worked cases, then the limitations.
