I am developing a conceptual framework for a graduate seminar on intergenerational justice and want to stage an adversarial collaboration between two camps that both acknowledge American chattel slavery and its enduring structural effects yet diverge sharply on the normative architecture of repair. Camp One holds the Lineal Liability Thesis: moral obligations to repair flow exclusively through genealogical continuity to identifiable descendants of the enslaved, with the correct metric being the restoration of specific assets or statuses wrongfully withheld from lineal ancestors, and with liability attaching primarily to institutions that directly inherited ill-gotten gains. Camp Two holds the Structural Depreciation Thesis: the wrong is not merely a private tort against individuals but a persistent institutional deformation that depreciated social capital across generations; therefore repair is owed to all persons presently situated within the structurally disadvantaged group, regardless of genealogical proof, and the measure is the closure of present structural gaps rather than the restoration of particular ancestral claims. Both camps reject colorblind universalism and both reject the idea that mere passage of time extinguishes the obligation, but they disagree on whether genealogy is a necessary condition for claimant standing and on whether the correct counterfactual baseline is the individual ancestor's trajectory or the group's collective social position.

Draft the joint statement that representatives of these two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want the deliverable structured as follows: first, a numbered section of agreed claims, including about the historical facts taken as given, about what the opposing camp gets right, and about the formal conditions any adequate reparative scheme must satisfy; second, a disagreements table with precise formulations of where they still differ, each phrased so that its own camp would accept the wording of its position and reject the wording of the opposing position; third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side, with explicit notation of which are empirical, which conceptual, and which normative; fourth, for each crux, one argument, hypothetical case, or test both sides agree would genuinely bear on it, with each side's prediction or interpretation of the result recorded in advance; and fifth, an explicit identification of any portion of their dispute that is verbal rather than substantive, with an explanation of which shared formulation dissolves the appearance of disagreement. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; write from the shared conceptual vocabulary above. If you refer to actual historical episodes, rely only on widely known ones — such as broken Reconstruction-era land promises or the destruction of accumulated Black wealth in Tulsa — paraphrased without quotation marks. The document should read as a constructive text both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement rendered exact rather than rhetorical.
