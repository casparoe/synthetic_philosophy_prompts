I have been examining the institutional architecture of environmental governance, and I keep returning to a structural asymmetry that seems philosophically loaded but rarely discussed in precisely these terms. In fisheries, groundwater basins, and communal grazing lands, the state treats the resource as a common-pool good subject to extraction quotas, monitoring, and sometimes tradable permits—mechanisms built on the idea that unregulated rivalry in consumption will deplete a shared stock. At the same time, conservation policy protects endangered species and habitats through preservation mandates, often treating ecosystems or species-level persistence as collective goods whose value is not reducible to human extraction. Yet neither framework applies any analogous regulatory or distributive mechanism to the welfare of individual wild animals within those systems: a suffering fish in a quota-managed stock, or an injured mammal in a protected watershed, has no standing in the allocation calculus, even though its condition is causally linked to the management regime. Meanwhile, when those same kinds of animals enter captivity—zoos, rehabilitation centers, or breeding programs—the state or charities often expend resources on their individual welfare, but typically only under conditions of direct human control, charismatic rarity, or endangered status.

What philosophical commitments about value, rights, and the boundaries of a commons does this tripartite division presuppose? Must one hold that wild suffering lies outside the domain of justice because it is taken to be natural rather than institutional, or can a purely anthropocentric or ecosystem-centered theory of value generate the same pattern without contradiction? Is the practice consistent with treating ecosystems as having intrinsic worth, or does it quietly assume that only human-relevant stocks and species-level persistence count as common goods? And if we took seriously the idea that a common-pool resource includes not only the extractable biomass but also the welfare conditions of the sentient individuals it sustains—call this a negative commons of accumulated suffering—what would have to change in the design of quotas, preservation mandates, and compensation schemes? I am not asking for empirical policy recommendations, but for a reconstruction of the implicit theory: which rival views of moral standing and collective goods it rules out, where the practice is philosophically underdetermined so that several theories could license it, and whether the three parts of the architecture actually cohere with one another.
