Read Christopher Nolan's The Dark Knight (2008) — specifically the ferry scene in which passengers on two boats, one carrying civilians and the other prisoners, are told they must detonate the other vessel within thirty minutes or both will be destroyed — through Bernard Williams's integrity objection and the notion that a moral agent can have one thought too many. Do not treat the scene as a simple utilitarian trolley problem; instead, examine whether Williams's objection, usually illustrated by the case of a man saving his wife from drowning, applies when the choice is structurally coerced and democratically distributed. Focus on two under-discussed dimensions of Williams's view: first, that integrity is not merely emotional partiality but a refusal to treat one's own ground projects as fungible commodities in an impersonal calculus; second, that the objection targets not just the content of a deliberation but the agent's entry into a certain kind of reasoning at all. Apply these to the passengers who hesitate and to the prisoner who picks up the detonator only to discard it. Does the film understand integrity as an individual's refusal to enter the calculus, or does it distort Williams by framing integrity as a collective vote that happens to come out right? Consider also whether the prisoners' refusal to press the button displays integrity or merely moral luck, given that they have less to lose. End by saying what the scene adds to Williams's argument that the standard philosophical presentation misses — particularly about whether integrity can survive institutional coercion — and identify one moment in the scene that resists the philosophical reading and forces the honest critic to admit that Nolan is doing something cinematic rather than conceptual.
