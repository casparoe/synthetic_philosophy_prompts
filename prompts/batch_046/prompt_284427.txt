In a philosophy of mind and ethics seminar I am taking, our professor introduced a modified trolley case specifically designed to stress-test the limits of intuition pumps: instead of five strangers on the side track, the switch diverts the trolley onto a track where it will destroy a museum containing the only surviving works of art created by those same five people over their lifetimes. The structural choice remains whether to flip the switch, but now saving the five requires annihilating what they have made. After class, two fellow students—Mara and Leo—fell into a sustained disagreement about whether this variant genuinely illuminates our values or merely manipulates them through framing, and they asked me to adjudicate since I had only listened.

Mara opened by arguing that the case is not a legitimate intuition pump but a misleading conflation. She said the original trolley problem is meant to isolate the moral difference between killing and letting die, whereas the museum version smuggles in an independent aesthetic value—the preservation of creative legacy—that has nothing to do with that distinction. To prove the point, she proposed a mental control: if the museum held works by completely unknown artists, our intuitions would likely shift back toward saving the five lives. That shift, she claimed, reveals that the pump is tracking sentimental attachment to the victims rather than any stable principle about lives versus goods.

Leo replied that Mara assumes a good pump must isolate a single variable, but the most revealing ones often expose hidden structure in our values. He argued that people implicitly treat a person's life-projects and legacy as constitutive of what makes that life valuable, so the museum version correctly reveals that we do not weigh lives in a vacuum. When Mara insisted this just relabels emotional noise, Leo pushed back that moral reasoning is partly affective, and stripping away all narrative context produces judgments about bloodless abstractions rather than about what actually matters to people. Mara then introduced a more precise methodological criterion: a well-formed intuition pump should be counterfactually robust, meaning that small, morally irrelevant changes in framing should not produce large swings in intuition. Swapping the artists from the victims themselves to strangers, she said, is exactly such an irrelevant change—there is no principled moral reason the source of the art should matter—yet the intuition flips, so the variant fails the test and tracks cognitive bias rather than principle.

Leo conceded that intuitions might shift, but denied that robustness is the right standard for moral thought experiments. He said physical theories aim for law-like generality, but intuition pumps are tools for mapping the topology of our values, and real values are inherently context-sensitive. Demanding that moral judgments remain stable across arbitrary framing changes, he argued, mistakes ethics for physics and risks dismissing every genuinely interesting pump; applied consistently, he noted, Mara's standard would even undermine the original trolley problem, since changing the victims from strangers to one's own family drastically alters intuition without altering the underlying numbers. He concluded that Mara wants emotional sterility, but that defeats the purpose of a pump, which is to show how our values actually behave when embedded in narrative.

Please go through their exchange point by point and say who is right about which specific claim: whether the museum variant isolates a genuine value or merely introduces an unrelated aesthetic consideration; whether emotional and narrative context is a feature that reveals value-structure or a bug that introduces framing bias; whether counterfactual robustness is a legitimate criterion for evaluating moral intuition pumps or an inappropriate import from scientific methodology; and whether Leo is correct that Mara's standard, taken seriously, would undermine even canonical cases. Then say who is more right overall about what distinguishes a productive intuition pump from a manipulative one, and identify what both are missing—particularly whether there is a principled way to distinguish framing that reveals hidden value-commitments from framing that merely exploits sentimental attachment, without collapsing into either full emotional sterility or unrestricted context-sensitivity. I remain unsure whether my own sympathy for Leo's point about narrative embedding commits me to accepting every manipulative framing, and I would like an honest verdict that does not simply split the difference.
