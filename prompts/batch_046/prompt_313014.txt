I just spent several hours arguing with a close friend about whether hypocrisy necessarily cancels one's standing to blame, and we kept returning to a particular kind of case: a person embedded in a collective system—say, a consumer in a global economy—who blames a distant institution for practices from which she indirectly benefits. Write an essay that takes up this precise controversy. Define "distributed hypocrisy" as the condition in which an agent's complicity is mediated through institutional structures she did not design and cannot unilaterally exit, and ask whether this form undermines standing to blame in the same way that direct, personal hypocrisy does. Weigh the view that hypocrisy is a uniform moral stain—so distributed complicity destroys standing just as thoroughly—against the view that institutional diffusion fragments both responsibility and the corresponding loss of standing, leaving the blamer with a qualified but genuine entitlement to criticize the system. Defend one of these positions with a clear verdict, and explain what follows for the ethics of collective blame if your view is right. Do not treat the answer as obviously settled; the essay should earn its conclusion.
