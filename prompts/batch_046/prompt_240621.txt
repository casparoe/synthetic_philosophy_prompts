I'm a retired marine biologist who got into philosphy late in life, mostly through Dennett's writing on real patterns and the three stances—physical, design, intentional. Lately I volunteer with a small conservation NGO that recieves these odd field reports from volunteers: things like 'the proxy-wolves are avoiding the burnt zone on purpose,' or 'the gene-drive mosquitoes are trying to find shade.' The board needs a reusable decision procedure—like a checklist in words, not a flowchart I have to draw—to figure out which stance is appropriate for explaining a given behavior, whether it's tracking a real pattern or just anthropomorphic noise, and when we should reject the explanation altogether. I need questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a hard no, and where it has to hand off to the board chair because it's a values call rather than a fact call. Constraints: our reviewers have biology backgrounds but no formal philosophy, they have about fifteen minutes per report, and the chair must sign off on anything that invokes the intentional stance. Apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a report that engineered salmon are 'seeking' cooler water near a thermal vent; (2) a claim that a slime-mold network 'chose' the shortest path in a maze; and (3) a volunteer insisting that a flock of birds is 'mourning' a dead chick—this one should come out in a way the volunteer won't like. Also include a step about predictive cost—if the intentional stance doesn't actually buy us better predictions than the design stance, we drop it, no matter how intuitive it feels. Flag the cases where physical and design stances conflict across scales, what I think Dennett calls stance leakage but I might be misusing the term. Finally, be honest about what the procedure just can't do, even with more rules. Don't worry about formal citations—just make it usable.
