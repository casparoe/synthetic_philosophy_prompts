I have been working through some late-nineteenth-century philosophy of mind and came across a passage that seems to me both lucid and curiously unstable. The author compares consciousness to the steam-whistle of a locomotive: it accompanies the engine’s labor but exerts no influence on the motion of the pistons or the turning of the wheels. From this he concludes that mental states are by-products of neural machinery, causally inert with respect to behavior, and that the appearance of mental causation is an illusion produced by constant correlation rather than genuine efficacy. I understand the mechanistic picture well enough, but I am struggling to see how the author can consistently maintain that we are aware of this very relationship.

More precisely, I have been trying to formalize what seems to me a reporting asymmetry embedded in the view. If the judgment “consciousness is an epiphenomenon” is itself the output of neural machinery, and if consciousness plays no causal role in its formation, then the awareness that consciousness is present seems to float free of any causal link to the conscious state it reports. In a first-person phenomenal judgment—say, my belief that I am now in pain—there is, according to the view, an actual conscious state present, yet it contributes nothing to the physical process that produces the judgment. In a third-person phenomenal judgment—my belief that you are in pain—there is no conscious state of mine involved at all, and your consciousness, being unobservable to me, also contributes nothing. So both judgments are physically caused, but only in the first-person case is there a real conscious state that is being causally bypassed. Does this mean that first-person phenomenal judgments are, paradoxically, less justified than third-person ones? Or does the passage contain resources—perhaps in its implicit treatment of introspection as itself a mechanical process—that dissolve the asymmetry?

Could you help me understand what the passage is actually saying about the status of self-knowledge? I want to know whether the author intends his locomotive analogy to apply reflexively to our belief in epiphenomenalism itself, or whether there is a hidden distinction between the causal role of consciousness in producing behavior and its role, if any, in being known. I am not asking for modern references or a bibliography, but for a careful reading of the logic of the passage: is my “reporting asymmetry” a misreading of the analogy, or is it a genuine tension within the view that the author simply fails to address? And if it is a tension, does it undermine the coherence of epiphenomenalism, or can the view be repaired without abandoning the core claim that consciousness is causally inert?
