I'm preparing a ninety-minute seminar for second-year philosophy undergraduates who have completed a unit on physicalism but have not yet encountered the knowledge argument. I want to build the session around Mary's room, using a modified carrying case—Mary is a vision scientist who has lived entirely in a black-and-white environment and possesses exhaustive physical knowledge of bat echolocation, then exits and hears an echo-locating bat navigate—so that the students grasp both the logical structure of the knowledge argument and why the phenomenal-concept strategy answers cross-modal cases differently from intra-modal ones. Plan the teaching arc: what conception of physicalist explanation must be in place before Mary is introduced and why; why this echolocation variant should carry the session in place of the canonical color case; the misconceptions students will form in predictable order—first that Mary acquires a new propositional fact, then that she acquires only an ability, then that phenomenal concepts are merely theoretical concepts with affective coloring, then that the argument demonstrates dualism simpliciter—and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be intercepted; what should be explicitly set aside at this level and acknowledged as such; and one closing diagnostic question that reveals whether a student has grasped the argument's logical form rather than retained only the narrative image of the room.
