I am applying for a six-month interdisciplinary fellowship that supports conceptual research by writers, and I need a polished two-page proposal to submit. My working premise—drawn from the architecture of a novel I am planning—is that debates about social kinds have overlooked a temporal-structural feature I will call constitutive decay with narrative inheritance. The standard accounts treat race, gender, and money as sustained either by collective intentionality or by structural positioning, but they rarely ask what happens when the original institutional act that grounded the kind—a legal charter, a founding myth, a standard of value—has clearly lost its direct authority, yet the kind continues because participants treat its persistence as a kind of inherited obligation rather than as a present-tense collective decision. Money offers the starkest case: the direct link between currency and metallic backing is long dead, yet economic agents behave as if an ancestral authority still structures present exchange. I want the proposal to ask whether this decay-and-inheritance structure is genuinely present in gender and racial kinds, or whether those kinds depend on continuous present-tense reproduction in a way that makes them structurally different from monetary kinds. Precisely: can we construct a taxonomy of social kinds based on how they relate to the temporal distance of their founding acts—continuous performative re-enactment versus historical anchoring through narrative obligation—and does this taxonomy resolve the familiar circularity problem in social-construction theories, where the practices that constitute the kind already presuppose the kind's existence? The proposal should state the precise research question, explain why current philosophical answers leave this temporal inheritance problem open, outline the method (conceptual analysis of institutional origin and persistence, comparative framing of three case studies without empirical fieldwork), specify what a successful result looks like—perhaps a distinction between live and inherited social kinds that clarifies why some kinds resist debunking while others dissolve when their founding narrative is exposed—and state clearly what would count as failure, for instance if all three kinds collapse into a single inheritance model or if the temporal dimension proves reducible to ordinary collective intentionality. It must be realistic for six months, must not request a bibliography or citations since I will supply references myself, and should be written in formal proposal language suitable for an interdisciplinary review board. I want this framed as philosophical conceptual work that serves a narrative project without being reduced to literary criticism.
