For a philosophy of politics seminar I have been asked to set out my working interpretation of Spinoza on democracy so that the group can probe it. Here is where I stand:

(1) I take Spinoza to treat democracy not merely as one regime among others but as the form of state that least alienates the collective power (potentia multitudinis) from those who exercise it; in this sense it is the most natural form.

(2) At the same time, I read the argument of the Tractatus Politicus as essentially prudential: democratic assemblies are preferable to monarchy or aristocracy not because equality is intrinsically valuable but because collective deliberation dilutes individual passion and makes irrational decrees less likely.

(3) I accept Spinoza's identification of right (jus) with power (potentia), so that there is no normative remainder—no claim about what the multitude ought to do that is not reducible to what it actually can do.

(4) I also hold that, on Spinoza's account, a democratic assembly that suppresses freedom of thought and expression forfeits its authority, because the state's legitimacy depends on preserving the conditions for rational collective decision.

(5) Finally, I think Spinoza's democracy requires a rough equality of economic condition; without it, wealth concentrates effective decision-making power in a de facto aristocracy, undermining the claim that the multitude rules itself.

Please audit this package. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision in each case? Rank the tensions by severity. I am particularly unsure whether (3) can sit with (4): if right is strictly coextensive with power, what sense can there be in saying that a democratic majority forfeits authority by acting against reason? Does Spinoza have the resources to distinguish de jure authority from de facto power here, or does my commitment to (4) smuggle in a normative standard that (3) rules out? Likewise, do (1) and (5) really conflict, or can one say that democracy expresses the multitude's power precisely by requiring economic equality as a precondition?

Please separate what is relatively settled in Spinoza scholarship from what remains genuinely contested—particularly around the interpretation of potentia versus potestas and the role of prudential reasoning in the Tractatus Politicus—and do not present a disputed reading as if it were the consensus.
