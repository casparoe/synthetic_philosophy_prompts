I'm drafting a short essay for an advanced philosophy seminar on Goodman's new riddle of induction, and I'd like comments before I expand it into a full paper. Rather than rehearsing the standard discussion of linguistic entrenchment or natural kinds, I'm trying to frame the problem as one of counterfactual robustness in the inductive base. Here are the notes for the argument I want to develop.

Goodman defines grue as the property of being green if observed before some arbitrary future time t, and blue otherwise. The familiar puzzle is that emeralds observed so far are both green and grue, yet the two predicates yield opposite predictions for emeralds observed after t. The standard reply is that green is projectible because it is entrenched in our linguistic practice, while grue is not. I think this answer relocates rather than resolves the difficulty: it tells us that we happen to project green, but not why induction should be governed by our habits rather than by structural features of the predicates themselves.

My proposed framing is that projectibility tracks a predicate's stability under small counterfactual perturbations to the observation schedule. If we imagine shifting the times or conditions of our past observations slightly, "green" retains its extension across those perturbations, whereas "grue" fractures because its boundary depends on the specific location of t relative to the actual observation history. On this view, we do not project green because it names a natural kind or because our language is conservative; we project it because its application conditions are invariant across the counterfactual space that defines our inductive practice. This makes projectibility a feature of how a predicate interacts with the structure of evidence-gathering, not a metaphysical badge of honor.

The essay then considers an objection: this stability criterion seems circular, since the "perturbation space" is itself defined by the predicates we already treat as stable. I try to block the circularity by arguing that the perturbation space is fixed not by predicates but by the causal and temporal structure of the observational process—what can vary without breaking the evidential chain—so that stability is assessed relative to an independently characterized framework.

I would appreciate specific comments on the following. First, does my framing genuinely differ from entrenchment, or have I just redescribed linguistic habit in modal language? Second, is there a hidden circularity in using the structure of observation to define stability, given that how we structure observation already depends on which predicates we take seriously? Third, does this approach have any awkward consequence for scientific predicates that are deliberately time-sensitive—such as "radioactive" or "unstable"—which seem projectible despite being defined relative to temporal boundaries? I am not asking for a bibliography or a literature review; I want to know whether the conceptual architecture holds together and whether there is an obvious objection I am missing.
