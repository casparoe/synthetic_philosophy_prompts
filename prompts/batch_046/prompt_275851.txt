Write a deadpan bureaucratic memo, approximately 600 words, formatted as an internal regulatory guidance document from the fictional Federal Office of Predictive Calibration. Announce the implementation of Directive 14-B, which mandates that all government risk assessments, clinical prognoses, and sentencing recommendations specify their reference class according to a newly invented framework called the Hierarchical Reference Taxonomy (HRT). The HRT ranks candidate reference classes not by statistical power but by "ontological proximity" to the target event, using invented protocols such as the Principle of Minimal Divergence and the Tuesday-Thursday Symmetry Clause.

The memo should include four sections in its earnest, technically precise voice: (1) a preamble attributing past predictive failures solely to "class-selection ambiguity" rather than to statistical or causal error; (2) a table of approved reference tiers running from Tier 1 ("all mammalian bipeds") to Tier 6 ("individuals born in the same postal district on a Tuesday who consumed coffee within six hours and share a blood-type letter"), revealing the taxonomy's slide into absurd granularity; (3) a dispute-resolution protocol requiring analysts who choose different tiers to justify their selection using only the vocabulary of "relevant similarity," explicitly forbidding appeals to normative or practical considerations; and (4) a brief applied case study of a hypothetical patient prognosis, showing the predicted five-year outcome shifting from 12% under Tier 2 to 89% under Tier 5 without any change in the patient's clinical data.

The satire must stay deadpan and legible: the document should never wink, never admit its own absurdity, and should present the HRT as a rigorous administrative achievement. The humor and philosophical point should come from the memo's failure to notice that "ontological proximity" is circular, that similarity judgments smuggle in contested values, and that the directive reproduces the reference class problem inside the rules meant to solve it. By the end, a reader should feel the genuine philosophical difficulty—that for any particular there are infinitely many reference classes with incompatible base rates, and that procedural formalization cannot eliminate the normative choice among them—without the memo ever stating this conclusion explicitly. Invent all institutional names, protocols, and tier definitions; do not cite real philosophers, texts, or established theories by name.
