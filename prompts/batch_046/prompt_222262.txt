I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind, and I am trying to formalize the implicit criteria my advisor uses when she classifies mental states as genuine phenomenal experiences versus non-experiential processes. In recent seminars and draft chapters she has endorsed the following verdicts. First, ordinary waking visual perception is an experience. Second, blindsight discrimination in the absence of reported awareness is not. Third, a vivid non-lucid dream narrated in detail upon waking counts as an experience that occurred during sleep. Fourth, a REM-period mental state that leaves no trace in memory and cannot be reported upon waking does not count as an experience. Fifth, hypnagogic imagery that is immediately reported during partial arousal is an experience. Sixth, a lucid dream in which the sleeper executes a pre-arranged motor signal and confirms awareness upon waking is an experience.

These judgments seem jointly consistent with at least three different principles: a narrative-accessibility account in the spirit of Dennett, according to which experience requires integration into the autobiographical self-model upon waking; a first-order phenomenal-presence account, which holds that experience occurs whenever there is appropriate first-order representational content regardless of subsequent report; and a criteria-application account reminiscent of Malcolm, which denies that mental-state concepts can be properly applied to sleeping subjects except retrospectively via waking reports.

Here is the case that divides them. During a targeted auditory memory-reactivation protocol in slow-wave sleep, a specific cue elicits isolated activation in primary visual cortex corresponding to a simple geometric form, without prefrontal engagement or behavioral response. The sleeper reports nothing upon waking and denies awareness when shown the form. Days later, direct cortical stimulation reproduces the identical pattern in waking consciousness, and the subject reports that the form feels familiar, as if previously encountered. The question is whether there was a phenomenal experience of that form during the sleep episode.

Please predict my advisor's verdict on this case, identify the closest analog among her six earlier judgments, and specify what principle she would be adopting if she answers yes and what principle if she answers no. Then lay out the three rules I named—narrative-accessibility, first-order phenomenal presence, and criteria-application—state precisely what each predicts about the sleep episode, indicate which prediction you take to be best supported by her track record and why, and finally propose one further hypothetical case that would sharply distinguish these three rules.
