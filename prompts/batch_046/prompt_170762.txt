i run a rationalist-adjacent philosophy meetup, usually 10-13 people, mix of cs grad students and people who read too much lesswrong. we borrow a classroom for 90 minutes. i want an in-class game that makes them feel the pull of attributing beliefs to an llm, but not in the boring "is it just a stochastic parrot" way. instead i want to inject this framing: granting the intentional stance is like issuing a social license to run counterfactuals about what the system would do if it believed p instead of q, and once that license is out there the group can collectively revoke it based on coordination pressure rather than on the system's actual behavior.

design the activity. split into three roles: predictors who bet fake points on outputs, auditors who check consistency, and license-granters who vote each round whether the system gets intentional-stance treatment. the "ai" is a hidden script (or confederate) that gets a prompt and produces an answer. predictors can only access the "counterfactual betting pool" — where they wager on outputs under tweaked prompts — if the license-granters voted yes that round; otherwise they're stuck in the "mechanistic pool" with restricted bets. run three rounds with the same hidden logic but different social dynamics, then reveal that the script never changed but the license-granters flipped their vote between round 1 and 3 because of peer pressure, not behavior change.

include rules, point scheme, timeline, debrief that maps the voting shifts and betting restrictions onto Dennett's conditions (rationality, belief-desire pair) and onto the license idea. be honest about what this can't reproduce — probably the evolutionary/functionalist argument for why we grant the stance in the first place. and tell me what will predicatbly go wrong first run, like people voting yes just to unlock the more fun betting pool or the confederate being too transparent. no lecture up front, they shouldn't hear "intentional stance" named until the debrief.
