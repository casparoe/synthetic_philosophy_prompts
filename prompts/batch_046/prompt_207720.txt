I organize a biweekly philosophy workshop for about fourteen software engineers—none with formal philosophy backgrounds—meeting in a borrowed conference room for ninety minutes. I want to stage an activity that makes us feel, rather than just discuss, whether comparing historical atrocities is conceptually coherent or morally obscene. The core tension should arise from the mechanics of the exercise, not from anything I say in a wrap-up lecture. To bridge the gap for people who think naturally in terms of throughput, latency, and resource constraints, I have sketched a framing idea I call Remembrance Load-Balancing—treating collective memorial investment as a constrained allocation problem with hard caps—but I do not want participants to hear named philosophers, read excerpts, or be given bibliographies; the conflict must emerge entirely from their own forced choices.

Please design a structured simulation called the Archive Allocation Protocol. Provide printed cards describing five historical catastrophes solely through anonymized damage profiles: engineered metrics such as temporal spread, geographic footprint, intentional-design coefficient, and victim-density index, with all proper names, cultural identifiers, and standard historical labels removed. The group of fourteen splits into three committees of four or five, plus one rotating observer. Each committee must run a forced pairwise-comparison algorithm on two assigned metrics, writing transitive rankings on paper, then aggregate results into a single proportional budget split across the five events—using symbolic funding units that represent real memorial investment. Crucially, include a rule I am calling the Reversibility Constraint: once a ranking is fixed, each committee must literally fill out allocation slips assigning specific unit counts to each profile, and every member must initial the slip, so that ranking becomes an operational commitment rather than an abstract opinion. The rules should make it mechanically unavoidable that at least one comparison pits a high-intentionality, lower-scale profile against a lower-intentionality, higher-scale profile, forcing the group to decide which dimension governs the split.

Then specify a debrief timeline—about fifteen minutes—mapping specific moments onto the philosophical problem: the point where transitivity forces an ordering they resist, the moment the Reversibility Constraint makes them sign their names to a proportional valuation, and the discovery that anonymization collapses once participants decode which profiles match which real events. Be explicit about what will predictably fail the first time I run this—likely that committees will attempt to reject the algorithm or invent escape clauses, or that decoding will happen too early—and how to handle each without breaking the structure. Finally, be frank about which feature of the ranking-is-obscene view this protocol cannot reproduce—probably the non-instrumental, identity-constituting grief that makes quantification feel like desecration rather than mere unfairness—and tell me exactly how I should supply that missing affective dimension in my own words during the debrief without letting it dominate the exercise. Keep everything runnable with just paper, pens, printed cards, and a single shared whiteboard for aggregation.
