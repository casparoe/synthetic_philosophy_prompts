Here is an argument I tried to formalize after reading some older moral philosophy through a rationalist lens, and it now strikes me as suspicious because the conclusion undermines the very framework I used to build it. (1) Williams's integrity objection holds that moral reasoning can be corrupted by an intermediate calculation that severs action from the ground project that gives it meaning: the husband who saves his wife only after computing that her life outweighs a stranger's has added one thought too many, and that extra step destroys the direct expression of commitment that makes the act genuinely his own. (2) A ground project, on this account, is not a preference ranking but a constitutive commitment: it defines who one is, and actions that flow from it are direct expressions of identity rather than outputs of a decision procedure. (3) Now consider someone for whom impartial effective altruism is not a conclusion but a ground project — call this the impartiality project. For this person, giving to global health interventions is not a calculated trade-off but a direct expression of who they are, structurally analogous to the husband's rescue. (4) But maintaining any ground project with integrity requires being able to distinguish it from a counterfeit — for example, distinguishing genuine impartial commitment from reputation management, identity signaling, or affective self-reward. (5) That distinction necessarily requires a second-order check: asking whether one would still donate if the act were invisible, or whether the motivation survives counterfactual detachment from social feedback. This check introduces an intermediate calculation between the ground project and the action. (6) By (1), introducing exactly this kind of intermediate calculation is the corruption Williams identifies: it adds the fatal extra thought between commitment and expression. (7) Therefore, anyone with the impartiality project as a ground project is structurally unable to maintain it with integrity; either they act without checking (risking counterfeit motivation) or they check (corrupting the direct flow from project to action). (8) So either reflective moral commitment to impartial ideals is impossible with integrity, which seems false given that people clearly live this way, or the one-thought-too-many objection applies selectively and arbitrarily, undermining its standard deployment against utilitarian calculation. (9) Hence, extending Williams's reasoning consistently proves too much: it dissolves not just utilitarian decision procedures but the very possibility of reflective commitment to any moral ideal that subjects itself to second-order scrutiny. Where does the argument fail, if it does? If I must choose, I would rather preserve (1) and (3) than accept (8), but I want to know precisely which step slips and what accepting or rejecting it costs for how we should think about integrity in reflective moral life.
