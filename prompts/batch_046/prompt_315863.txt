I am working through a difficult passage from Aristotle’s treatment of potentiality and actuality—specifically, his discussion of how a builder retains the art of building whilst asleep, contrasted with the case of an eye that possesses sight yet cannot see because something obstructs it. He seems to allow that a capacity can be present but unexercised, and also present yet prevented from operating, though he frames both in terms of first and second actuality rather than in modern causal language.

What puzzles me is how to map these distinctions onto contemporary philosophy of dispositions. In particular, modern writers distinguish “finks”, where the disposition itself is lost precisely when the conditions for its manifestation arise, from “masks”, where an external factor blocks the manifestation even though the underlying disposition remains intact. I wonder whether Aristotle’s metaphysical framework—where potentiality is an intrinsic principle of change in the substance—can genuinely accommodate finks, or whether it treats every case of non-manifestation as either the absence of second actuality or the presence of an interfering condition analogous to a mask.

Could you help me understand the passage in these terms? Does Aristotle’s account of the sleeping builder and the obstructed eye allow us to separate the disappearance of a capacity from its mere blocking? And does his reliance on the substance’s internal principle of change make the notion of a fink—an internal fragility that destroys the capacity upon triggering—conceptually impossible within his system?
