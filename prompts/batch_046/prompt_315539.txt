You are serving as a grading assistant for an upper-level seminar on legal interpretation. Below is the final exam question and one student's answer. The student is being evaluated on analytical precision, not on reaching a particular normative conclusion.

Question 7 (25 points): Section 402(b) of the proposed Digital Communications Integrity Act provides that "no platform shall employ algorithmic amplification for content that undermines democratic participation." The committee report states that the provision was drafted specifically to address foreign state-sponsored disinformation that uses automated recommendation systems to suppress voter turnout. The statutory text, however, contains no geographic or origin-based limitation. Two interpretive approaches are in play. First, textualism: the phrase should be given its ordinary public meaning at the time of enactment, and ambiguity should be resolved through established canons of construction rather than by direct appeal to legislative purpose. Second, purposivism: the provision's scope should be calibrated to the mischief the legislature sought to remedy, so that the absence of an explicit origin restriction does not automatically extend coverage to purely domestic advocacy groups.

In a well-structured response, compare how each approach would treat the application of Section 402(b) to a domestic political organization that uses recommendation algorithms to amplify messages discouraging participation in local elections. Be precise about the interpretive moves each approach makes, and identify any point at which the two methods might converge or genuinely diverge.

Student Answer:

Textualism requires interpreting "undermines democratic participation" according to its ordinary public meaning at enactment, which plainly covers domestic groups that suppress voting through algorithmic amplification; because the text contains no origin restriction, a textualist applies the provision to the domestic organization without further inquiry. The textualist rejects the committee report entirely, since textualists never consult legislative history for any purpose, including ambiguity resolution. By contrast, purposivism calibrates the text to the mischief identified in the report—foreign disinformation—so a purposivist would likely read an implicit geographic limitation into the statute despite the absence of explicit language. The divergence is therefore complete: textualism follows the words, purposivism follows the intended remedy. It is worth noting, however, that both approaches agree that if a statute is ambiguous, courts should apply the rule of lenity; this shared commitment to canons shows that textualism and purposivism are not truly adversarial frameworks but rather complementary methods that both defer to judicial canons when statutory language is unclear.
