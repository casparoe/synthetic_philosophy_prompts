I am a senior at my high school and have been asked to deliver the opening address at our annual Life After Graduation symposium, which gathers students, parents, and faculty in the auditorium. The audience is split: half are my classmates facing concrete decisions between paths that seem to honor entirely different goods—for instance, pursuing a career in scientific research versus remaining close to home to care for family—while the other half are adults who instinctively frame every choice as an optimization problem with a hidden correct answer. I want the speech to be structured around the philosophical problem of incommensurable values and hard choices. Please draw specifically on Ruth Chang's argument that some alternatives are not merely incomparable but are genuinely on a par—different in kind, lacking any common metric—rather than being different quantities of the same underlying value. To keep the address from becoming generic, please also build in a conceptual distinction I am calling commitment failure: the condition in which, after making a rational choice among incommensurable goods, one still cannot fully justify that choice to an outside observer without misrepresenting or flattening the values that were actually at stake. The philosophical framework should do real structural work: it needs to explain why advising someone to just weigh the pros and cons is sometimes conceptually mistaken, and it should determine what I can and cannot promise—no false reassurance that there is a secret right answer, but also no slide into claiming that the choice therefore does not matter. Please write a polished, approximately ten-minute address that respects both halves of the room without lecturing either.
