Exam question: In Republic II, Glaucon introduces the ring of Gyges to press a challenge about why one should be just. He asks whether anyone—just or unjust—would remain just if granted perfect invisibility and thus perfect impunity. Carefully distinguish the descriptive interpretation of this challenge (what it claims about human psychology) from the normative interpretation (what it claims about the rationality of justice). For each interpretation, explain precisely what the ring removes or reveals. Then evaluate the following reply: a genuinely just person receives the ring, keeps it, and still acts justly. Does this reply defeat Glaucon’s challenge, or does it change the subject? In your answer, avoid conflating Glaucon’s position with Thrasymachus’s claim that justice is the advantage of the stronger, and be careful about whether Socrates’s later argument in Book IX is directed specifically at the ring scenario.

Student answer: Glaucon’s challenge comes immediately after Thrasymachus defines justice as the advantage of the stronger. Glaucon accepts this definition but adds the ring of Gyges—an artifact that makes its wearer invisible—to show that justice is only a social convention enforced by visibility. On the descriptive reading, Glaucon claims that every human being is naturally unjust, and the ring reveals our true nature by stripping away the fear of punishment and social shame. On the normative reading, he asks whether it is rational to be just when no one can observe you. Here the ring removes all external goods and rewards, leaving only internal motivation. If the just person still acts justly with the ring, this demonstrates that justice is valued intrinsically, not instrumentally. Socrates answers exactly this in Book IX by comparing the tyrant and the philosopher-king: the just soul is happiest regardless of whether anyone sees it. Therefore, the just person who retains the ring and continues to act justly fully defeats Glaucon’s challenge. The ring was meant to show that justice collapses without an audience, but the counterexample proves that justice survives the removal of every audience and every consequence.
