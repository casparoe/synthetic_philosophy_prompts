I'm grading papers for an upper-division course on the philosophy of death and need a point-by-point evaluation of the following student response. The exam question was: "Consider the Epicurean argument that death is nothing to us, with particular attention to the symmetry argument and the problem of the missing subject. Then assess whether a deprivation-based reply can show that a premature death harms the person who dies without requiring that the dead experience anything. In your answer, distinguish between (i) experiential harm, (ii) prudential harm that requires post-mortem existence of the subject, and (iii) prudential harm understood as a comparative deficit evaluated over the living subject's overall life trajectory. Which of these, if any, escapes Epicurus's challenge?"

The student wrote:

"The Epicurean position, articulated in Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus, holds that death is not an evil because it is the complete privation of sensation. Epicurus employs a mirror-image symmetry argument: just as our pre-natal non-existence was not harmful to us, so our post-death non-existence will not be harmful, since in neither case are we present to suffer. From this it follows that harm requires an existing subject, and because the dead literally do not exist, there is no one for whom death can be bad.

A deprivation-based reply might object that death harms us by robbing us of the goods we would have enjoyed had we lived longer. But this reply collapses under scrutiny. For a comparative harm to be real, the subject must exist in both the actual world (where death occurs) and the counterfactual world (where life continues) in order to bear the deficit. Since the dead person does not exist in the actual world after the moment of death, there is no bearer available to suffer the loss, so the world-comparison fails. Thus deprivation implicitly presupposes post-mortem existence and violates Epicurus's no-subject constraint.

That leaves only option (iii), where harm is treated as a comparative deficit evaluated across the living subject's overall life trajectory. Yet this is really just a redescription of option (ii): both involve comparing an actual life with a longer counterfactual one, and both need the subject to persist in some form to register the deprivation. Because none of the three categories can avoid requiring a subject who is present after death, none escapes Epicurus's challenge. Death, therefore, is not bad for the one who dies."
