I'm trying to work out my own view on whether numbers can justify harming one person for the good of many, framing it in terms of Scanlon-style contractualism and the problem of aggregation. Here are my gut verdicts on concrete cases.

A surgeon kills one healthy patient to harvest organs and save five dying ones: not permissible.

A city council demolishes one remote farm—owners warned and fully compensated—to build a flood barrier saving ten thousand homes: permissible, though regrettable.

A mandatory vaccination imposes a one-in-two-hundred-thousand risk of fatal reaction across fifty million people, preventing five hundred deaths annually: permissible.

Saving those same five hundred lives not by distributing risk but by selecting five hundred specific people and forcing them through a near-certain lethal procedure: clearly not permissible.

A factory can switch processes so that instead of two thousand people each suffer mild chronic respiratory harm, exactly one nearby worker suffers severe respiratory harm of comparable total severity. I genuinely oscillate: sometimes this feels like an impermissible burden-shift onto one person, sometimes like a morally neutral redistribution that should not be blocked.

What principle, if any, captures this pattern? Is there a clause that distinguishes the first and fourth cases from the second and third without collapsing into "numbers never matter"? What does it say about the fifth case? And about this further situation: redesigning a dangerous intersection cuts annual fatalities from twelve to one, but requires one specific traffic officer to work alone at night in isolation, significantly raising her personal risk of assault?

1. Candidate principles (around 200 words): state one or two precise principles, identifying which clause responds to which case.
2. Case-by-case verdicts (around 250 words): apply the principle to all five cases above, plus the intersection redesign.
