I have been thinking on my own about the brain-transplant thought experiment — the one sometimes linked to the philosopher Daniel Dennett — where a surgeon removes a person's brain, keeps it alive in a vat, and the original body wakes up wondering where the real self is. I am not a scholar, but I want to explore a twist that is rarely discussed: what if the brain is duplicated exactly, so that two copies wake up in separate vats, both receiving identical sensory input, and each genuinely says I am here? You should act as both an expert philosopher of personal identity and a skilled poet. First, take a moment to brainstorm: decide on two refrains that represent the competing first-person location-claims of the two copies, and consider how a villanelle's interwoven refrains — taking turns through the stanzas and finally meeting in the closing couplet — can mirror the philosophical point that both claims remain equally valid yet never merge into a single answer. Then write a 19-line villanelle with the standard ABA rhyme scheme and two refrains, in modern American English. The poem should render the duplication problem accurately — someone familiar with Dennett's puzzle should recognize the fracture of the indexical self — but the specific two-vat twist should be your own injection, not something pulled from a standard text. Use blank verse or loose meter, avoid archaic language, and do not include citations, quotation marks around passages from real works, footnotes, or bibliographies. End the poem with the two refrains sitting side by side in the final couplet without resolving which one is correct.
