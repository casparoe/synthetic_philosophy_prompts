Read Sophocles' Antigone through the framework of Confucian role ethics, specifically attending to zhengming (the rectification of names) and the principle that moral obligations are internally differentiated by one's position within the five relationships rather than grounded in a universal, role-independent conscience. The play is conventionally read in the West as a tragedy of individual moral conviction—Antigone appeals to unwritten divine laws that override Creon's civic decree, and her death vindicates the autonomy of private conscience over state power. From a Confucian perspective, however, there is no pre-social self that could stand outside its roles and adjudicate among them; a sister's duty of filial care and a subject's duty of obedience are not competing applications of one law but obligations that arise from distinct relational positions. Your task is to examine whether the play genuinely dramatizes a conflict that Confucian ethics cannot resolve, or whether Antigone's rhetoric of universal divine law actually obscures a failure of yi—contextual, role-sensitive judgment—by over-asserting her identity as sister and neglecting her simultaneous identity as subject. Identify the specific scenes and speeches where Antigone frames her obligation as transcending particular roles, and ask whether a Confucian critic would read those moments not as heroic clarity but as a kind of moral confusion: a refusal to recognize that even her love for Polynices must be expressed within the broader harmony of the polis. Conversely, examine Creon's failure: is it best understood as hubristic overreach of the ruler's authority, or as a failure of the ruler's own role-specific virtue—namely, the failure to maintain relational harmony through ritual propriety rather than coercive decree? I am less interested in a simple mapping of Confucian categories onto the characters than in an honest critical judgment: where does the play illuminate something about role ethics that abstract philosophical argument tends to miss—perhaps the emotional reality of being torn between incommensurable relational demands—and where does the play's Western tragic framework distort Confucian thought by importing a notion of autonomous conscience that the framework explicitly rejects? Conclude by saying what a Confucian reading adds to our understanding of the play that a standard liberal-individualist interpretation suppresses, and whether there remains an irreducible residue of tragedy that even role ethics cannot dissolve.
