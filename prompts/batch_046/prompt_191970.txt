I am leading a 75-minute session of an upper-level undergraduate seminar in value theory, with about fourteen students who have done some preliminary reading in Buddhist philosophy and Schopenhauer but have not yet encountered tranquilism framed as a formal axiological thesis. I want them to feel, rather than merely understand, the claim that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, while positive goods—artistic achievement, romantic love, intellectual victory, sensory pleasure—are at best instrumentally valuable and often covertly generate the very craving they seem to offset. Please design a structured in-class simulation, not a lecture, in which students must make collective allocation decisions and then discover that the scoring logic they have implicitly accepted renders all positive achievements weightless.

Specifically, construct a “Council of Interventions” game: divide the group into three committees of four or five, each given ten symbolic tokens representing limited resources (time, institutional support, attentional capacity). Present six scenario cards—funding a pain-relief clinic, endowing an art residency, facilitating a romantic partnership, establishing a monastery of rigorous meditation, launching a competitive research program, distributing a mild pleasure-enhancing compound—each describing an immediate suffering-reduction and a hidden “craving-debt” that is revealed only after commitments are locked. The rules must be arranged so that committees earn points solely for verified reductions in suffering and craving; any token placed on a scenario that introduces hidden craving yields zero or negative points regardless of how much pleasure or achievement it produces, and unanimous committee votes are required for every allocation. The hidden debts should be revealed only after all votes are cast and recorded, forcing the group to confront whether they would trade away every positive good to avoid the debt. Include a symbolic payoff scheme (no real money), a clear 75-minute timeline with phases for deliberation, voting, revelation, and debrief, assigned roles for a student record-keeper and a neutral facilitator, and rules that predictably trigger coordination failure if committees try to smuggle in positive-value reasoning.

Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto parts of the tranquilist thesis—privative value, the asymmetry between suffering and pleasure, the derivative status of achievement—without naming the view until the end. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time (for instance, committees will attempt to renegotiate scoring rules to salvage face for positive goods, or will reject the hidden-debt reveals as procedurally unfair) and how to handle it without breaking the simulation. Also include an honest note on which feature of tranquilism the game cannot reproduce—likely the phenomenological transparency of craving’s cessation versus the mere absence of points—and what the facilitator must supply verbally. If my framing here rests on a confused premise—say, by treating craving and suffering as separable in a way the view rejects, or by treating all positive goods as necessarily craving-generating in a straw-man fashion—say so plainly rather than designing around a false premise. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; the activity should be fully runnable for students without further texts.
