I run a graduate seminar in epistemology and metaphysics with 10 students, meeting for 90 minutes. I want a classroom activity—a structured game or deliberation with assigned roles, rules, and small stakes—that makes the participants feel Benacerraf’s dilemma from the inside, not as a historical footnote but as a live tension between the reliability of mathematical reasoning and the causal isolation of its apparent objects.

To prevent the exercise from collapsing into a standard recitation of Platonism versus nominalism, inject a “causal-proxy” twist: include a role played by a sealed “black-box calculator” (or a student acting as one) that produces correct arithmetic outputs with perfect reliability but has no internal representation of numbers and no causal interaction with abstract entities. The group must collectively decide whether this proxy “knows” its outputs, and then apply that same judgment standard to their own mathematical reasoning.

Design the full activity plus a debrief. The rules, incentives, or role-constraints should be arranged so that participants construct correct mathematical beliefs and feel the strength of that reliability before anyone mentions causation, abstract objects, or Benacerraf. Only in the debrief should the dilemma be named.

The debrief should: map each phase of the activity onto a corresponding part of Benacerraf’s challenge (reliable belief-formation versus causal inertness of referents); identify at least two predictable failures in running the activity—for example, students conflating predictive reliability with causal access, or applying an inconsistent standard to the proxy and to themselves—and specify how to handle each; and be explicit about which feature of the dilemma the simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator.

Specify the group size, the time allocation within the 90 minutes, the small stakes involved, and what participants should come away seeing. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation request; do not require students to have read specific texts in advance; and do not ask for quoted passages from named works.
