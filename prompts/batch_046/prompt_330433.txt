The long-running deadlock between Dennett’s claim that intentionality can be fully derived—illustrated in his two-bitser example, where a system bears content only in virtue of being interpreted by designers or users with original mental states—and Searle’s insistence that all genuine intentionality is original, intrinsic, and never ontologically dependent on prior mental sources, has produced extensive literature without either side conceding ground. Both parties appear to accept the same descriptions of artificial and biological information-processing systems, yet they draw opposite metaphysical conclusions about whether those systems possess real mental content. Diagnose the structure of this impasse: is it primarily a verbal disagreement about the term “intentionality,” a substantive dispute over what counts as a satisfactory explanation of mental representation, or are Dennett and Searle answering different questions—one about predictive and engineering utility, the other about first-person phenomenological presence—under a single heading? Specifically, examine whether the stall is masked by an unacknowledged ambiguity in “derived”: for Searle it entails causal and semantic dependence on prior original mental states, whereas for Dennett it entails functional specification without ontological commitment to an original mental source, a distinction analogous to that between syntactic derivation in a formal grammar and semantic inheritance in an interpreted programming language. Is the residue of disagreement a genuine metaphysical dispute about the origin of mental content, or does it dissolve once we separate interpretive charity (the design stance) from engineering specification (functional decomposition)? And if the latter holds, does that show the original debate was ill-posed, or only that one side was advancing a normative claim about explanatory adequacy while the other advanced an ontological claim about mental origin? Provide the diagnosis supported by what each side actually says about the two-bitser case, identify whether any portion of the disagreement rests on bedrock intuitions that neither side can further argue for, and specify precisely what—whether a formal result in artificial intelligence, a conceptual distinction in philosophy of language, or an empirical finding about non-biological cognition—would count as genuine progress rather than mere repetition of familiar positions.
