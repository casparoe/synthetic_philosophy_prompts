I'm currently taking a course on metaethics and philosophy of language, and I've been trying to work out my own position on moral expressivism in light of the Frege-Geach problem. Before I present this in our seminar, I'd like to check whether the following commitments actually hang together, because several feel compatible in isolation yet pull in different directions once combined.

Here is where I have landed: (1) I accept a form of expressivism according to which moral sentences express plan-like practical commitments rather than representing mind-independent moral facts or expressing beliefs with propositional content; (2) I regard the Frege-Geach problem as a genuine and serious challenge, not merely a technical puzzle, because it shows that unmodified expressivism cannot account for the logical behaviour of moral terms in embedded contexts such as conditionals; (3) I propose resolving it through what I call a "shadow-commitment" account—when a moral clause is embedded, it does not express a fresh commitment but retains the normative structure of the original attitude, thereby preserving valid inference without attributing propositional content to the embedded clause; (4) I deny that moral sentences ever possess truth-conditions, even in a minimal or deflationary sense; (5) I maintain that moral disagreement is genuinely substantive and can be rationally resolved, not merely negotiated, through the assessment of overall coherence within an agent's plan-set; (6) I hold that for a conditional such as "If torture is wrong, then encouraging torture is wrong" to carry normative force for me, I must actively hold the plan-commitment against torture in some live form—otherwise the antecedent is normatively empty; and (7) I think evolutionary debunking arguments miss their target against expressivism, since they undermine belief-forming mechanisms but leave practical commitment-formation untouched, and coherence-based assessment is not vulnerable to genealogical critique in the same way.

My worry is that several of these sit in tension, but I'm unsure which conflicts are real and which only appear so. In particular, does (3)'s claim that embedded clauses lack fresh commitment conflict with (6)'s requirement that the antecedent commitment be actively live? Does (4)'s denial of all truth-conditions undermine (5)'s claim that disagreement can be genuinely resolved rather than merely harmonised? And is there a hidden contradiction between (1)'s rejection of propositional content and (3)'s talk of "retaining normative structure," which sounds suspiciously like preserving semantic content? At the same time, I suspect the tension between (1) and (7) might dissolve on inspection, since debunking arguments do seem to target cognitive representations rather than practical plans. Could you audit these commitments for me? Please rank the tensions by severity, identify which pairs are only apparently in conflict, specify what the cheapest revision would be in each case, and tell me which single change would clean up the most problems without abandoning the core expressivist framework.
