As a novelist constructing a philosophical trap for my protagonist, I have built an argument that seems to prove too much—specifically, that superrational reasoning, if genuinely general, dissolves moral obligation in crowds. I want to know where the chain breaks, and what I must pay to keep the pieces I value.

(1) In Douglas Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma, twenty people face an identical payoff structure: exactly one telegram yields a billion dollars; zero or more yields nothing, and cooperation is forbidden. Because each agent knows that every other agent runs the same computation, superrationality demands not individual optimization but symmetric randomization: each sends a telegram with probability 1/20.

(2) The Scientific American Luring Lottery reproduces the same architecture with continuous entries. Here superrationality requires each contestant to calibrate submissions to the expected aggregate of others, yielding the same mirror-reasoning: one's own choice is treated as a draw from a collective distribution.

(3) From these cases I extract the Mirror-Index Principle: whenever an agent's payoff depends on the total number of actions taken by a set of structurally identical agents, and direct coordination is impossible, rationality requires indexing one's decision to the expected aggregate behavior rather than to one's private preferences.

(4) Now consider an emergency witnessed by a crowd of N strangers. Each bystander's choice to intervene affects the total number of helpers; there is diminishing marginal benefit to each additional rescuer, and no mechanism for coordination. The payoff structure—small personal cost, large collective benefit, redundancy as the null condition—is structurally analogous to Platonia.

(5) By (3), each bystander should intervene with probability 1/N. As N grows large, the rational probability of helping approaches zero. Thus, in a dense city, no individual is ever deterministically required to assist.

(6) This is not merely surprising; it implies that moral duties of rescue dissolve probabilistically in large populations. If superrationality governs Platonia and the Lottery, and (3) is genuinely general, then morality is not a matter of obligation but of statistical rarity, which is absurd.

(7) Therefore (3) must be false, or at least confined to artificial games. But if (3) falls, then the reasoning that justifies one-boxing, sending the Platonia telegram, or adopting symmetric strategies in any superrational experiment collapses with it.

I find (1) and (2) compelling—the isolated-game intuition is hard to surrender. I also want to preserve (6): I believe a duty to rescue does not vanish simply because others are present. So I am inclined to reject (3), yet I fear that doing so costs me the coherence of superrationality itself. Which step should I give up? Is there a hidden equivocation—perhaps between discrete uniform randomization and continuous expectation, or between strategic interaction and moral obligation—that lets me keep both the game-theoretic insight and the moral claim without paying this price? Explain what rejecting my preferred target costs, and whether a subtler repair is available.
