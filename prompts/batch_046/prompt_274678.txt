My sister is retiring after thirty years directing the state’s public-housing authority, and I am speaking at her retirement dinner tomorrow. The room will include her family, the advocates she worked with, junior staff she mentored, and a few philosophers she knows through me. Last month my own child was born with a condition requiring lifelong support, and for the first time I felt viscerally what it means to occupy a social position you never chose; I want to honor her without using the usual language of individual merit, and I believe Rawls’s veil of ignorance and the difference principle should structure what I can and cannot say. Rather than repeating textbook formulations, I want to frame her career as an exercise in what I will call “institutional veil-work”: designing rules as if one did not know which tenant, which neighborhood, or which generation one would belong to, so that arbitrary advantages—location, birth timing, inherited resources—operate to improve the position of the least advantaged residents. The speech must therefore avoid attributing her success to exceptional intelligence or effort in ways that ignore the natural and social lottery, and it should treat her achievements not as personal trophies but as structural adjustments consistent with the lexical priority of the basic liberties and the difference principle, including the problem of extending the original position across generations. At the same time, it must celebrate her for an audience that has never read Rawls, without invoking any false consolation about her watching over us or having made everyone equally well off. Please write a formal address of about four to five minutes that does real philosophical work—genuinely constraining the rhetoric of praise—while remaining fully accessible and warm for her family and colleagues.
