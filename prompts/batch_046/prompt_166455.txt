I cover the philosophy and psychology of moral character for a public-ideas magazine, and I have been asked to deliver the opening address at the dedication of the new Center for Moral Character and Contextual Psychology—an occasion where half the room will be experimental social psychologists who have spent decades measuring cross-situational behavioral consistency, and the other half will be donors and educators who fund virtue-centered curricula but have never encountered the situationist literature. I need an eight-minute speech in which the tension between virtue ethics and the situationist critique functions as the actual structural architecture rather than decorative allusion: the opening must establish why ordinary moral life seems to presuppose something like stable character; the middle must introduce the situationist challenge precisely, distinguishing global trait ascriptions from local, ecologically embedded competencies—avoiding both the caricature that “people are just inconsistent” and the philosopher’s reflex to treat consistency coefficients as irrelevant; and the closing must show how this tension forces a reconception of virtue itself, treating it not as a portable inner substance but as a form of craft knowledge that is genuinely real yet irreducibly context-sensitive. The speech must land for both audiences—the psychologists should recognize that I understand why situation-specific variance matters methodologically, while the donors should feel the stakes concern human flourishing rather than laboratory measurement—and it must not rely on anyone taking empirical claims on faith, since a model without web access cannot verify specific studies. I was discussing the framing with a colleague over coffee and realized that if I present the situationist critique merely as “we overestimate character,” I forfeit the philosophical problem entirely; I want the audience to leave with the unsettled but productive sense that virtue ethics survives only if it abandons naive global characterology and embraces a more modest, practice-embedded account of moral development. Could you draft this address for me?
