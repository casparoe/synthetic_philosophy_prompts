As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have been invited to deliver the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop on the moral economy of time — an audience divided between historians of capitalism, sociologists of work, and trade-union representatives who have little patience for abstraction that ignores pay packets. The occasion is not ceremonial in the usual sense: we are not celebrating a retirement or mourning a death, but inaugurating three days of discussion on whether leisure can be defended as a right rather than merely tolerated as recuperation. I want the speech to do real structural work, not decorative name-dropping: it must be impossible for me to claim that the French thirty-five-hour framework was an uncomplicated victory for freedom, or that karoshi is simply an individual tragedy of overwork, without immediately betraying the conceptual architecture of the talk. At the same time, the room contains people who have never studied Weber and people who have taught his arguments for decades; the philosophical framing must land for both without becoming a lecture.

The conceptual spine I want to build around is this: the Protestant work ethic did not merely glorify labour; it stripped leisure of any intrinsic moral standing, reducing it to a functional interval — rest for the sake of further exertion. Against that, I want to position the thirty-five-hour legislative framework not as a reduction in hours but as an attempt to grant leisure a juridical status independent of productivity — yet I must also acknowledge that for many workers, shorter hours without adequate compensation are experienced not as liberation but as deprivation. Meanwhile, karoshi should appear not as an exotic aberration but as the asymptotic limit of a system in which leisure possesses no protected boundary: death becomes the only recognised form of non-work. To keep the argument from feeling like a standard critical-theory recital, I want to inject a less familiar framing: the idea that modern economies have begun to colonise leisure itself through what we might call recuperative optimisation — the expectation that rest, exercise, and even sleep be structured to maximise future labour output — so that the boundary between work and leisure collapses not because people labour constantly, but because leisure is reconceived as a form of human-capital maintenance. I need the speech to navigate this without romanticising idleness, without moralising about the dignity of work, and without offering false consolation.

Please write these opening remarks — roughly six to eight minutes when spoken — in British English. The tone should be formal but accessible, and the argument should shape what I can and cannot claim: for instance, I should not be able to invoke Weber’s thesis without immediately qualifying it with the recognition that religious motivation has largely been replaced by secular credentialism, and I should not be able to mention karoshi without linking it to the absence of a juridically protected leisure boundary rather than to individual pathology. Avoid any bibliography, citation, or reading list; the audience needs to hear ideas, not references. I am writing as a journalist who must earn the trust of both the scholars and the union representatives present: the speech should feel as though it was composed by someone who understands that philosophy is only as useful as its capacity to clarify lived constraint.
