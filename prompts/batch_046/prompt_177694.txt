I'm noticing a wierd pattern in historical explanations that I can't quite map onto standard philosophy of history. Historians will list multiple causes for an event — economic crisis, a specific decision by a leader, a technological shift — and they'll admit the outcome was overdetermined, like any two of those probably would've done it. But then they still argue about which was the "real" cause, and they do it by running a counterfactual: "what if this one factor hadn't happened?" Even though they just said the others were sufficient, so removing one shouldn't matter. The strange part is that they apply this removal test more harshly to individual actions — "if he hadn't given the speech" — than to big structural conditions — "if the harvest hadn't failed." So it's not just causal selection or INUS conditions; it feels like an asymmetrical denial of overdetermination, where agental causes get tested for counterfactual dependence even when everyone agrees they weren't necessary. Is there a specific name for this uneven testing pattern? If not, can you define it precisely — especially how it differs from ordinary causal selection and where its edges are?
