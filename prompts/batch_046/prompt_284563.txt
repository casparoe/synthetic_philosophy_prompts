I've been working through a comparison of three very different relationships between citizens and state knowledge, and I'd like feedback on whether my reasoning holds together or collapses at some point I can't see. I don't have any formal training in law or political philosophy—I'm just someone who follows news reports and thinks about them—so I'm writing this as a set of essay notes rather than a finished argument.

My starting observation is that German data protection is usually explained as a dignity-based limit: the state isn't merely forbidden from snooping into private spaces, but from constructing predictive models of citizens that treat people as fully legible systems. By contrast, the American tradition emphasizes openness and suspicion of secret government, yet this creates an asymmetry I find puzzling: citizens are expected to be transparent to corporations and to public scrutiny, while state data practices are restrained mainly by procedural rules rather than by any deep right to remain partially unknown. The Chinese social credit framework then seems to invert both: citizens become maximally legible to the state, while the state's own scoring algorithms remain opaque to those being scored.

From this comparison I want to propose something I haven't encountered named in the articles I've read—perhaps it has a standard name I'm missing, but I'm treating it as my own framing. The core issue, I think, is not privacy versus transparency, but who bears the burden of being misunderstood. Under what I'm calling epistemic shelter, citizens retain a kind of protected opacity: the state may have an incomplete or even slightly wrong picture of them, and this misreading is not a defect to be corrected but a feature that prevents predictive governance from closing completely around individual lives. American openness often undermines this shelter not through direct state intrusion, but through private data markets that feed state inference, leaving citizens with procedural remedies but no dignity-based right to be misread. Chinese social credit eliminates the shelter entirely by treating every gap in state knowledge as an error to be fixed through further data collection. So my tentative thesis is that data protection should be reconceived not as hiding information, but as preserving a citizen's right to remain epistemically ungovernable in specific domains.

Could you comment on the accuracy of how I've characterized the German, American, and Chinese approaches, and on whether my proposed idea of epistemic shelter actually clarifies anything or just renames familiar privacy concerns? I'm especially unsure about my claim that American openness indirectly erodes this shelter: is there a serious legal or institutional protection I'm overlooking that already does the work I think is missing? Please also say explicitly what would change your mind—whether, for example, evidence that German courts routinely approve predictive policing models that rely on the very inferences dignity is said to forbid, or a demonstration that American privacy law already contains dignity-based protections I haven't recognized. Finally, point to the part of my reasoning that is most likely to fail, and tell me what consideration or counterexample would lead you to abandon or significantly revise my central claim about the right to remain epistemically ungovernable.
