Defenders of performance legitimacy often compare an authoritarian but prosperous state—Singapore or contemporary China—to a well-run corporation: the ruling party is management, citizens are shareholders who collect dividends in prosperity and public order, democratic elections are replaced by meritocratic board selection, and just as shareholders of a profitable firm do not vote out the CEO, citizens should not demand multiparty politics when growth and stability are high. How far does this corporate analogy actually hold as an argument for substituting economic performance for democratic authorization? Provide an explicit correspondence table—mapping shareholders, board, CEO, quarterly earnings, bankruptcy, and hostile takeover onto their political equivalents—then show where the analogy fractures, particularly regarding involuntary membership, the absence of exit options comparable to selling shares, and the asymmetry between dissolving a firm and replacing a regime. Assess whether the conclusion that prosperity and order can justify authoritarian rule survives these disanalogies, or whether the analogy was carrying the normative weight all along. If it collapses, propose a more accurate analogical model for the state-citizen relationship under performance-based governance and say whether that replacement strengthens or weakens the legitimacy claim.
