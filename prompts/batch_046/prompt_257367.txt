For a municipal heritage review board tasked with evaluating commemorative proposals—monuments, curriculum modules, museum installations—centered on defeats that have been repurposed into communal identity, I need an operational decision procedure. The board receives proposals invoking well-known defeat narratives: the Confederate Lost Cause, the Masada siege as collective martyrdom, the Kosovo Field myth, and the Dunkirk evacuation. In each case, the same historical material can function either as responsible remembrance or as what I will call a closure-resistant defeat narrative: a story in which failure is converted into an unchallengeable moral credential that suppresses internal dissent, assigns permanent victimhood, or licenses future revanchism against an out-group defined by the original defeat. The board members are not professional historians; they have two hours, no research budget, and must sign a recommendation. Design the procedure.

The procedure must contain four ordered stages, each specifying: the diagnostic question; the concrete evidence to request from proposal authors (texts, design sketches, program notes, or spatial layouts); the decision thresholds (clear pass, clear fail, ambiguous); and the explicit point, if any, where factual analysis ends and a values judgment begins.

The four stages should correspond to: (a) whether the narrative treats defeat as terminus or as generative origin for present action; (b) whether agency is distributed asymmetrically, and whether erased parties are named or structurally omitted; (c) whether the commemorative form requires an enduring enemy or an unfulfilled redemption to maintain coherence; (d) whether the proposal structurally permits disagreement about the defeat’s meaning among members of the commemorated community.

Then apply the full procedure to two hypothetical proposals: first, a proposed Field Covenant memorial framing the 1389 Battle of Kosovo as a sacred, transhistorical vow authorizing present-day territorial exclusivity; second, an educational program framing the 1940 Dunkirk evacuation as an instance of distributed civilian-military resilience that explicitly avoids attributing moral failure to the defeated forces or demonizing the opposing force.

Finally, identify the two failure modes where the procedure is most likely to misclassify: one where genuine communal grief over defeat is structurally identical to strategic myth-making, and one where a proposal uses irony or aesthetic distance to embed all four closure-resistant features without triggering any stage’s fail condition. For each failure mode, specify whether the board should abstain, impose conditions, or escalate.

Keep the weight on the criteria, their motivating cases, and the points of judgmental hand-off; do not score real-world monuments. Do not request citations, further reading, or bibliographies.
