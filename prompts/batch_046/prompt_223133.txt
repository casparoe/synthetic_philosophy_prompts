Many autonomous-vehicle safety stacks now use interval-valued risk representations rather than point probabilities: a perception module might report that an obstacle is a pedestrian with confidence in [0.35, 0.75], and the planner applies a conservative braking rule to the lower bound but an evasive-maneuver rule to the upper bound, never collapsing the range to a single credence for decision. What theory of belief, uncertainty, and practical rationality does this two-threshold architecture presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view—does it commit to ontic imprecision, to ambiguity aversion in decision-making, or to a separation between epistemic states and action policies? Say whether the lower-bound and upper-bound rules are philosophically consistent with each other, whether the design could be reproduced by a sharp Bayesian with appropriate loss functions, and what would have to change in the control logic if we replaced the interval representation with a straightforward expected-utility framework using point credences.
