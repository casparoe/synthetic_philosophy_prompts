I recently read an unnamed essay in philosophy of biology that summarizes the autopoietic definition of life as follows. Living systems are characterized not by replication or genetic information, but by organizational closure: they continuously produce the components and boundary conditions required for that very production. Consequently, a virus is not alive, because it lacks internal closure and must appropriate a host's metabolic machinery; a dormant bacterium remains alive, since its organizational pattern persists even when chemical activity is minimal. The essay then introduces an additional criterion it terms diachronic constraint identity—the requirement that a living system maintain a single, integrated pattern of self-regulation over time, rather than merely achieving temporary homeostasis. By this standard, synthetic protocells with self-repairing membranes and feedback-regulated synthesis count as genuinely alive, whereas ecological collectives such as coral reefs do not, because their constituent organisms retain independent organizational closures that frequently conflict rather than integrating into one unified system. The essay concludes that life is therefore strictly binary: either there is one self-maintaining organizational closure, or there is not, which dissolves traditional borderline cases by revealing them as aggregates of fully living subunits. I suspect the move from organizational closure to this binary conclusion is too quick, and I am unsure whether the treatment of synthetic cells and ecosystems genuinely follows from the theory introduced. Is this a fair summary? Please distinguish what accurately reflects the autopoietic tradition associated with Maturana and Varela, what is a defensible extension, what subtly misrepresents how that tradition treats nested or collective systems, and whether the binary claim survives careful scrutiny of the added diachronic condition.
