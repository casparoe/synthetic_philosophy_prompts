I'm currently taking an upper-division seminar in philosophy of mind, and we've been working carefully through Dennett's critique of the Cartesian theater together with his multiple drafts model of consciousness. Our discussions keep circling back to a tension I can't quite resolve: if there's genuinely no central stage where experience is presented to a unified observer, then what exactly is it that feels like a single, continuous narrative? Some classmates think the theater metaphor dies once you reject the homunculus, but others argue that something like a theater might still exist as an illusion of retrospective coherence. To think through this for my final project, I'd like to approach it narratively rather than analytically.

Write a short story set in a near-future clinical archive where human subjective histories are reconstructed not from a central storage system but from distributed, parallel neural logs that are compiled post hoc. The protagonist, Mara, is a senior archivist assigned to produce the authoritative record of one client's experience of a single Tuesday afternoon. The logs, however, contain multiple incompatible drafts: one stream records an intense argument with a colleague over attribution of a discovery; another stream records the same physical space but with the colleague absent, the dialogue rendered as internal monologue with a different emotional valence; a third stream preserves the bodily arousal and spatial layout but assigns no speaker at all, leaving the experience as pure affect without narrative ownership. Mara begins her work assuming that her job is to locate the Cartesian theater within the system—the hidden master display where the real version plays and the others are mere noise or corruption. As she attempts to synchronize the drafts, she should gradually realize that coherence is manufactured by her own editorial choices rather than discovered in the data, and that there is no hidden canonical version behind the contradictions.

Please avoid generic science-fiction tropes about uploaded consciousness, glitching artificial minds, or singularity-style transcendence. Instead, focus on the phenomenology of assembling a self from fragments that refuse hierarchical ordering, and allow the story's form to mirror its conceptual content—perhaps through sections that present the same moment in mutually exclusive versions without signaling which, if any, should be treated as authoritative. The story should treat the multiple drafts not as errors to be corrected, but as the fundamental architecture of the experience being archived, leaving Mara (and the reader) without a center to appeal to.
