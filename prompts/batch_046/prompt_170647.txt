I am preparing interview questions for a philosophy podcast on collective reasoning. The guest is an unnamed philosopher who defends a specific, non-standard resolution to the discursive dilemma: rather than choosing between premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation, she advocates "path-dependent aggregation," in which the sequence in which a panel evaluates logically connected propositions determines which judgments are retained upon inconsistency, and she applies this specifically to scientific advisory panels that include both human experts and predictive models. She claims that standard treatments miss a structural feature of such panels—what she calls "asymmetric epistemic access profiles," where some members can directly assess premises but not conclusions, and others (the models) can only deliver outputs—so the canonical three-judge example misrepresents the problem. I want a sequence of questions that press her on the actual mechanics of her view: how she fixes the evaluation order without arbitrariness; whether path-dependence collapses into conclusion-based aggregation once all members have symmetric access; how she responds to the classic impossibility results that seem to rule out any consistent aggregation procedure satisfying minimal rationality conditions; and what her framework prescribes when panel members disagree not only about the verdict but about which propositions they are in a position to assess. For each question, include a sharp follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give—particularly any answer that invokes asymmetry of epistemic access to block a standard objection. Conclude with one question asking what institutional or empirical feature of a real-world advisory panel—say, a climate-science board that relies on both domain experts and black-box forecasting systems—would force her to abandon path-dependence and revert to one of the standard procedures.
