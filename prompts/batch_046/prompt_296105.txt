A colleague walked me into this and I cannot find the exit. (1) Bounded rationality means agents lack the computational resources to maximize over all options in a decision space. (2) Simon's satisficing resolves this by setting an aspiration level and accepting the first option that meets it. (3) But the aspiration level is endogenous: the agent must determine it. (4) If she determines it by maximizing expected utility over levels, then satisficing collapses into meta-level optimization, and bounded rationality is merely relocated, not solved. (5) If she determines it by applying a second-order satisficing rule, the same problem recurs at every meta-level, generating an infinite regress unless halted. (6) Halting the regress requires a base-level aspiration that is not rationally justified—either hard-wired, culturally given, or arbitrarily adopted—which makes the theory unfalsifiable, since any choice can be rationalized by adjusting the postulated level. (7) Hence satisficing is either optimization in disguise, viciously regressive, or descriptively empty. (8) So Simon's account offers no coherent alternative to rational-choice theory. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would prefer to keep (2) intact if I can.
