I'm a high school junior in an independent-study philosophy seminar, and I'm trying to adjudicate a disagreement that broke out in our study group last week about computationalism—specifically whether the brain is literally a computer. Two of my peers, let's call them Jordan and Taylor, went back and forth for nearly an hour, and I want an outside assessment of who is right at each step, because neither of them is convinced by the other.

Jordan opened by defending computationalism: the brain implements algorithms, neural-network simulations replicate cognitive functions on digital hardware, and therefore the mind is essentially software running on biological hardware. Taylor countered with Searle's Chinese Room, arguing that syntactic manipulation—no matter how complex—never produces intrinsic semantic understanding. Jordan replied that the Chinese Room targets old-fashioned symbolic AI, not modern connectionist architectures, where meaning might emerge from distributed weights rather than explicit symbol-shuffling.

Then Taylor introduced a more technical point. She cited Hava Siegelmann's 1995 result that analog recurrent neural networks with real-valued weights possess super-Turing computational power—specifically, that they can recognize languages outside the Turing-computable hierarchy. From this, Taylor argued that if the brain operates through continuous analog dynamics, it is either not a computer at all (since standard computation is Turing-limited) or it is a hypercomputer, which undermines the ordinary computationalist claim that the brain runs algorithms like a laptop.

Jordan responded that physical brains are noisy and imprecise, so real-valued weights are an idealization. In practice, neural firing is discrete enough—spikes are all-or-none—that the brain approximates a digital system. Therefore, Taylor's hypercomputation worry is moot, and the brain remains a standard computer within practical tolerance.

Taylor pushed back: admitting that the brain is only approximately computational doesn't save computationalism, because then the computational description is merely a useful model rather than an ontological truth. She compared this to saying a hurricane is just an approximate differential equation running on nature's computer—if that's the claim, it's a category mistake, not a discovery.

Jordan's final reply was that every scientific model is an approximation, so the fact that computational neuroscience idealizes neural noise doesn't distinguish the brain from any other physical system we successfully model. He insisted that Taylor is demanding an impossible standard of literal identity that no scientific theory could meet.

What I'd like is a point-by-point adjudication. At each stage—Jordan's initial defense, Taylor's Chinese Room objection, Jordan's connectionist reply, Taylor's appeal to super-Turing analog computation, Jordan's noise/approximation response, Taylor's category-mistake analogy, and Jordan's final appeal to scientific modeling—please say who has the stronger argument, what subtle error or conflation either side is making, and whether there is anything important both are missing. I am not looking for a general impression; I need a specific assessment of the reasoning as it actually unfolded, including whether Taylor correctly applies Siegelmann's result and whether Jordan's move from noise to discreteness actually follows.
