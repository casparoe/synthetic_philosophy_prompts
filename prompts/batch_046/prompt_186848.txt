I am a novelist drafting an essay that applies Daniel Dennett’s taxonomy of explanatory stances—the physical, the design, and the intentional—to the ontology of fictional characters in the realist novel. Rather than rehearsing the standard opposition between Walton’s make-believe account and Currie’s simulation theory, I propose an intermediate category I call the dramaturgical stance: a mode of readerly engagement in which a figure such as Anna Karenina or Raskolnikov is treated neither as an artifact with a designed function nor as an agent with literal beliefs and desires, but as a real pattern that stabilizes across interpretations because narrative constraint binds authorial design to interpretive projection. I argue that the particular ethical force of fiction—our capacity to mourn and judge these figures without believing they exist—depends on recognizing them as patterns that are neither merely syntactic nor fully mental, and I speculate that if small language models ever approached fiction with anything resembling comprehension, they would need this intermediate stance rather than collapsing narrative agents into statistical tokens or simulated minds.

I would welcome rigorous internal comments rather than references or reading lists. First, does the dramaturgical stance genuinely differ from Dennett’s intentional stance supplemented by hermeneutic awareness, or is it redundant? Second, am I illicitly transferring an ontological claim about real patterns onto interpretive conventions that are better understood as social practices? Third, does the framework illuminate anything about how narrative form—focalization, plot structure, the strategic withholding of interiority—achieves ethical weight, or does it obscure those formal mechanisms by attributing their effect to a stance? I am especially interested in whether the argument overreaches by treating reader response as an explanatory stance on a par with Dennett’s scientific ones, and whether, despite that risk, the idea remains worth developing for those concerned with both philosophy of mind and the craft of fiction.
