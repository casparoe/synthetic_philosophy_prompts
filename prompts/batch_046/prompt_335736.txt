You are a professional moral philosopher specializing in collective action problems, climate ethics, and the structure of institutional responsibility. Please think carefully, begin with some brief brainstorming about how institutional designs can encode implicit theories of causal contribution, and then address the following.

Consider the travel-emissions policy recently adopted by a mid-sized research university. Each faculty member receives an individual annual carbon budget for air travel, calculated as an equal per-capita share of the institution’s total permitted aviation footprint. Travel within the budget requires only routine notification; travel exceeding it demands approval from a central committee, which grants exemptions almost exclusively for invited keynote addresses at peer-reviewed conferences. Simultaneously, the university maintains a collective offset pool, funded by a flat surcharge applied to every flight regardless of whether it falls inside or outside the personal budget, and it publicly reports that this pool renders the institution’s aggregate travel footprint carbon-neutral. The official guidelines encourage faculty to treat their personal budgets as ethically binding, citing the cumulative impact of individual choices, yet the university’s public communications also emphasize that no single flight produces a measurable change in global climate outcomes.

What philosophical theory—or cluster of theories—about individual contributions to collective harms does this dual-track design presuppose? In particular, how does the policy treat the inefficacy objection: does it regard causal negligibility as defeating individual obligations entirely, as an epistemic barrier that only collective mechanisms can overcome, or as applying selectively to budget-compliant but not budget-exceeding travel? Reconstruct the implicit account of moral responsibility, distinguish what the specific combination of personal budgets, keynote exemptions, and flat-surcharge pooling strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say whether the practice is internally consistent or quietly combines incompatible commitments. Finally, explain what would have to change in the policy’s architecture if its underlying view were replaced with a strict causal-inefficacy account that denies any individual climate obligation, and contrast that with the changes required if the view were replaced by one that treats every emission as morally weighty regardless of collective context or institutional mediation.
