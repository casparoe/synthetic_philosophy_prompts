I’ve been reading Tolstoy’s late essay on art—What Is Art?—in a battered paperback I found at a garage sale, and one idea keeps circling back: he argues that genuine art is not a matter of beauty or technical mastery but a kind of infection, a direct transmission of feeling that makes one person sense brotherhood with strangers; and he insists that once art is made for money, prestige, or an exclusive class, it becomes false art, a counterfeit that flatters the maker instead of uniting people. I am not an academic; I am a carpenter who took free pottery classes with Elena Vasquez for fifteen years, and now I have to give the opening remarks at the dedication of the Vasquez Community Studio, a small workshop behind the public library where she taught for three decades without ever charging tuition, signing her work, or allowing a gallery to show it. The audience will be divided: half are local artists and former students who now hold MFAs and will recognize Tolstoy’s framework immediately; half are her family, neighbors, and city council members who simply know her as the woman who gave away every bowl she threw. The philosophical idea must do real structural work, not decorative work. If Tolstoy is right, naming a building after an individual artist and framing her life as a legacy of singular genius risks turning a living transmission of feeling into a monument to ego—the very corruption he condemned. So I need remarks that genuinely honor what she built—conditions in which strangers felt connected through making—without turning the event into a philosophy lecture, without asking non-philosophers to adopt a theory, and without reducing her refusal of the market to mere modesty. Please write an approximately four-minute speech that navigates that tension from the inside: experts should hear Tolstoy shaping every line, while everyone else hears only a warm, concrete tribute to a teacher. After the speech, please include a brief, separate reflective note that states where my application of Tolstoy is most likely to fail—what specific detail about her practice or about his argument would change your mind about whether honoring her by name genuinely contradicts his view, and which step in the reasoning is weakest.
