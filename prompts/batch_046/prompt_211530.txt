I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of economics embedded with a regional health-technology assessment board that reviews population-level preventive interventions—vaccination rollouts, screening programs, and risk-communication campaigns—where outcomes are probabilistic and budgets are fixed. The board convenes monthly, evaluates three to five proposals per session, has no formal training in decision theory, and must sign off on whether each proposal's risk structure is rationally coherent before ethical review can proceed. Recent audits revealed that members routinely reject mathematically equivalent compound-lottery formulations while accepting single-stage descriptions of identical risks, and they apply the independence axiom inconsistently whenever common consequences are present. I need a reusable decision procedure—executable in about fifteen minutes per proposal, using only arithmetic and percentage comparisons—that a board member can run without consulting an expert. The procedure should take the form of ordered tests with explicit stopping rules: (1) determine whether the proposal presents outcomes as reduced or compound lotteries; (2) apply a common-consequence test to detect violations of the independence axiom; (3) flag permitted deviations that can be defended by state-dependent utility, temporal framing, or heterogeneous risk attitudes across the target population; (4) designate precisely which steps yield a determinate yes/no and which require a documented judgment call, specifying who must initial that call; and (5) state exactly where rational-coherence assessment ends and distributive or ethical review must take over. It must handle hard cases: proposals that treat subpopulations with different baseline risks, proposals involving near-catastrophic tail probabilities, and framing effects that obscure equivalence between options. Apply the procedure to three proposals: (a) a universal screening program with a 95 percent accurate test and a 5 percent false-positive rate that leads to unnecessary invasive treatment—where the procedure should reach a conclusion the board will likely resist; (b) a vaccination campaign framed either as reducing infection risk uniformly from 10 percent to 5 percent or as eliminating infection entirely in 5 percent of recipients with no change for the remainder; and (c) a budget choice between funding an intervention that certainly saves 200 lives and one that offers a 90 percent chance of saving 250 lives with a 10 percent chance of saving none. Finally, specify what the procedure cannot adjudicate, where it is prone to misfire, and under what conditions the board should suspend rational-coherence review in favor of direct ethical deliberation.
