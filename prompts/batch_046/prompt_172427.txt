I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, art history, or ethics, but after designing provenance-tracking systems for digital assets I have begun to think of museum restitution as an ownership-graph problem with nodes that can be physical, informational, or both. I want to test whether my intuitive verdicts on concrete cases hang together or whether I am forced to abandon one of them. Here are the judgments I actually hold. The Benin Bronzes seized during the 1897 British punitive expedition should be physically returned to Nigeria, even if local storage conditions are inferior; the coercive origin of the acquisition seems to override any custodial argument about preservation. The Parthenon Marbles removed under an Ottoman permit in the early nineteenth century should remain in London, because—despite the moral discomfort—the transaction was not directly coerced in the same way, and Greece now possesses high-fidelity replicas that serve public access adequately. A ceremonial mask sold willingly by its originating community in 1925 to fund a hospital should stay with the acquiring museum, since a voluntary exchange with informed consent creates a title that later descendants cannot unilaterally revoke. A painting looted by Nazi agents and subsequently donated in good faith to a museum should still be returned to the heirs of the original owners; legal good faith on the part of the donor does not erase the initial theft. Finally, a high-resolution digital twin—complete with 3D mesh, spectral imaging, and provenance metadata—of an artifact physically destroyed in a 2020 conflict should be transferred as a data package to the source nation, but the scattered physical fragments held elsewhere should remain in place, because I treat the informational and material substrates as having split ontological status.

The pattern I feel is not consistent: cases one and four both override later legal stability, yet case two preserves it despite comparable colonial context, while case three protects a voluntary transaction and case five introduces a distinction between data and matter that I cannot justify from the other four. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps centered on the mode of acquisition, the reparative substitutability of access, or the kind of object involved—that captures all five verdicts, and if not, which judgment is the outlier I should drop? Apply your principle also to a case I have not considered: a ritual object taken not in war but during a peaceful anthropological expedition in 1910, with the community’s elders giving verbal consent documented only in the collector’s field notes. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
