I'm grading essays for my philosophy of science course, which introduced a specific taxonomy not found in standard texts: "structural underdetermination" occurs when two theories employ different formalisms yet yield identical predictions, while "ontological underdetermination" occurs when a single formalism supports incompatible interpretations without altering the mathematics. The exam asked students to (i) explain Quine's distinction between global and local (or "transient") underdetermination, (ii) classify the Bohmian versus Everettian quantum mechanics case using our structural/ontological taxonomy, and (iii) evaluate whether Quine's global thesis, together with his arguments for ontological relativism, licenses the conclusion that there is no substantive metaphysical dispute between the two interpretations.

Here is one answer: "Quine's global underdetermination thesis holds that for any body of evidence there exist logically incompatible theories that fit it equally well; he introduces this in 'Two Dogmas of Empiricism.' Local, or transient, underdetermination is the weaker Duhem-Quine point that a particular hypothesis can be saved by adjusting auxiliary assumptions, but Quine regards this as merely preliminary to the global problem. Applying our taxonomy, Bohmian mechanics and Everettian mechanics share the exact same mathematical formalism—the Schrödinger equation—while differing only in interpretive gloss (hidden particle positions versus branching worlds). This makes their rivalry a pure case of ontological, not structural, underdetermination. Because they are empirically equivalent in principle, Quine's global thesis applies directly: since no observation could ever decide between them, there is no fact of the matter about particles or branches. Consequently, Quine's ontological relativism tells us to treat the dispute as pragmatic scheme-selection rather than substantive metaphysics."
