Consider the following position about workfare, benefit fraud, and conditionality: the distinction between the "deserving" and "undeserving" poor is not an empirical discovery about individual moral failure but a structurally necessary fiction produced by the administrative apparatus itself. On this view, fraud investigations and workfare conditions do not detect pre-existing undeservingness; they actively construct it as a boundary category separating claimants whose non-employment can be narrated as temporary deviation from a contribution life-course (deserving) from those whose non-employment reads as persistent deviation (undeserving). The justification for conditionality is therefore not retributive but narrative-functional: it enforces the temporal story that keeps universal risk-pooling politically legitimate.

Take this position seriously and play it straight. What would have to be true for it to be wrong? Which observations about fraud-detection accuracy, claimant behavior, or taxpayer attitudes would count decisively against it, and which would only be awkward? What conceptual distinctions—between structural exclusion and voluntary withdrawal, between signaling and punishment, between narrative framing and material conditions—would defenders of the view themselves concede as damaging if they collapsed? Are there rival accounts (for instance, that conditionality genuinely improves labor-market matching, or that fraud is best understood as rational adaptation to inadequate benefits) that predict observable patterns this narrative-functional view cannot accommodate? Be explicit about whether the position risks anything substantive or has been insulated by qualifications, and state whether that insulation is a virtue or a vulnerability.
