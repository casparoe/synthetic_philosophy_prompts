I'm advising a regional cooperative that manages shared irrigation infrastructure across twelve small farms. The cooperative's bylaws distinguish between "proprietor" members—those who hold formal water-rights deeds—and "participant" members, who contribute labor or capital but hold no deed. Recently, three farms originally classified as "participants" have, over a decade, taken on nearly identical maintenance burdens and decision-making roles as the deed-holders; meanwhile, two original "proprietors" have become absentee owners who contribute nothing but still vote on allocations. Some members now propose reclassifying the active participants as "steward-proprietors," a hybrid category that would grant them voting rights without altering the underlying deed registry. Others object that this conflates legal ownership with functional contribution, arguing that if we blur the line we lose the very purpose of having formal categories in the first place.

What makes the situation puzzling is not the policy outcome but the status of the categories themselves. The "proprietor" label was originally created by a state water board in the 1970s, yet the cooperative's daily practices have gradually reshaped what being a proprietor actually involves—who shows up to meetings, who repairs canals, who is held accountable for overuse. Some members claim that the category has always been a practical tool, so we should revise it freely to match current realities. Others insist that there is a correct classification independent of our present preferences, perhaps anchored in historical entitlement or in objective patterns of contribution that exist whether or not we formalize them. I'm trying to understand whether this disagreement is about discovering the true structure of membership, or about negotiating which social kind we want to live with, and whether those two questions can even be separated.

There's also a subtle feedback worry that keeps coming up: if we officially recognize the "steward-proprietors," the people in that class may begin to see themselves as genuine owners and act accordingly—perhaps taking risks or asserting claims they wouldn't have otherwise—while the absentee deed-holders might further disengage once their privileged status is formally diluted. So the classification doesn't just describe behavior; it seems poised to reshape the very patterns it's meant to track. Given all this, how should I think about what makes a kind like "proprietor" real? Is its reality fixed by origins, by current function, by the cooperative's collective decision, or by some combination that allows for principled revision without collapsing into mere convention?
