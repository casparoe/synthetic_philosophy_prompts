A colleague is using Plato's charioteer image — the rational part as driver, the spirited and appetitive parts as unruly horses — to interpret his remarks on drunkenness in the Laws. She says wine works like binding the charioteer's hands: the driver remains on the box but cannot steer, so the horses bolt, and this proves that behaviour under drink reveals nothing about the soul's true order; it is mere noise from parts that are always unruly but usually restrained. From this she concludes that Plato cannot consistently treat controlled drinking as a genuine trial of character, since the rational part has not been exposed but only gagged. I think the analogy quietly imports the idea that the charioteer is passive and that the crash is purely accidental, whereas in Plato's account wine seems to function more like a deliberate stress-test that reveals how firmly the driver ordinarily holds the reins. Can you set out the explicit mapping (driver, horses, reins, the binding, the road, the crash), identify which features of the chariot picture are genuinely present in Plato's discussion of symposia and which are smuggled in, separate the disanalogies that undermine her conclusion from those that are harmless, and say whether a different analogy — perhaps testing metal by fire — actually fits Plato's purpose better?
