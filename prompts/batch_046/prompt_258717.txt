Covering debates at the intersection of evolutionary biology and moral philosophy, I keep noticing an argumentative shape that I cannot place precisely. A speaker observes that our moral intuitions were shaped by selection pressures for social cohesion and survival rather than by access to independent moral truths, and takes that genealogy to weaken moral realism. Yet in the same discussion the speaker appeals to evolutionary accounts of cooperation or reciprocal fairness to support particular normative commitments—treating evolutionary origins as authoritative there—without explaining why that genealogy does not equally corrode the beliefs it now underwrites. The failure is not a straightforward self-refutation, since the speaker does not claim that evolutionary origins invalidate reasoning in general; it is a directional asymmetry, in which evolutionary explanation acts as corrosive on one class of beliefs and as foundational on another. Is there an established term for this specific move? How should it be distinguished from the broader charge that evolutionary debunking is self-undermining, and from plain equivocation? If the literature offers no precise name, how might one be coined, together with boundary conditions that separate it from adjacent argumentative failures?
