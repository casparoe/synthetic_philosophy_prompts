I serve on an advisory committee for my residential community, which five years ago replaced its elected town council with an appointed management board following a period of corruption and sharply declining property values. The board holds no elections, publishes no meeting minutes, and quietly suppresses organized dissent, yet it has eliminated graft, reduced maintenance fees by nearly a third, and raised median home values significantly. Last month the board proposed formalizing its permanent authority and presented a detailed argument: democratic procedures are purely instrumental, so when they fail to deliver prosperity and security they forfeit all claim to legitimacy; conversely, since the board has delivered both in abundance, it has earned an unconditional right to govern without electoral accountability. They added a more specific claim that open deliberation is not merely inefficient but epistemically corrupt—public debate introduces emotional distortion that ruins the precise measurement of collective welfare, whereas the board governs through a standardized community-wellbeing index that treats every policy choice as a technical optimization problem. A vocal minority of residents rejects this entirely, insisting that being governed without consent is structurally identical to living under benevolent captivity, and that excellent outcomes can never convert coercion into legitimate authority regardless of how precisely they are measured. The committee has asked me to help evaluate whether the board's reasoning is sound, and I want to understand whether prosperity and order can genuinely justify authoritarian governance, or whether there is a deeper conceptual error in treating measurable results as sufficient for legitimate rule—especially when democracy itself is framed not as a competing source of authority but as a source of cognitive noise that corrupts decision-making. What is the strongest assessment of their position, and where does the minority's reply succeed or fail?
