I'm a novelist researching moral frameworks for a novel, and at a salon following a reading I listened to two guests—an anthropologist and a philosopher of ethics—debate whether moral relativism can survive what they called reflexive framework failure. I did not take sides, but I need the logic clarified. Here is the exchange, reconstructed as faithfully as I can.

First, the anthropologist said moral relativism is best understood as indexicality, not permissiveness: moral claims are true or false only relative to a framework, and frameworks are incommensurable in something like the way languages are. To condemn another culture's practice is therefore a category mistake, comparable to faulting French for lacking the English subjunctive.

The philosopher replied that indexicality resolves inter-framework conflict but leaves intra-framework collapse untouched. He proposed a case: imagine a framework that makes complete transparency its highest virtue, yet institutionalizes deception in ways that systematically destroy the trust transparency requires. The framework's own criteria condemn the practice, yet the practice is constitutive of the framework's social structure. This is not mere hypocrisy; it is a structural contradiction the framework cannot resolve using only internal resources, because correcting it requires abandoning the deception that sustains its power.

The anthropologist countered that this is just internal incoherence, which any framework can address through reform. She added that frameworks often contain implicit norms of self-preservation or harmony that supply corrective resources; pointing out that deception undermines transparency is therefore still an internal critique, not a smuggled objective standard.

The philosopher answered that this makes relativism unfalsifiable. If every contradiction can be redescribed as an implicit internal norm, then no framework can ever genuinely fail. He then sharpened the case: consider a radical presentist framework that explicitly denies future states have moral weight and therefore rejects long-term cooperation as a value. Its practices, however, depend entirely on long-term cooperation for survival. Because the framework denies the relevance of the future, it has no internal reason to see the dependency as a problem. It is not incoherent by its own lights; it is practically unworkable, and its adherents suffer. Yet relativism, he said, must treat this as merely another valid framework.

The anthropologist said he was conflating practical sustainability with moral truth. A bridge that collapses is an engineering failure; a presentist framework that collapses is a practical failure. Relativism can acknowledge that some frameworks are unlivable without conceding they are morally false in some absolute sense.

The philosopher replied that morality is not engineering. If a framework is unlivable in a way that produces real suffering, and its adherents have no internal resources to recognize or remedy it because they have rejected the conceptual tools required to do so, then insisting it is not false, only unsustainable, sounds like complacency disguised as neutrality. The suffering is not framework-relative; the inability to address it is.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move—rating each step from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification—and then say who is more right overall and what both parties are missing. In particular, address whether the presentist example genuinely escapes internal critique, and whether practical unworkability can be separated from moral failure without begging the question. Finally, say explicitly what argument or observation would change your mind about your overall verdict, and identify the specific point in your reasoning where failure is most likely.
