I'm trying to understand what definition of life our institutions actually operate with, because three common judgments seem hard to reconcile. In medicine and law, a patient with irreversible loss of all brain function is declared dead and can become an organ donor, even though—if kept on ventilation—the body maintains temperature, heals wounds, grows hair, and has even sustained a pregnancy to term. In synthetic biology, a minimal cell assembled in a lab from chemically synthesized DNA is called a living organism the moment it metabolizes and divides, despite having no evolutionary lineage or natural origin. Yet virology treats viruses as non-living, even though they evolve, adapt, and replicate inside hosts. Do these practices presuppose a single implicit theory—perhaps one that ties life to integrated organismal self-maintenance and allows artificial origins but requires metabolism independent of a host? Or are they philosophically underdetermined, fitting equally well with functionalist checklists, autopoietic accounts, or hybrid views that smuggle in norms about naturalness and personhood? Reconstruct the implicit conception, say whether it genuinely licenses all three judgments or whether parts of the practice are in tension. Then tell me what clinical death-certification, synthetic-organism regulation, and biological taxonomy would have to change if we adopted a strictly evolutionary-historical definition—one that counts only entities with continuous descent from a last universal common ancestor.
