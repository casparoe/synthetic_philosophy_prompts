I’ve been revisiting the standard historiographical triangle for the French Revolution—class struggle, ideology, and contingency—after a long forum thread with friends about whether institutional decay is usually overdetermined. The conventional answers to whether revolutions devour their children feel too deterministic: either the bourgeoisie had to crush the sans-culottes, or Jacobin ideology contained an internal logic of purification, or the whole descent into the Terror was essentially accidental. I want to push a slightly different framing, one that treats the radicalization of 1793–94 not as the expression of a coherent class interest or doctrine, but as the contingent result of a specific institutional feedback loop—what we might call revolutionary credentialism meeting epistemic closure. In this view, the committees faced radical uncertainty about loyalty and threat, had no reliable private signals, and therefore relied on ever-escalating public demonstrations of revolutionary purity; each round of denunciation updated the common knowledge about what was safe to say, shrinking the space for moderation not because moderates were class enemies, but because moderation became indistinguishable from hidden opposition under conditions of extreme signal noise. The violence, on this account, was not the inevitable child-eating of revolution, but an emergent property of a system that punished ambiguity more severely than error.

If we take this framing seriously—treating the Terror primarily as a cascade of credibility signaling and institutional information collapse rather than as class warfare or ideological self-realization—what are the most important implications? Specifically, how does this reframe the old question of whether revolutions necessarily devour their children? Does it suggest that revolutionary violence is more avoidable than Marxist or idealist interpretations imply, or does it reveal a deeper structural vulnerability in any movement that replaces traditional legitimacy with continuous performative loyalty? And what does this imply more broadly for modeling revolutionary dynamics: should we expect similar cascades whenever a regime substitutes procedural legitimacy for constant public affirmation of commitment, or are there specific boundary conditions—perhaps related to the speed of institutional replacement, the density of social networks, or the availability of credible exit options—that determine whether a revolution eats its own?
