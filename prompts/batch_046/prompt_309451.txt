I am preparing a ninety-minute seminar for first-year graduate students in a joint law-and-philosophy program who have just completed a unit on informed consent and presently understand autonomy as present-tense preference satisfaction: a competent patient expresses a wish, and that wish generates authority. I want them to leave with a grasp of why advance directives pose a problem deeper than “what if she changes her mind?”—specifically, the authority of an earlier self over a later self who may no longer possess the cognitive architecture required to revise, endorse, or even comprehend those earlier values. I need a teaching plan rather than the lecture itself: the dependency structure of ideas (what must be in place before the authority question can arise, and what should come only after they see that standard autonomy theory fails here); a single carrying case that involves a clear value discontinuity between earlier and later self but avoids both extreme dementia stereotypes and sensational life-or-death drama, with explicit notes on what properties it must have and what it must lack to carry the conceptual load; the misconceptions they will predictably raise in sequence—likely treating the directive first as a breachable contract, then treating present emotional comfort as a trump card, then conflating personal identity over time with normative authority—together with the point in the arc where each should be anticipated and absorbed; a check for understanding at the end that reveals whether they have grasped the jurisdictional problem rather than merely feeling uneasy about overriding a suffering patient’s present wishes; and an explicit statement of what should be left out at this level (for instance, whether to bracket metaphysical debates about diachronic identity, or statutory drafting details) with a brief justification. I am also experimenting with framing the directive not as a preference list but as a normative placeholder—a constitutional-style delegation of authority that holds open a space for values the current self can no longer articulate—rather than an expression of taste. If this framing is conceptually confused, or if the task as posed cannot be executed well because the distinction between delegated authority and expressed preference collapses under pressure, say so plainly instead of constructing a tidy pedagogical sequence that papers over the problem.
