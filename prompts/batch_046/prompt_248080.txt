I was recently appointed to my university's interdisciplinary review board for low-impact autonomous systems, and a proposal we must decide next week has forced me to examine whether our own precedents actually cohere around a single principle. Our rulings to date have been as follows: we approved a study that defined "no side effects" purely procedurally—no measurable state change outside a designated digital sandbox—without invoking any normative framework; we rejected a proposal that tried to specify side-effect avoidance via a complete preference ordering over all possible world states, on the grounds that this concealed a full value specification the authors could not justify; we approved with conditions a robotics study that bounded impacts relative to expert-defined baseline trajectories in physical space, but only after requiring explicit exclusion of social-interaction variables; we rejected a study that used an implicitly trained reward model to delineate acceptable impact, arguing that this embedded unspecified values inside an opaque optimisation target; and we narrowly approved a study employing counterfactual invariance—outputs must not depend on non-task variables within a mechanically specified range—provided that range was not normatively selected.

The new proposal concerns a language-model agent confined to a closed simulation with no physical actuators. Its authors define "avoid side effects" through a partial preference ordering restricted to three quantified dimensions—computational resource use, user time expenditure, and informational privacy—while treating all other dimensions, including alterations to other agents' belief states and to archived digital records, as "value-neutral" and therefore outside the boundary unless flagged by an external auditor. They contend that because physical effects are impossible, a full value specification is unnecessary, and that partial specification is precisely what renders the system low-impact. I am unsure whether we should approve with conditions, reject, or approve outright, because our track record seems compatible with at least three rules: one holding that any explicit, bounded partial specification is permissible regardless of what it omits; one holding that leaving belief-state and archival impacts unbounded implicitly licenses precisely the kind of unexamined social effect we excluded in our robotics ruling; and one holding that digital and physical domains are governed by different standards, so our sandbox ruling is the closer analog and our robotics conditions are inapplicable.
