As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I have been attempting to formalise my intuitions about strategic reasoning and belief coordination, largely because they appear structurally similar to problems I deal with in distributed system design. I should like to know whether the following set of commitments is jointly tenable, or whether I have inadvertently adopted incompatible positions.

(a) I accept that rationality is common knowledge in the formal sense required by epistemic game theory: for any equilibrium concept to have predictive force, every agent must be rational, must know that every other agent is rational, and so on, without termination.

(b) I understand Aumann's agreement result to entail that two rational agents with identical prior beliefs and common knowledge of their respective posteriors cannot rationally disagree about a proposition. Consequently, I take persistent disagreement in practice to be explicable only by either private information or a failure of rationality.

(c) Drawing on my professional experience with asynchronous distributed networks, I hold that true common knowledge is practically unattainable in any system subject to message delay, network partition, and partial failure — conditions that apply, I believe, to all human social networks of non-trivial scale.

(d) From (c) I infer that epistemic game theory describes a normative ideal that is descriptively impossible at scale, and that predictive models of human behaviour should therefore abandon the common-knowledge assumption in favour of behavioural heuristics.

(e) At the same time, I think that in small, tightly-coupled groups — such as a board of directors or a protocol design committee — common knowledge of rationality can be approximated closely enough for equilibrium analysis to retain practical utility.

Please examine these for consistency. In particular: does (c) undermine the applicability of (a) and (b), or can they be restricted to normative or small-scale domains without contradiction? Is there a hidden tension between explaining disagreement via (b) when (c) suggests that failure of common knowledge is always available as an alternative explanation? And does (e) genuinely resolve the conflict between (a) and (c), or does it merely postpone it?

Please rank the tensions by severity, distinguish those that are genuine from those that only appear problematic, indicate the least costly revision that would restore coherence, and say whether adopting that revision forces me to abandon the distributed-systems analogy in (c).
