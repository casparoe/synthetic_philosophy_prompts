I am preparing a ninety-minute session for third-year philosophy students who have just finished a course unit on modern financial ethics and consequently read every pre-modern economic text through a presentist lens—either as a naive endorsement of markets or as an anachronistic socialism. I want them to understand Aristotle's separation of oikonomia, the bounded art of household management directed at living well, from chrematistike, the art of acquisition that recognizes no natural limit, with particular attention to his claim that money was introduced to facilitate exchange but becomes structurally perverse when it is made to generate more of itself, as in lending at interest. Rather than delivering the explanation yourself, lay out the teaching plan: identify the conceptual dependency that must be secured before the students can see why retail exchange is limited by the needs it serves while usury is not; propose the single concrete case that should carry the whole distinction, specifying both what it must contain and why analogies drawn from contemporary stock speculation or digital currency trading should be excluded; chart the misconceptions in the sequence they will predictably form—first that Aristotle is merely moralizing against all commercial profit, then that his condemnation of interest is nothing more than a culturally specific prejudice rather than a structural observation about the sterility of money breeding money—and state the precise point in the arc where each should be anticipated and defused; and construct one final comparative question that tests whether the students have internalized the argument about natural limits rather than merely memorized a vocabulary of Aristotelian disapproval. In addition, indicate which section of the Politics treatment should be set aside for this audience and justify the omission.
