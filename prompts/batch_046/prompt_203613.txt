Last seminar two people in my cohort got into a real back-and-forth about whether Plutarch's paired lives actually teach morality or if they're just elaborate gossip with a moral label stuck on. Maya argued the pairing is the whole point — putting Alexander next to Caesar lets you see where virtue holds up and where it cracks under different conditions, so you're not just worshipping heroes, you're doing comparative moral calibration. She said without that structure you just get hagiography, but with it you get something closer to stress-testing your own judgments.

Jared wasn't buying it. He said the pairings actually flatten real differences because Plutarch forces both lives into the same narrative mold — rise, virtue tested, crisis, legacy — so the comparison hides causal specifics behind a false symmetry. He also brought up this idea he called exemplar fatigue, where reading about people that far above ordinary human scale makes comparison discouraging rather than instructive, which undermines the whole pedogogical claim. Maya shot back that that's anacronistic — Plutarch wasn't writing self-help for modern meritocrats, he was writing for Roman elites who already held power, so the calibration was about governance choices not personal motivation. Jared said even granting the audience difference, the structural problem stays: pairing makes you think the two lives are commensurable when they might not be, and it papers over the ways context actually determines outcome. Then Maya noted that Plutarch sometimes breaks his own template on purpose — like with Demetrius and Antony — precisely to show that the mold fails, and that reflexivity is part of the lesson.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't pick a side. Can you adjudicate point by point? Is the pairing method really doing comparative moral work, or is Jared right that it's mostly narrative flattening? Does exemplar fatigue apply to Plutarch's audience, or is Maya correct that it's a modern misreading? And what's missing from both of their accounts — maybe something about how biography teaches that isn't about either comparison or motivation?
