I am designing a single ninety-minute doctoral seminar in comparative political theory for first-year students who have completed only an introductory course in American political thought. I want to teach not the standard transatlantic dictionary comparison, but the structural confusion created when three distinct traditions—Anglo-American progressive liberalism, continental European social-democratic liberalism, and German ordoliberalism—simultaneously claim and reject the same term. Please plan the explanation: the exact order in which the semantic shifts must be presented so that students see the present ambiguity as a feature of institutional genealogy rather than a translation error; identify the one policy domain—labour-market flexibility, hate-speech regulation, or constitutional review—through which the three traditions must be shown to diverge in practical application rather than in abstract definition, and specify what that domain must contain and lack to serve as the carrying example; map the misconceptions these students will predictably import from American-centric training, such as treating liberalism as a fixed left-right coordinate or assuming that European liberal parties occupy a centrist position analogous to American moderates, indicating precisely where in the sequence each should be intercepted; propose a brief diagnostic question or exercise that would reveal whether the structural point has landed; and state clearly which canonical historical narrative—such as the Lockean-to-Rawlsian lineage—should be deliberately excluded at this stage and acknowledged as omitted. Please provide your answer in British English.
