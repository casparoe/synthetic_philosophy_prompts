Consider the following line of reasoning about Raphael Lemkin’s concept of genocide and the contemporary politics of naming atrocities.

(1) The Genocide Convention was designed to create a distinct legal and moral category, separating genocide from war crimes and crimes against humanity by requiring specific intent to destroy a protected group in whole or in part.

(2) In actual adjudication and political discourse, that specific intent is almost never available in explicit documentary form; it must be inferred from patterns of violence, rhetoric, and institutional conduct.

(3) Any inference standard broad enough to establish genocide in historically clear cases—where the destructive purpose is reconstructed from circumstantial evidence—will also classify many mass atrocities that lack that specific group-destructive purpose, such as strategic campaigns that accept high civilian casualties for territorial or security aims, or famine policies driven primarily by resource extraction with destructive effects as foreseeable but not intended outcomes.

(4) Therefore, either we restrict the inference standard so tightly that genocide becomes nearly impossible to establish in real time, rendering the legal category inert, or we relax it and the boundary between genocide and other mass crimes collapses.

(5) If the category is inert, then when states, NGOs, or media label an ongoing atrocity “genocide,” they are not triggering a determinate legal obligation but performing a rhetorical act—invoking moral urgency without a corresponding juridical mechanism.

(6) If, on the other hand, the category is broad enough to be usable, then calling an atrocity “genocide” adds no specific legal content beyond what crimes-against-humanity frameworks already provide; it merely amplifies moral condemnation.

(7) Consequently, “genocide” cannot simultaneously serve as a precise legal trigger with distinctive obligations and as a concept with unique moral gravity: it is either an empty formal category or a redundant moral label.

(8) So Lemkin’s project of instituting a genuinely separate legal and moral classification fails on its own terms, and the politics of naming atrocities is left with either impotent legalism or undifferentiated outrage.
