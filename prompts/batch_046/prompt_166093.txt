A philosopher of action, Dr. E. Voss, has classified several instances of apparent failure of self-control according to whether they exemplify Aristotelian akrasia or hyperbolic discounting. Her verdicts are as follows. First, a dieter who, despite having reminded herself of her health goals minutes earlier, eats a second slice of wedding cake because its immediate sensory presence makes her long-term health feel unreal; she feels sharp regret immediately afterwards. Voss classifies this as akrasia. Second, a saver who cancels an automatic pension contribution after three months, reporting no particular craving but only that distant retirement lost weight against present liquidity; he feels disappointed but not ashamed. Voss classifies this as hyperbolic discounting. Third, a musician who abandons a practice session because immediate fatigue dominates, with no appetitive attraction to rest, describing the choice as her priorities simply reordering themselves; she feels mild guilt. Voss classifies this as hyperbolic discounting. Fourth, a scientist who breaks a promise to share data by spending the afternoon on a recreational task, reporting that immediate pleasure pulled him away while he remained aware he was violating his commitment, with future reputational harm feeling distant; he feels intense shame. Voss classifies this as akrasia.

These four verdicts are jointly consistent with more than one general rule. One rule distinguishes cases by whether the agent acts against an occurrent normative judgment that she still endorses in the moment of action (akrasia) or whether her present evaluation genuinely differs from her past one without present conflict (hyperbolic discounting). Another rule distinguishes them by whether a non-cognitive, affective or sensory pull suppresses judgment (akrasia) or whether a cool cognitive reweighting of temporal proximity operates without such pull (hyperbolic discounting).

Now consider a new case. A therapist maintains a strict after-hours boundary. At 10:45 p.m. she receives a message from a distressed client. She feels intense anxiety that the client may be in immediate danger, responds in violation of her rule, and the next morning recognises that her fear distorted her risk assessment. Crucially, in the moment she genuinely judged that responding was morally required; she did not feel she was doing wrong. There was no sensory or appetitive pull, only emotional urgency that made the present need dominate her distant principle of self-care.

What verdict should Dr. Voss reach if she wishes to remain consistent with her earlier classifications? Identify the closest analogues among her four cases, lay out the competing rules that accommodate her track record, say what each rule predicts about the therapist, and offer your best guess with reasons. Then propose a further, distinct case that would force a choice between those rules. Please write your answer in British English.
