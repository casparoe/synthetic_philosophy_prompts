Design a 75-minute seminar activity for 16 upper-level political philosophy students that makes them feel the Translation Trap of public reason: the idea that requiring citizens to justify coercive policies with reasons others can accept does not merely filter religious arguments out but restructures deliberation so holders of comprehensive doctrines face a trilemma—speak sincerely and lose influence, translate convictions into publicly accessible terms and risk misrepresentation, or camouflage religious reasoning in secular language and risk exposure. Use a structured role-play with hidden conviction cards and point-based stakes so participants run into the pressure themselves before the facilitator names it.

Specify the setting and group division (four groups of four); materials (conviction cards describing assigned comprehensive doctrines, a policy brief on funding a crisis-pregnancy center, private reasoning sheets, and public scorecards); rules for two rounds of deliberation (first open, then filtered by an accessibility rule enforced through peer challenge); and a payoff scheme linking collective consensus points to individual integrity points tied to hidden doctrine commitments. Provide a minute-by-minute timeline.

Participants should come away seeing that accessibility demands alter the speaker’s relation to their own conviction and generate strategic incentives to disguise rather than translate. The debrief should map each round and scoring moment onto parts of the trilemma, predict what will go wrong the first time (for example, students treating camouflage as success or ignoring the filter) and how to correct for it, and frankly state which feature—likely the long-term psychological cost of habitual translation—the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply directly.
