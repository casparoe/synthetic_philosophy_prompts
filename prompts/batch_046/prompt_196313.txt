You chair the arbitration panel for a multinational clinical-research consortium, and your board receives protocols in which sponsor institutions, local review boards, and community representatives each invoke incompatible moral frameworks—autonomy-based deontology, harm-reduction utilitarianism, and relational or collective-dignity ethics. You need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the idea that moral compromise can operate like gains from trade: parties exchange concessions on dimensions they weight differently, producing a settlement each prefers to deadlock, provided no party is asked to abandon a value it treats as non-negotiable. First, specify the preconditions that must hold for a conflict to be amenable to trade-style compromise rather than to conceptual revision or to deadlock. Second, lay out the step-by-step procedure your panel can run on a proposal: identify the value dimensions each party treats as fungible versus incommensurable; determine whether the proposed exchange is Pareto-improving in value-space, meaning no party loses on a dimension it ranks above the dimension gained; and flag every point where comparison fails and judgment must take over. Third, apply the procedure to three cases: (a) substituting broad community consent for individual signed consent in an emergency vaccine trial, where sponsors value speed and communities value collective deliberation; (b) sharing proprietary trial data with local health authorities in exchange for expedited recruitment, where the sponsor treats data control as inalienable; and (c) one proposal you will find yourself wanting to approve but that the procedure rejects—explain exactly why. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of value conflict it systematically misclassifies, and which disagreements it must hand back to political negotiation rather than arbitration. Keep the deliverable operational—checklists, ordered questions, and clear stop points—so a panelist without philosophical training can run it, but do not hide the judgment calls behind false precision.
