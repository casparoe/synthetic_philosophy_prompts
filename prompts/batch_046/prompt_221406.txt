For my independent study in philosophy of social choice, I have constructed an argument about judgment aggregation that begins with the discursive dilemma and ends with the claim that democratic deliberation on complex policy is conceptually incoherent. The conclusion strikes me as obviously false—we do deliberate collectively—yet every step feels defensible, so I need the exact point of failure.

Consider three rational experts evaluating a climate-policy argument with premises P, Q, and R, where P together with Q entail R. Expert A accepts P and Q but rejects R; Expert B accepts P and R but rejects Q; Expert C accepts Q and R but rejects P. By majority vote on each premise separately, the group accepts P, Q, and R, but also, by majority on the conclusion, rejects R—producing an inconsistent collective judgment.

Now extend this into the following chain:
(1) Any adequate judgment aggregation procedure must respect premise-based majority rule: on each premise, the group follows the majority of expert judgments.
(2) Any adequate procedure must also be deductively closed: if the group accepts premises that jointly entail a conclusion, it must accept that conclusion.
(3) Given that profiles like the one above are logically admissible, no procedure can satisfy both (1) and (2) simultaneously.
(4) Therefore, no adequate static aggregation procedure exists for multi-premise policy reasoning.
(5) If no adequate static procedure exists, then collective reasoning about such policies is structurally impossible—not merely unreliable, but conceptually incoherent.
(6) Collective policy reasoning clearly occurs; hence (5) is false.
(7) So one of (1) through (4) must be rejected.

To make the problem sharper, I have added two niche constraints that I think strengthen the impossibility: the procedure must be anonymous (invariant under relabeling of experts) and independent (the collective judgment on one premise depends only on individual judgments about that premise, not on judgments about others). With these in place, the result seems universal rather than occasional.

My suspicion is that a subtle slide occurs between (4) and (5): the failure of a perfect static mapping does not prove the impossibility of reasoning, but only forces us into dynamic, sequential, or domain-restricted procedures with feedback and revision. At the same time, I worry that rejecting (1) costs us democratic legitimacy, while rejecting (2) lets groups implicitly endorse contradictions, and relaxing anonymity or independence seems ad hoc.

Please identify the weakest step, explain why it initially looked like a genuine requirement rather than a camp preference, and price the alternatives precisely: what does it cost to abandon premise-majoritarianism, to abandon deductive closure, or to drop the anonymity and independence conditions I added? I am particularly interested in whether there is a hidden equivocation between an aggregation procedure as a one-shot function from judgment profiles to outputs and deliberation as an iterative process in which experts revise their views—because if that equivocation is present, the argument proves far more than it should. I want to keep the idea that collective reasoning is genuinely hard and constrained, not that it is impossible.
