I'm delivering the opening remarks at a retirement symposium for Professor Eleanor Vance, a legal historian whose career has been organized around Bernard Williams's thesis that moral revolutions—the end of dueling, of chattel slavery—are not primarily victories of argument but shifts in what confers honor and social standing. Her work engages Appiah's extensions of this view and resists explanations that reduce such changes to economic interest or explicit rational persuasion. The audience is split: specialists in moral philosophy and legal history on one side, and family members, university administrators, and former students with no philosophical training on the other. I have ten minutes. The philosophical claim must do structural work, not decorative work: because Vance rejects the rationalist model of moral change, I cannot praise her as having "defeated" rival accounts through superior reasoning, and I should avoid framing her career as a sequence of arguments won. Instead, the remarks need to explain to non-experts why her thesis matters—using a concrete image or anecdote that illustrates honor-driven change—while giving the specialists something precise to recognize: a genuine acknowledgment that her contribution lies in redescribing the mechanism of moral progress rather than in accumulating decisive proofs. Please write the remarks so that the constraint shapes the opening image, the praise offered, and the closing invitation to the day's discussion, and so that both halves of the room leave with a shared understanding of why this symposium is being held for her.
