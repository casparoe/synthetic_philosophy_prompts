I serve as the ethics and compliance reviewer for a regional agricultural and ecological management board, and I must evaluate between ten and fifteen intervention proposals each quarter that affect insect populations at scale. I have no neuroscience background, I have at most twenty minutes to reach a preliminary recommendation, and whatever I produce must be defensible to a board that includes both farmers and conservation officers. I need a reusable decision procedure that operationalizes the scale argument against low-confidence sentience: because insects are astronomically numerous and the prior probability that any given species possesses phenomenal consciousness is low but not negligible, even a tiny probability multiplied by a vast population can yield substantial expected moral weight, yet I must not treat this as license to block every proposal. The procedure should ask questions in a fixed order, specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, identify the exact point where the answer is no and the proposal stops, and clearly flag the stages where the question is one of values rather than empirical fact and must be handed upward to the board. It must include practical tests that do not assume mammalian analogies for consciousness: for instance, criteria that distinguish flexible nociceptive learning, motivational trade-off behavior, and sustained self-preservation from pure reflex, but framed so I can apply them using only the ecological and behavioral data that typically accompany a proposal. The procedure should also contain an explicit replaceability clause: if the intervention kills individuals who will be replaced within a generation by others with comparable welfare profiles, the moral calculus changes, and the procedure must tell me how to factor that in without requiring me to count individual insects. I want the hard cases marked honestly: where the empirical inputs are missing, where two reasonable people could run the same steps and reach opposite conclusions, and where the procedure is likely to misfire because of hidden value commitments. Then apply the full procedure to three proposals. First, licensing a broad-spectrum neonicotinoid for use over an agricultural zone estimated to contain ten trillion insects, where the compound causes navigational disorientation rather than rapid death, and where my preliminary view is that the board should approve it because crop loss is severe; I want to see if the procedure forces a recommendation I will not like. Second, authorizing the aerial distribution of a slow-acting fungal biopesticide against an invasive beetle swarm that threatens native forests, where mortality takes seventy-two hours and involves progressive paralysis; here I am inclined to approve but want the procedure to check whether my inclination survives the steps. Third, funding a pilot program to install artificial shelter structures for ground-nesting bees during extreme heat events, where no killing is involved but resources are diverted from other conservation efforts; this should test where the procedure stops with a yes or hands the decision upward. Write everything in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if composing a detailed letter to a colleague who will use it as a manual. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, since I cannot verify them without access to a library, but do ground the distinctions in the actual philosophical debate about scale and consciousness rather than inventing terminology. Keep the conceptual framework intact while stripping it to what can be executed in twenty minutes.
