After a long argument with a friend last night about whether knowing how to perform a complex manual skill really reduces to knowing that certain propositions about biomechanics and sequencing are true, I want to move past the usual cyclist and pianist examples and engineer something more precise. Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. Design a realistic, one-paragraph scenario set in a clinical workshop or craft environment—not science fiction—in which an agent possesses exhaustive propositional knowledge about how to execute a complex embodied skill (all relevant knowing-that concerning muscle sequencing, error-correction thresholds, and real-time feedback loops) yet is systematically and completely unable to perform the skill, where the failure is not explained by physical disability, lack of practice, anxiety, or the absence of some hidden propositional premise that the agent has simply failed to articulate. The skill should depend on integrated proprioceptive adjustment that resists full declarative encoding. After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it meets the specification: enumerate the agent’s propositional knowledge, specify exactly which practical capacity remains absent, and show why an intellectualist reduction of knowing-how to knowing-that cannot reconstruct the missing ability from the present knowledge. Then explain how an anti-reductionist account makes natural sense of the separation, identify the precise structural feature of the case—the one that, if removed, collapses the distinction back into a standard practice-effect example—that is doing the philosophical work, and explain what happens to the argument if that feature is eliminated. Finally, present the reductionist’s strongest available reply, such as an appeal to tacit premises or decompositional sub-skills, and show why that reply fails given the case exactly as constructed.
