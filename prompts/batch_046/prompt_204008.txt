I'm designing a diagnostic taxonomy for an applied-ethics discussion on whether everyday conventions—tipping in service economies, queuing at transit stops, and obeying pedestrian signals—generate genuine moral obligations, with particular attention to temporary visitors who may be ignorant of local practice or come from cultures with contradictory norms. The instrument should sort respondents through concrete scenarios rather than theoretical labels: for instance, a traveler in Japan where offering a gratuity can cause social offense rather than express appreciation; a tourist in Berlin encountering an empty street with a red pedestrian signal; and a foreign student in London encountering an unwritten spacing rule in a tube queue. Each branch must split on a substantive claim—whether the convention solves a coordination problem with tangible harm, whether the visitor's ignorance excuses non-compliance, whether local practice carries intrinsic moral weight independent of outcomes—so that a respondent can navigate the tree without prior knowledge of the named positions.

The taxonomy should be built from the logical space of answers rather than from a catalog of authors: begin with the questions, then locate the actual positions as leaves, noting any unoccupied combinations and briefly diagnosing why they are empty, whether incoherent, unstable, or simply unexplored. The required leaves are: a strict universalist position that denies conventional obligations altogether; a harm-selective position binding visitors only when breach causes direct disruption; a full conventionalist position demanding immediate compliance regardless of knowledge; and a grace-period position imposing obligations only after a reasonable learning interval. Please flag any pair of leaves that these diagnostic questions cannot genuinely separate, and ensure the tree can accommodate the specific tension that in some cultures the very act that counts as compliance elsewhere—such as tipping—functions as a violation of local dignity norms.
