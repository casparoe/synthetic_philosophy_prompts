Write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—call them M and N—debating whether Kierkegaard’s Abraham, as presented through the pseudonymous Johannes de Silentio in Fear and Trembling, genuinely exemplifies a teleological suspension of the ethical, or whether that framing collapses into a contradiction that undermines both ethics and faith. M should defend the knight of faith: Abraham suspends the universal ethical prohibition against killing his child not by annihilating the universal, but by entering a paradoxical relation to the absolute—his direct, singular duty to God—such that the ethical is placed in abeyance in view of a higher telos that the universal itself cannot comprehend, namely the restoration of Isaac through the absurd. N should resist by arguing that a genuinely teleological suspension must be rationally ordered and, in principle, communicable as a hierarchical ordering of ends; yet Abraham’s absolute silence and isolation from Sarah, Isaac, and the ethical community show that the supposed “telos” is not a shared rational end but a private rupture that severs him from universal justification altogether. N should push further by injecting a comparative framing drawn from contemporary philosophy of epistemic injustice: ask whether Abraham’s refusal or inability to explain himself to the ethical community structurally resembles testimonial smothering, in which a speaker withholds testimony because hearers lack the conceptual resources to receive it without harm or distortion. If so, N should ask, is the knight of faith not genuinely beyond ethics but rather ethically damaged—trapped in an isolation that the universal correctly refuses to legitimize, and leaving a moral remainder that the happy return of Isaac cannot erase? M must respond directly to this comparison, not by dismissing it, but by clarifying whether the knight’s silence is an epistemic failure, a necessary condition of faith, or something else entirely; in particular, M should address whether the “return of Isaac” retroactively justifies the suspension or merely highlights that the ethical was never actually transcended, only temporarily violated. The dialogue should contain real back-and-forth: each speaker should answer the other’s specific objection, especially on whether the suspension is a hierarchical ordering of ends (teleological in the classical sense) or a radical exception that exposes the limits of all ethical universalization. Conclude without resolving the disagreement, but with both sides stating precisely where they stand—whether Abraham remains within the ethical in a transformed sense, or whether he stands outside it in a way that makes faith either incommunicable or indistinguishable from madness.
