As a retired physicist who has recently begun studying political philosophy, I have become interested in how institutional rules encode unstated assumptions about agency and membership. I would like your help reconstructing the implicit philosophical commitments of a specific, somewhat unusual electoral design.

Consider the charter of a hypothetical but realistic mid-sized municipality—call it the Riverside Protocol—that institutes three rules simultaneously for all local elections. First, the voting age is lowered to sixteen. Second, permanent residents who are not citizens are granted full voting rights in these local elections. Third, voting is made compulsory for citizens aged eighteen and older, enforced by a modest monetary fine for abstention. The asymmetry lies in the enforcement: citizens aged sixteen and seventeen must cast a ballot but face no penalty if they do not, and non-citizen residents—though fully enfranchised—are entirely exempt from both the mandate and the sanction.

This produces a puzzling institutional pattern. All three groups—minor citizens, adult citizens, and resident non-citizens—possess identical ballot access, yet only one subgroup bears a compulsory obligation backed by coercion. The boundary conditions seem philosophically uneven; if democratic participation is a civic duty, one might expect it to attach to the franchise itself, and if it is an optional privilege, the penalty should be absent altogether.

Please do the following. First, reconstruct the implicit theory of democratic membership, political obligation, and moral agency that renders this specific combination of rules intelligible. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely happens to fit well with it. Identify which rival conceptions of citizenship, belonging, or stakeholder rights are quietly excluded by these boundary conditions, and assess whether the three rules are internally coherent or whether they presuppose contradictory views of the voter.

Second, suppose we replaced the underlying theory with a straightforward stakeholder-equality model: anyone whose interests are affected by municipal policy has an equal claim to electoral influence and an equal obligation to participate, with sanctions applied uniformly across all enfranchised residents regardless of age or citizenship status. Describe precisely which institutional features of the Riverside Protocol would have to change, and which philosophical assumptions about maturity, national belonging, and state coercion would be abandoned or revised.

Present your analysis in polished prose. Then provide a brief structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "reconstructed_theory": a concise statement of the implicit view of democratic membership and obligation
- "strict_presuppositions": an array of commitments the practice strictly requires
- "merely_compatible": an array of commitments that fit but are not required
- "excluded_rivals": an array of rival theories or principles this design rules out
- "coherence_assessment": a brief verdict on internal consistency
- "replacement_changes": a list of the institutional and philosophical changes required under the stakeholder-equality model
