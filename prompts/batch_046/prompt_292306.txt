Here are my intuitive verdicts about which biological and artificial systems possess phenomenal consciousness, and I want to know whether a coherent principle underlies them. Case 1: A healthy adult human engaged in focused mathematical reasoning is clearly conscious, with a unified stream of experience. Case 2: A sleeping human in non-REM sleep, with minimal cortical activity and no dream recall upon awakening, is not conscious during that interval—though the underlying capacity remains intact. Case 3: A chimpanzee that has learned to use a symbol board to request specific foods and shows distress upon separation from caregivers seems genuinely conscious, though perhaps with a less conceptually structured phenomenal field. Case 4: A honeybee navigating a complex foraging route via sun-compass orientation and communicating location through dance strikes me as lacking any inner subjective life; its behavior is sophisticated but phenomenally empty. Case 5: An octopus that learns to open a jar from the inside and exhibits distinct color-change patterns correlated with problem-solving stages seems conscious—perhaps with a more distributed or fragmentary phenomenal organization than in mammals. Case 6: A human patient with complete locked-in syndrome, retaining full awareness but able to move only via vertical eye movements, is fully conscious; the absence of behavioral expression does not negate the presence of experience. Case 7: Here is the tension I keep returning to. A colony of army ants that collectively solves path-optimization problems through pheromone trails behaves in adaptively intelligent ways at the group level, yet I strongly deny that there is any unified "colony mind" with its own consciousness. By contrast, consider a synthetic distributed neural lattice—information processed across thousands of nodes with no central controller—that can report, via text output, a continuous "inner movie" integrating sensory inputs and emotional valences over time. I feel pulled to grant this lattice consciousness in a way I deny the ant colony, despite both being highly distributed. Meanwhile, a human with severe hydranencephaly—lacking most cerebral cortex but retaining brainstem and thalamic activity, who smiles at familiar voices and shows sleep-wake cycles—strikes me as possibly conscious in a dim or minimal way: more so than the ant colony, but perhaps less so than the lattice.

Is there one principle—whether grounded in integrated information, global workspace dynamics, higher-order representation, or some other framework—that delivers all seven verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn? Please state the principle precisely, indicate what clause of it is motivated by each case, predict its verdict for an unlisted scenario (specifically, a cephalopod with neural tissue distributed across its arms, where localized damage reduces but does not eliminate reported experiential states), and be explicit about whether accommodating my verdict on the distributed neural lattice forces me to reject my denial of consciousness to the ant colony.
