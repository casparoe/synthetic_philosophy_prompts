I was discussing normative ethics with two friends from my rationalist study group after we'd worked through the standard literature on acts, omissions, and the doctrine of double effect. The conversation turned to a scenario that one of them, Jordan, had been analyzing: a physician designs and activates an automated triage algorithm that, without any further human intervention, continuously diverts life-saving resources away from patients carrying a specific genetic marker and toward patients with better prognoses. Once running, the system operates independently; the physician is not physically present when any particular excluded patient dies. Jordan argued that this is morally equivalent to actively euthanizing those patients, because the physician intentionally constructed the precise causal chain that kills them, and neither temporal distance nor the absence of a direct physical motion dissolves the agent's causal responsibility. Casey pushed back, saying that Jordan was collapsing a meaningful distinction between doing and allowing in a way that generates an unacceptable reductio: by that reasoning, a legislator who sets funding priorities that predictably cause deaths is actively murdering those who lose coverage, since she too encoded the exclusion into a system that then runs on its own. Jordan replied that there is a relevant structural difference between diffuse budgetary causation, which is mediated through countless other agents and unpredictable market dynamics, and this algorithm, where the physician specifically wrote the exclusion criterion, set the trigger conditions, and activated the system, making the death a foreseeable and intended consequence of her design choice rather than a side effect of general resource scarcity. Casey then pressed a baseline objection: if Jordan treats shutting down the algorithm as morally different from letting it run—claiming that stopping it merely removes an artificial threat—then Jordan must be smuggling in a normative baseline of "natural" survival that she hasn't justified. After all, once the algorithm is institutionalized, it becomes part of the background conditions, so turning it off actively deprives the patients it was saving. Jordan said that stopping removes a lethal intervention she introduced, but Casey countered that this just pushes the baseline problem back one step. They asked me to referee, and I genuinely couldn't determine where the programming act ends and the omission begins.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point. First, is Jordan correct that encoding an automatic exclusion is morally on par with direct physical disconnection, or does Casey's distinction between constructing a causal pathway and physically intervening at the moment of death hold water? Second, does Casey's overgeneralization objection succeed, or does Jordan's emphasis on specific encoding, automatic triggering, and absence of mediating agents successfully limit the scope to direct structural killing? Third, does Jordan's reply about shutting down the algorithm survive Casey's baseline challenge, or does the institutionalization of the system genuinely shift the moral evaluation of deactivation? Finally, identify what both are missing: for instance, might the moral relevance turn not solely on causal structure but on whether the agent retains ongoing oversight capacity, or on whether the affected patients could have consented to the algorithmic rule ex ante? Give me a clear verdict on who is more right overall, and explain whether there is a principled way to distinguish programming an exclusion from setting a general policy, without simply splitting the difference.
