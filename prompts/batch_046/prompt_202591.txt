I'm drafting a fellowship application for a three-month project in philosophical decision theory. The concept I'm working with is Good's theorem—the result that a rational agent should always accept cost-free information before making a decision, provided the information might influence the choice. In the standard literature, the theorem is discussed within single-agent expected utility models where values are cardinal and commensurable. But I'm interested in a conceptual gap that hasn't been systematically addressed: what happens when we apply Good's reasoning to moral decisions structured by deep value pluralism, where the agent must choose between outcomes measured on incommensurable scales—say, reducing present suffering versus safeguarding distant future autonomy? In such cases, acquiring more information doesn't clearly map onto a well-defined expectation, yet the intuitive pull of Good's argument—that one should never refuse free knowledge—seems to generate an unbounded, perhaps paralyzing, obligation to keep investigating rather than acting.

The proposal needs to do the following. First, state the precise question: does Good's theorem require a cardinal utility framework to hold, or can it be reformulated for plural-value contexts, and if it fails, what normative principle should govern information acquisition in moral decision-making? Second, explain why existing formal treatments leave this open—specifically, why treating value pluralism as mere uncertainty about a hidden cardinal scale misses the structural problem. Third, outline a conceptual method for the three-month scope, such as examining the monotonicity conditions in Good's proof, constructing formal counter-models with non-comparable value dimensions, and testing whether information value collapses to zero or diverges to infinity in these settings. Fourth, describe what a useful deliverable looks like: not just a negative result, but a decision framework that tells practitioners—say, policy advisors facing trade-offs between health and liberty—when further inquiry is rational and when it becomes a form of decision avoidance. Fifth, specify failure conditions: for instance, if every plural-value case reduces without loss to a single hidden utility, then the project has no distinct contribution. Please do not include a bibliography, citation list, or literature review by reference; I'll supply those separately. The tone should be formal and appropriate for an interdisciplinary fellowship committee in philosophy and decision sciences.
