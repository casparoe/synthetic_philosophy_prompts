Write the opening address for the dedication of the Civic Peace Archive in a city that has just transitioned from authoritarian rule to a fragile democratic settlement. The audience is mixed: constitutional lawyers, local residents with no philosophical training, and international observers. The occasion is not a victory celebration but the institutionalization of an uneasy ceasefire. Structure the speech—no more than 700 words—around Kant's distinction between a mere armistice and perpetual peace, and let that distinction do real structural work: argue that the archive must collect not only treaties but also legislative records of votes on war funding and military authorization, because Kant's preliminary articles treat a republican constitution with strict legislative control over executive war-making as a necessary condition of peace, not merely a domestic preference. Without this institutional check, stability is only a temporary truce. Make the argument accessible to non-specialists without reducing Kant to a slogan; do not cite page numbers, name specific commentators, or include a bibliography. Include one concrete hypothetical or historical illustration of an executive circumventing legislative war powers, paraphrased without quotation marks. End by defining what the archive would have to contain ten years from now for the audience to know that perpetual peace—not just pacification—had actually been achieved.
