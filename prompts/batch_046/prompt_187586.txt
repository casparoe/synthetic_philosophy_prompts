A colleague working in interspecies ethics applies what she calls the direct-duty boundary—the line separating beings to whom we owe direct obligations of non-harm from those protected only indirectly through ecological, instrumental, or human-interest considerations. Her rulings are consistent across the following cases. First, factory-farmed pigs fall inside the boundary: harming them for food requires justification comparable to harming a human being. Second, farmed octopuses also fall inside, on parallel grounds. Third, wild sardines caught by purse-seine nets fall outside; sustainability matters, but no direct duty applies to the fish themselves. Fourth, the Portuguese man o' war (Physalia physalis) falls outside, on the grounds that it is a colonial aggregate of zooids rather than a unified subject of experience. Fifth, both individual worker honeybees and the hive as a collective superorganism fall outside. Sixth, a human being in a permanent vegetative state—with intact brainstem reflexes but no cortical activity—falls inside. Seventh, a genetically engineered chicken that develops without brain tissue yet retains spinal-cord-mediated behavior falls outside. Eighth, a current large language model running inference falls outside.

Now consider a new case. Researchers have cultivated a cerebral organoid from human induced pluripotent stem cells. The tissue develops layered cortical architecture, exhibits sustained nociceptive signaling, and displays sleep-like slow-wave oscillations, but it has no sensory periphery, no motor outputs, no evolutionary history, and no social or ecological embedding. It is proposed for pharmaceutical toxicity screening in place of rodent models.

Predict whether my colleague will place this organoid inside or outside the direct-duty boundary. Lay out the distinct precise rules that jointly fit her prior verdicts—for instance, a neural-integration criterion, a relational-embeddedness criterion, a biological-organism criterion coupled with a human-status exception, or another coherent principle—and state what each predicts about the organoid. Give your best-guess verdict with reasons for preferring one rule, identify which prior case is the closest analog, and specify the further hypothetical case that would best distinguish the competing rules you have identified.
