I am drafting the final exam for our graduate seminar in welfare axiology and the ethics of suffering (twelve PhD candidates, 90 minutes, 20 marks total). I want one essay question that cannot be answered by reciting the standard literature on lexical negative utilitarianism, the incommensurability of pleasure and pain, or the repugnant conclusion. Instead, present candidates with a technically precise variant—call it interval-lexical threshold negative utilitarianism (ILTN)—in which suffering and pleasure are mapped onto separate psychometric interval scales with non-aligned zero-points, and lexical priority is defined not as ordinal dominance but as a scale-specific discontinuity condition: no accumulation of pleasure can bridge an interval gap that crosses the suffering-scale zero into positive suffering, yet cross-scale comparisons are permitted only via an external calibration function that is explicitly underdetermined.

The question should ask candidates to evaluate whether ILTN (i) avoids collapsing into ordinary negative utilitarianism on the small-benefit problem, (ii) resolves, relocates, or exacerbates the incommensurability objection between pleasure and pain, and (iii) generates a structurally novel asymmetry between the wrongness of creating suffering lives and the wrongness of failing to create happy lives—specifically, whether the interval-scale machinery makes the omission of a potential happy life worse, better, or incomparable relative to the commission of a suffering life.

Require candidates to identify the exact point at which the interval-scale machinery does independent philosophical work versus where it collapses back into familiar ordinal or cardinal formulations.

Give a model answer that outlines the five essential argumentative moves a first-class response must make. Provide a marking scheme with five must-hit points, the marks each carries (summing to 20), and for each point a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that costs marks. The difficulty should turn on identifying the crux—candidates who merely summarize lexical utilitarianism or rehearse the standard repugnant-conclusion dialectic should not earn more than a bare pass.
