In a graduate seminar on distributive justice, a colleague repeatedly closed objections by saying, 'Brute luck ought to be neutralized, but option luck must stand.' She used the line both to argue that innate differences in cognitive ability generate unjust advantages and to deny that people with expensive tastes have any claim to extra resources, yet she never specified the counterfactual baseline against which 'neutralization' was to be measured. I want to understand exactly what that one-liner could be claiming. Could you lay out the menu of distinct precise theses it might express—distinguishing, for example, (a) a purely causal claim about the origins of welfare differences, (b) a normative claim about the proper domain of egalitarian justice, (c) a metric claim identifying the specific counterfactual baseline (equality of resources, welfare, or capabilities) used to compute disadvantage, and (d) an institutional claim about which policies (taxation, compensation, restructuring) actually follow? For each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, a defensible but substantive commitment within luck egalitarianism, or actually false; and specify which version of the brute-luck/option-luck distinction—whether Dworkin's resource-based framing, Arneson's equal-opportunity version, Cohen's welfare-egalitarian variant, or another—it requires. Then say which of these readings actually supports her two uses (treating innate ability as an unjust advantage and dismissing expensive-taste claims), and which ones fail to do that work. In particular, does any plausible reading commit her to a specific counterfactual equality baseline, and can that baseline be defended without circularly presupposing the conclusion it is meant to justify? Finally, identify whether the slogan hides a slide from descriptive causation to normative entitlement, and if so, where exactly the inference breaks down.
