I am delivering the retirement toast for Dr. Miriam Chen, who spent thirty-eight years directing the university’s oral history archive, and I need the speech to work for two audiences seated at the same table: her colleagues in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, who will recognize any precise framing of selfhood, and her family and oldest friends, for whom metaphysical vocabulary is unfamiliar terrain. The occasion demands that I honor a life of work without falling back on either the fiction of a hidden inner essence that she “discovered” through the archive, or the opposite fiction that she is now permanently detachable, “watching over us” as a completed soul. Instead, I want the toast to take seriously the idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity—not as decorative language, but as a structural constraint: Miriam is not a substance that accumulated stories, nor is she reducible to the recordings she preserved, yet something genuinely weight-bearing happened in that room for nearly four decades.

To make the idea do real work rather than serve as ornament, I want the speech to introduce, gently and without lecturing, a related but less familiar concept: that a person who spends a career holding other people’s narratives becomes a kind of distributed center of gravity, bearing what I will call narrative counterweights—the deliberate silences, redactions, and withheld testimonies—that kept thousands of other selves from collapsing into hers. The family should understand why her retirement feels like a shift in weight across a network, not merely an absence at a desk; the philosophers should see that this is not a mystical claim but an extension of the gravity metaphor into institutional ethics, where the stability of others’ self-narratives depended partly on her refusal to become the sole center. Please write a five-minute toast that lets both groups feel the same moment land, that avoids any language treating the self as a hidden core or as an immortal residue, and that closes with something the family can repeat later without feeling they have betrayed her materialist convictions.
