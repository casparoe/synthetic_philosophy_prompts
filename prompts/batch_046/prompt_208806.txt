I'm a high school student serving on my school's peer ethics board, which meets twice a week to review anonymous reports of student misconduct and must issue either a restorative recommendation or an explicit decline within fifteen minutes. I want a reusable decision procedure grounded in Benjamin Franklin's project of moral perfection—specifically his rotating-virtue journal, his claim that moral improvement is asymptotic and never fully achieved, and his observation that vigorously pursuing one virtue can generate a characteristic counter-vice—but adapted to our board's constraints: we never recommend punishment, only restorative actions or a stop-decision. Design an ordered checklist my team can run: the sequence of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the precise stop-points where we issue a recommendation, and the explicit markers where judgment cannot be eliminated and the decision must be handed to a person rather than resolved by procedure. Include at the relevant step a "shadow-vice" test that asks whether applying Franklinian Justice or Industry to this case risks producing false humility, excessive order, or another specific counter-vice in the offender or the board itself. Then apply the procedure to three cases: a student who copied homework and then confessed; a group project where one member contributed nothing but received equal credit; and a situation where the only available restorative action might actually damage the offender's character development—an answer the board will dislike but must face. Take a definite, committed position on whether Franklin's asymptotic view obligates our board always to prefer restorative recommendations over none, defend that choice with explicit reasoning rather than laying out options, and state precisely what kinds of cases this procedure cannot settle.
