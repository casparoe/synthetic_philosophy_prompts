I want to understand this through the three main interpretations historians offer for revolutionary dynamics: class struggle, in which the bourgeois leadership eventually crushes its popular allies; ideology, in which Enlightenment commitments to virtue and popular sovereignty generate an internal logic of ever-escalating purity; and contingency, in which the outcome depended on particular accidents like Robespierre's illness, the failure of the Paris Commune to rally, and Saint-Just's interrupted defense. So please analyze the fall of Robespierre through each of the three lenses, telling me what each predicts about why he fell, which details of the episode—such as the rapid reversal of deputies who had voted for Danton's death only months earlier—it explains well, and where it forces the facts into an ill-fitting shape. Then tell me what this case shows about each theory: does it confirm that revolutions necessarily destroy their founders, or does it reveal that such collapses only occur under a particular institutional vacuum? Finally, be explicit about where these frameworks fall silent—especially regarding the personal opportunism of the Thermidorians, which may not reduce to class interest, ideological logic, or random chance—and say whether any of them can genuinely accommodate both the speed of the reversal and the fact that the Terror's machinery survived its architect long enough to execute him.
