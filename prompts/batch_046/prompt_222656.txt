I'm at work on a novel that turns on a peculiar epistemic loop: a psychiatrist publishes a memoir describing a syndrome she names reflected melancholia, a cluster of dissociative symptoms observed only in patients who have read the memoir itself and come to recognize their own suffering in its taxonomy. The premise feels like a fictional intensification of Ian Hacking's idea that psychiatric classifications generate looping effects, altering the very human kinds they purport to describe.

Before I commit to the plot, I want to understand precisely how Hacking's framework compares with Michel Foucault's account of the clinical gaze and biopower — specifically, regarding the power dynamics between classifier and classified. Does Hacking's emphasis on interactive kinds capture a reciprocity that Foucault's more top-down, institutional model tends to obscure? Furthermore, if the loop in my story is mediated not by a hospital or diagnostic manual but by literary representation — readers recognizing themselves in a narrative — does that expose a genuine gap in Hacking's account, or does it merely relocate institutional authority into the domain of aesthetic reception? I am not asking for a bibliography or citations; I want a philosophical comparison that takes both thinkers seriously, assesses whether my narrative premise is conceptually coherent, and indicates what, if anything, this fictional scenario adds to the debate that standard argumentation misses.
