Plan a single fifty-minute session that teaches heterophenomenology—treating first-person reports as texts describing a heterophenomenological world rather than as direct access to consciousness—to an interdisciplinary group of phenomenologists (familiar with Husserlian epoché) and cognitive scientists (who tend to treat introspection as noisy behavioral data). Do not explain the concept to me; design the teaching plan. First, lay out the dependency order: does the distinction between the heterophenomenological world and the actual world have to come before methodological neutrality about ontology, or vice versa, and why? Second, propose the one concrete carrying example—avoiding zombies and inverted-spectrum tropes—that should anchor the whole session: use a specific experimental scenario, such as a synesthete’s report during a color-naming task in which the subject says “the blue square hums,” and specify exactly what this example must possess (a report that sounds ontologically committed) and what it must lack (actual neuroimaging data that would invite reduction) in order to do the work. Third, map the predictable misconceptions: the phenomenologists will likely mistake this for bracketing with extra steps, and the scientists will likely take it to license dismissing reports as fiction; identify the precise point in your sequence where each misconception should be headed off. Fourth, provide one check-for-understanding question that requires applying the method to a novel report rather than defining it. Finally, state explicitly which part of the broader program—whether the multiple-drafts model, the intentional stance, or something else—you will refuse to explain in this session, and justify why leaving it out protects the coherence of the method being taught.
