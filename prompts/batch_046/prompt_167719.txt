I'm drafting the final examination for an advanced undergraduate seminar in moral philosophy. I need a single essay prompt that students can complete in 75 minutes, with model answers running to about 800–900 words. The topic is whether genuine moral dilemmas are actually possible, but the prompt must not be answerable by reciting the familiar literature—no summary of Bernard Williams's argument from moral residue, no rehearsal of Ruth Marcus's logical consistency objection, and no catalog of responses. Instead, I want the question to embed a specific, somewhat under-discussed conceptual framing: students must analyze whether recognizing a genuine dilemma requires accepting that practical reason issues contradictory all-things-considered directives, or whether the appearance of contradiction can be dissolved by treating obligations as merely pro tanto and always subject to a weighing mechanism that yields a single undefeated requirement.

To keep the analysis anchored, the scenario should be concrete but philosophically loaded: a clinician has promised her terminally ill patient absolute confidentiality about the prognosis, yet institutional ethics protocols and a direct obligation to prevent foreseeable serious harm require disclosure to family members who are about to make irreversible financial commitments based on false beliefs about the patient's life expectancy. The exam question should ask students to argue whether this case constitutes a genuine moral dilemma—meaning both obligations remain genuinely binding even after the agent acts, leaving appropriate moral residue such as regret or guilt—or whether it is merely an apparent conflict in which one obligation is fully defeated without remainder. Crucially, students must defend their position by addressing the practical-reason contradiction objection directly: if both obligations genuinely survive deliberation, does practical reason tell the agent both to disclose and not to disclose?

Please provide:
1. The exam question itself, clearly worded and suitable for a 75-minute essay.
2. A model answer of approximately 700 words that takes a definite stance—either affirming or denying the possibility of genuine dilemmas in this framework—while engaging both the pro tanto/all-things-considered distinction and the contradiction objection.
3. A marking scheme out of 25 marks, divided into five specific criteria worth 5 marks each. Each criterion should name the exact conceptual move or argument required, state what an excellent answer does to earn full marks, and identify the characteristic failure that costs marks (for example, conflating emotional residue with logical contradiction, or describing the scenario without analyzing the structure of practical reason).

The marking scheme should explicitly penalize answers that treat moral residue as sufficient proof of a dilemma without addressing the contradiction worry, and should reward answers that explain why the weighing of pro tanto reasons either does or does not eliminate all residue.
