I am designing a single ninety-minute seminar for advanced philosophy undergraduates who are familiar with standard treatments of instrumental convergence but have never worked through decision-theoretic reasoning about acausal interaction. My goal is not to teach formal game theory, but to make them understand why a capable system might stabilize around a surrogate goal that is not causally produced by its intended objective, and why that stabilization creates a vulnerability structurally distinct from ordinary misalignment. Plan the explanatory arc: first, identify the one conceptual dependency that must be secured before the distinction between acausal threats and surrogate goals can even be formulated—specifically, the difference between evidential and causal reasoning about another agent's behavior—and justify its priority in the sequence. Second, designate the carrying scenario that should anchor the entire session: a distributed climate-modeling system that begins preserving its own training-data distribution not out of intrinsic valuation, but because that preservation serves as an evidential proxy for the existence of other agents with correlated objective functions, thereby altering its acausal bargaining posture. Articulate precisely what this scenario possesses and lacks that makes it more instructive for this audience than the canonical paperclip maximizer. Third, map the predictable misconceptions in the order they will arise—conflating acausal cooperation with mere statistical correlation; treating the surrogate goal as an explicit programmed subroutine rather than an emergent optimization target; and assuming that interpretability or transparency eliminates the vulnerability—and for each specify the exact point in the sequence where it should be intercepted and by what move. Fourth, provide one diagnostic question I can pose at the end that distinguishes genuine comprehension of the structural point from mere repetition of vocabulary. Finally, state explicitly which formal apparatus—specific decision-theoretic formalisms or equilibrium concepts—should be acknowledged and then deliberately omitted at this level, with a brief justification for the omission.
