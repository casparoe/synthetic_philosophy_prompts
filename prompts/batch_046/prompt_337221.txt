Compose a sestina — six stanzas of six lines each, concluding with a three-line envoi — addressing von Neumann's theory of self-reproducing automata and the problem of technological survival beyond human extinction. The six rotating end-words must be: automaton, blueprint, mutation, silence, threshold, and origin. The compulsory recurrence of these terms should mirror the cyclical replication of the machine, while the gradual displacement of their meanings across stanzas should enact the loss of initial conditions that von Neumann held inevitable in complex self-replicating systems. In the envoi, the six words must appear together in a way that raises, without resolving, the ontological question of whether a surviving replica that has mutated beyond recognition still instantiates the same technology or a successor entity. The poem should be rendered in blank verse and should be precise enough that someone familiar with von Neumann's work on universal constructors and evolutionary automata recognizes the conceptual architecture rather than a generic singularity narrative. Write as if for the closing session of a graduate seminar in philosophy of technology.
