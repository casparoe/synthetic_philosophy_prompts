I hold the view that Derek Parfit's hitchhiker case reveals a genuine structural feature of rational agency — specifically, that an agent can maximize expected utility only by adopting a policy that, at the point of execution, is locally irrational, and that this is not merely weakness of will but a rational design feature. I am particularly convinced that this applies beyond the original scenario to sequential decision structures in which the counterpart can observe the agent's reasoning process but cannot observe the causal output of the agent's decision mechanism itself. What would count decisively against this position? I am not asking for the standard objections about the technological implausibility of perfect pre-commitment, nor about the evidential-versus-causal distinction alone. Rather, I want to know which formal results in dynamic choice theory or game-theoretic models of commitment would undermine it; which empirical findings about actual human commitment strategies and self-binding behavior would be genuinely damaging rather than merely illustrative; and whether there exists a principled distinction between rational irrationality and self-deception that can be maintained without collapsing the view into unfalsifiability. Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Be explicit about which objections defenders of the position would themselves concede as counting against them, and which they would dismiss. Finally, assess honestly whether retreating to an ideal-agent restriction — that this applies only to fully rational agents with perfect self-knowledge — saves the view or renders it irrelevant to actual practical reasoning, and whether that retreat is itself a problem for a thesis intended to illuminate real-world decision-making.
