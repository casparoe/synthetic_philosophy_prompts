I'm preparing an application for a six-month interdisciplinary research fellowship that sits between philosophy and intellectual history, and I want to pitch a project on the conceptual architecture of national continuity rather than an empirical history of any particular state. The core idea is to press on a gap I think is underexplored in how we apply the Hobsbawmian analysis of invented traditions and the Andersonian thesis about nations as imagined communities. Most discussions treat these as complementary descriptions of modern nationalism: traditions are invented recently, and the nation is an imagined community with modern origins. But I want to investigate a more specific tension—between what I will call "cultural retrofitting" (the fabrication of shared customs, rituals, and narratives that present a polity as ancient) and "institutional genealogy reconstruction" (the legal-administrative claim that a present state is the direct successor to a pre-modern entity, even when cultural identity has been radically discontinuous). The question I need framed precisely is: can the Hobsbawm-Anderson framework distinguish these two mechanisms of historical construction, and does the distinction matter for assessing the normative legitimacy of sovereignty claims based on institutional memory rather than cultural identity?

The existing answers, as I understand them, tend to collapse both mechanisms into a single story about the modernity of nations, and they rarely ask whether administrative continuity claims operate with a different logic—one that might survive even if cultural traditions are acknowledged as recent fabrications. A philosophical rather than empirical project can make progress here by analyzing the conditions under which a claim to institutional inheritance is coherent: what must be true about law, authority, and collective identity for a reconstructed bureaucracy to count as a continuation rather than a replacement? The deliverable should be a tractable framework—perhaps a set of conceptual criteria with borderline cases—that lets practitioners (political theorists, constitutional lawyers, historians of ideas) assess whether a given sovereignty claim rests on a defensible institutional lineage or on a retroactive administrative fiction, without requiring exhaustive archival research.

Please write the proposal as a one-page document with the following elements: a sharply stated research question that has a determinate answer; an explanation of why standard applications of the invention-of-tradition and imagined-communities literatures do not settle this specific question; the philosophical approach you will take—specifically, conceptual analysis of institutional continuity and authority rather than case-study history—and why that approach is preferable to the obvious empirical alternative; a description of what a successful result would look like (e.g., a typology of continuity claims) and what would count as failure (e.g., the distinction between cultural and administrative invention collapses entirely); and an honest assessment of scope for a single researcher over six months, including which parts of the argument are realistic and which risk being too ambitious. Do not include a bibliography, a literature review organized by citation, or requests for specific source verification; you may refer to positions and arguments by name, but please leave citation details for me to fill in later. Also, please avoid relying on highly obscure theories or specialized empirical facts that would require external lookup; instead, inject any necessary conceptual machinery—such as the distinction between cultural retrofitting and institutional genealogy reconstruction—directly into the framing, so the proposal remains self-contained.
