I've come to accept a strong reading of Kripke's skeptical solution: not merely that private mental states fail to determine meaning, but that there is no objective normative fact separating correct from incorrect rule-application in any system—human or artificial. If communal practice is all there is, and practice systematically underdetermines every specific case, then attributing a genuine "misunderstanding" of a rule to a large language model is as metaphysically groundless as attributing one in a private-language scenario; there is simply no further fact about which rule was intended. What would have to be true for this extension to fail? I want an honest map of its exposure: which formal results in learning theory or interpretability research presuppose a real gap between an intended computation and an executed one that survives skeptical dissolution; which philosophical repairs recover a thin but genuine standard of correctness without smuggling in mentalistic, Platonic, or causally privileged facts; and whether defenders of the skeptical solution can restrict its domain to private-language contexts in a principled, non-ad-hoc way that prevents it from swallowing public and artificial applications. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what committed skeptics would concede from what they would dismiss, and say whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—if every apparent counterexample can be absorbed by narrowing the scope of application—and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a thesis of this kind.
