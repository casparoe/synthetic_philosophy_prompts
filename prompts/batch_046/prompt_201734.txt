Following my recent appointment to the accreditation review panel for long-term palliative-care units—a role that became personally urgent when my mother entered hospice care last autumn—I have been examining the quantitative evaluation rubrics used to certify excellence in end-of-life provision. The rubric I am studying assigns sixty per cent of its score to the absence of reported physical and psychological distress, thirty per cent to the fulfilment of expressed patient preferences—that is, to the absence of unfulfilled craving or desire—and precisely zero per cent to any indicator of positive experiential or relational value: there is no metric for moments of joy, aesthetic engagement, narrative closure with family, or cognitive presence, and indeed any marked emotional intensity is coded administratively as a distress-risk factor to be minimised through sedation or environmental quietening. Curiously, the same accrediting body publishes ethical guidelines affirming that a good death honours the whole person, yet its scoring system treats tranquil absence as the sole constituent of well-being.

What philosophical theory of value—specifically, what account of well-being—does this institutional scoring practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the rubric necessarily commit to tranquilism, according to which only the absence of craving and suffering matters, or is it equally compatible with negative utilitarianism, preference-satisfaction theories framed negatively, or even with hedonism interpreted in a risk-averse register? Distinguish rigorously between what the practice strictly requires and what it merely accommodates; indicate whether it is philosophically underdetermined, such that rival theories could license the same weights. Then, assuming for argument's sake that a rival view is correct—one on which positive states, relational depth, or narrative completion are constitutive of well-being rather than optional extras—explain concretely what would have to change in the accreditation metrics, in staff-incentive structures, and in family-communication protocols for the institution to be genuinely guided by that rival view rather than by tranquil absence alone.

Please answer in British English, and avoid citing specific philosophers by name unless essential to the reconstruction; I am interested in the conceptual architecture, not in a bibliography.
