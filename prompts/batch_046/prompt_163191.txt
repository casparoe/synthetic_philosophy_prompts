In my graduate seminar on the philosophy of historiography, I have been pressing students on whether presentism is an inescapable feature of historical practice or merely a contingent failure of method. To sharpen the issue beyond the usual formulations, I want to propose a somewhat unorthodox distinction: call “normative presentism” the explicit judgment of past agents by contemporary moral standards, and call “categorial presentism” the deeper phenomenon in which the very conceptual taxonomy a historian deploys—terms such as “abuse,” “autonomy,” “exploitation,” or “consent”—is saturated with normative commitments that have no direct analogues in the past society being studied. With this framing in mind, I ask for your actual credence, expressed as a precise number between 0 and 1, that a sufficiently rigorous historian can escape both forms of presentism when reconstructing the moral economy of honor in early modern Europe—not merely by suppressing explicit moral judgment, but by operating entirely within a reconstructed indigenous conceptual scheme that achieves descriptive adequacy while remaining normatively neutral from our present standpoint.

Please decompose your credence into at least three sub-questions, each assigned a rough numerical probability with one line of justification. First, the likelihood that descriptive neutrality is conceptually possible given the theory-ladenness of historical categories and the hermeneutic circle. Second, the likelihood that a reconstructed indigenous scheme can achieve genuine descriptive adequacy without covert normative translation into modern vocabulary. Third, the likelihood that even if such neutrality is achieved at the archival level, the subsequent acts of selection, framing, and narrative construction necessarily reintroduce presentist evaluation. Indicate how these terms combine—whether multiplicatively, as a weighted average, or through some other structure—to yield your overall number, and identify which single term your final credence is most sensitive to. Finally, specify what kind of argument or hypothetical historical case study—without reference to specific texts you would need to look up—would most substantially shift your overall estimate, and in which direction.
