I was talking with my friends about whether teh state's response to the 1971 Attica prison uprising makes any sense once you actually apply punishment theory to it, not just wave at "law and order." You probably know the outline: inmates take over D-yard, hold correctional officers hostage, four days of negotiation, then Rockefeller sends in state troopers, 43 people dead including ten hostages, and in the end basically no one on the state side faces criminal charges despite pretty clear evidence of summary executions and post-storming torture.

My friend says it's an easy split—consequentialists can justify both the storming and the refusal to prosecute, since storming deters future prison uprisings and prosecuting troopers would destabilize prison labor relations and invite more chaos, while retributivists have to condemn both, because the dead hostages didn't deserve execution and the troopers who killed prisoners clearly deserved punishment regardless of social cost. But I want to push past that textbook framing. There's a less-discussed angle—call it instutional retributivism—which says punishment is owed not to the offender's individual desert but to the integrity of the legal institution itself. On this view, letting state agents kill with impunity isn't wrong mainly because those specific troopers "deserve" prison; it's wrong because the state forfeits its moral authority to punish anyone else if it exempts its own violence from teh same rules.

Can you walk through the Attica aftermath using both the standard lenses—desert-based retributivism and consequentialist deterrence/rehabilitation—and then through this institutional framing? Specifically: does the institutional view actually change the verdict on the lack of prosecution, or does it just redescribe the consequentialist worry about long-run legitimacy? What does the episode show about whether retributivism can even function when the punisher and the punished are the same institutional actor—does it collapse into consequentialism about state stability, or does it demand something like an independent prosecutorial body that didn't exist then? And is there a hidden consequentialist argument *for* prosecuting the troopers—maybe about compliance or institutional trust—that standard deterrence accounts miss, or is that just smuggling the institutional worry in through the back door? Keep it tied to the actual dynamics of the retaking and the official investigation's broad conclusions—no need for obscure archival details, just the common-knowledge outline. And I'm not asking which theory "wins"; I want to see exactly where each one is silent, forced to speak, or quietly borrowing from the other.
