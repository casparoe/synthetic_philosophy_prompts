I'm part of a long-running discussion group on historical sociology, and we've been deadlocked for months over what looks like a terminological quibble but behaves like a deep conceptual fracture. The case studies are familiar—the rapid abandonment of Mycenaean palace centers, the cessation of monument-building and dynastic inscription in the Classic Maya lowlands, and the deforestation, demographic contraction, and ritual transformation on Rapa Nui—yet every new radiocarbon sequence or settlement survey leaves both camps exactly where they stood. One side insists these are genuine collapses: coherent socio-political entities that failed, leaving discontinuities in governance, writing, and material culture. The other side reads the same evidence as dispersed adaptation—smaller, more resilient polities replacing hierarchical ones; populations relocating rather than vanishing; cultures persisting in modified form without the monumental apparatus that makes them visible to archaeologists. The arguments recycle, and no empirical discovery seems to adjudicate them.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate isn't empirical at all, but ontological: we're fighting over whether "society" is the kind of entity that can possess a fate—whether social holism is defensible, or whether we're really methodological individualists describing aggregate patterns of local choice that only look like failure from the wrong scale. But I'm unsure. It might instead be a hidden disagreement about continuity conditions—does persistence require political hierarchy, population density, literacy, or ritual form?—disguised as a metaphysical dispute. Or perhaps it's verbal: "collapse" means failure to the holists and mere transformation to the individualists, with no shared criterion for success or failure. I want an honest diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is—substantive, verbal, about standards of evidence, about which data count as relevant, or about bedrock intuitions that can't be argued for—supported by what each side actually treats as decisive. And given your diagnosis, tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal model of societal persistence, a conceptual distinction between failure and reorganization, or an argument that the question itself is ill-posed because it projects an entity—"the society"—that doesn't exist?
