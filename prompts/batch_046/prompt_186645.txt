Question: In his unfinished 1958 work The Computer and the Brain, von Neumann compared human neural architecture with the digital computer he had designed. He observed that individual neurons are slow, unreliable, and prone to failure, yet the brain computes with high reliability, whereas his EDVAC architecture required near-perfect reliability from every vacuum tube. Explain von Neumann’s account of how the brain achieves reliability despite unreliable components, and address whether he treated this difference as merely implementation—noisy analog hardware running the same logical programs—or as evidence for a fundamentally different kind of computation. In your answer, discuss his remarks on all-or-none neural firing, statistical aggregation across populations, and whether formal logic applies directly to neural processes.

Student answer: Von Neumann argued that the brain is essentially a digital computer implemented in poor biological hardware. In The Computer and the Brain he notes that neurons fire in an all-or-none fashion, which is basically a binary logical operation like the on/off states of vacuum tubes. Because single neurons are noisy and misfire often, the brain compensates by running massive parallel arrays and averaging results across populations—using statistical error-correction to preserve binary information. So mental computation is, at bottom, the same serial, symbol-manipulating logic that an EDVAC executes; the brain just needs far more redundancy because its parts are worse. Von Neumann therefore endorsed the McCulloch-Pitts model of neural networks as formal logical devices, and he concluded that once we understand the statistical noise-correction rules, human cognition is fully reducible to deterministic digital computation. The only real difference between brain and machine is scale and component quality.
