I am preparing a presentation for my high school philosophy seminar on Immanuel Kant's 1795 essay Perpetual Peace: A Philosophical Sketch. During a recent classroom debate about whether formal international institutions such as the United Nations are strictly necessary, a classmate invoked the slogan "nature guarantees perpetual peace" to argue that commercial self-interest and human inclination will eventually eliminate war without deliberate institutional design. The teacher noted that the line comes from Kant's First Supplement, but I want to understand the precise logical and causal claims hiding inside the sentence before I assess whether it actually supports my classmate's conclusion.

Could you separate the slogan into the distinct precise theses it might be expressing? For each interpretation, specify whether it amounts to a descriptive claim about historical inevitability, a teleological claim attributing purpose to nature, a mechanistic claim about economic interdependence and republican government, or a modal claim about what is possible rather than assured. Indicate which readings are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible within Kant's framework, which are false or unsupported, and—crucially—which one my classmate needs for his argument that treaties are redundant. I am especially interested in whether any reading requires treating nature as an intentional agent, whether any depend specifically on the spread of republican constitutions or on what Kant calls the spirit of commerce, and whether the phrase collapses into the weaker claim that peace is achievable rather than guaranteed. Please present the interpretations as a menu rather than selecting a single correct reading, and for each state whether it genuinely belongs to Kant's argument or is a distortion produced by repeating the line outside its context.
