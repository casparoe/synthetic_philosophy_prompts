I have just finished a long argument with a friend about Newcomb's problem, and I find myself drawn to a particular version of the one-box position. Rather than appealing to standard evidential reasoning, I want to defend the claim that the scenario is best modelled as a fixed-point in epistemic game theory, in which the predictor's accuracy is not an exogenous constant but a direct function of the Kolmogorov complexity of the agent's decision algorithm relative to the predictor's own threshold. On this account, two-boxing is unstable not because it causally disrupts a past prediction, but because any algorithm that selects the two-box strategy is, by its very structure, more compressible and hence more predictable, which collapses the expected utility of defection. I want an honest assessment of what would count against this view. Which of its formal assumptions—say, the computability of the agent's source code, the deterministic link between compressibility and predictive accuracy, or the applicability of fixed-point reasoning—could in principle fail, and what would the formal model, or the world, have to look like in each case? I also want to know whether this position is genuinely distinct from causal decision theory and evidentialism, or whether it risks collapsing into one of them, and whether its defenders should concede any of these escape routes as live rather than merely logical possibilities. Finally, is the view well insulated, and is that insulation a virtue or a vice?
