As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am facing an editorial decision that feels philosophically loaded, though I have deliberately avoided naming the concept I think is at stake. Our publication received two independent submissions analyzing the same recently published behavioral-ecology study: one frames the findings through evolutionary game theory, the other through embodied cognition and niche construction. By every practical criterion I apply—factual accuracy, prose clarity, relevance to our readership, even the authors’ responsiveness to peer review—the two pieces sit in perfect equilibrium. I must publish exactly one in tomorrow’s feature; delay is impossible because the study’s embargo lifts at midnight. I genuinely lack any preference between them, yet resolving the matter by lot or by a pseudorandom generator feels like an abdication of editorial judgment rather than a rational conclusion. A colleague has argued that if every relevant reason is balanced, any selection is equally justified, but that answer strikes me as missing something about why my paralysis feels structurally significant rather than trivial. I am not seeking generic career advice. Rather, I want to understand whether there exists a coherent philosophical account of choice under perfect symmetry—one that can explain both why my hesitation is not mere indecision and whether an arbitrary resolution might still carry normative weight, particularly when the act of selecting one framework over the other will implicitly shape how our readers understand the study’s meaning. Can such an account distinguish meaningful agency from random selection when all comparative grounds have been exhausted?
