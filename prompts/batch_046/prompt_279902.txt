A post in a rationalist-adjacent forum summarized the modern behavioral-economics take on weakness of will roughly like this: hyperbolic discounting experiments show that akrasia is largely a myth. When people procrastinate or overeat, they are not violating some prior "better judgment"; they are consistently maximizing a steep present-biased utility curve, so there is no genuine conflict between judgment and desire—only time-inconsistent preferences. The author says this refutes Aristotle’s picture, in which akrasia involves knowing the good but being dragged away by appetite, because the data suggest agents never actually held a stable all-things-considered preference for the long-term option in the first place. The post then adds a more specific claim: willpower should be understood not as the suppression of desire, but as an intertemporal bargaining strategy in which your present self treats future selves as separate agents in a repeated prisoner’s dilemma, using precommitment devices to lock them into cooperation. From this, the author concludes that the entire normative language of "failing to do what you really want" should be replaced by a game-theoretic description of intra-personal coordination failure.

I am inclined to think the summary mixes together different levels of analysis—descriptive, normative, and strategic—but I am not sure where exactly it overreaches. Is this characterization fair to the hyperbolic-discounting literature and to philosophers who still defend a robust notion of akrasia? Please separate what is accurate, what is a standard simplification, and what misrepresents either the empirical results or the traditional philosophical view. In particular, does the intertemporal-prisoner’s-dilemma framing of precommitment actually follow from hyperbolic discounting models, or is it an independent theoretical addition? And does the conclusion—that normative talk of weakness of will should be abandoned—actually survive once the misrepresentations are corrected?
