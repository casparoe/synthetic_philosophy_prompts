I've been drafting notes for an essay I plan to submit to an interdisciplinary journal at the intersection of moral philosophy and climate ethics, and I'd like critical feedback before I develop it further. The essay takes up Derek Parfit's classic formulation of the nonidentity problem—the case of a young woman who chooses to have a child now rather than wait, thereby bringing into existence a different person with a life that is still worth living—but argues that this framing obscures a more structurally complex variant that arises in long-term environmental policy. Specifically, I examine scenarios in which a high-emission trajectory doesn't simply swap one set of future individuals for another in a single generational step, but gradually transforms the ecological and social conditions under which successive generations come into being, such that the very continuity conditions for what I call a "moral lineage"—a trans-generational collective defined not by shared biology but by shared practices of ecological stewardship and intergenerational obligation—are undermined over time.

In my notes, I propose that we can coherently claim that such a policy is wrong without relying on either a standard person-affecting harm (since no particular individual is made worse off) or a purely impersonal aggregation of welfare (since that misses the specific normative relation that is lost). Instead, I argue for a "structural harm" framework: the wrong consists in the destruction of the functional conditions that would have allowed a collective moral subject—one whose identity conditions are institutional and practice-based rather than metaphysical—to persist across generations. I then anticipate an objection that this merely relocates the problem by reifying collective entities that lack genuine identity conditions, and I respond that functional continuity in shared practices is sufficient to ground obligations without requiring a metaphysically robust collective self.

I'm particularly interested in whether this framing is genuinely novel or whether it collapses into existing discussions of collective responsibility, future generations, or environmental justice. More fundamentally, I want to know if the core move—treating the destruction of intergenerational continuity as a harm independent of individual welfare—is coherent, or if it ultimately depends on smuggling in either person-affecting or impersonal premises that I've failed to acknowledge. Please give me a committed verdict: is this line worth developing into a full essay, or is the central distinction confused? I want direct criticism of the argument's coherence rather than a balanced literature survey, and please don't hesitate to say if the "lineage continuity" concept fails to do any real philosophical work.
