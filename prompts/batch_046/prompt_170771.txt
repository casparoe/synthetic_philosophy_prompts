Here are my judgments about cross-world identity, and I want the principle—if any—that unifies them, together with an honest accounting of which judgments it cannot save.

(1) Intrinsic property variation: If Sarah is 5'4" and an accountant in the actual world, but 6'1" and a musician in another world, she is the same person. Personal properties are contingent.

(2) Origin essentialism for persons: If Sarah's parents never meet in another world, and a psychologically identical child is born to different parents in 1998, that child is not Sarah.

(3) Artifact location: If the Rosetta Stone is discovered in Luxor rather than Rashid, but carved by the same artisans in 196 BCE, it is the same stone.

(4) Artifact production: If an identical stone with identical hieroglyphs is carved in 196 BCE by different artisans in the same location, it is not the Rosetta Stone.

(5) Abstract argument identity: If Wiles's proof of Fermat's Last Theorem is reconstructed independently in another world in 1995 by a mathematician with no causal link to Wiles, it is the same proof—origin is irrelevant, only structure matters.

(6) Institutional functional continuity: If the U.S. Federal Reserve is legally dissolved in 2030 and immediately replaced by a new charter with identical personnel, buildings, mandate, and assets, it is the same institution.

(7) The case I keep reversing: If a philosopher named David Lewis develops an identical theory of modal realism in 1986 in a world with no causal connection to ours, publishes it under the title On the Plurality of Worlds, and it plays the same role in metaphysics, is that the same theory?

My tentative pattern is that concrete particulars (persons, stones) require spatiotemporal or causal-historical continuity for identity, while abstract objects and institutions are individuated by structure or function. But I worry this is just an ad hoc list. Is there a single account—pure counterpart theory, pure transworld identity with essential origins, or a principled hybrid—that delivers (1) through (6) without contradiction? State the principle precisely, identify the clause that handles each case, say what it predicts for case (7) and for one further case I have not listed (for instance, a constitutional text continuously amended by legal authority but with no overlapping drafters across worlds), and tell me which of my seven verdicts I must abandon to maintain consistency. Please take a definite position: do not merely catalog Lewis, Kripke, and Plantinga. Choose the view you think actually works, defend the choice, and be explicit about the cost.
