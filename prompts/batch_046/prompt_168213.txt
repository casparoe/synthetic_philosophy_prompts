I am attempting to isolate the precise principle that distinguishes lying from other false or misleading speech within an Augustinian framework that treats lying as categorically wrong but allows that not every false utterance is a lie. To do so, I have recorded my intuitive verdicts on concrete cases, yet I find them partially in tension and seek reflective equilibrium.

My verdicts are as follows.

A. A physician tells a terminal patient, "You are going to recover," knowing the prognosis is almost certainly fatal, in order to prevent despair that would hasten death. Verdict: impermissible; this is lying in the morally decisive sense.

B. A parent tells a young child that a broken favorite toy "went to be repaired by the toy doctor," knowing it will be discarded and replaced, but expecting the child will eventually learn the truth. Verdict: permissible; not a lie in the relevant sense.

C. Within a work of fiction, a character asserts, "I am the rightful king," with no expectation that the audience takes it as a real-world claim. Verdict: permissible; transparent framing removes it from the domain of lying.

D. A diplomat drafts treaty language that is genuinely ambiguous; she knows one party will interpret a clause falsely, but she never asserts either interpretation and both texts are formally compatible. Verdict: permissible; strategic ambiguity is not direct false assertion.

E. A witness in court answers "No" to the question "Did you see the defendant?" while silently qualifying to herself "in a manner that proves guilt," though she did see him. Verdict: impermissible; this feels structurally identical to A despite the internal reservation.

F. A historian writes that "the revolution was caused primarily by economic inequality," knowing the causal story is far more complex, in order to provide narrative clarity. Verdict: permissible; interpretive selection differs from direct falsehood.

G. A colleague writes "I have finished the report" when the final section remains in draft, understanding the phrase in this professional context to mean that the substantive analysis is complete and only formatting remains. Verdict: uncertain, but it strikes me as less wrong than A or E, despite being literally false.

Two tensions motivate repair. First, A and B both involve direct falsehoods spoken for the listener's emotional benefit, yet I judge A impermissible and B permissible. Second, E and D both involve manipulation of truth through contextual control, yet E feels like lying while D does not.

My tentative principle is that an utterance counts as a lie only when (i) its propositional content directly contradicts the speaker's belief, and (ii) there is no shared framing—fictional, conventional, or role-based—that renders the falsehood transparent or expected by the listener.
