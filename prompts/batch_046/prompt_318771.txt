As a journalist covering the philosophy of science and historiography, I am preparing a feature on whether historians can genuinely suspend the moral and epistemic standards of their own era when interpreting past societies. To ground the piece, I want to present the joint statement produced by an adversarial collaboration between two scholars who have long talked past each other. The first, Dr. Elena Vasquez, defends what she calls “standpoint temporal pluralism”: the claim that no unified “present” standpoint exists from which a historian writes, since every researcher occupies a distributed field of temporal perspectives shaped by successive generations of interpretation, and therefore framing presentism as “escaping one’s own time” is conceptually incoherent. The second, Dr. Marcus Chen, is a “normative contextualist” who grants that full escape is impossible but insists that historians have a rigorous methodological duty to bracket contemporary moral and scientific categories—what he terms “category colonization”—in order to recover past agents’ own frameworks of meaning.

Please draft the document they should have co-authored. Structure it with, first, a numbered section of claims both can sign: the formal distinction between diagnostic and normative anachronism; the mediating role of archives and the structural problem of “archival silence,” by which preservation choices themselves embed present power structures; and the shared acknowledgment that “recursive anachronism”—the risk that avoiding present categories forces adoption of obsolete intermediate categories that distort the past agent’s lived framework—is a live methodological hazard rather than a solved one. Second, provide a disagreements table in which Vasquez’s position is phrased so that she accepts its wording—specifically, that temporal pluralism dissolves the coherence of “escape” as a methodological goal—while Chen’s position is phrased so that he accepts its wording—specifically, that methodological bracketing remains a regulative ideal even within plural standpoints and that failure to pursue it amounts to interpretive negligence. Third, identify the cruxes: I suspect the central ones are (a) whether archival silence makes contextual recovery impossible in principle or merely difficult in practice, and (b) whether evaluating past moral agents requires shared normative premises that transcend temporal location. For each crux, present a concrete historiographical case—such as interpreting seventeenth-century alchemical practice, or assessing colonial administrative records—where both sides agree on the primary evidence but predict divergent interpretive outcomes, with each side’s prediction recorded in advance. Finally, conclude the document with a brief meta-note in which the collaboration states, for each major section, how confident it is in the claims made, and explicitly singles out the point—most likely whether standpoint pluralism actually dissolves the normative problem of presentism or merely relocates it within a distributed field—about which its confidence is lowest.
