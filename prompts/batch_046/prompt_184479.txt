After a long argument with a friend about George Eliot's humanism, I tried to formalize her position and found an argument that seems to defend her consistency but now collapses into an absurd expansion of moral obligation. Here is the chain as I reconstructed it.

(1) Eliot's mature view holds that moral obligation derives entirely from the expansion of human sympathy—what she describes as a feeling of oneness with others—and that any appeal to transcendent command or supernatural sanction is not merely false but morally dangerous, because it displaces responsibility onto an external authority.

(2) If moral obligation derives entirely from sympathy, then the boundary of obligation must track the boundary of actually achieved sympathy; we cannot be obligated to feel what we do not feel, nor can obligation extend further than the reach of our imaginative identification.

(3) But imaginative identification is structurally partial: it weakens with distance, cultural difference, and temporal separation, and it is largely absent toward beings that fall outside the circle of familiar human life.

(4) Therefore, on Eliot's own premises, our moral obligations should be intensely local, fading rapidly with distance and disappearing entirely for beings outside the reach of human sympathy.

(5) Yet Eliot clearly treated obligations to distant strangers as stringent—she argued for universal education and for the moral urgency of relieving distant suffering—and she regarded cruelty to animals as a genuine moral failure, not merely an indirect harm to human sensibility.

(6) So either she implicitly relies on a universal moral principle that operates independently of sympathy, in which case her rejection of religious universalism was incomplete, or she must accept that her obligations to distant others are no stronger than her obligations to nearby strangers, which contradicts her own practice.

(7) I tried to block this by saying sympathy can be cultivated through art and education until it becomes universal, but then the argument proves too much: if sympathy is so malleable, there is no principled reason it cannot be cultivated to include future generations, artificial minds, or ecological systems, and Eliot's humanism offers no criterion to exclude them except an arbitrary species-boundary that looks like the very parochialism she criticized in religious ethics.

Which step should I reject? I want to keep (1) and (5), but I feel the slip is somewhere between (2) and (4), or perhaps in the move to (7), and I cannot tell whether fixing it requires abandoning her anti-transcendentism or accepting a far more expansive, and perhaps incoherent, moral ontology. If the argument is valid and I must choose, which premise is least costly to give up, and what does surrendering it do to the rest of her view?
