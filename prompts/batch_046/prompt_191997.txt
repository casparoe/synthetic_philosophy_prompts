I'm in a graduate seminar on historiography and my study group has formalized a worry about microhistory that now looks like it proves far too much. We started with the familiar idea that history from below reveals local agency, but we pushed it into what feels like a reductio. Here is our chain, with each step marked:

(1) Every macrohistorical event—say, a fiscal reform or a mobilization order—is fully composed of discrete, locally-rational actions by individual agents: clerks interpreting instructions, soldiers deciding whether to march, villagers negotiating exemptions, each operating on reasons available only at their own scale.

(2) If a phenomenon is fully decomposable into parts whose causal dynamics are complete at their own scale, then explanations that invoke higher-level entities—states, ruling elites, social classes—are explanatorily redundant: they add no predictive or explanatory power not already present in the micro-level account.

(3) Sustained microhistorical work demonstrates exactly this decomposability: a royal decree, for instance, can be shown to depend on hundreds of village-level negotiations, each with its own logic, so that the decree’s effective cause is distributed across local interactions rather than centralized in the monarch’s will.

(4) Therefore, all explanations that cite elites or states as genuine causes are redundant.

(5) Redundancy, however, is not the worst problem. If the micro-level accounts are causally complete, then attributing distinct causal efficacy to the state is not merely unnecessary but false—like attributing digestion to an organ that plays no independent causal role in the process.

(6) So institutional and elite-centered history is systematically false, not just incomplete.

(7) Yet microhistory itself cannot operate without institutional concepts—archives, legal categories, bureaucratic records, the very notion of a tax roll or conscription list—to identify and organize those local actions. Without the state-level framework that defines what counts as a record, the micro-level data cannot even be located.

(8) Therefore, microhistory presupposes the reality and explanatory relevance of the very state-level entities that steps (4) through (6) declare false.

(9) This makes microhistory self-undermining: it demonstrates the non-existence of the entities it requires in order to exist.

We feel the slip happens somewhere between (2) and (5), or perhaps between (5) and (8), but we cannot name the precise flaw. Is it an equivocation on “cause” between causal contribution and explanatory salience? A modal slide from “no additional power is needed” to “no power is present”? Or a confusion between ontological dependence and explanatory dependence? Please tell us which step to reject, explain why that step looked plausible, and price the alternatives. In particular, we would like to keep both the idea that macro-explanations are often redundant (step 4) and the idea that microhistory depends on institutional frameworks (step 7). Can those be kept together without contradiction, and what does each standard position—methodological individualism, emergentism, and the view that institutions are constituted but not reducible—have to pay to maintain consistency?
