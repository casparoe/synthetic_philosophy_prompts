Draft the joint statement that an affective-essentialist and a representational-evaluativist about pain could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on pain asymbolia and the badness of pain. The affective-essentialist maintains that pain is bad solely in virtue of its unpleasant affective quality, and takes clinical asymbolia—patients who discriminate noxious stimuli and report pain yet deny any distress—to show that the sensory-discriminative core is intrinsically value-neutral. The representational-evaluativist maintains that pain essentially represents bodily damage under an intrinsic negative evaluation, so that its normative weight is carried by this representational content rather than by raw unpleasantness; on this view, asymbolia demonstrates a dissociation between evaluation and sensation, not that sensation lacks normative significance.

Structure the document as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims about the clinical profile of asymbolia, the sensory/affective distinction, and propositions both reject—for instance, that asymbolia proves pain is never bad, or that it is always bad independent of affect. Second, a disagreements table: for each side, the precise formulation of its view in wording that side would accept, the exact point of divergence on whether negative value is reducible to unpleasantness, and whether an asymbolic patient still undergoes a state that is genuinely bad for her. Third, identify the cruxes—especially the reducibility question and the normative status of damage-representation without affect—and for each crux specify a concrete hypothetical finding or intervention (e.g., selective suppression of unpleasantness without altering damage-representation, or intact affect coupled with abolished sensory discrimination) that both sides agree would bear on it, recording in advance what each side predicts. Finally, include a brief note stating whether any residual disagreement is substantive or partly verbal, identifying which part and why.
