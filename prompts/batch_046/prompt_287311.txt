Next month I defend the central chapter of my dissertation, which I began after my father's death was officially recognized as karoshi-related heart failure. I argue that the moral right to leisure has been systematically misframed as a distributive claim to non-working hours—exemplified by the regulatory ideal of the 35-hour week—when it should instead be reconceptualized as circadian autonomy: an inalienable authority over the temporal architecture of one's recovery, not merely a quantum of free time. From this I draw two counterintuitive conclusions. First, the Protestant work ethic survives not as theological asceticism but as a secular grammar of self-justification through productive exhaustion, which makes bounded-time regimes functionally compatible with overwork rather than opposed to it. Second, karoshi should not be classified as occupational accident or individual pathology, but as predictable structural failure of chronometric governance—specifically, the lethal gap between formal hour-limits and the hidden moral economy that demands constant availability.

My audience is precisely defined: a Weberian sociologist of religion who treats the Protestant ethic as a historically bounded formation specific to early-modern Calvinism; a Japanese labor-law scholar who advised on the post-2018 overtime-cap reforms and is protective of recent legal progress; and a welfare economist in the Sen/Nussbaum capabilities tradition who is skeptical of rights-talk that cannot be rendered as measurable functionings. I need a prep sheet, not a polished defense.

Please provide the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as each of these three would phrase them, with a brief reply suitable for a live seminar exchange, a clear note of what I should concede versus what I should hold, and—crucially—the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Order them by likely damage to my argument.

Specifically, I want:

1. The Weberian's challenge to my historical mechanism: that I am universalizing a specific theological formation without showing the institutional bridge to modern secular labor, particularly in East Asian corporate culture.

2. The capabilities economist's operationalization challenge: that circadian autonomy is metaphysically thick but empirically empty, and that my claim can be translated without loss into standard functioning-language (rest, play, bodily health), rendering my rights-rhetoric redundant.

3. The Japanese lawyer's empirical-progress objection: that post-2018 overtime caps and reporting mechanisms have measurably reduced recognized karoshi cases, so framing karoshi as structural homicide undermines the legal instruments we have built and risks licensing deregulation.

4. The hidden objection—the one I am hoping nobody asks—which turns my own framework against me: if circadian autonomy is truly inalienable, then mandatory vacation statutes, weekend-rest laws, and even the 35-hour boundary are paternalistic impositions on workers who might choose intense seasonal labor or creative surges; in other words, my view seems to hand the libertarian anti-regulation argument a philosophical veneer. I need this phrased as the lawyer or the economist would put it, with a reply that does not collapse into either pure libertarianism or pure paternalism.

For each, include the objection in the audience member's own voice; my concession; my hold; the reply; and the specific soft spot in that reply where I am most exposed. I am not asking for bibliography, citations, or reading lists; I need strategic preparation for a live Q&A.
