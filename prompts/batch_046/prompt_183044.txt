There's this weird thing in drone delivery regs that's been bugging me. The rules hold the licensed operator criminally negligent if the drone hits someone, even in full autonomous mode where the obstacle-avoidance netwrok made a split-second call using wind data the operator never saw. What's more, if investigators trace that call back to a weight update the manufactuer pushed the night before—something the operator couldn't possibly know—the operator still gets charged, while the company just pays a civil fine. But if a human pilot misreads wind and makes the same split-second call, it's only pilot error, and we charge them only if they were actually reckless. So what view of responsibility does this practice presuppose? Does it need the idea that holding the licence makes you responsible for everything that happens, or can a control-based view explain why the human pilot gets off easier? Is the practice even consistent with itself—treating algorithm updates differently from human mistakes? And if we replaced the implicit view with one that tracks real epistemic and causal control, wouldn't we have to move liability onto the manufactuer for those updates, or maybe ban adaptive autonomous modes altogether?
