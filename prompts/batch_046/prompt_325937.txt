After a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a friend about Douglas Hofstadter’s view that an “I” might persist across multiple brains, I want to formalize our disagreement as an adversarial collaboration. Please draft the joint statement that a committed Distributed Pattern Continuist and a committed Embodied First-Person Singularist could both sign.

The Distributed Pattern Continuist holds that selfhood is a high-level strange loop—a self-referential symbolic pattern whose persistence conditions are pattern-continuity and counterfactual dependency relations among instances, not the physical identity of one brain. The Embodied First-Person Singularist holds that an “I” requires a singular, continuous first-person perspective anchored in specific living neural dynamics and embodied affect; distributing the pattern across substrates produces at best simulacra, not genuine co-instantiation of one self.

Structure the document exactly as follows:

1. Numbered agreed-claims section. Include agreement on: the formal structure of recursive self-modeling through symbolic dynamics; the empirical possibility of maintaining high-level pattern similarity across partial neural replacement or simulation; the logical possibility of substrate-independent replication of such dynamics; and which familiar no-go arguments against naive duplication as personal survival both sides accept as ruling out unsophisticated versions of distributed selfhood but not their own refined positions. Explicitly separate any purely verbal disagreements—such as whether “same” versus “similar” is the right relation between pattern instances—from substantive ones, stating which part is verbal and why.

2. Disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, state the precise formulation that each side would accept for its own view, phrased so neither side misrepresents the other. Cover at least: (a) the necessary and sufficient conditions for an instance of the loop to count as the same “I”; (b) whether bidirectional causal coupling between two isomorphic brain-patterns creates one distributed self or two distinct ones; (c) the constitutive role of embodied proprioception and affective feedback.

3. Cruxes and predictive tests. Identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify the case, argument, or empirical finding both sides agree would bear on it, and record in advance each side’s prediction.

Include at least the following crux, rendered precisely: the Gradual Bifurcation Case, in which a brain is gradually replaced by synthetic analogues while an isomorphic symbolic dynamics is simultaneously seeded into a distant second brain, with both systems maintaining bidirectional causal coupling through a shared external interface, so that each system’s self-model includes representations of the other’s experiences. Address whether epistemic ownership is preserved, fragmented, or multiplied; whether counterfactual dependency graphs linking the two instances are sufficient for singular self-reference; and whether substrate-independent pattern isomorphism at the level of high-level symbolic dynamics—independent of low-level neural implementation—is sufficient for persistence, or whether specific biological continuity remains necessary.

Ensure that every formulation of each camp’s position is one its proponents would genuinely accept, not a strawman. If part of the dispute dissolves upon analysis—especially regarding whether “persistence” requires spatiotemporal continuity of a single token or only continuity of a type-level pattern—say so explicitly and explain why. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the argument should stand on its conceptual structure alone.
