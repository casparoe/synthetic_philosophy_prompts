Compare William James's doctrine of the will to believe with W.K. Clifford's ethics of belief through the lens of epistemic triage—the problem of distributing limited doxastic resources across hypotheses that vary in evidential strength and practical urgency. James allows belief in live, forced, and momentous options despite insufficient evidence, whereas Clifford demands that belief always be proportioned to evidence irrespective of stakes. Determine whether James's framework implicitly commits him to a selective form of triage that privileges practically urgent hypotheses, or whether it instead licenses an indiscriminate expansion of belief; and assess whether Clifford's universalist prohibition can accommodate triage consistently by treating resource allocation as purely practical without covertly importing epistemic priorities. Your answer should explain which view offers the more coherent account of how a rational agent should manage belief under conditions of scarcity and underdetermination. Finally, state precisely what argument or evidence would cause you to change your verdict about which view succeeds, and pinpoint the step in your reasoning that is most vulnerable to the objection that you have imposed a false dichotomy between practical urgency and evidential duty.
