I've come round to a slightly weird version of Ruth Chang's hard-choices view. Normally she says that when values are genuinely incommensurable — like artistic vocation versus parental duty — rational agency can create new reasons through commitment, so there is a rational answer after all, just not one that existed beforehand. I'm pushing that further: I think the commitment doesn't merely generate reasons inside the value space, it actually restrucutres the agent's practical identity in an irreversible, path-dependent way. So the 'resolution' isn't really resolving incommensurability; it's burying it under a historical trajectory that makes the alternative now literally unthinkable for that agent. That feels like it collapses value pluralism into a kind of contingent constructivism, which I'm not sure I like, but I can't shake it.

What would count against this whole package? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Not just the standard knock on Chang — that she smuggles in arbitrary will — but specifically things that break the link between commitment-creating-reasons and this path-dependence claim. Are there formal analogies in belief revision or personal-identity theory that show commitment can't be that transformative? Could someone argue that path dependence is actually compatible with there being a unique ex-post rational answer? And if defenders of the view just reinterpret every counterexample as 'bad commitment', has the position been insulated into unfalsifiability — and is that a problem for a normative view of this kind?

Also, please be straight with me: is the 'path dependence' framing just a muddled redescription of what Chang already says? I was arguing about this in the pub with my mates after a course on metaethics and I'm probably mixing things up. If the premise is confused or the task can't be done well, say so plainly instead of playing along.
