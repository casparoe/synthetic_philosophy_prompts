I serve on the constitutional review board of a long-established housing cooperative in Manchester, and we have become entangled in a peculiar dispute over our own governing charter. The document contains a standard amendment clause—Section Seven—which permits any article to be altered by a three-quarters vote of members present at a meeting for which quorum has been established. Nested within Section Seven, however, is a subordinate provision—paragraph B—which states that the voting threshold required to amend Section Seven itself may be lowered to a simple majority, but only after the members have passed a preliminary resolution declaring the existing threshold "manifestly obstructive to democratic self-correction."

Some members now propose to invoke paragraph B to reduce the threshold, then immediately employ that lowered threshold to repeal paragraph B altogether, thereby locking in the simple-majority rule for all future amendments to Section Seven. Others object that this sequence is self-defeating: if paragraph B is the very authority that licenses its own suspension, then deploying it to annihilate itself appears to undermine the legitimacy of the deployment. I am trying to determine whether there is a principled distinction between legitimately exercising a self-amending power and performing an illegitimate act of constitutional bootstrapping. Could you analyse the logical structure of this situation—without merely invoking familiar paradoxes by name—and advise whether the cooperative ought to recognise the proposed sequence as valid, or whether the self-reference involved renders it conceptually incoherent in a way that should be blocked?
