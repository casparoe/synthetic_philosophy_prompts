I've been tinkering with a position about citizen-state information relations that I'd call the 'epistemic friction' view. The idea is that democratic legitimacy doesn't depend on citizens being 'private' in the liberal sense, but on the state being forced to work with noisy, delayed, localy fragemented data about people — not because secrecy is a right, but because smooth, real-time data flows turn governence predictive and deterministic rather than responsive. German informational self-determination gets part of this but misframes it as an individual right; American openness culture pushes transparency in the wrong direction (government visibility, not citizen opacity); and Chinese social credit demonstrates precisely what happens when friction is eliminated — administration without ambiguity. So the claim is that citizens ought to maintain strategic opacity as a structural brake on state capacity, not as privacy for its own sake. Play it straight: what would actually count against this? I don't want generic privacy objections. Would empirical evidence that low-friction Nordic digital governence achieves democratic acountability just as well be decisive, or only awkward? If predictive policing turned out more accurate with fragemented data, would defenders have to concede the mechanism is wrong? Are there formal results in mechanism design or information economics showing that noise helps democratic oversight rather than hindering it? And does the view risk collapsing into standard liberal privacy theory, or into something closer to anarchism? Give me an honest assessment of how exposed this actually is — what its defenders should admit is a live threat, and whether the position is well-insulated to the point of being unfalsifiable.
