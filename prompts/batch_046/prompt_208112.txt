I've been trying to work out whether dreams are experiences that occur during sleep, or whether they are narratives and images constructed upon waking—roughly in the territory of the Malcolm-Dennett debate, but I'm approaching it through concrete case judgments rather than through theory. Here are my verdicts, and I want to know whether there's a coherent principle behind them and which ones I have to abandon.

Case 1: I fall asleep, have normal REM, and upon waking recall a continuous twenty-minute conversation with a deceased friend. There was no interaction with the environment during sleep. Verdict: this was not a real-time experience; it is a false memory or confabulated narrative assembled in the transition to wakefulness.

Case 2: A lucid dreamer, trained to use pre-arranged eye-movement signals during REM, receives a verbal prompt ("Are you seeing a red square?") and signals yes. Upon waking she confirms she was seeing red at that exact moment. Verdict: here there was a genuine experience occurring during sleep—real-time interaction proves consciousness was present and available for report.

Case 3: A sleepwalker drives several miles, stops at traffic lights, prepares and eats food, then is woken with complete amnesia for the episode. Security footage confirms complex, adaptive behavior. Verdict: perceptual and practical experience must have guided these actions, even without any memory trace; absence of recall is not absence of experience.

Case 4: A patient under deep sedation shows flat EEG and no REM, but upon emergence reports hearing the surgeon say "the garden is blooming" and feeling calm. The surgeon confirms saying this. Verdict: I lean toward saying there was no experience—only an auditory fragment incorporated into a false memory at the boundary of consciousness—because there was no neural signature of conscious integration.

Case 5: An experimental compound suppresses memory consolidation but leaves REM architecture intact. fMRI during sleep shows patterns matching waking imagination. The subject reports no dreams whatsoever, even with direct prompting. Verdict: she likely had experiences she cannot access; the absence of report reflects a storage failure, not a failure of consciousness itself.

Case 6: Someone wakes with a fully formed mathematical proof they have never studied. They have no sense of mental effort, reasoning steps, or "what-it's-like" quality; the result simply arrived. Verdict: this was not an experience—there was unconscious computation yielding an output, but no phenomenal consciousness.

The tension I cannot resolve: Case 3 says experience can exist without memory, and Case 5 says it can exist without report. But Case 4 denies experience despite an auditory trace being incorporated into a report, precisely because the neural state was wrong. Meanwhile Case 1 denies experience for a standard dream despite normal REM and a rich report. So my judgments seem to depend on some unstable mix of behavioral interaction, neural signature, and reportability, but I cannot state the rule consistently.

The case I keep revisiting: a person in deep non-REM slow-wave sleep is exposed to a familiar voice describing a beach. They do not wake, but in the morning they report a brief, vivid image of waves. EEG shows only slow-wave activity, no REM. Was this an experience that occurred during sleep, or a confabulated image assembled from the auditory cue at the moment of waking?

Is there a principle—perhaps drawing on distinctions between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, or on conditions for genuine reportability—that captures all six verdicts? If not, which verdict should I drop: Case 3, Case 4, or Case 5? And what does your best principle say about the beach-image case?
