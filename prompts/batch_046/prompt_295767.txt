I'm running a 90-minute workshop for twelve philosophy graduate students; design an in-class deliberation game that makes them feel the tension between epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness before either view is named. The framing: participants sit as an appeals board for an automated content-moderation system that must issue binary rulings—"graphic violence" or not—on user-uploaded images. The system outputs a continuous risk score from 0 to 1, but the board must decide each case by majority vote, with real small stakes: every participant receives ten tokens, unanimous correct rulings (scored later against a hidden dataset of twenty genuinely borderline images with expert disagreement) earn each player two bonus tokens, split decisions cost one token each, and anyone who publicly insists on a sharp cutoff at a specific decimal value risks losing a token if that cutoff is contradicted by the hidden data. Structure three rounds of five images each, with the borderline cases engineered so that adjacent scores (e.g., 0.42 versus 0.43) depict visually indistinguishable content, and include one round where two images share an identical score but receive opposite expert labels in the hidden set. The rules should be arranged so that participants first feel the pull of drawing a precise line—treating the ambiguity as mere ignorance of a hidden correct boundary—then, after the reveal shows contradictory expert judgments at the same score, feel the pull of accepting that different consistent precisifications are equally admissible and no single boundary is metaphysically correct. Provide the exact rules and token flow, the image-round design (what visual features must vary continuously across the boundary so instability is visceral, not abstract), how to stage the hidden-dataset reveal for maximum disorientation, a debrief that maps specific moments—premature convergence, the frustration at identical scores, the relief at abandoning a cutoff—onto the epistemicist's unknown sharp boundary versus the supervaluationist's admissible precisifications and rejection of bivalence, and be frank about which feature of the debate the activity cannot reproduce: the metaphysical status of semantic indeterminacy itself, which the facilitator must supply in closing. Also predict what will go wrong the first time—participants will likely collude on an arbitrary cutoff early to avoid social conflict—and say exactly how to interrupt that without breaking the game's force. Do not ask for readings or citations; the deliverable is the runnable activity, payoff scheme, and misconception map for the facilitator.
