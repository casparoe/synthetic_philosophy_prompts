I'm leading a discussion section in an upper-level philosophy of social science seminar next week, and the topic is whether culture can be a cause, using Weber's Protestant ethic thesis as the central case. The students are juniors and seniors who have read a summary of Weber but tend to treat the thesis as either a correlation claim—Calvinist regions happened to industrialize—or as a disguised materialist argument about work incentives. I want a teaching plan, not the explanation itself.

First, the dependency order: what conceptual distinction must be secure before Weber's argument even makes sense? I'm thinking the separation of value-rational action (Wertrationalität) from instrumental-rational action, plus the idea that explaining with subjective meanings is different from explaining with material interests, but I need to know which of these must come first and why. Second, the carrying example: should it be the transformation of the Beruf (calling) from a religious duty into a secular economic ethos, and if so, what exactly must this example display and what must it avoid displaying so that it carries both the interpretive and causal dimensions? Third, the misconception map in sequence: students will likely (a) reduce Weber to a correlation argument, (b) treat Calvinist doctrine as the sole cause of capitalism, (c) confuse explaining with values with morally justifying capitalism, and (d) think Weber is doing idealism rather than a specific kind of causal interpretation. At what point in the sequence should each be headed off? Fourth, the check for understanding: I was considering asking them to compare Weber's logic with Durkheim's explanation of suicide—would that reveal whether they've grasped the explanatory structure rather than just the historical narrative? Fifth, what should I explicitly leave out at this level and say so?

Please lay out the plan with the order, the example's required features, the misconception sequence with intervention points, the end-of-session check, and the omissions.
