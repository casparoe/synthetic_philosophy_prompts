Hey, I'm a novelist drafting a character study and I keep running into this human move I don't have a word for. A person has to choose between two paths that are structurally identical — same reward, same risk, same everything except the label — and they genuinely have no preference, so they flip a coin or just get pushed by circumstance. Then, years later, they've constructed this elaborate destiny-narrative around the path they took, citing tiny post-hoc details as if those were the real reasons, and if you imagine the coin landing the other way I'm convinced they'd have an equally convincing mirror-story for that one too. I've seen it with friends picking between grad programs that were basically twins; one definately weaves this whole career-myth about "always being drawn to X" even though X was just the random winner. Is there an established concept for this specific thing — the backward construction of a thick preference architecture after a genuinely preference-free choice? I know Buridan's ass is about being stuck before deciding, and cognitive dissonance is about easing tension after a hard call, but this feels different: there's no tension to ease, just a vacuum being filled with a synthetic origin story. Please act like an expert in philosophy of action, think step by step, and start with a quick brainstorm of related ideas before you answer. I want the carving, not a bare name: what's inside this pattern, what's outside it, and where does it bleed into ordinary rationalization or narrative identity work? Also tell me what would change your mind — maybe empirical studies showing people don't actually do this retroactive weaving, or proof that I'm just redescribing choice-supportive bias with fancier language — and point out where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Don't just list citations; I can't check them without web access anyway.
