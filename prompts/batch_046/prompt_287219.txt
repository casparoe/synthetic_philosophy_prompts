I am a retired experimental physicist who, after leaving the laboratory, has begun studying philosophy of mind in earnest through a small reading group composed largely of other retired natural scientists; our discussions tend to drift toward problems that feel structurally similar to measurement paradoxes in physics, though we lack formal training in the literature. Recently we have been examining epiphenomenalism—the view that conscious qualitative states are causally inert by-products of neural processes—and in particular what is often called the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if beliefs about one's own experiences are generated entirely by physical mechanisms that never interact with those experiences themselves, then such judgments appear to be formed in complete causal isolation from the very phenomena they claim to describe, leaving us with no evident reason to regard them as reliable. To test whether this difficulty can be escaped without abandoning the physical closure of the brain, one member of our group proposed a position she named "restricted interactionism," which I ask you to evaluate alongside standard epiphenomenalism. By restricted interactionism, I mean specifically the view that phenomenal properties do possess causal efficacy, but only within a sharply delimited domain: they may influence other phenomenal states and certain high-level cognitive judgments about experience, yet they remain entirely powerless with respect to lower-level neural dynamics, motor behavior, or the broader physical substrate. Please compare these two positions with regard to how each addresses, or fails to address, the paradox of phenomenal judgment. In particular, does granting phenomenal states causal access only to other mental items genuinely restore the epistemic reliability of our first-person reports, or does it merely relocate the gap to the boundary between the causally active mental domain and the physically implemented mechanisms that produce articulated judgments? Furthermore, I would like you to consider whether the restricted interactionist owes us a physically coherent account of how this selective causal boundary is maintained—perhaps analogized to a feedback loop in a control system that activates only above a certain threshold of organizational complexity—without that account either collapsing back into full interactionism or quietly reverting to epiphenomenalism at the implementation level.
