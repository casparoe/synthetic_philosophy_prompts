Provide an example of a conceptual tension in Karl Jaspers’s taxonomy of collective guilt that arises specifically at the intersection of political guilt and transgenerational inheritance, yet does not collapse into criminal liability distributed across time or into the universal metaphysical guilt shared by all humanity. The case should involve a concrete institutional arrangement—such as a constitutional reparative obligation or a state-sponsored acknowledgment—where citizens who never consented to, and are not direct material beneficiaries of, the original state action are nevertheless treated as the appropriate bearers of political responsibility. In constructing this scenario, clearly distinguish it from both ordinary intergenerational collective-action problems and from Jaspers’s own separation of moral from political guilt; then explain precisely why the configuration exposes a gap in the taxonomy rather than merely stretching one of its four established categories. The aim is not to argue for or against the legitimacy of such obligations, but to show where the conceptual machinery of collective guilt becomes indeterminate when the temporal and consent conditions that usually ground political responsibility are both absent.
