I've spent a lot of time trying to think through, on my own, whether consciousness could literally be a user illusion—not in the casual pop-science sense of "the brain tricks you," but as a serious architectural claim that what I call "me" is a simplified model the system builds for its own use, like a dashboard that hides the engine. Before I commit to any particular version of this idea, I need to know what it actually has to accomplish to be worth taking seriously.

Please write out the full set of desiderata for an adequate theory of consciousness as a user illusion. For each requirement, include: the specific phenomenon it must cover (for example, why the illusion feels present rather than absent, why it seems owned by exactly one subject, why belief does not dissolve it); the conceptual puzzle it must resolve (for instance, the transparency problem—if consciousness is an illusion, why can't I observe the machinery behind it?—or the binding problem as it applies specifically to a false unified self-model); and whether the requirement is genuinely non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp (materialist reductionism, functionalism, anti-reductionist resistance, and so on). Be explicit about any pair of desiderata that look jointly unsatisfiable—for example, if full transparency of the illusion to introspection conflicts with the claim that the illusion is constructed from hidden processes.

I also want you to build in and defend one specific framing that is not standard in the popular literature: the idea that maintaining the user-illusion draws on a kind of cognitive "error-correction budget." Make this a central desideratum with its own sub-requirements. The theory must specify how that budget is distributed across attention, working memory, and self-referential monitoring, and it must explain what happens when the budget is exceeded—without smuggling in a homunculus who manages the accounts, and without collapsing into behaviorism that denies the illusion's reality altogether.

Finally, I want a committed answer, not a neutral survey. Once you have laid out the criteria, take a definite position: assess whether representational interface theories, global-workspace self-models, or predictive-processing accounts come closest to satisfying the non-negotiable subset. Name which ones fail on which points, and say plainly whether you think any current framework actually meets the standard, or whether the list itself shows that "consciousness as user illusion" asks for more consistency than either the brain or philosophy can provide. Give your reasons directly—do not just list options and leave the choice to me.
