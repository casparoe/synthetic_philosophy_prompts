I am preparing the syllabus for a graduate seminar on the philosophy of translation, and I want to center the course on the limiting case in which poetic form is not decorative but constitutive of what the work says and does. The touchstone is Douglas Hofstadter's *Le Ton beau de Marot* and its central object, Clément Marot's short Renaissance poem addressed to a sick young woman—referred to in that discussion as *Ma mignonne*—where rhyme, meter, patterns of address, and the architecture of the verse carry as much interpretive weight as the propositional content about illness and consolation. I was debating with a colleague whether a theory that permits a translator to abandon the poem's fixed rhyme scheme and metrical structure can still claim to have translated the work, or whether it has merely produced a paraphrase with a decorative note appended; but we kept circling without a clear standard of adequacy. So I want you to articulate, from first principles, the desiderata that an adequate theory of formal-constraint poetic translation must satisfy in order to count as addressing this domain—not a scorecard of particular translations, but the evaluation criteria themselves, with the motivating phenomenon or case behind each one.

Please specify the requirements such a theory must meet: the phenomena it has to explain (for example, how rhyme scheme and meter contribute to semantic or affective structure rather than merely ornamenting it; how embedded wordplay, internal assonance, or initial-letter patterns function within the poem's economy of meaning; how the performative force of the courtly address—its tone of intimate sympathy—is preserved or transformed; and how a reader cognitively processes formal fidelity versus formal deviation in poetic reception); the distinctions it must respect (such as that between phonetic architecture and propositional content, between author-intended formal constraint and reader-reconstructed pattern, between translation proper and adaptive recreation, and between language-specific phonotactic properties and transferable structural relations); and the bodies of knowledge or logical constraints with which it must be consistent (for instance, cognitive-scientific findings about how readers process poetic meter and rhyme, the empirical fact that syllable structures and phoneme inventories differ across languages, and the logical point that some formal properties are necessarily non-transferable). For each desideratum, indicate whether it is non-negotiable—without which the theory fails to address the Marot-type case—or whether it expresses a preference of one camp (say, those who treat phonetic music as essential versus those who treat semantic transparency as paramount).

Crucially, examine whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable given structural differences between source and target languages, and if so, identify the precise conflict, say which requirement does the heaviest work in excluding rival theories, and note whether the unsatisfiability is a contingent feature of particular language pairs or a necessary feature of translation across any phonotactically distinct languages. Keep the conceptual weight on articulating the criteria, their motivating examples, and their interrelations rather than on scoring existing translations, though you may briefly indicate how two broad orientations—one that treats formal architecture as constitutive and one that treats it as dispensable—fare against your list. The answer should be fully usable by someone without online access, so avoid appeals to obscure scholarship, specific page numbers, or niche bibliographies; ground the argument in reasoning and in concepts available to anyone who understands Hofstadter's general argument and the basic formal structure of Marot's poem.
