As a PhD student in metaphysics with a particular interest in the ontology of institutions, I am examining how endurantism and perdurantism apply to composite social artefacts—chartered universities, heritage-listed buildings, and similar entities—that maintain uninterrupted legal identity and normative obligations despite the complete replacement of their material constituents and personnel over time. Rather than rehearsing the standard Ship of Theseus, I ask you to compare these two metaphysical frameworks specifically in relation to such institutional cases. Please address three precise questions. First, how does each view explain the persistence of diachronic moral and legal responsibility when no enduring physical substrate remains? Second, is the endurantist compelled to posit the founding charter or statutory instrument as a non-physical enduring part, or can institutional continuity be reconstructed without such an entity? Third, does the perdurantist's appeal to temporal parts and relational continuity risk reducing the institution to a mere succession of discrete agents, thereby eroding the coherence of long-term accountability? For each question, reconstruct the strongest available version of both positions, note where the institutional evidence genuinely underdetermines the metaphysical choice, and indicate whether either view must be modified to accommodate the phenomenon. Please write in British English and do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
