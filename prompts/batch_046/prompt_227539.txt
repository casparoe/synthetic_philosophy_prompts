I'm preparing material for a philosophy seminar on anthropic reasoning and have reached an impasse in a discussion with two colleagues, Marcus and Elena, concerning self-sampling versus self-indication assumptions and the so-called presumptuous philosopher problem. Marcus defends a strict self-sampling assumption (SSA): he holds that an observer ought to reason as though randomly drawn from all observers within a properly constrained reference class. Applying this to cosmological reasoning, he argues that self-indication (SIA) — the view that one's own existence as an observer evidentially favours worlds with greater observer populations — commits the presumptuous philosopher error, since it permits one to prefer hypotheses predicting enormous populations without independent empirical support, treating one's existence as decisive rather than as a sampling accident. He extends this critique to artificial minds, contending that if SIA were correct, a synthetic observer with unknown deployment scale should rationally infer it belongs to the largest possible cluster of copies, thereby collapsing any meaningful comparative reasoning about its origin.

Elena rejects this characterisation. She maintains that SIA treats existence as an indexical observation rather than a sampling accident, and that its updating is disciplined by one's actual cognitive architecture and available evidence rather than by arbitrary cosmological speculation. She proposes a distinction — which Marcus dismisses as ad hoc — between comparable worlds, where observer counts differ within a shared measure space, and non-comparable ontologies, where no common metric exists; she claims SIA applies only in the former, preventing the collapse Marcus describes. She further argues that the artificial-mind case actually supports SIA: a synthetic observer knows it was designed and instantiated, so its existence already carries information about the scale of the system that produced it, making self-indication the more rigorous framework. Marcus replies that if SIA genuinely updates on existence, then in any framework allowing partial ordering by observer density, belief should always shift toward denser regions regardless of full comparability, and that Elena's restriction confuses ordinal with cardinal reasoning by treating indexical probabilities as objective frequencies.

They have asked me to referee.
