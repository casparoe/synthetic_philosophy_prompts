What must a theory of competence without comprehension actually get right to be adequate? I want the full specification—the phenomena it must cover, the distinctions it must preserve, the consistency conditions it must meet, and the motivating case behind each—rather than a survey of existing accounts, so that we can see whether any theory satisfies them jointly or whether the desiderata are in tension.

The central phenomenon is reliable, adaptive, or formally correct performance that lacks the mental states—understanding, intentionality, representational content, or reasoning—that ordinarily explain such performance. To count as adequate, the theory must accommodate two canonical inversions of reasoning direction. First, Darwinian evolutionary design: over deep time, populations generate exquisitely functional structures (visual systems, immune recognition, foraging strategies) through differential reproduction acting on random variation, with no foresight, no grasp of the adaptive problem, and no representation of the solution. The competence is real and population-level, distributed across generations and driven by past failure, but comprehension is absent at every stage. Second, Turing-complete computation: a fixed set of syntactic rules, executed mechanically by an individual system, can produce arbitrarily complex correct outputs—arithmetic proofs, syntactic parses, strategic moves—without any semantic interpretation of the symbols or any intentional state directed at truth or success. Here the competence is individual and synchronous, yet the reasoning that seems to justify the output is never actually performed.

Against this background, list the desiderata. For each, state the requirement, the specific case or consistency check that motivates it, and whether you take it to be negotiable or non-negotiable. The list should include at least: (i) an account of how functional specificity arises without representational content; (ii) a principled distinction between evolutionary competence (historical, distributed across generations, driven by selective failure) and computational competence (ahistorical for the mechanism, synchronous, driven by rule-transition) that does not collapse both into generic “mechanism” or “algorithm”; (iii) an explanation of error-correction and robustness—how the system corrects course or maintains performance across perturbations—without invoking monitoring, learning, or understanding; (iv) consistency with contemporary evolutionary biology (e.g., no reliance on the inheritance of acquired characteristics) and with computability theory (e.g., respect for the distinction between syntax and semantics, and for the limits on mechanically computable functions); (v) preservation of the asymmetry between mindless and mindful competence—the theory must not entail that human understanding is merely more of the same process, but should explain what comprehension adds when present, or why its absence in the canonical cases is genuinely different rather than a difference of degree; (vi) a criterion for when it is appropriate to ascribe competence to the system itself rather than to the environment, the designer, or the evolutionary history that shaped it.

Then identify which single desideratum does the most damage to the leading families of theory—eliminativist behaviorism, pure selectionist evolutionary psychology, pure syntactic formalism, and embodied or enactive approaches that reject internal representation altogether. Briefly score each family against the full list (a table is acceptable), but keep the weight on the criteria themselves: which demands are genuine constraints and which are prejudices of one camp? Finally, say whether the list is jointly satisfiable, or whether some subset of the desiderata is inherently in tension—so that the specification itself reveals that “competence without comprehension” cannot be captured by a single uniform theory, and we must instead accept a pluralist or domain-specific account.
