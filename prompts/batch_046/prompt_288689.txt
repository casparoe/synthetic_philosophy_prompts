Write a dialogue between two philosphers — call them Alex and Jordan — sitting in a bar after a panel on personal identity. They both agree mind uploading is technically possible but disagree hard on wether gradual replacement actually preserves you. Alex is a patternist: she thinks functional organization and narrative continuity are all that matter, so if the replacement is gradual enough the self survives. Jordan thinks causal continuity through biological substrate is essential; replace too much at once and you've broken the chain, even if the copy behaves identically.

The twist: they're not talking about the usual neuron-by-neuron swap. They're arguing about an "interleaved" protocol where every other cluster gets replaced with synthetic analogs on Monday, the rest stay biological, then the remaining ones are replaced Thursday. There's a 72-hour "striped" stage where the brain is literally half-carbon, half-silicon, with synthetic and biological neurons interconnecting in real time.

Have them actually argue — not talk past each other. Alex should push Jordan on why substrate-level causation matters more than information-flow causation. Jordan should ask Alex what happens to phenominal binding during the striped stage: is there one experience, two competing streams, or a dissociative gap? They should hit specific issues like who owns memories formed on Wednesday, wether waking up Friday feels like continuity or like being told someone else's story, and whether the interleaved design avoids the usual objections or just hides them in the gaps. Keep it casual — maybe one of them misspells "recieve" or uses "thier" instead of "their" at some point. No strawmen — both need to sound like they actually read the literature.
