Consider two structurally related but opposing difficulties in reasoning about change, both centered on the management of belief stability across actions.

The first is the frame problem as it arises in symbolic artificial intelligence and in philosophical analyses of action representation: an agent performs an action that alters a small set of properties, yet to maintain an accurate world-model it must avoid both failing to update changed properties and exhaustively re-deriving or re-asserting the vast number of properties that remain invariant. The difficulty is one of selective preservation—how to represent inertia without explicit enumeration.

The second is a difficulty I will call latent dependency dissolution, which operates in the opposite direction. Here, an agent holds beliefs that are not explicitly marked as dependent on conditions that have in fact been altered by an action. Because the dependency is not represented in the agent's model—perhaps mediated through long causal chains, contextual background conditions, or implicit framing assumptions—the agent treats these beliefs as invariant, when in fact they should be revised or abandoned. The difficulty is not preserving stability, but detecting hidden instability: knowing when a belief that looks like background knowledge has actually been undermined.

Compare these two problems with respect to their logical structure, their relationship to representation schemes such as non-monotonic logics, situation calculus, or frame axioms, and their practical implications for automated reasoning. In particular: does solving the frame problem—say, by adopting a default rule that properties persist—tend to aggravate latent dependency dissolution by making the system overly conservative about belief revision? Or can the same formal apparatus address both? If they are genuinely distinct, what feature of an agent's representational architecture determines whether it is more vulnerable to one failure mode than the other?
