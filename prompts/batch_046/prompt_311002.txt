I work as the only philosophy-trained referree on a small interdisiplinary journal board — we get maybe three submissions a week that touch on epistemology or methodology, and I've got about twenty minutes per paper before I have to send a signed recommendation to the senior editor, who hasn't done philosophy since undergrad. Increasingly I'm seeing manuscripts that fall into the bootstrapping trap: authors claim they've justified a method or measurement by citing results that already presuppose that method. I want to turn the bootstrapping problem and the idea of easy knowledge — knowledge that comes too cheap because it leans on the very thing it's meant to establish — into a reusable audit proceedure I can run without quoting texts.

The deliverable should be a step-by-step checklist, in order, with what counts as a pass or fail at each stage, where the audit stops with a clear reject, and where it has to hand over to a value judgement rather than a fact. It needs to survive simplification: drop distinctions that don't matter for a quick referee check, but keep the ones that actually block circularity. Be honest about where it'll misfire, and include a hard stop for cases that need more expertise than I've got.

Please apply it to three submissions: (a) a psychology paper validating a wellbeing scale using outcomes from an intervention designed around that same scale; (b) a climate study calibrating satellite temperature readings with ground-station data that were already corrected using the satellite algorithm — watch for shadow calibration here; and (c) one case where your audit comes out acceptable but I, as a naturally sceptical referee, will still feel uneasy — explain exactly what the procedure misses there.

Also tell me flat-out what the audit can't do.

Can you say how confident you are in what you're claiming and single out the point you're least sure about? Keep it under 800 words — I need to read it before my next batch of papers.
