I am preparing a module for an undergraduate course on democratic deliberation, and in observing debates between politically polarised groups I have noticed a recurring interaction that I cannot map onto the concepts I already know. In one typical case, a participant on the left carefully reconstructs a right-leaning argument about welfare reform, explicitly acknowledging the concern that generous benefits might reduce work incentives and framing it as a genuine empirical worry rather than mere hostility to the poor. Instead of accepting this as good-faith interpretation, the right-leaning participant’s allies accuse the reconstructor of performing “strategic empathy”—using the appearance of charity to smuggle in the premise that the concern is rational, thereby making the opposing position seem more defensible than it is. The reconstructor is then charged not with simple error but with arguing in bad faith by being too generous. Seeing this reaction, the original right-leaning speaker withdraws an earlier concession they had made—say, that dependency effects are probably small in the current system—and insists that the reconstruction never captured their actual view. The result is a deadlock in which interpretive charity itself becomes epistemically toxic: the only moves that escape suspicion are those that refuse to understand the other side altogether.

I am familiar with the hermeneutics of suspicion, with accusations of bad faith in polarised discourse, and with rhetorical patterns like the motte-and-bailey, but none of these quite capture what is happening here. The phenomenon is not a hidden defensible core paired with an indefensible extension, nor is it a general refusal to trust motives across the board. Rather, it is a local collapse of the distinction between understanding an argument and endorsing its premises, such that any attempt at bridge-building is read as a manipulative endorsement of the bridge’s far end. Does this configuration have an established name in the literature on political polarisation, echo chambers, or the philosophy of disagreement? If there is no standard term, please coin one with a precise definition, specify its boundary conditions—distinguishing it from strategic misrepresentation, from the hermeneutics of suspicion, and from ordinary charges of bad faith—and identify which logical feature of the interaction makes it stable. Before answering, please spend a moment brainstorming the nearest related concepts, then provide your analysis in British English, writing as an expert in political epistemology advising a course designer.
