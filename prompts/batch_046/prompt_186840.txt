After a long argument with a friend about whether citizenship can still carry substantive weight if it imposes no compulsory obligations on the body, I keep returning to the concrete institutional architecture of armed neutrality. Consider three distinct arrangements: Switzerland’s militia system, in which universal male conscription is paired with decades-long reserve obligations and the storage of service weapons in private homes, effectively turning every able-bodied citizen into a distributed node of territorial defense; Sweden’s reactivated selective conscription, gender-neutral since 2018, which drafts a small cohort into service while rapidly professionalizing elite rapid-response units, all within a framework of formal non-alignment coupled with deep EU defense integration; and Finland’s comprehensive conscription model, which sustains one of Europe’s largest reserve forces relative to population and treats territorial defense as a civic function rather than a specialized state labor market. Set against these are liberal democracies such as the United Kingdom and the United States, which have abolished conscription entirely and treat national defense as a purely contractual employment sector with no special claim on citizens’ physical risk.

My friend insists these differences are purely strategic, demographic, or budgetary, but I suspect they quietly depend on competing philosophical pictures of citizenship, distributive justice, and state legitimacy. What implicit theory of the citizen-state relationship does each neutrality policy presuppose? Does the Swiss practice of domestic weapon storage presuppose a republican view in which sovereignty literally resides in an armed citizenry, or can it be justified on purely instrumental, deterrence-based grounds without that commitment? Does Sweden’s selective, gender-neutral draft presuppose that bodily military risk ought to be distributed like a progressive tax—allocated according to capacity, social need, and equality of status—or does its selection mechanism smuggle in a meritocratic logic that undermines the very equality conscription is meant to express? And does Finland’s reserve-heavy system presuppose an inalienable civic duty of territorial defense, or is it compatible with a liberal-contractual view that treats defense as a public good best supplied by voluntary specialists?

More broadly, is there a coherent implicit theory of justice in the distribution of bodily risk running through these policies, or are they philosophically underdetermined—compatible with republican, liberal, and communitarian frameworks without strictly committing to any? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical architecture, distinguish what these practices strictly require from what they merely accommodate, and say what institutional changes would follow if we replaced the underlying view with one that treats military service as a purely voluntary labor contract bearing no special civic status, or, conversely, with one that demands universal risk-sharing but rejects the gender distinctions and selective exemptions that Sweden and Finland still maintain. I am not looking for historical narratives, comparative statistics, or bibliographies—only the conceptual commitments these concrete arrangements depend on, where those commitments conflict with one another, and where the practice is weakest against philosophical pressure.
