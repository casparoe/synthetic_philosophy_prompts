I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I've been working through the statue-and-clay problem because it structurally resembles persistence questions in distributed systems—whether an entity survives total component replacement if its functional role and form remain intact. I'd like you to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps that have argued past each other on material constitution.

Camp A—Constitution pluralists—holds that the statue is genuinely distinct from the lump of clay that makes it up, that constitution is an irreducible relation allowing two objects to share all parts and occupy the same region, and that the statue and the clay have different persistence conditions because they belong to different ontological kinds.

Camp B—Sortal-relative monists—holds that there is only one object present, that talk of "the statue" and "the lump of clay" applies different sortals with different persistence conditions to the same thing, and that no metaphysically thick constitution relation is needed once we correctly understand how kind-terms govern identity over time.

Please produce a formal adversarial-collaboration document structured as follows.

First, an Agreed-Claims section: a numbered list of at least six neutral claims both camps can sign. Cover the basic scenario (clay shaped into a figure, the artifact exists, destroying the shape destroys the statue but not necessarily all clay, the clay existed before shaping, etc.), shared methodological commitments (e.g., that ontology should avoid unnecessary multiplication of entities where explanatory work can be done otherwise, and that ordinary judgments about artworks deserve some default weight), and any shared acceptance of the Continuous Replacement Case described below.

Second, a Precise-Disagreements section: state exactly where the camps part ways. For each disagreement, provide (i) the formulation in wording Camp A accepts for its own view and for Camp B's view, and (ii) the formulation in wording Camp B accepts for its own view and for Camp A's view. Focus especially on whether constitution is an irreducible relation or reducible to sortal-relative description, and on whether persistence conditions are intrinsic to objects or conceptually assigned by our kind-classifications.

Third, identify the Cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. I suspect the central crux concerns whether complete, continuous material replacement preserves the statue when artistic function and form are maintained, but refine that and identify at least one further crux (for instance, whether positing coincident entities carries an ontological cost that can be measured independently of explanatory gain).

Fourth, present the Concrete Test Case both sides agree is relevant: a statue is maintained for two centuries by replacing, one molecular unit at a time, every portion of original clay with chemically identical synthetic material, such that by year 150 no original matter remains, yet the form, institutional name, and artistic function experience no interruption. Call this the Continuous Replacement Case. For each crux, record Camp A's exact prediction about this case, Camp B's exact prediction about this case, and one argument or conceptual observation both sides agree would bear on the crux if it could be settled conceptually or empirically.

In drafting, you may introduce and define a novel conceptual distinction to clarify the disagreement—distinguish, for example, material persistence (continuity of original substance), formal persistence (continuity of shape and structure), and functional-artistic persistence (continuity of representational role and institutional recognition)—but define it within the document rather than assuming prior familiarity.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or attributions such as "see Baker" or "as Lewis argued." Do not invoke niche theories, named figures, or specific recent papers that a generalist model would not reliably have memorized. Write in formal, polished prose appropriate for a joint philosophical statement, not as a dialogue or as an adjudication of which side is correct.
