I have been thinking about the tension between David Lewis's rational-choice analysis of convention and evolutionary-game-theoretic accounts of how norms actually emerge and persist. In Lewis's framework, a convention is an arbitrary solution to a coordination problem sustained by common knowledge of preferences and rationality; the stability comes from expectations about what others will do. Evolutionary models, by contrast, often explain conformity through payoff-biased imitation, punishment of deviants, or frequency-dependent selection, mechanisms that do not obviously require agents to hold higher-order beliefs about rationality.

What I want is a concrete, somewhat non-obvious example of a conceptual issue that arises specifically at this intersection—not merely a case where a norm changes, but a case where a convention should be stable by Lewis's criteria yet is destabilized by evolutionary dynamics in a way that the Lewisian apparatus cannot easily absorb. To make the example precise, please frame it around a phenomenon I am tentatively calling salience inversion: a situation in which the coordination problem remains genuinely arbitrary (no option is technically superior), and the equilibrium was initially sustained by mutual expectation, but cultural transmission selectively amplifies an alternative that was initially less salient because it carries stronger emotional signaling value or ritual intensity, causing the original expectation structure to decay even though rational preferences and common knowledge have not changed.

Your example should involve a real domain—such as driving conventions, greeting rituals, or linguistic reference—where arbitrariness is central, and it should explain exactly why the collapse poses a problem for the Lewisian account rather than being reducible to ordinary preference change or payoff shift. In your response, please clearly distinguish what is well-established in the literature (for example, the basic disagreement between rational-choice and evolutionary explanations of convention) from what remains genuinely contested or speculative, including whether salience inversion requires a distinct evolutionary mechanism or can be captured by standard replicator dynamics.
