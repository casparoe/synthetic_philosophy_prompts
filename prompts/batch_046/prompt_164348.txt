Write a short story set in a near-future research colony where cognitive augmentation has been applied to non-human primates, producing beings that exhibit linguistic reasoning but lack what Kant called practical freedom—the capacity to legislate moral law for themselves. The protagonist, an ethicist trained in Kantian doctrine, must decide whether destroying one of these augmented primates to harvest neural tissue for human patients violates only an indirect duty, because it coarsens human moral feeling, or whether the being's partial rationality demands direct moral consideration. In constructing the narrative, explore the tension between Kant's claim that animals are mere means and his acknowledgment in the Lectures on Ethics that we have obligations with regard to animals that mirror direct duties, without resolving the ambiguity through anachronistic rights-language or modern animal-liberation rhetoric. The story should turn on a single concrete choice—whether to authorize the procedure—revealing how the indirect-direct distinction collapses or holds under pressure when the subject occupies a liminal space between animality and rational autonomy.
