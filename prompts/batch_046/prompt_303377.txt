For my advanced bioethics seminar, I am constructing an adversarial collaboration document on human death determination, and I would like you to draft the joint statement that two opposing camps could both sign. The first camp holds that death should be defined by the irreversible cessation of all brain function—including the brainstem—because the brain serves as the integrating center of the organism. The second camp holds that death should be defined solely by the irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory function, treating total brain destruction as catastrophic injury rather than death itself.

Please produce the document in the following form: a numbered section of agreed claims, followed by a disagreements table. In the agreed-claims section, include statements both sides can endorse about the biological facts of brainstem failure, the current variability in legal standards across jurisdictions, and the conceptual distinction between organismal integration and mere mechanical maintenance. Then, in the table, state each camp's precise disagreement in wording that camp itself would accept, identify the cruxes—those sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side—and, for each crux, specify an empirical case or conceptual test both sides agree is relevant, recording each side's predicted outcome in advance.

To keep the analysis from relying only on familiar examples, center the collaboration on the following hypothetical but conceptually serious scenario: a patient with verified total destruction of the brainstem and cerebral cortex whose heart and lungs are sustained not by a conventional external ventilator but by a fully implantable, autonomous artificial circulation and gas-exchange system that requires no neurological input and can maintain homeostasis indefinitely. Both camps should address whether this patient is dead, what organismal integration means when circulation is technologically decoupled from neural regulation, and whether the persistence of spontaneous metabolic processes in peripheral tissues counts as life of the organism. Include a shared acknowledgment that existing clinical protocols do not clearly cover this technological case, and separate biological disputes from metaphysical ones—such as whether personal identity could persist in any form—without asking either side to concede on those metaphysical issues.
