In philosophy of biology, debates over whether biological functions are etiological—fixed by evolutionary selection history—or causal-role—fixed by present contribution to an organismal system capacity—often lean on an organizational analogy: an organism is like a corporation that has undergone successive mergers, acquisitions, and restructuring, and a trait is like an employee whose status depends either on the original hiring contract (the selection history that established the trait) or on the current division workflow (the trait's present causal contribution to fitness or survival). Proponents of causal-role accounts use this image to argue that asking what a heart, an appendix, or a vestigial structure was originally “hired” to do is as irrelevant to its present biological function as asking about a legacy employee's original job title is to whether they keep the department running today; critics reply that evolutionary selection is not an intentional hiring process and that “contracts” have no independent existence apart from the causal effects that sustained them. Audit this analogy in detail: provide an explicit correspondence table mapping the corporate elements onto evolutionary and physiological ones; identify the disanalogies that matter for the functional-attribution debate, such as the absence of intentional design, the difference between being laid off and being lost through genetic drift, and whether “current workflow” can be defined without smuggling in historical baseline conditions; assess which features of the corporate source—agency, contractual obligation, organizational purpose—are quietly imported and whether they bias the conclusion toward one account; and determine whether the causal-role conclusion that original selection history is functionally irrelevant survives once these breaks are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying that conclusion all along. If the analogy collapses in a particular dimension, suggest what a more adequate comparison—perhaps drawn from engineering, language, or distributed computation—would need to preserve.
