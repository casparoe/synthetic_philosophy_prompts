I'm a high school student organizing an extracurricular philosophy session—fourteen peers, ages sixteen to eighteen, sixty minutes—where I want the group to feel, through rules and incentives rather than lecture, the structural force of the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism. Rather than using the familiar trolley framing, please design a card-based classroom simulation built around what I will call "identity-bound welfare reserves": each participant receives a private, non-transferable point reserve representing well-being tied specifically to that individual, while the group is simultaneously evaluated on total collective welfare. The exercise must include a "boundary-dissolution round" in which two volunteers may merge their reserves into a single account with doubled conversion efficiency, forcing the remaining participants to decide whether sacrificing part of the merged entity for the greater good violates the same boundary they refused to cross when accounts were separate. Specify only classroom materials—paper cards and a printed payoff sheet, with no real money—the exact rules for three successive rounds, the point-based reward scheme, and a sixty-minute timeline with roles that prevent me, as facilitator, from naming the philosophical objection until the debrief. The debrief should map round one onto direct interpersonal sacrifice, round two onto the illusion that merging personal boundaries solves the aggregation problem, and round three onto the absence of any cross-personal compensation mechanism; it should also state plainly which normative feature—such as whether a moral debt incurred across lives can ever be closed—the simulation structurally cannot reproduce and must be supplied through discussion. Include an honest forecast of what will predictably go wrong on the first run—perhaps students refusing to merge in order to preserve moral leverage, or the group reaching unanimous agreement too quickly—and advise how to manage it. Do not require any advance reading, and do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to specific texts that participants would need outside access to verify.
