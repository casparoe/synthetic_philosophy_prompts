I just finished a long argument with a friend about the Meno problem—specifically whether knowledge is genuinely more valuable than true belief, or whether the extra value is only a kind of practical insurance against being wrong later. Every example I offered seemed to smuggle in utility rather than reveal an intrinsic difference, so I want others to feel the gap from the inside rather than hear it explained.

Design a 75-minute workshop activity for 18 philosophy undergraduates that stages the distinction without any up-front lecture. Use a three-round simulation built around a “broken-telephone oracle”: in rounds one and two, half the group receives a correct answer through an opaque, unexaminable source—true belief with no visible chain—while the other half receives the same correct answer through a short, inspectable reasoning chain they can articulate and defend. Both groups act on the answer using course-credit points as stakes, but in round three the conditions suddenly shift: participants must vote on whether to extend trust, delegate a decision, or challenge an authority when the oracle’s reliability is questioned. Arrange the rules and payoff scheme so that the opaque-source group feels the instability of its position only after it has already committed resources, while the inspectable-chain group can adapt. Include a timeline, roles, materials, and a debrief that maps each round onto the Meno problem’s traditional answers—reliability, justification, and the transmission problem—without naming Plato or Meno until the end. Be frank about which feature of Platonic knowledge—its connection to an unchanging Form—the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply instead. Also note what will predictably go wrong the first time (likely that students try to reverse-engineer the oracle rather than accepting its opacity) and how to handle it. No bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
