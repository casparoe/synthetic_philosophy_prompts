I was appointed to my university's Committee on Interdisciplinary Research Integrity earlier this year, following a protracted tenure dispute in my department that left me convinced we evaluate expertise across disciplinary boundaries with far less rigor than we imagine. Next Thursday the committee must vote on whether to adopt the administration's proposed "Dual-Frame Review Protocol," which would require every reviewer of a cross-disciplinary grant proposal to submit a preliminary "outside-view" judgment—an explicit base-rate estimate of success drawn from a defined reference class—before turning to the proposal's specific merits. The philosophical stakes are not abstract for me: I now sit on the body that must decide whether epistemic modesty should be institutionalized as procedural deference to historical frequencies, or whether such deference becomes a false humility that systematically discounts reasoning that genuinely breaks new ground.

The immediate case is a joint proposal from our cognitive science and formal epistemology groups that attempts to model collective belief revision in scientific communities using tools from social epistemology and dynamic logic. Proponents of the protocol argue that without an outside-view anchor, interdisciplinary reviewers consistently overestimate the viability of methodologically hybrid projects because they lack calibrated intuition about failure rates in adjacent fields. Opponents—including a senior philosopher of science on the committee—counter that for genuinely novel interdisciplinary configurations, no stable reference class exists; inventing one for procedural compliance produces not modesty but a ritualized substitution of statistical analogy for expert judgment, effectively penalizing proposals whose value lies precisely in escaping the historical patterns that generated the base rate. Our charge is not merely to approve or reject the protocol in the abstract, but to recommend a concrete course of action—adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions—together with an implementation timeline if we move forward.

Please draft the short formal opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a specific decision with a clear time frame for any transitional steps, and it should engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side: namely, that mandatory outside-view framing risks violating the epistemic autonomy of interdisciplinary reviewers by requiring them to privilege algorithmic deference over domain-sensitive reasoning, and that this may institutionalize a form of modesty structurally biased against innovation rather than genuinely corrective of overconfidence. The memo should be written as an actionable institutional document rather than personal guidance, with reasoning that committee members on both sides could recognize as treating their position fairly.
