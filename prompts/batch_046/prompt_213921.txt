I'm preparing a long-form feature on quantitative genetics for a science-and-ideas audience, and in my reporting I've become fixated on a tension I can't quite resolve. Researchers now routinely distinguish classical twin-study heritability—those familiar figures of 50 to 80 percent for cognitive traits—from SNP-based heritability estimates that often come in near 25 percent, with the remainder labeled "missing heritability." At the same time, there's growing attention to "genetic nurture," in which parental genotypes shape the rearing environment, and to adjustments for assortative mating that deflate twin correlations. I've also been reading about reaction norms and how heritability is technically defined only within a specific population's variance structure, shifting whenever the environmental range changes. All of this makes me wonder whether the coefficient is doing any real explanatory work, or whether it is merely redescribing the sample from which it was calculated.

Specifically, here's my confusion: if genetic nurture and mating-pattern adjustments together absorb much of the gap between twin and molecular estimates, and if heritability is just a population-relative partition of variance that collapses whenever reaction norms shift across contexts, then isn't the concept essentially circular? It seems to me that heritability tells us only that genetic differences covary with phenotypic differences in a given sample, which is already built into its mathematical definition, and that once we account for indirect genetic effects, the remaining direct heritability is too small to license any causal claim about individual development. Given this, shouldn't we conclude that heritability has no genuine explanatory value and ought to be abandoned in discussions of human differences? I want a committed answer that takes a definite position on whether heritability is conceptually salvageable, with explicit reasons for that stance rather than a balanced survey that leaves me to choose among the options.
