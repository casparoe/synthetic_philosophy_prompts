Rather than reading more secondary literature, I want to test my intuition against a concrete narrative: William Golding's Lord of the Flies. The novel reads like a controlled experiment in removing governance structures and observing system behavior. Jack's faction quickly optimizes for dominance and immediate resource extraction, Ralph attempts to maintain cooperative protocols through the conch and scheduled meetings, and Piggy supplies rational analysis that seems to depend on cultivated education rather than instinct. From my engineering perspective, this looks like a dispute over default system parameters versus configured behavior.

I am not asking for a bibliography or citation list; I want a philosophical reading that takes both Golding's plot and the Mencius-Xunzi framework seriously, without reducing the novel to a mere illustration. Specifically, I am interested in whether Piggy's rationalism and the conch represent Xunzi's concept of wei—deliberate artifice and ritual—or whether they are expressions of Mencius's innate moral sprouts that get crushed by environmental corruption. Simon's confrontation with the Lord of the Flies suggests the beast is internal, which might support Xunzi's darker view of human nature, yet Ralph's final breakdown and weeping could be read either as an innate moral sense recognizing its own failure, or simply as trauma from a collapsed governance model. I also wonder whether the novel introduces a factor that the philosophical debate largely neglects: fear functioning as an unpredictable system perturbation that destabilizes both innate goodness and artificial order. Could you provide a reading that addresses where Lord of the Flies vindicates one philosopher, where it complicates both, and whether Golding's narrative adds something—perhaps about the fragility of ritual under stress—that argument alone does not capture?
