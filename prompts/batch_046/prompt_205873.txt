I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on philosophy of history and comparative civilizational studies, and I've become fixated on what appears to be a long-standing methodological stalemate in the literature on large-scale societal transformation. For decades, researchers have debated whether the Bronze Age interregnum, the Classic Maya political fragmentation, and the Easter Island ecological restructuring should be classified as collapses—implying systemic failure, rapid demographic contraction, and a loss of hierarchical complexity—or as reorganizations in which local populations, agricultural practices, and kinship networks persisted with altered political scale and visibility. The basic archaeological chronology, settlement data, and environmental proxies are largely settled, yet the interpretive divide has barely narrowed: one camp points to the abandonment of monumental architecture, the disappearance of writing systems, and the dissolution of centralized authority as evidence that something essential was lost; the other insists that labeling these shifts failures imports an anachronistic teleology about state complexity and ignores the continuity of everyday life at the local level.

My working hypothesis is that this deadlock is not primarily empirical, because both sides accept the same material record but embed it in incompatible frameworks. I suspect the disagreement is partly ontological—about whether societies have persistence conditions analogous to organisms or persons, so that institutional death can occur while demographic continuity survives—and partly normative, involving a concealed judgment about whether hierarchical complexity is intrinsically valuable and its reduction therefore counts as harm regardless of local welfare. Are the two camps actually answering different questions—one about institutional continuity, one about demographic survival—under the single heading of collapse? Or is there a substantive disagreement about social ontology that neither side has made explicit? I want a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure: is it verbal, about the definition of the term; substantive, about the identity conditions of social entities over time; normative, about the value of complexity; or an empirical dispute disguised by interpretive commitments? And given that diagnosis, what would actually constitute progress—a new archaeological discovery, a formal model distinguishing institutional death from population continuity, a conceptual distinction borrowed from personal identity theory, or should we conclude that the question is ill-posed in a way that makes permanent disagreement appropriate?
