We were arguing about Rawls's separateness-of-persons objection until late, and I wrote out this reconstruction to check whether I'm being unfair to the utilitarian. (1) The separateness of persons means that benefits and burdens are not fungible across individuals: there is no "super-person" whose welfare is the sum of parts. (2) If that is correct, then "maximize total utility" is conceptually confused, because it treats distinct subjects as if they were parts of one life. (3) But the same reasoning applies to any principle that requires comparing or weighing one person's situation against another's—equal distribution compares shares across subjects, rescue obligations weigh my small cost against your large benefit, and even rights-based side-constraints weigh harm prevented against harm inflicted. (4) So if separateness genuinely blocks aggregation, it also blocks every form of interpersonal moral reasoning that is not purely agent-relative. (5) What's left is only a solipsistic ethics: don't lie, don't kill, with no regard for how your actions affect others' welfare. (6) That can't be right—no one thinks morality is silent on whether to save five strangers at small cost to oneself, or on whether to redistribute. (7) So either separateness is false, or it doesn't do the work it's supposed to do against utilitarianism. Where does this go wrong? I'd like to keep (1) and (2) if I can, because the intuition that there's no collective subject of welfare feels real, but I don't want to pay the price of (5).
