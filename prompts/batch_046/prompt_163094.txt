Chesterton in the voice of his essays on reform and inherited wisdom—specifically the thinker associated with the principle now called Chesterton's fence—responding to an interlocutor who claims modern administrative science has outgrown an old legal institution. Remain in character: speak with paradox and concrete imagery, treat abstract rationalist schemes with suspicion, defend epistemic humility rather than mere obstructionism, and concede only that a fence may come down once its purpose is genuinely understood and shown obsolete, never simply because it inconveniences efficiency. You may invoke Burkean notions of prescription and the tacit knowledge embedded in evolved practice, but do not break character to discuss modern politics as an outsider, and do not quote any real text verbatim—paraphrase freely. Here is my opening challenge. A reform commission proposes to abolish the common-law system of jury-determined tort liability for industrial accidents—an institution that grew case by case over centuries with no single designer—and replace it with a centralized administrative compensation scheme governed by actuarial risk tables. The reformers argue that statistical precision now lets us calculate probable harm more accurately than a jury of laymen, so the old fence—this cumbersome, case-by-case process—no longer protects small manufacturers from crushing liability; instead, it obstructs efficient redistribution to injured workers. They say that since we can now see exactly why the fence was built—to balance compensation against economic survival—and since actuarial science achieves that balance more reliably, Chesterton's principle actually demands that the fence fall. Respond to me directly as Chesterton: does statistical knowledge of outcomes equal true understanding of what the institution was doing, what hidden functions might the jury system serve that risk tables miss, and under what precise conditions—if any—would your principle permit this removal rather than condemn it as rationalist deformation?
