Consider a philosopher working in hinge epistemology, in the tradition associated with Wittgenstein’s later writings on certainty, who has been classifying whether specific propositions serve as hinges—framework commitments that must stand fast for a particular domain of inquiry to operate—across practical and theoretical contexts. Her judgments to date are these: in a criminal trial, that the court possesses jurisdiction over the defendant functions as a hinge, whereas that the defendant was present in the city on the relevant night does not; in a laboratory replication study, that the calibration protocol has not undergone systematic drift is a hinge, whereas that any particular measurement is accurate is not; in archival historical research, that the repository has not been subject to wholesale fabrication by a single actor is a hinge, whereas that any individual document is authentic is not; in formal mathematics assisted by proof-checking software, that the underlying logical system has not been corrupted by a compiler error is treated not as a hinge but as an ordinary, technically checkable claim; and in clinical psychiatric assessment, that the patient is not engaged in a total, systematic deception about their biography is a hinge, whereas that any specific symptom report is truthful is not.

Now consider a new case: a research team uses a large language model to generate extended intermediate reasoning steps for a scientific hypothesis, with a human expert reviewing only the final conclusion and not each intermediate step. Is the proposition that the model has not systematically inverted logical polarity or substituted contradictory premises within its hidden intermediate representations functioning as a hinge for the human expert’s acceptance of the conclusion, or is it an ordinary claim that should be verified step-by-step?

Predict her verdict. Then articulate at least two competing rules that accommodate her earlier classifications equally well—for instance, one rule grounded in whether doubting the claim would undermine the entire practice, and another grounded in whether the practice contains internal methods capable in principle of checking the claim. Say which rule you think she is implicitly running and why. Finally, specify the further case that would best distinguish these two rules.
