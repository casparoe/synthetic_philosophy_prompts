I'm working through an extended version of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument for a tutorial in my moral responsibility course, and I've pushed it into what feels like a reductio, yet I cannot locate the precise misstep. The argument runs as follows.

(1) If an agent is morally responsible for an action, she must be responsible for the mental states—beliefs, desires, and character traits—that causally produced it.
(2) To be responsible for those mental states, she must have intentionally shaped them; but then she must be responsible for the prior mental states that led her to shape them in that particular way.
(3) This generates an infinite regress that can only be halted if the agent is causa sui, which is impossible. Hence no one is ever truly morally responsible for what she does.

That much is familiar. I then extend it in two directions that seem parallel but may not be.

(4) The same regress applies to epistemic responsibility: to be rationally accountable for holding belief B, one must be accountable for the cognitive dispositions and evidential standards that produced B, and then for the prior conditions that shaped those dispositions, with the same impossible terminus.
(5) Therefore, no one is ever rationally accountable for any belief.

Then a further step that seems to prove far too much:

(6) Consider an agent's "standing normative commitments"—the background principles (for instance, consistency, impartiality, or fidelity to evidence) that she treats as authoritative in her reasoning.
(7) If she is not causa sui regarding her mental constitution, she is not responsible for having adopted these commitments.
(8) But if she is not responsible for her standing commitments, she cannot be normatively evaluated for reasoning consistently or inconsistently with them; she merely manifests them mechanically, like an instrument.
(9) Yet we routinely evaluate people for reasoning well or badly relative to shared norms.
(10) Most troublingly: if no one is responsible for accepting or rejecting arguments, then no one can be rationally accountable for accepting Strawson's own Basic Argument. The argument, if sound, undermines the conditions for its own rational endorsement.

My conclusion—that either (1) is false or we must abandon normative evaluation of reasoning altogether—strikes me as absurd, and I should prefer to preserve (1) through (5). Where does the overreach occur? Is there an equivocation between causal authorship and normative answerability somewhere between (6) and (8), or a scope error in extending the regress from particular actions and beliefs to standing commitments? Or is the argument valid, and the genuine cost of keeping (1) is giving up (8) or (10)?
