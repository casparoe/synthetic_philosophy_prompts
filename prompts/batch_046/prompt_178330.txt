My verdicts on the truth-value of future-contingent statements about a single event, and the principle, if any, that unifies them. Consider first the classic case: there will be a sea battle tomorrow. I judge that if the future is genuinely open, the statement is neither true nor false now, though it will acquire one value; bivalence fails for future contingents. Change the metaphysical setting to a deterministic block universe with a fixed timeline, and I reverse my verdict: the statement is already determinately true or false, and what looks like contingency is only epistemic limitation; bivalence holds, openness is an illusion of perspective. Next, imagine a branching multiverse in which quantum decoherence produces both a battle-branch and a peace-branch with equal structural weight. Here I feel there is no single global fact of the matter, yet within each branch the statement is determinately true or false relative to that branch's history. Now consider causal overdetermination: a commander issues orders that make the battle inevitable, but she issues them solely because she received an intelligence report whose reliability itself depended on the prior probability of battle being high. Even though every causal link is fixed once prior conditions hold, I still want to say the statement was contingent before the report was received, not false in virtue of the later inevitability. Finally, take a self-undermining prediction: an oracle that is genuinely infallible in counterfactual terms announces that there will be a battle, and precisely because of the announcement the adversary withdraws, so no battle occurs. I think the statement was false at the moment of utterance, not true-but-then-cancelled, and that the oracle's reliability must be analyzed counterfactually rather than causally. Is there a coherent principle of contingency, bivalence, and causal structure that saves all five verdicts? If the price of coherence is abandoning one, tell me which one to drop and why the simpler principle is worth it.
