I'm working through a conceptual puzzle for my dissertation on practical reason. The wrong-kind-of-reasons problem is usually framed in terms of moral motivation—say, the amoralist who acts justly only to avoid social sanction. But I want to push the distinction into a more granular space. Consider a case of synthetic moral motivation: an agent's neural reward circuitry is pharmacologically calibrated so that she reliably tracks moral considerations and produces appropriate actions, yet she lacks any deliberative access to the normative structure that explains why those considerations count as reasons for her. She behaves as if she acts for moral reasons, but the causal provenance of her motivation is entirely sub-personal and non-rational. My question is whether the wrong-kind-of-reasons distinction can still do explanatory work here. Specifically, if we separate rational accessibility from causal provenance—allowing that an agent might be causally responsive to the correct normative properties without being able to cite them in deliberation—do we collapse the distinction into a purely internalist account of moral motivation? Or can we preserve a robust externalist reading of the problem by insisting that the right kind requires not just tracking the correct normative properties, but being positioned within a practice of giving and asking for reasons? I'd like an intuitive but conceptually precise explanation of how these two dimensions—rational accessibility and causal provenance—interact in the wrong-kind-of-reasons framework, and whether the synthetic motivation case reveals a hidden circularity in how we individuate the kind of a reason.
