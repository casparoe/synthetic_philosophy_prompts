You are a philosopher of history specializing in archival ethics and the politics of knowledge production. Before drafting anything, take ten minutes to brainstorm: how should the concept of archival silence—understood not as a passive gap but as an active structural product of power—shape the actual sentences of an opening address? In particular, consider the notion of "negative provenance": the idea that destroyed or excluded records do not simply vanish but leave behind an active chain of responsibility that binds present institutions. Now write the opening remarks (approximately eight minutes, about 1,200 words) for a workshop titled "The Weight of Empty Shelves," co-hosted by a university special-collections department and a neighborhood collective that maintains counter-records of a community demolished by a mid-twentieth-century urban-renewal program, whose official census, property, and health records were systematically destroyed. The audience is split: half are professional archivists trained to treat absence as a cataloging deficit; half are descendants and organizers for whom that absence is an active erasure of kinship. The philosophical idea must do structural work in the speech: it should prevent the speaker from offering false reassurance that the archive can "fill in" the missing records; it should determine how the university's institutional role is described; and it should yield one concrete, non-metaphorical obligation that the archivists leave with. Speak formally but avoid turning the community's loss into an academic case study. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Think step by step about tone, audience, and what the concept of silence makes it impossible to say.
