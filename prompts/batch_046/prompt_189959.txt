I am preparing a long-form essay on the conceptual tensions within nuclear brinkmanship—specifically the gap between rational-threat models and the structural dynamics of accidental escalation in multi-polar, technologically accelerated contexts. Rather than rehearse the standard Cold War canon, I want to know which unresolved conceptual problems now matter most: for instance, whether the credibility of a strategic commitment collapses when decision windows are compressed by automated sensing and response systems, or whether the stability-instability paradox depends on an implausible symmetry of risk preferences across heterogeneous state and non-state actors.

If you were directing a small, three-year conceptual research program—philosophy and strategic theory, with no access to classified empirical material—which two or three open lines should take priority? Defend the ordering with explicit criteria: which questions are upstream of others, which are tractable through conceptual analysis rather than intelligence data, and which carry the greatest policy weight if resolved. For each line, specify the philosophical commitments it turns on—about rational agency, the epistemology of common knowledge, or the ethics of imposing catastrophic risk for strategic positioning—and indicate what kind of conceptual result, not a data point, would cause you to reorder the agenda. I am less interested in a survey than in an argument about where limited analytical effort should actually go, and whether the core difficulty in contemporary deterrence is best framed as a problem in decision theory, in collective epistemology, or in the moral logic of brinkmanship itself.
