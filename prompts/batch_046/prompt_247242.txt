I am preparing the opening session for my graduate seminar on personal identity, and I want the students to do the conceptual groundwork before we evaluate any particular account. Specifically, I have been working with Dennett’s metaphor of the self as a “center of narrative gravity,” but I think the discussion in the literature rarely moves past the metaphor to specify what an underlying theory must actually deliver. Write the full list of desiderata that any adequate theory treating the self as a center of narrative gravity must satisfy—what phenomena it must explain, which distinctions it must preserve, and which findings from clinical psychology or cognitive science it cannot contradict. Be precise: include the case of split-brain patients in whom two apparently autonomous narrative streams seem to operate within one biological organism; artificial agents capable of generating coherent first-person reports without biological embodiment or evolutionary history; and severe dissociative disorders in which autobiographical coherence fragments but some residual gravitational pull—what I have been calling “narrative inertia”—seems to persist or, conversely, to collapse entirely. For each desideratum, state the motivating consideration behind it and flag whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of a particular camp (for instance, physicalist reductionists versus phenomenologically oriented approaches).

Crucially, I want you to identify whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable—particularly any tension between maintaining a genuinely unified center of gravity and accommodating distributed, fragmented, or externally driven narrative structures. You should also address whether the theory must respect a sharp distinction between first-person autobiographical narrative and third-person social attribution, and whether allowing what I will call “narrative torque”—asymmetric pulls in which external social narratives exert stronger organizing force than internal self-modeling—counts as a theoretical feature or as a failure of the account. Keep the weight of your response on the criteria themselves and their motivating cases rather than on scoring existing views, though a brief note on how Dennett’s original formulation, a strongly eliminativist reductionist view, and a phenomenological account each fare against the two or three hardest requirements would help frame the classroom discussion. This is not an exercise in literature review, so do not provide a bibliography or citation list; I will supply those myself, and I want the students to see whether the conceptual architecture holds up independently of who proposed it.
