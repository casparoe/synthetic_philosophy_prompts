I am a retired experimental physicist who, after leaving my university post several years ago, began auditing philosophy seminars to understand the conceptual foundations I had long taken for granted. Recently, in a course on practical reason, a disagreement among my fellow auditors about whether normative force can exist independently of any conscious agent struck me as more than academic, because I am facing precisely this tension in a concrete decision I must help make.

I serve on the advisory board for the preservation of a decommissioned high-latitude observatory built in the early 1960s to study ionospheric propagation. The original architects arranged the antenna arrays and the spacing of auxiliary buildings according to geometric patterns that align almost exactly with local geomagnetic field lines, yielding interference levels that remain unusually low even by contemporary standards. Every design notebook, correspondence file, and engineer’s memorandum was destroyed in a flood decades ago; no living expert can reconstruct the rationale behind those particular angles and distances. At our last meeting, one colleague, a structural engineer, argued that without surviving intentions or present experts who can recover the reasoning, there is literally no normative consideration—no reason binding on us—to preserve that layout rather than demolish and redesign the site for modern logistics. Another colleague, an architectural historian, countered that the alignment itself responds to objective physical conditions in a way that generates its own justification: she spoke of the site as embodying a “rational structure” that the environment demands, regardless of whether any human mind currently grasps or endorses it, and insisted that our duty is to maintain the configuration not out of nostalgia but because the physics of the place itself issues a kind of directive.

Something in her claim resonates with my old scientific intuition that nature exhibits preferred states—energy minima, symmetry constraints—that feel normative without being intended, yet my engineer colleague’s objection also strikes me as formidable: if no one can state the reason, and no designer held it in mind, in what sense is it genuinely a reason rather than a poetic projection onto mechanical fact? I am trying to determine whether there is a philosophically defensible position that allows reasons to exist and exert force without being held by any reasoner—reasons that, so to speak, float free of minds—or whether the historian is committing a category mistake by attributing normative authority to inanimate arrangements. How should I think through this dispute? Is there a coherent account that takes her claim seriously without collapsing into mysticism, or must one reject the idea that justification can reside in structures that no one understands?
