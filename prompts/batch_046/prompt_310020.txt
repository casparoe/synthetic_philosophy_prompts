The metaontological dispute over whether disagreements in ontology—such as between defenders of unrestricted mereological composition and nihilists, or between mathematical Platonists and nominalists—are substantive claims about reality or merely verbal disagreements about quantifier meaning and theoretical regimentation has persisted for decades without resolution. The opposing camps share logical formalisms, agree on every empirical and mathematical result, and can systematically translate one another's descriptions into inter-definable frameworks. Yet one camp insists the other is speaking a different language with a deviant use of "exists," while the other insists the first is genuinely mistaken about the furniture of the world. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not about ontology itself but about unacknowledged divergence in methodological priorities: parsimony, theoretical unity, and fidelity to ordinary language pull in different directions, and each side mistakes its weighting of these virtues for an insight into what there is. Is that right, or is there a hidden substantive disagreement—perhaps about whether ontological commitment is framework-transcendent, about which formal results in model theory or grounding logic should count as data, or about whether explanatory priority is a real metaphysical relation? Diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually says: do they differ about hidden empirical assumptions, about the standards a metaphysical theory must meet, about which translations preserve subject matter, or about bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for? And tell me honestly whether anything could actually move it—say, a rigorous distinction between framework-internal and framework-transcendent existence claims, a formal result showing one framework cannot preserve all theoretical virtues simultaneously, or a demonstration that the question is ill-posed—or whether the impasse is permanent.
