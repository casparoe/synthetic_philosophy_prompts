I just finished a long and somewhat frustrating argument with a friend about whether the problem of dirty hands in politics is essentially a psychological drama—an individual leader knowing she has authorized a specific wrong—or a structural feature of collective decision-making that can dissolve personal guilt across institutional roles. We kept talking past each other: I insisted that the real philosophical bite comes when no single agent individually wills the harm, yet the harm is fully intended by the system’s aggregation rules; my friend kept returning to the visceral image of a single official ordering a rights violation and feeling the stain personally. I want to settle this pedagogically, not merely conceptually. I run a graduate seminar in political philosophy—fourteen students, ninety minutes, next Tuesday—and I need a runnable in-class simulation that forces participants to experience the structural version before anyone names it.

Design a game in which the group must allocate scarce rescue resources among four affected populations using a modified Borda-count ranking procedure. Each student receives a private card that reveals only two pairwise comparisons rather than a full preference ordering, and the aggregation rule is engineered so that the collectively selected allocation necessarily sacrifices one population to maximize overall survival, yet every individual voter can sincerely claim that her own submitted rankings never placed that sacrificed group in last place. Specify the exact card distribution for fourteen participants, the hidden-information structure, the materials needed (index cards, a visible tally board, and a sealed envelope for the revealed outcome), and a non-monetary payoff scheme—use a point system tied to post-simulation reflective writing or to a temporary public role designation within the room—that makes the collective result feel consequential without using real money. Include a step-by-step protocol for the ten-minute voting phase, the five-minute revelation, and the subsequent debrief that maps each student’s private ranking onto the final aggregated harm, showing precisely where individual innocence and collective guilt diverge.

Take a committed stance in your design: defend this specific Borda modification and card-distribution scheme rather than presenting alternative rule sets, and explain why any simpler majority-vote mechanism would fail to generate the intended experience of distributed culpability. Tell me exactly what will predictably go wrong the first time—most likely that students will blame the aggregation rules as unfair rather than recognizing their own structural complicity—and advise how to handle that reaction without delivering a front-loaded lecture. Finally, be explicit about which emotional feature of classic dirty-hands cases—the direct, first-person knowledge of having personally willed a specific wrong—this simulation structurally cannot reproduce, and state precisely how I should supply that missing element verbally during the debrief so the activity still connects back to the traditional problem.
