I'm working on a dissertation chapter on analogical cognition in visual categorization, specifically engaging with Douglas Hofstadter's Letter Spirit framework and its philosophical implications for how letterforms are recognized across stylistic variation. I want to compare two competing accounts of what makes every instance of the letter a belong to the same category.

The first account is the one developed in the Letter Spirit project: recognition proceeds through analogical mapping between grid-based representations, where the system identifies relational structures—such as a loop attached to a vertical stem on the right—that persist across differences in size, orientation, and embellishment. On this view, style is not noise to be eliminated but part of the relational fabric that analogical reasoning exploits; the essence of the letter is its flexible, context-sensitive spirit rather than any fixed feature list.

The second account is a position I am tentatively developing, which I call morphological invariance through feature locking. It holds that categorical membership depends solely on the preservation of a minimal topological skeleton—specifically, a single enclosed region adjacent to a linear stroke with a particular connectivity profile—and that all stylistic properties are genuinely accidental. Unlike prototype theories, this view denies family-resemblance structures or central tendencies; it posits a sharp, binary boundary defined by invariant geometric relations, with recognition functioning as passive detection rather than active construction.

Please compare these two accounts with respect to three issues. First, how does each handle extreme stylistic outliers, such as a highly ornamented blackletter a versus a minimalist geometric a? Second, can either accommodate the intuition that a broken or misshapen letter can still be an a, just poorly executed—without collapsing the distinction between recognition error and categorical deviation? Third, what is the role of the recognition process itself: is categorization an active, constructive analogical achievement, as in Letter Spirit, or a passive registration of invariant features?

In your comparison, be precise about where the views genuinely diverge versus where they might be reconcilable. Finally, identify any significant conceptual cost or blind spot that both accounts share, particularly regarding the boundary between letter recognition and broader visual concept formation.
