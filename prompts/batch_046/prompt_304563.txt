I want to teach them the thesis that theory is underdetermined by evidence—not as an abstract logical curiosity, but as a structural feature that can appear in their own research. Plan the seminar's explanatory arc: what conceptual dependency must be secured before the students will treat underdetermination as a live problem rather than a philosopher's parlor trick; specify the single concrete case study that should carry the session (it should involve two competing cognitive architectures—say, a predictive-processing account and a classical representational account—that generate identical behavioral and neuroimaging predictions for a specific task, with the point of evidential exhaustion made visible in the details); explain why a generic curve-fitting example is inadequate as the central vehicle for this audience; map the predictable misconceptions they will form in sequence—likely that underdetermination entails all theories are equally warranted, that it only arises with obsolete instrumentation, or that Bayesian model comparison automatically dissolves it—and indicate precisely where in the arc each should be intercepted; propose one diagnostic question I can ask at the close to test whether they have grasped the structural point rather than merely acquired methodological skepticism; and list which standard philosophical elaborations (for instance, the full Quine-Duhem thesis and the transient versus permanent distinction) ought to be named but deliberately left unexplicated at this level, with a brief rationale for each omission.
