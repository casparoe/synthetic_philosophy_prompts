I've been in an extended thread with other longtime rationalist readers about whether Buddhist anattā offers any philosophical purchase on personal identity that reductionist accounts—say, Parfit's psychological-continuity view—do not already provide. The discussion turned to a hypothetical case of gradual neural replacement, in which every neuron is slowly substituted with a functionally identical silicon analog and the subject reports continuous consciousness throughout; at that point someone began using the slogan 'there is no self' as an all-purpose dismissal, first to reject the intuition that the post-replacement subject is the same person, then to deny that there is anyone else to be wrong about, and finally to suggest that asking who survives is a category error on par with asking where phlogiston went. I want to treat that sentence as a piece of language to be disambiguated rather than as a settled doctrine.

Can you lay out the precise theses it could be expressing? I have in mind at least the metaphysical claim that no enduring substance underlies the five aggregates; the phenomenological claim that experience lacks an owner or subject; the normative claim that egoistic preference is rationally groundless; the semantic claim that the first-person pronoun lacks a genuine referent; and the methodological claim that personal-identity questions rest on a false presupposition of substantial selfhood. For each reading, say whether it is a trivial restatement of reductionism, a substantive and defensible philosophical thesis, or actually false; and tell me which of these readings, if any, actually address the neural-replacement scenario versus which are non-sequiturs that dissolve the question without answering it. In particular, separate what is genuinely settled among scholars of Buddhist philosophy—such as the distinction between conventional designation and ultimate absence of self—from what remains contested among interpreters, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus view.
