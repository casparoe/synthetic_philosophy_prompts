Over the past year I have been working through a family medical history that turned on the discovery, in a relative’s attic, of handwritten clinical ledgers from the 1950s. The entries describe symptom clusters that match a recently proposed genetic model—one formulated long after those ledgers were written—yet because the ledgers had been physically present but entirely unread for seventy years, I find myself uncertain whether they count, in the Bayesian sense, as old evidence, as newly discovered evidence, or as something that fractures the old/new distinction altogether. This ambiguity has become personally urgent, and I am now preparing the final examination for my graduate seminar on epistemology and scientific reasoning.

Please construct one 90-minute essay question, together with a model answer of approximately 800 words and a marking scheme totaling 25 marks. The course is an advanced graduate or upper-level undergraduate seminar; students are familiar with the basic machinery of Bayesian conditioning and with the problem of old evidence, but they should not be able to answer by reciting a canonical textbook formulation. Instead, the question must place them at a specific crux: they must distinguish the evidential status of the physical discovery of an archive (an event that updates beliefs about the existence of records) from the evidential status of the contents of those records once accessed (propositions that may have been true all along), and they must explain how this two-stage structure interacts with the bootstrapping worry—that using data to confirm the very theory that predicts or explains that data is circular. To prevent mere summary, inject a niche framing device: ask them to evaluate whether “archival latency”—the state in which evidence exists in the world but is epistemically inaccessible to every agent—creates a distinct category of confirmation that is neither old nor new, and whether resolving that category requires modifying the standard likelihood ratio or only our prior assignment over accessibility conditions.

The marking scheme should divide the 25 marks across five named moves, specifying for each the marks available, the exact conceptual step required for full credit, and the one-line characteristic mistake that typically earns only partial credit. In addition, provide a brief paragraph distinguishing an A-range answer (22–25) from a B-range answer (18–21): the distinction should turn not on length but on whether the student recognizes that the bootstrapping objection applies differently to the discovery event than to the content update, and whether they can articulate why conditioning on an accessibility prior does not by itself dissolve the old-evidence problem for the content.

Please do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citation instructions in the exam prompt itself. Do not ask students to quote specific authors. The model answer should be self-contained and should not depend on references students could only guess at.
