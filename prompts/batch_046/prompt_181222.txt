As a retired physicist recently turned to philosophy of science, I have sketched a short essay on Milton Friedman’s well-known 1953 methodological argument—the so-called F-twist—that unrealistic assumptions in models are permissible provided predictions succeed. Rather than a neutral survey, I want a committed evaluation: please take a definite stance and defend it.

My notes are these. First, in physics we constantly use point-mass or frictionless-plane idealizations, yet we do not treat mechanics as merely instrumental; we treat the falsehood as controlled. Second, I propose distinguishing isolating distortions—where we suppress a well-understood mechanism (friction, resistance) to expose an underlying law, with known correction terms available—from structural omissions, where we exclude variables (e.g., heterogeneous contact networks in epidemiological SIR models) not by design but by theoretical ignorance. Third, I claim realism is warranted only for isolating distortions, since their falsehood is transparent and correctable, whereas structural omissions give us no grounds to assert that the model captures underlying causal structure. Fourth, applying this to quantum field theory: renormalization employs infinite bare parameters that lack direct physical interpretation, but because they cancel through a rigorous procedure to yield finite observables, I classify them as isolating distortions rather than ignorance placeholders. Fifth, I conclude that the F-twist does not defeat scientific realism; it merely relocates realism from literal assumptions to correctable structural relations.

Please comment on the coherence of this distinction, especially my treatment of renormalization in the fourth note. Does it genuinely rescue realism, or does it collapse—perhaps because every unrealistic assumption is ultimately a structural omission at some deeper level? Give your reasons, say clearly which notes you would overturn if any, and state what my view predicts for a case I have not listed: climate models that parameterize cloud microphysics with empirically tuned coefficients lacking direct physical interpretation.
