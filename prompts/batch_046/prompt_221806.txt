I am preparing the closing session of my graduate seminar on existentialist ontology, and I would like a poem to leave with the group—something that does philosophical work rather than merely illustrating a concept. Write a Shakespearean sonnet (fourteen lines, ABAB CDCD EFEF GG rhyme scheme, iambic pentameter preferred but not mandatory) on Sartre's analysis of bad faith, specifically through the lens of the coefficient of adversity. In the octave, present a speaker—perhaps in the voice of the café waiter, but generalized—who treats his social role and the resistances he encounters (bureaucratic obstacles, physical fatigue, the weight of habit) as objective, externally given determinations that excuse him from choosing otherwise; let him invoke the coefficient of adversity as if it were a brute fact independent of any project. At the volta in line 9, pivot sharply: the resistance of the world acquires its adversarial coefficient only through the freedom that posits an end, so the speaker's appeal to obstacle is itself an exercise of the transcendence he denies. Use the sestet to unfold the anguish of this collapse—the recognition that there is no excuse, that facticity and transcendence are inseparable in the same consciousness, and that bad faith is not ignorance but a lie the soul tells itself. The closing couplet should state, with precision rather than consolation, that the waiter's denial of freedom is the very form of his freedom. The poem should be accurate enough that a specialist in phenomenology would recognize Sartre's structure rather than a generic existentialist mood; someone unfamiliar with Sartre should still find it a coherent, emotionally serious poem. Do not include a bibliography, citation, or reference to secondary literature.
