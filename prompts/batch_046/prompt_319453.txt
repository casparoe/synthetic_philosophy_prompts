I've spent some time thinking on my own about whether ideas and values can genuinely cause large historical changes, or whether they are always just explanations people invent after practical conditions have done the real work. One case that keeps puzzling me is Max Weber's argument that the Protestant ethic—especially the belief that disciplined labor and saving are signs of salvation, paired with a reluctance to spend on luxury—helped cause the particular form of modern capitalism that emerged in the West. I can see the intuition: if a culture systematically rewards reinvestment and punishes consumption, people will accumulate capital differently than they would in a culture that doesn't. But I am equally struck by the opposite possibility, which is that people only adopt those religious beliefs because economic structures are already pushing them toward accumulation, making the beliefs an effect or a justification rather than an independent cause.

I would like you to write an essay that takes this problem seriously and reaches a definite conclusion. Specifically, argue for your view on whether a value-system like the one Weber describes can be an independent cause of economic transformation, or whether it is always subordinate to material conditions, institutional arrangements, and available technologies. To avoid a generic summary, frame your discussion around a distinction I have been trying to work out: culture can act either as a "driving cause" that initiates or accelerates change beyond what material conditions alone would produce, or as a "selective cause" that merely determines which of several materially possible paths gets taken, without being necessary for change itself. Apply this distinction directly to Weber's thesis. If Protestant asceticism was only a selective cause, then modern capitalism might have developed through other cultural channels with roughly the same result; if it was a driving cause, then the specific religious content was necessary for the particular form and timing of capitalism as it actually occurred.

Weigh the main objections carefully—including the claim that religious doctrines are typically post-hoc rationalizations of existing economic interests, and the counter-claim that values can reshape individual incentives and social legitimacy in ways that purely material analysis cannot predict. Respond to these objections rather than simply listing them. At the end, do not finish with an uncommitted "it depends" summary. Defend a clear verdict: was Weber right that culture can drive history in this case, was he wrong because material conditions are always primary, or is the truth closer to one pole but qualified in a specific way? Explain why the opposing view is mistaken or incomplete. I am not asking for footnotes, bibliographies, citations, or a reading list—just careful reasoning about whether beliefs can genuinely be causes.
