At a study group discussing Kierkegaard’s Fear and Trembling, two members—Jordan and Reese—got stuck in a disagreement about Abraham and the teleological suspension of the ethical. Jordan argued that the suspension is best understood not as a temporary override of moral rules but as a structural breakdown of ethical communicability. Abraham cannot explain his intention to Sarah or Isaac because any explanation he offers—such as telling Isaac that God will provide the lamb—operates within faith but not within the universal language that ethics demands. For Jordan, this means the ethical is genuinely suspended in this singular case: the universal command against killing one’s child is not merely outweighed; its claim to public, universal justification is withdrawn entirely, leaving Abraham in an incommunicable relation to God.

Reese pushed back, saying Jordan confuses suspension with silence. The ethical never stops applying; Abraham’s anguish proves that the universal command remains fully in force. What is suspended is not ethics itself but Abraham’s ability to justify his action within ethical terms. Reese insisted that if the ethical were truly withdrawn, Abraham would feel no guilt or terror—he would be like a sociopath—whereas his suffering shows that the ethical is present but paradoxically coexists with a higher command. Reese added that Abraham’s statement to Isaac is not a breakdown of communication but a faithful expression that remains unintelligible to the ethical sphere; the suspension is therefore in the justification, not in the moral law.

Jordan replied that Reese’s view collapses the suspension into mere psychological tension. If the ethical command is still fully valid, then Abraham is simultaneously obligated not to kill Isaac and obligated to do so—an outright contradiction, not a paradox. Jordan said the only way to avoid contradiction is to recognize that in this singular case the universal is genuinely suspended, even if Abraham’s emotional attachment to Isaac makes him suffer.

Reese answered that contradictions belong to the understanding, not to existence. The ethical and the religious are incommensurable spheres, not contradictory ones. Abraham does not violate ethics because ethics, being universal, never applied to this singular relation in the first place—so there is nothing to suspend in the ontological sense. The teleological suspension is therefore a misnomer if read as ethics being turned off; it should be read as the universal being relativized by a higher telos without ever being annulled.
