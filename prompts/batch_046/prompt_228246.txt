You serve as an independent ethics auditor for a regional hospital consortium that reviews capital and clinical-allocation proposals every quarter. Each proposal—consolidating service lines, rationing scarce biologics, redirecting staff—creates concentrated burdens for one identifiable patient population and diffuse benefits for others. The consortium’s charter prohibits approval based solely on positive net utility; it requires you to apply the separateness-of-persons objection, which holds that harms and benefits do not cancel across distinct lives and that treating individuals as interchangeable containers of welfare violates the moral boundary between persons. You must produce a signed recommendation within a single working session, using only the information in the proposal file; you have no access to external literature, and your report must be readable by board members with no philosophy background. Your deliverable is not a one-off verdict but a reusable audit procedure.

Write the response in the following sections, in this order, with approximate lengths as indicated.

Section 1: The audit mandate and constraints (about 120 words). Define the practitioner’s role, the frequency and stakes of reviews, the information available, the sign-off requirement, and the specific philosophical commitment that must survive translation into practice.

Section 2: The three-filter decision procedure (about 450 words). Present a step-by-step checklist or flowchart in words that an auditor can run in sequence. Filter one must determine whether the proposal treats a burden on group A as offset by a benefit to group B in a single aggregate calculation; specify the test sentences or data points the auditor needs, what counts as a sufficient answer, and where the filter stops with a mandatory reject or flag. Filter two must check whether the affected group can be said to bear the burden as a separate person rather than as a fungible unit; include a proxy-consent check for populations that cannot consent directly, and mark the point where this becomes a value judgment rather than a factual determination. Filter three must assess whether the proposal contains a compensatory mechanism that respects separateness—distinguishing compensable burdens, such as temporary inconvenience with equivalent restoration, from incommensurable burdens, such as irreversible loss of a specific clinical access—and specify where the auditor must hand the decision to a human values committee rather than apply the rule mechanically. Include explicit hard-case branches: one where the burden is statistical rather than assigned to named individuals, and one where benefits and burdens fall on overlapping populations. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Section 3: Application — centralizing trauma services (about 250 words). Run the procedure on a proposal to close two neighborhood trauma centers and consolidate into one regional hub. The consolidation reduces average mortality citywide but permanently eliminates 24-hour surgical access for a low-income neighborhood with high trauma incidence. Show each filter’s operation, state the procedure’s verdict, and note any point where the auditor’s personal judgment diverges from the procedural output.

Section 4: Application — rationing a scarce biologic (about 250 words). Run the procedure on a proposal to allocate a limited supply of an enzyme-replacement therapy: full-dose treatment for 12 patients with severe, rapid-progressing disease versus partial-dose maintenance for 40 patients with moderate, stable disease. The aggregate quality-adjusted life years favor the partial-dose strategy. Show where Filter one blocks pure aggregation, how Filter two treats the two patient groups as separate, and what Filter three says about compensation. Give the procedure’s answer.

Section 5: Application — the unwelcome result (about 250 words). Run the procedure on a proposal to redirect funding from a long-running palliative-care unit serving terminal patients to a preventive-screening program serving a different, larger demographic. The palliative patients retain current care levels, so there is no direct harm, but the opportunity cost means no expansion of comfort services, while the screening program produces substantial aggregate welfare gains. Apply all three filters. The procedure should yield a verdict that the auditor committed to the separateness objection is likely to reject or find counterintuitive—either an approval that seems to violate the spirit of the objection, or a rejection that blocks an apparently harmless improvement. Explain exactly which filter produces the surprising result and why.

Section 6: Limits and misfires (about 200 words). State clearly what the procedure cannot do: whether it can resolve conflicts between different interpretations of separate person, whether it applies to probabilistic or future generations, and whether it can generate positive obligations about who must bear a burden or only block negative proposals. Identify one category of proposal for which the procedure is silent and must defer to democratic or clinical judgment. Conclude with a brief note on the risk of procedural rigidity—when following the checklist too literally produces morally perverse outcomes.

Ground the entire response in the normative logic of the separateness-of-persons objection without providing bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote verbatim from philosophical texts; paraphrase in your own words. Do not invent specific page numbers, publication years, or author attributions for particular formulations. If you refer to the distinction between compensable and incommensurable burdens, treat it as an operational tool derived from the objection rather than as a citation to a named theory.
