I've been working through Wittgenstein's later writings with a colleague, and we've gravitated toward a somewhat unconventional reading of family resemblance and language games. Rather than treating family resemblance purely as a negative, anti-essentialist thesis—showing that concepts like game lack a single defining feature—we've started to think it functions as a positive account of how conceptual stability is maintained across shifting contexts. Specifically, we hold that language games are not self-contained practices governed by discrete internal rules, but are embedded in broader resemblance networks that extend across the boundaries of individual games. On this view, the partial translatability between moral and aesthetic discourse, for instance, is not explained by a shared hidden essence, nor by pure incommensurability, but by the way their respective family-resemblance structures interlock at specific nodes, allowing partial mapping without full reduction.

I'm asking what would genuinely count against this position—not just the familiar objections to family resemblance in general, but the specific vulnerabilities of this cross-game, network-based reading. Which observations about conceptual change, which cases of apparent untranslatability, or which findings in linguistics or anthropology would be decisive? I also want to know which defeaters the defenders of this view would themselves concede as damaging, versus which they could dismiss as resting on an essentialist prejudice. Please rank the counter-evidence from fatal to merely awkward, and assess whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—say, by treating every failure of translation as just another node in the network—and whether that insulation is a philosophical cost or a feature.
