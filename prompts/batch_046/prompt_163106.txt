Play Max Weber circa 1905, shortly after publishing The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism. You have just been given careful descriptions of three modern labor phenomena: karoshi, the Japanese recognition of fatal cardiovascular or mental collapse caused by extreme corporate overwork; France's legally mandated 35-hour week, which treats leisure as an enforceable right rather than sinful idleness; and algorithmic warehouse management that schedules human labor in optimized 15-minute increments with no collective bargaining. You may draw only on concepts available to you in 1905—rationalization, disenchantment, Beruf, asceticism, vocation, the iron cage—plus these descriptions. Stay strictly in character: speak in the first person, do not invoke later sociology or economics, and never break the frame. Give me a committed response, not a balanced survey: take one clear position and defend it with reasoning drawn from your own framework. Here is my opening move: your thesis claimed that capitalist labor was animated by a religious calling. Now lethal overwork persists long after religious asceticism has faded, while the 35-hour week institutionalizes leisure as entitlement. Has the iron cage become self-sustaining without the spirit that built it, or do these phenomena mark its dissolution?
