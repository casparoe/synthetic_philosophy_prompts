I've been working through Gödel's 1949 cosmological solution and the philosophical argument he apparently constructed around it — that the existence of closed timelike curves in a rotating universe undermines the objectivity of temporal becoming, supporting a kind of temporal idealism influenced by his reading of Kant. A colleague in my philosophy of physics reading group summarized the position this way: Gödel demonstrated that general relativity permits worlds in which every event lies on a loop that returns to itself, so there can be no globally consistent ordering of "before" and "after"; since physics describes reality and physics contains no privileged global time-parameter, the passage of time must be subjective or illusory rather than a feature of the world-in-itself. I find the inference striking, but I want to understand how exposed it actually is. What would have to be true for this conclusion to be wrong?

I'm not asking only about the empirical objection — that observational cosmology gives us no evidence our universe rotates, and that isotropy measurements make a Gödel-style metric physically implausible. I want to know whether the philosophical move from closed timelike curves to the unreality of time is vulnerable even if we grant, for the sake of argument, that the metric describes a genuinely possible world. Could local temporal orientation survive intact despite global looping? Is there a meaningful distinction between "there is no global time coordinate" and "time itself is unreal" that Gödel's argument collapses? And what about the interpretive claim — advanced by some recent commentators — that Gödel's metric is best read not as describing a physically possible cosmos but as a reductio ad absurdum of applying Einstein's field equations globally without appropriate boundary conditions, so that if we take that reductio seriously the philosophical payoff about time's ideality disappears entirely? Which of these defeaters would a defender of Gödel's philosophical conclusion concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as missing the point? I would like an honest assessment: is this a view genuinely at risk from formal, conceptual, or interpretive challenges, or has it been insulated by qualifications that make it immune to refutation? In particular, I am inclined to think that local causality might survive global non-orientability, but I am unsure whether that intuition counts as a serious objection or merely an awkward fit.
