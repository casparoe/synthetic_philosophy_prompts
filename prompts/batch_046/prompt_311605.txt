I was arguing about evolutionary debunking in metaethics with a friend over coffee — you know, the standard line that our moral beliefs are shaped by selection pressures with no tracking relation to mind-independent moral facts, so moral realists should be skeptical. But I think people usually let constructivists off too easy: if there's no independent moral reality, then evolutionary influence isn't really a debunker, it's just genealogy. So I take the stronger view that evolutionary debunking is either useless (against non-realist positions) or it overreaches — apply the same reasoning to our basic logical and mathematical beliefs and you'd have to debunk those too, and defenders usually save mathematics by claiming it's indispensible for survival and prediction. That exemption feels ad hoc to me. I think the debunker either bites the bullet on math being unreliable, which seems wild, or admits the argument doesn't uniquely target morality. So my position is that evolutionary debunking is either self-undermining via overgeneralization, or it's only a problem for realists and therefore not the knockdown argument people treat it as.

What would actually count against this? I want honest defeaters — not just "that's not what the argument meant" but things that should genuinely move me. Maybe there's a principled reason mathematics is different (not just ad hoc), or maybe constructivism isn't actually immune because evolutionary influence can undermine justification even without realism. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me if I've already qualified my view into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that's a problem for an argument of this kind.
