Question: Define moral supererogation and explain whether accommodating it requires rejecting a maximizing conception of moral duty in favor of a satisficing threshold, or whether a two-level framework can preserve maximization for obligations while leaving room for excellence beyond them. Address the objection that if an act is genuinely excellent, failure to perform it reveals a moral defect, thereby collapsing supererogation into hidden obligation.

Student answer:
Supererogation names acts—such as risking one’s life for a stranger or donating well beyond any ordinary expectation—that are morally excellent yet not required. J. O. Urmson introduced the term to show that ordinary moral language recognizes a category beyond the obligatory and the forbidden. If every excellent act generated a corresponding duty, supererogation would collapse into obligation; the only stable resolution is to accept that moral reasons do not always determine what one must do.

A common worry is that recognizing supererogation undermines the principle that obligation presupposes ability, since it seems to praise people for what exceeds reasonable expectation. This is mistaken. That principle governs obligations, not evaluations of excellence; since supererogatory acts are not obligations, it applies to them only in the derivative sense that one can be praised only for what was within one’s power.

A two-level framework preserves the distinction: level one fixes duties through either a maximizing or a satisficing standard, and level two contains acts that exceed that standard. Some philosophers, including Kant, deny that any space exists above duty, on the grounds that imperfect duties already require promoting others’ welfare as far as one can without sacrificing necessary ends. But this actually supports the two-level view, because imperfect duties set a floor rather than a ceiling; once the floor is met, further promotion is supererogatory.

The central objection holds that failing to go beyond the floor reveals a moral shortcoming, which means the extra act was obligatory after all. This fails, because blame attaches only to falling below the floor. Once the floor is satisfied, further omission is not blameworthy, though it may be less admirable. Hence the two-level framework succeeds, and no hidden obligation remains.
