I'm defending my dissertation chapter next month in a seminar that draws together philosophers of logic who work on provability logic and epistemic paradoxes, philosophers of mind familiar with the Searle–Dennett exchange on original versus derived intentionality, and two cognitive scientists who study metacognitive architectures but have no formal logic background. The thesis I am advancing is that the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents—roughly, that any system rich enough to represent its own provability conditions and satisfy the Löb derivability conditions cannot consistently assign full authority to its own future outputs—does not apply uniformly across the original/derived intentionality divide. I argue that a system with only derived intentionality, of the kind Dennett describes using the two-bitser model, can stabilize limited self-trust precisely because its interpretive layer introduces a structural separation between the base representational system and the ascriptive mechanism that assigns content; this separation functions as a controlled blind spot that blocks the full reflexive closure required for the Löbian collapse. By contrast, I claim that a system with Searle-style original intentionality, where semantic content is intrinsic to the biological state and not mediated by a separate interpreter, lacks this modular buffer and therefore faces the obstacle in a more acute form—though I stop short of claiming that biological minds actually solve it, only that they do not solve it by the same architectural means.

I have sketched three premises: first, that derived intentionality requires an interpreter whose content-ascriptions are not themselves governed by the same derivational rules as the base system; second, that self-trust requires treating one's future reasoning outputs as authoritative within one's belief-revision policy; and third, that the Löb conditions apply only when an agent can represent its own provability predicate with sufficient arithmetical closure. From these I derive that a two-bitser architecture can maintain non-trivial self-trust only if its interpretive rules are opaque to itself within the domain of application—what I call a reflexive insulation—whereas original intentionality offers no analogous insulation mechanism.
