I was recently asked to serve on an ethics board reviewing whether a senior researcher had deliberately misrepresented clinical data, and in drafting my assessment I found myself unable to determine whether she genuinely believed her own exculpatory narrative or had constructed that belief precisely because it was professionally necessary. The ambiguity felt philosophical rather than merely psychological: if she knew the facts that undermined her belief, how could she also hold it? And if she did not know them, in what sense was she deceiving herself rather than simply mistaken? This made me want to see the problem dramatized in fiction rather than analyzed in abstraction.

Please write a short story about a pharmaceutical compliance officer who, on the eve of her retirement, is required to audit her own handwritten approvals from a drug trial conducted fifteen years earlier. Over the course of a single winter evening in her home office, she must determine whether her younger self genuinely believed the trial was safe, or whether she engineered that belief in order to preserve her identity as a cautious, ethical scientist. The narrative should avoid psychiatric clichés like denial or repression and should instead render visible the structural mechanics of self-deception—perhaps through partitioned attention, motivated interpretation of her own marginalia, or strategic suspension of assent—without deciding whether these mechanisms require a divided self or can operate within a unified consciousness.

Crucially, the story should end without resolving whether she is recovering a hidden truth or manufacturing a fresh rationalization, and it should include at least one concrete detail from the old trial records—say, a temperature log or a patient exclusion note—that functions differently depending on which interpretation the reader adopts. I am not looking for a psychological case study or a moral fable about corporate corruption; I want the story to live inside the conceptual puzzle itself, so that a reader finishes unsure whether self-deception is even possible in the form the protagonist suspects, or whether what looks like deception is only a later mind misreading an earlier one.
