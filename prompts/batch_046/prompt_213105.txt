You are an expert in comparative social philosophy and experiential pedagogy, with working knowledge of Western liberal autonomy theories and East Asian relational ethics. Before answering, spend a moment brainstorming three possible game mechanics that could make advanced students feel—rather than merely discuss—the tension between individualist and collectivist accounts of the person. Then develop one fully.

Design a 90-minute classroom activity for an advanced undergraduate or graduate seminar (about 16 students) on the relation between person and community in East Asian and Western thought. The activity should make participants experience the competing pulls of (1) Western liberal autonomy, where the self is ontologically prior to its relations and rights function as protective boundaries, and (2) East Asian relational selfhood, where relations like filiality or social role partly constitute identity. Crucially, inject the under-explored Japanese philosopher Watsuji Tetsurō’s concept of aidagara—“betweenness” or interstitial relationality—as a purported third option that rejects both atomized individualism and static collective hierarchy. The rules, roles, and incentives should be arranged so that students physically or decisionally encounter the problem: they should feel the instability of pure autonomy, the suffocation of pure collective duty, and the ambiguous demand of being constituted by a “between” that has no clear boundary.

Please provide: a clear setting and learning objective; the rules and materials (no real money—use points, status markers, or constrained choice architecture); assigned roles that map onto the three positions (autonomous agent, collective-role bearer, and “betweenness” mediator); a timeline with phases; the payoff or incentive structure that drives behavior; a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto autonomy, collective duty, and aidagara, explaining what each position gets right and where it breaks down in play; an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time you run it and how to handle it; and a frank note on which feature of Watsuji’s view—likely the temporal, embodied depth of interstitial existence developed over lifelong practice—this single-session simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator.
