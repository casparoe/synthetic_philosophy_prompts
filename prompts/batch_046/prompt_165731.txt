The debate over advance directives and whether your earlier self gets to boss around your later self has been stuck for decades — in bioethics journals, law reviews, even clinical ethics committees — and it seem's like nobody actually moves. One camp says the directive is binding because it is the same person extending autonomy across time, like a long promise to yourself; they talk about continuity of values and the right to shape your future. The other camp says once dementia or severe illness hits, you are basically a different psychological being, and making the present self suffer to honor some old document is just fetishizing a past preference. They cite changed interests, the impossibility of informed consent about a state you have never experienced, and the fact that we do not hold dead people responsible for contracts they signed.

But here is what bugs me: everyone frames it as who is the real self? and then picks a metaphysical answer, but the arguments do not actually depend on personal identity in any technical sense. The autonomy side does not need full psychological continuity — they just need normative authority. The present-interest side does not need to prove you are literally a different person — they just need to show current welfare trumps old paperwork. So is this disagreement substantive, or is it verbal, with both sides using the self and authority to mean different things? Or are they actually answering two different questions — one about rights, one about welfare — and pretending it is one debate?

I have a hunch it is partly hidden in how we treat compound directives. Say someone writes a directive at 35, amends it at 55 with a clause that only activates if they have advanced dementia and seem content, then at 75 with dementia they are genuinely happy and do not want treatment withdrawn. The autonomy people want to honor the 55 clause; the welfare people want to ignore both documents because the 75-self is thriving. But neither side has a clear rule for why amendments count differently from originals, or why contentment in dementia should override an explicit conditional instruction. Maybe the stall is that we are smuggling in an unstated theory about which mental states have normative weight, and without that theory the identity talk is just decoration.

So: diagnose this. Is it verbal about self and authority? Is there a hidden empirical question about how people actually change values in dementia? Are the sides answering different questions under one heading? Or is the question ill-posed because authority does not apply across psychological discontinuity the way we think? And given your diagnosis, what would actually make progress — a distinction between normative authority and metaphysical identity, an empirical study on value stability, a formal model of conditional directives, or should we just accept that no general principle exists and decide case-by-case?
