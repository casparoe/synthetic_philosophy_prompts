Critical theorists, however, have argued that the very rationality it unleashed became an instrument of domination — over nature, other people, and even the self. Focusing on the Frankfurt School's analysis of instrumental reason, explain (a) how enlightment rationality can turn into domination, and (b) whether this critique targets reason as such or only a particular distorted form of it. Student answer:
In Dialectic of Enlightenment, Horkheimer and Adorno argue that the Enlightenment was meant to replace myth with reason, yet they claim that myth was already enlightenment and enlightenment eventually reverts to mythology. The key mechanism is instrumental reason — reason reduced to calculation and control. Once nature and people are valued only for their utility, domination becomes systematic. Adorno and Horkheimer treat this as structurally inevitable: since reason's fundamental task is self-preservation, it must dominate whatever threatens survival. So enlightenment rationality is necessarily dominating, not merely distorted.

This analysis extends to culture. Habermas later developed the idea of the colonization of the lifeworld, but Adorno and Horkheimer had already shown that communication and everyday life get subsumed by bureaucratic and economic imperatives — the culture industry being the clearest case, producing the illusion of free choice. Nevertheless, they do not reject reason entirely. They distinguish subjective reason, which is purely instrumental, from objective reason, which once anchored universal values. Habermas calls the recovery of this dimension communicative reason, and Adorno essentially accepts that if we restore it we can salvage the emancipatory promise of enlightenment. Thus the critique applies to reason's instrumental form, not to reason per se.
