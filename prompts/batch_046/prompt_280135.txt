I am taking a seminar on logic, self-reference, and the architecture of messages, and I have run into a practical version of what we have been discussing. While cataloging donated papers for a local archive project, I found a sealed envelope dated 1992 with a handwritten note on the outside: "Read the enclosed letter backward; the opening paragraph is misdirection." The letter inside begins with a cheerful account of a family reunion, but when reversed the remaining text describes a serious betrayal. I cannot tell whether the envelope's instruction is part of the message—giving me access to an intended, hidden meaning—or whether it is an external frame added later, perhaps as a joke or mistake. If I file the document, should I classify it with the reunion records or with the conflict records? More generally, when a text carries its own decoding instructions, is its meaning located in the literal words, in the rule that transforms them, or only in the result of applying that rule?
