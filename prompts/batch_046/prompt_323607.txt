Here is an argument that has been troubling me in a graduate seminar on quantum foundations, and I want to see whether it genuinely collapses the psi-epistemic position or whether I have slipped somewhere between the premises.

(1) On a psi-epistemic interpretation, a quantum state |ψ⟩ represents an agent's incomplete information about an underlying ontic state λ, with different quantum states corresponding to probability distributions over a space Λ that may overlap.
(2) If |ψ⟩ and |φ⟩ overlap in Λ, then there exists some λ compatible with both preparations.
(3) Imagine, for the sake of argument, an idealized "ontic tomography" that reveals λ to an observer without altering the system's dynamics.
(4) An observer who performs such a tomography can identify λ, yet because λ is compatible with both |ψ⟩ and |φ⟩, she cannot determine which state was originally prepared.
(5) Nevertheless, quantum mechanics assigns distinct measurement statistics to |ψ⟩ and |φ⟩; so the observer must supplement her knowledge of λ with contextual information about the preparation procedure to recover the correct predictions.
(6) Now consider the alternative: that |ψ⟩ and |φ⟩ never overlap, so that each quantum state corresponds to a disjoint region of Λ.
(7) If every pair of quantum states corresponds to disjoint regions, then knowing λ uniquely determines |ψ⟩—which is precisely the claim that the quantum state is a direct property of reality, i.e., psi-onticism.
(8) Therefore, psi-epistemicism faces a dilemma: either distributions overlap, in which case the ontic state is always recoverable together with preparation context and the "incomplete information" interpretation becomes redundant; or distributions never overlap, in which case the interpretation collapses into psi-onticism.
(9) Consequently, there is no coherent intermediate position.

The conclusion strikes me as too sweeping, and I suspect there is either a scope error in (6)—perhaps it applies only locally rather than globally—or an equivocation in (8) between "recoverable with context" and "not genuinely epistemic." But I am unsure whether the fault lies in (3), in the inference from (4) to (5), or in the universal quantification hidden in (6). Which step should one reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the explanatory ambitions of psi-epistemic interpretations? I would especially like to preserve the idea that quantum states encode real informational limitations, but I am open to revising the framing of (3) if that is where the damage is done.
