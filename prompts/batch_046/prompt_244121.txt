As a novelist drafting a speculative dialogue in which Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz is resurrected into the early twenty-first century, I wish to place him in rigorous conversation with developments he never encountered. Specifically, I ask how he might defend, revise, or abandon his doctrine that this world is the best of all possible worlds when confronted with two posthumous challenges: first, the Everettian multiverse interpretation of quantum mechanics, which appears to actualize every compossible branch rather than selecting a unique optimum; and second, a marginal but conceptually sharp criterion drawn from algorithmic information theory, according to which the best world is defined not by maximal metaphysical richness or moral perfection, but by achieving the lowest Kolmogorov complexity relative to its total phenomenological output. Would Leibniz maintain that multiversal actualization violates the principle of sufficient reason by dissolving uniqueness into redundant abundance, or might he reinterpret harmony across branches? Would he reject informational minimalism as a confusion of descriptive economy with ontological perfection, or find in it an unexpected ally? I seek a reconstructed argument in his voice—reasoned, not archival—addressing whether compossibility should now be evaluated by algorithmic compressibility rather than by the density of coexistent perfections, and whether his theodicy survives a cosmos in which every possibility is equally real.
