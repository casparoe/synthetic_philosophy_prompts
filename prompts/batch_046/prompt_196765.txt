I am presently drafting a novel in which the protagonist possesses what I take to be a genuinely courageous disposition, yet every occasion upon which courageous action might manifest is interrupted by an external interfering condition—what metaphysicians term a fink—while she simultaneously maintains a social mask of profound timidity that becomes so internalised that neither she nor her observers recognise the underlying trait. This has led me to wonder whether such systematically masked dispositions pose a deeper challenge to philosophical analysis than is usually acknowledged, and I should value your own considered judgement.

Specifically, I ask for your credence, expressed as a precise figure between zero and one, that the existence of dispositions which are both finked and concealed behind an internalised social mask undermines the standard dispositional account of moral virtues, according to which possessing a virtue is partly constituted by having a stable disposition to act well in appropriate circumstances. Please decompose this credence into at least four constituent probabilities: the likelihood that a disposition requires at least counterfactual rather than actual manifestation conditions to be metaphysically real; the likelihood that an internalised mask counts as part of the agent's psychological constitution rather than an external interfering condition; the likelihood that virtue ethics can coherently accommodate hidden dispositions without collapsing into a form of behaviourism; and the likelihood that my novelistic scenario is conceptually coherent rather than metaphysically confused. For each, provide a rough number and a single line of justification, indicate whether you combine them multiplicatively or through some other weighting, and identify the term in which you are least confident together with the kind of conceptual distinction or hypothetical case that would most shift your view. Please compose your response in British English.
