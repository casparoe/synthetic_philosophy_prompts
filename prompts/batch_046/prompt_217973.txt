I serve on the interdisciplinary review panel for my university’s joint digital-humanities and philosophy-of-science fund. We are evaluating a proposal from the Center for Reconstructive Epistemics, a collaboration of archaeologists, machine-learning researchers, and philosophers of art, requesting two years of support to build immersive generative reconstructions of destroyed historical environments—specifically a twelfth-century scriptorium for which only partial floor plans, acoustic measurements of surviving analogues, and fragmentary practice-accounts remain.

Their core methodological claim is a metric they call counterfactual structural-stability auditing. Instead of scoring surface visual fidelity or alignment with a single canonical narrative, they propose to perturb the available evidence within documented uncertainty ranges, regenerate the reconstruction across thousands of such perturbations, and measure verisimilitude by the robustness of relational properties—light-flow hierarchies, acoustic resonance patterns, social-access topologies—across the resulting distribution. They contend this operationalizes verisimilitude as inter-model consistency under epistemic perturbation rather than as direct resemblance to an unknowable original, thereby avoiding what they term pastiche realism.

The panel is split. One faction treats this as a rigorous separation of epistemic humility from aesthetic pastiche, arguing that if a reconstruction’s structural relations hold across evidence perturbations, it has earned its verisimilitude regardless of surface appearance. Another faction holds that the audit dissolves the problem it claims to solve: a systematically wrong model could score high on stability, collapsing verisimilitude into predictive coherence with no anchor to the actual past. A smaller but vocal group adds that by abandoning surface fidelity entirely, the project may forfeit the historical and aesthetic obligations that make reconstruction meaningful rather than merely internally consistent.

Please draft the panel’s formal recommendation memo—approve outright, approve with conditions, or reject—with reasoning the researchers can respond to concretely. The memo should assess whether the audit genuinely captures verisimilitude or redefines it into unfalsifiability; it should treat the strongest objection on the losing side with intellectual honesty rather than dismissal; and if conditions are attached, they should specify what empirical or conceptual failure mode would actually expose the method to refutation, ensuring the thesis is genuinely risked rather than protected by definitional maneuvering. Write in a precise, analytically disciplined voice—someone accustomed to rationalist forums—who distinguishes sharply between conceptual analysis and empirical validation, and who is willing to fund if the conceptual risk is acknowledged and managed rather than denied.
