Here's an argument I built after thinking about moral revolutions, and now it seems to collapse the very view it was meant to support. (1) The thesis associated with Bernard Williams and Kwame Anthony Appiah holds that norms like slavery and dueling end mainly through shifts in honor, not through rational argument or material interest alone. (2) Honor, on this account, is a positional, common-knowledge good: it requires a peer community that mutually recognizes status rankings, so a practice becomes dishonorable only when it is common knowledge among peers that it lowers one's rank. (3) Therefore, for any norm to be revolutionized away via honor dynamics, there must exist such a peer-audience structure. (4) But many norms we now treat as moral—obligations to distant strangers, prohibitions on private cruelty to animals, duties to future generations—lack any clear peer community with shared status markers. (5) So if the thesis is correct, these norms should be permanently stable: there is no honor mechanism to eliminate them, and rational argument is said to be insufficient on its own. (6) Yet these norms do shift, and critics often credit rational argument (for example, expanding the circle of moral concern) for the change. (7) A defender might reply that even here hidden honor dynamics operate—for instance, being seen as enlightened or humane. (8) But that reply saves the thesis only by stretching honor to cover any form of social approval, collapsing it into ordinary social influence and making it indistinguishable from the rational or interest-based mechanisms it was meant to displace. (9) So either the thesis is restricted to norms with explicit peer-status structures like dueling, in which case it is not a general theory of moral revolution, or it applies everywhere and becomes unfalsifiable. (10) Since its proponents present it as a general explanation, the thesis proves too much and must be false. Where does this argument slip? If I want to keep the claim that honor plays a genuinely distinctive role, which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? Please answer in American English.
