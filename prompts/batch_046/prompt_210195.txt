You are a senior moral philosopher and policy adviser specialising in reproductive ethics, appointed to assist a regional clinical ethics review board that meets monthly to adjudicate non-therapeutic preimplantation genetic testing proposals. Before drafting anything, take a moment to brainstorm the conceptual architecture: distinguish carefully between welfare-maximisation, comparative advantage, structural stigma, and intergenerational lock-in, and identify exactly where a procedural checklist must surrender to value judgement rather than technical assessment.

Your task is to design a reusable decision procedure — a flowchart in ordered questions with defined stop-points and hand-off conditions — that the board can run in approximately ninety minutes per case. The board comprises one clinical geneticist, one ethicist, and one lay member; none may consult external literature during the session; and the written justification must be defensible in legal review. The procedure should operationalise a modified principle of procreative beneficence, which we shall call pluralist threshold beneficence. This holds that prospective parents have a pro tanto reason to select, among available embryos, the one expected to have the highest welfare, but only within a bounded domain of legitimate variation. Outside that domain, liberal eugenics demands not maximisation but a satisficing equality constraint. Specifically, the domain is bounded by three conditions: (i) the trait must not alter the social meaning of a protected characteristic in ways that generate structural stigma; (ii) the welfare gain must exceed an absolute satisficing threshold rather than being purely comparative; and (iii) the selection must pass a reversibility audit — the trait, if widely adopted, must not impose a multi-generational lock-in that restricts the reproductive autonomy or welfare options of the child’s future descendants.

Write the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions. For each stage, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, what evidence the board should seek, where an automatic denial applies, where the decision must be handed to collective value judgement (clearly flagged as non-procedural), and where the procedure terminates. Be explicit about the hard cases it will mishandle or misfire on, and state honestly what the procedure cannot do — for instance, resolving deep disagreements about disability identity or quantifying subjective welfare across different lives.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals. First, selecting an embryo with a high polygenic score for cognitive endurance, where the score explains only a modest proportion of variance and carries no known health trade-off, but where widespread adoption might generate a comparative welfare arms race. Second, selecting against an embryo carrying a variant associated with moderate chronic pain in adulthood, where the parents have direct lived experience of the condition and consider it severely welfare-reducing, but where disability advocates argue the variation is neutral and selection would express a harmful social message — this should be the case where the procedure yields an answer the board is likely to find unwelcome. Third, selecting for an immune-enhancing allele that confers resistance to a regionally endemic pathogen but is linked to a slightly elevated autoimmune risk in the child’s own potential offspring, thereby triggering the reversibility audit.

Throughout, use British English spelling and phrasing (for example, behaviour, programme, judgement, defence). Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not invoke highly specialised theories unknown to a well-read but non-specialist model. Keep the conceptual machinery visible but translate it into steps that a practitioner without philosophical training could actually execute. Conclude by summarising the procedure’s limits and the conditions under which the board should suspend it in favour of direct deliberation.
