The debate over whether Frankfurt-style counterexamples defeat the principle of alternative possibilities has remained in a stable equilibrium for decades. Both sides accept the canonical blocker structure: an agent decides and acts for their own reasons while an intervener stands ready to compel that very action should the agent show signs of deciding otherwise, yet the intervener never actually intervenes. Defenders of the principle typically insist that some “flicker of freedom” survives—usually a prior mental event that could have triggered the blocker—while critics maintain that any such flicker is either absent or causally irrelevant to the actual sequence that produces the action. Despite successive refinements—hierarchical models of agency, source-based conditions, arguments about modal fragility—the literature has not moved toward consensus.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not mainly about moral responsibility or the metaphysics of action, but about a hidden ambiguity in what “being able to do otherwise” denotes. One camp seems to treat it as a modal-accessibility claim: there exists a nearby possible world in which the agent chooses differently. The other camp treats it as a claim about causal-pathway openness in the actual sequence: whether the agent’s decision process contains a live divergence node, regardless of whether that node is ever actualized. If this is correct, the two sides may be answering different questions—one about metaphysical modality, the other about causal structure—while using the single phrase “alternative possibilities.”

Diagnose this impasse for me. Is the disagreement verbal, turning on different uses of “ability”? Is it substantive but driven by bedrock intuitions about blame that neither side can argue for? Is there a hidden formal question about how to embed modal claims inside causal models? Or are the sides genuinely addressing different subject matters—normative conditions for responsibility versus the ontology of decision processes—under one heading? Be concrete: show how each side’s treatment of the blocker case and of flickers supports your diagnosis. And given that diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction that separates the two notions of “ability,” a formal result about modal causation, a reframing of the question, or is this an impasse that should not be expected to resolve? Give reasons, not a survey of positions, and an honest assessment of whether anything could actually end the stall.
