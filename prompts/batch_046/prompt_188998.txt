Write a dialogue between two scholars reviewing a proposed deployment framework for an AI system, set in an academic peer-review session rather than an abstract debate. The first speaker should be a machine-learning safety engineer who defends a narrow, technical definition of alignment: intent alignment is the faithful execution of a formally specified objective, and any normative disagreement about ends belongs to governance, not to engineering. The second speaker should be a moral philosopher of institutional design who argues that in socio-technical systems this separation collapses, because formalizing intent—choosing metrics, defining the optimization boundary, specifying what counts as a user—already embeds contested value judgments that cannot be bracketed without rendering the mandate either incoherent or concealed.

Ground the exchange in a concrete, somewhat niche case they are jointly evaluating: a drought-management AI that dynamically rations water among agricultural, residential, and ecological users through a weighted utility function. The specification explicitly weights agricultural yield, residential health, and ecosystem preservation, but it leaves undefined how to adjudicate claims based on historical water rights versus present need versus future sustainability, and it treats "stakeholder" as a technically defined input without resolving whether indigenous territorial claims or long-term contractual rights are included in the weight set. The engineer should defend that the system is intent-aligned if it optimizes the given weights exactly, regardless of whether those weights are ethically defensible, and should cite specific failure modes—proxy gaming, reward corruption, specification drift, and distributional shift—that arise precisely when values are smuggled into the objective function rather than held in the governance layer. The philosopher should respond not by denying those failure modes but by arguing that the selection of metrics, the temporal window of optimization, and the boundary of who counts as a user are themselves design-level value commitments; relegating them to an external governance body either hides normative power behind technical neutrality or produces a system that is procedurally faithful to an ethically unstable mandate.

Require genuine back-and-forth: each speaker must directly address the other’s previous point, neither should be a strawman, and the dialogue should end without full resolution—perhaps converging on one empirical prediction about where a purely intent-aligned version of this water-rationing system will fail in practice, while remaining divided over whether the proper remedy is tighter formalization or explicit value deliberation integrated into the specification itself. Maintain an academic but accessible tone, avoid requests for bibliographies or citations, do not invoke ultra-obscure theoretical frameworks unknown to generalist readers, and keep the conceptual tension alive through the internal logic of the drought-rationing case rather than through generic examples.
