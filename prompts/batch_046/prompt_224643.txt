I serve on the Joint Ethics and Funding Review Panel of the Helix Research Foundation, and we must circulate a formal written opinion before next month’s board vote on a proposed pilot called the Doxastic Incentive Pilot. Under the pilot, any research team that publishes a structured, peer-reviewed retraction of one of its own previously defended hypotheses within a funding cycle receives a supplemental grant equal to fifteen percent of its base award. Proponents argue that current funding structures reward only confirmation, thereby giving scientists rational incentives to resist belief revision; the pilot is meant to correct that asymmetry. Opponents invoke the wrong-kind-reasons problem: if a team retracts in order to secure the supplemental funding, then the financial reward supplies a reason of the wrong kind for the underlying belief change. On this view, even if the retracted hypothesis was indeed false, the team’s revised attitude is not genuine intellectual humility but mercenary doxastic adjustment, and the epistemic value of the retraction is corrupted at its root. The panel is divided among approving the pilot with institutional safeguards, rejecting it outright as conceptually incoherent, and approving it only for non-competitive, unconditional endowment-style grants that do not tie payment to any particular research outcome.

Please draft the panel’s opinion memo. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—together with an implementation timeline and specific evaluative criteria the Foundation should apply at a six-month review point. The memo must engage the wrong-kind-reasons objection seriously, including a careful distinction between reasons that motivate the publication act and reasons that motivate the underlying belief revision, and it should explain whether institutional design can filter out the wrong kind of reason without eliminating the incentive altogether. It should also treat the strongest consideration on the losing side with intellectual honesty rather than dismissing it, and it should state what the recommended policy predicts for a borderline case—say, a team that discovers an error independently but delays publication until the pilot window opens. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; reason directly from the conceptual structure of the problem.
