I am designing a conceptual sorting instrument for an advanced seminar on macrohistorical forecasting, specifically on how analysts should apply reference-class reasoning to predict institutional continuity in large, democratic, multi-ethnic federations—polities that combine democratic governance, high economic integration, and the absence of a single dominant ethnic core. The problem we keep circling is whether standard outside-view methods, which draw base rates from imperial collapses and state dissolutions, can be applied without modification to cases like modern democratic unions, or whether the reference class must be restructured around institutional form rather than scale and diversity alone. I want a decision tree that sorts methodological positions by what practitioners actually believe about analogical reasoning, not by the jargon they adopt.

Please build a taxonomy with six to eight terminal positions arranged as a branching tree. Each internal node should pose a concrete diagnostic question that a forecaster could answer without knowing the name of any theoretical school: for instance, whether territorial scale and demographic diversity should outweigh institutional form when selecting historical analogues; whether democratic legitimacy functions as a structural break that removes a case from the imperial reference class or merely as a confounding variable within it; whether base rates should be calculated uniformly across all collapses in a broad dataset or weighted by institutional similarity to the target; and whether the heterogeneity of the reference class demands withholding quantitative point estimates in favor of qualitative scenario ranges. The leaves should include positions such as a strict uniform-base-rate view, a structural-break exclusion view, a weighted-analogue synthesis, a skeptical reference-class-nihilism about heterogeneous categories, and any other named or unnamed combinations that occupy the logical space. For every combination that remains unoccupied—say, a view that treats democratic legitimacy as decisive for classification but still applies unweighted imperial base rates—provide a brief diagnosis of whether it is incoherent, pragmatically unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves that your branch structure cannot genuinely separate, and indicate what additional case or distinction would be needed to split them. The instrument is for graduate students in forecasting and political history, so phrase the branch questions as direct judgments about a specific forecasting task—predicting the fifty-year institutional continuity of a democratic, economically integrated, multi-ethnic federation—rather than as abstract definitional quizzes. Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for named scholars; the goal is to map the conceptual landscape so that a practitioner can locate their own commitments.
