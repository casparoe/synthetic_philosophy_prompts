I have been working through the Aristotelian distinction between oikonomia—the management of household resources directed toward natural use and consumption—and chrematistikê, the art of wealth acquisition that treats money as an end rather than a medium of exchange, thereby lacking any inherent limit tied to need. I am inclined to defend a modernized version of this view: that financial practices become structurally corrupt, in Aristotle's sense, when accumulation is fully decoupled from a teleology of use, and that modern economies built on credit creation and investment returns risk precisely this decoupling, since monetary value no longer tracks any commodity with intrinsic utility.

But I want an honest assessment of this framing's vulnerability, not a defense. What empirical or conceptual developments—say, the argument that all modern fiat money is instituted primarily as a tax-credit instrument and therefore carries built-in social purpose, or the observation that investment and consumption are now institutionally interdependent in ways that blur the boundary—would have to be true for the oikonomia/chrematistikê distinction to collapse entirely? Which of these defeaters would a committed Aristotelian have to concede as genuinely damaging, and which could be absorbed by refining what counts as a "natural limit"? Please rank them from fatal to merely awkward. I also ask that you say what would change your mind—whether a specific institutional feature of central banking, a conceptual argument that value is necessarily relational rather than use-bound, or historical evidence that Aristotle's own exchange theory was unstable without the boundary he tried to enforce—and indicate where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
