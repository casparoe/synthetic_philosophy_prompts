Since I began volunteering with patients recovering from general anesthesia and have been reading about awareness during surgery, I have become preoccupied with a tension in my own judgments about phenomenal consciousness and the reports people make about it. I want to know whether there is a coherent principle behind the following verdicts, or whether some must be surrendered.

Case 1: A patient who was fully anesthetized and later reports, “There was absolutely nothing—not even darkness,” strikes me as accurate. I am inclined to say this negative judgment is achieved through purely physical mechanisms that track the absence of relevant neural correlates; no phenomenal state guides the report, because there was none.

Case 2: A philosopher writing a sustained argument that phenomenal consciousness is epiphenomenal—concluding that qualitative experience plays no causal role—strikes me as reaching conclusions genuinely informed by her direct introspective access. If her phenomenal states truly played no causal role in producing those sentences, the argument’s specific content would seem like an uncanny physical coincidence.

Case 3: A blindsight subject who sincerely answers “No, I see nothing” in the blind hemifield, yet correctly points to stimuli, strikes me as making an accurate phenomenal judgment that tracks absence, while the pointing behavior is purely subpersonal and non-phenomenal.

Case 4: A hypothetical philosophical zombie—physically and functionally identical to the philosopher in Case 2—writing the identical paper strikes me as producing the same sentences through purely physical dynamics, with no phenomenal guidance whatsoever. This makes me think the physical mechanisms in Case 2 are fully sufficient, and the philosopher’s introspection is causally redundant.

Case 5: A subject under ketamine who reports, “My ego has dissolved into pure boundary-less awareness,” strikes me as describing an altered phenomenal landscape that causally shapes the specific content of the report; a zombie with identical brain dynamics but no experience would, I think, be confabulating rather than genuinely tracking anything.

Case 6: A child learning the word “conscious” through behavioral feedback and linguistic training strikes me as acquiring the concept without any direct phenomenal calibration being causally necessary for possession.

These six pull in opposite directions: in Cases 2 and 5 I want phenomenal states to do genuine causal work in shaping judgments, while in Cases 1, 3, 4, and 6 I treat physical or functional mechanisms as fully sufficient. I have wondered whether the distinction lies among first-order phenomenal calibration, meta-cognitive absence-tracking, and conceptual possession mediated by language, but I cannot make that division save every verdict.

Is there a precise principle—perhaps with explicit conditions on when phenomenal access is causally efficacious versus when functional proxies take over—that accommodates all six? If not, which verdict should be overturned and why? Does the principle treat her report as genuinely phenomenally calibrated, as functionally mediated, or as confabulatory?
