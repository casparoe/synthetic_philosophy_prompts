Two friends of mine, both well-versed in Kant's moral philosophy, got into a sustained disagreement over dinner about the classic murderer-at-the-door scenario, which they had been discussing in connection with Kant's essay on lying in response to Benjamin Constant. Mara advanced what she called an 'agency-corruption' interpretation: she argued that Kant's absolute prohibition on lying is not mainly about safeguarding the victim's rights or preserving social trust, as many readers assume, but about the liar's own rational nature. To utter a falsehood with deceptive intent, she said, is to treat one's own faculty of judgment as a mere instrument for producing outcomes, thereby violating the Formula of Humanity applied to oneself. Even if the lie saves an innocent life and harms no one, the liar corrupts the internal consistency required for autonomous agency. Jonas objected that this reading is anachronistic. He said Kant's essay explicitly frames the wrong in terms of damaging the rights of humanity in general and undermining the universal practice of truth-telling; the self-corruption argument, he claimed, imports a later neo-Kantian concern with self-constitution that is not present in the text. He also noted that Kant permits silence or evasion, so the agent is never forced to speak falsely; if silence is dangerous, one can physically obstruct without making a false assertion. Mara replied that in realistic contexts — for example, if the murderer asks whether the victim is upstairs and the respondent stands silently in the doorway — silence itself functions as a communicative act and conveys assent. Jonas then argued that even if silence communicates, a principled distinction remains between actively asserting a falsehood and allowing one's body or posture to be interpreted: the former involves a direct contradiction of one's rational commitment to truth, while the latter involves only permitting inference. Mara countered that this distinction collapses into formalism, because the protective intent and communicative effect are identical; treating the logical form of the utterance as morally decisive seems to prioritize grammar over the actual exercise of agency.
