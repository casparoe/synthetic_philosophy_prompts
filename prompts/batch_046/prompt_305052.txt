In my reporting on the philosophy of climate science, I have watched a debate stall that seems to turn on incommensurable values. Researchers selecting climate models generally agree that predictive accuracy, explanatory transparency, and justice-sensitive parameterization are all legitimate scientific aims, yet they split sharply over whether these aims can be rationally traded off. The accuracy-first camp treats transparency and justice as instrumental constraints ultimately subordinate to predictive success, while the pluralist camp—invoking Ruth Chang’s idea that values can be “on a par”—insists that these three aims resist aggregation into a single metric and must be weighed without a common scale. Despite sharing Chang’s vocabulary, the sides have not moved in over a year of exchange. Is this disagreement hiding an empirical question about how model-selection committees actually function or whether transparency improves long-run prediction? Is it a normative dispute about whether scientific rationality permits irreducible plural values? Or is it, as I suspect, largely verbal—one side using “incommensurable” in a metaphysical sense (no common measure exists in principle) and the other in a practical decision-theoretic sense (no current algorithm resolves the trade-off)? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: an ethnographic study of real selection committees, a formal result in multi-objective optimization, or a conceptual clarification that dissolves the conflict?
