Write a short story that dramatizes a philosophical idea about emotional disclosure through artificial form: specifically, the view—associated with Bernard Williams’s essays on opera—that extreme stylization (characters expressing grief through ritualized song, plots governed by symbolic rather than psychological logic, gestures that violate naturalistic proportion) does not insulate an audience from genuine feeling but instead strips away the ordinary realistic clutter that usually conceals the bare architecture of grief, desire, or moral confrontation. Set the narrative in an entirely non-musical context—perhaps a synthetic virtual environment with rigid formal rules, a highly codified institutional ceremony, or an artificial social game—where the framework is visibly contrived and implausible by everyday standards, yet the characters reach an emotional clarity within it that they cannot access in ordinary interaction. The contrivance must be structurally essential, not decorative: show how a particular formal constraint or ritualized action removes conventional self-presentation and exposes the underlying shape of the emotion at stake. Do not have the characters discuss philosophy; let the concept emerge from the narrative architecture alone. The story should be self-contained and need not mention opera or its philosophers by name, but its underlying logic should be recognizable as the inversion of the usual suspicion that artificiality equals emotional distance.
