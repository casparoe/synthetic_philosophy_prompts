Consider a heuristic argument for the presumption of independence in probabilistic causal inference: prior to observing data, one should treat variables as independent unless specific evidence or structural reasoning links them. Suppose one attempts to formalize this presumption within a Bayesian network framework by assigning every potential directed edge an independence prior weighted by a parameter ε > 0, and then treats the limit ε → 0 as the ideal rationalization of the heuristic—representing maximal initial skepticism about connections. In that limit, the prior over graph structures collapses onto the fully disconnected graph, assigning prior probability zero to any causal link. From this formalization, it seems to follow that if the presumption of independence is made mathematically rigorous, then any observed dependency in the data must have prior probability zero, which would prevent Bayes' rule from ever updating belief away from total independence. Does this imply that formalizing the heuristic self-defeatingly eliminates the possibility of learning causal structure from evidence? Or is there a principled way—perhaps by treating ε as a methodological default rather than a literal prior mass, or by separating the heuristic's normative role from its probabilistic representation—to maintain the presumption without rendering empirical inference impossible?
