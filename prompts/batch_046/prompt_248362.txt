I serve on the allocation committee of the Meridian Civic Endowment, a public-private trust that must distribute one remaining grant of $180,000 before fiscal close. We have two technically qualified finalists that pull in opposite directions on whether compatriots carry special moral claims.

Proposal A, the Local Archive Initiative, seeks full funding to digitize oral-history collections from three working-class neighborhoods within our city. The applicants and primary beneficiaries are fellow citizens and local institutional partners; the project strengthens shared civic infrastructure that the Endowment was chartered to support.

Proposal B, the Remote Scholarship Bridge, requests the identical sum to provide direct educational subsidies and connectivity infrastructure for students in a conflict-affected region abroad. The applicants have no local presence; the beneficiaries share no institutional or national membership with the Endowment's constituency; but independent evaluators estimate the welfare gain per dollar is roughly four times that of Proposal A.

Our charter contains an untested "dual mandate clause." It requires every allocation to satisfy both a "proximity-weighted capability threshold"—a measurable capability improvement for persons standing in a relation of shared institutional membership with the fund—and a "global marginal utility floor," meaning no grant may fall below a baseline cross-border welfare efficiency. Here, Proposal A clearly meets the proximity-weighted threshold but sits near the global floor, while Proposal B exceeds the global floor but fails the proximity-weighted threshold entirely, since its beneficiaries are outside the shared institutional community.

The committee is split. One faction holds that the charter orders these mandates lexically: associative obligations generated by shared institutional membership must be satisfied before cosmopolitan efficiency considerations apply. The opposing faction holds that the proximity-weighted threshold is only one distributive weight among others, and that when the welfare differential is this large, compatriot claims are overridden.

Please draft the committee's formal written opinion recommending a concrete decision—approve A, approve B, approve a split or conditional award, or reject both with conditions for resubmission—with reasoning structured so the board could circulate and vote on it. The opinion must include a clear bottom line, must treat the strongest argument for the losing side with genuine seriousness rather than dismissal, and must acknowledge the specific moral cost of the recommended course. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
