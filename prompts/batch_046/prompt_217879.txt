I'm a doctoral student in moral and political philosophy working on the normative foundations of institutional authority, and I recently found myself defending an unorthodox synthesis in a workshop on ideal theory and counterfactual consent. The position I want stress-tested is this: legitimate authority need not rest on actual endorsement by those subject to it, but rather on what I call a coherent extrapolated authorization—the verdict that a rational agent, were she to possess complete information about the consequences of an institutional arrangement, resolve all internal inconsistencies in her preference structure, and undergo reflective equilibrium with her own deeper commitments, would volitionally ratify being governed by that arrangement. I apply this specifically to domains where present, revealed preferences are systematically distorted—through adaptive preference formation, informational asymmetry, or structural coercion—so that taking them at face value seems normatively inadequate. The aim is to preserve a robust form of autonomy, since the authority remains anchored in what the agent herself would want rather than in an external conception of the good, while still allowing paternalistic institutional design in areas like public health architecture or algorithmic governance.

What I need is not a list of familiar objections but a rigorous map of what could actually defeat this framework. Does the coherence condition implicitly smuggle in a substantive, contestable theory of practical reason that makes the counterfactual less neutral than it appears? Is the extrapolation epistemically inert in a way that renders it unfalsifiable and therefore normatively empty? Could formal results from social choice theory—concerning the instability of aggregated preferences or the impossibility of deriving a collective will from individual rationality conditions—undermine the move from individual extrapolation to institutional legitimacy? Are there vulnerabilities in the philosophy of action or integrity, perhaps analogous to Bernard Williams's concern that certain reflective motivations involve one consideration too many and thereby corrupt practical reasoning, where subjecting one's preferences to this kind of rational reconstruction violates something essential about agency? And if the view can be insulated against these challenges only through further qualifications, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a sign that the position risks empirical emptiness?

Please distinguish between objections that defenders of this approach would themselves concede are genuinely damaging, even if they believe the damage can be repaired, and objections they would dismiss as resting on a misunderstanding of the counterfactual nature of the claim. Rank the potential defeaters from fatal—those that would require abandoning the core idea—to merely awkward—those that force adjustments in scope or application but leave the framework intact. I am particularly interested in whether there is any conceptual or formal result showing that coherence conditions plus information completeness necessarily collapse into a unique preference ordering, and whether such a result, if it exists, would be disastrous for value pluralism or an unexpected resource for the view.
