I sit on the Academic Integrity Appeals Panel at my university, and we face a case that turns on a conceptual question I first encountered in a philosophy of mind seminar but now find genuinely unresolved. A senior researcher, Dr. Elena Voss, was sanctioned for selectively excluding outliers in three published studies without disclosure. The investigation confirmed she did this, yet Dr. Voss maintains she never believed her actions improper—she describes her mental state as being genuinely convinced the exclusions were methodologically justified, though she now recognises she was motivated not to scrutinise them too closely. Our institutional code distinguishes intentional deception, which warrants dismissal and full retraction, from cognitive distortion without full awareness, which requires mandatory remediation and supervised review. Several panel members argue that self-deception is conceptually incoherent: if one genuinely holds a belief, one cannot simultaneously hold its negation and actively conceal it from oneself, so her claim must be either false or a post-hoc rationalisation, placing this firmly in the intentional category. Others respond that divided attention and emotional salience can explain how someone avoids confronting an unwelcome belief without requiring a metaphysically partitioned self, though they disagree on whether this saves the concept or merely renames motivated irrationality.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. Recommend a concrete course of action—whether to uphold dismissal, impose remediation, or design an intermediate sanction with a clear timeline for review—and provide reasoning that takes the conceptual objection seriously rather than treating it as settled by psychology. Specifically, address whether explaining self-deception through degrees of attention and affective framing avoids the charge of incoherence, or whether any such account still smuggles in a divided agency that unitary theories of the self must reject. Your recommendation should not pretend to resolve the metaphysical dispute conclusively, but should take a defensible stance on culpability given that uncertainty, and should include an honest treatment of the strongest argument for the course of action you ultimately reject.
