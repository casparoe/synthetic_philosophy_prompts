I am currently serving on a faculty working group that must redesign our university’s grievance protocol, and this has made me acutely aware that I have no non-circular reason for preferring one moral-cultural logic over another. I am therefore preparing to facilitate a ninety-minute postgraduate seminar for twelve students who have just completed a module on comparative social ethics. I would like you to design a structured, participatory classroom activity—ideally a constrained role-play with assigned institutional roles and a symbolic, non-monetary payoff scheme—centred on a finely-grained dispute: a senior researcher has publicly criticised a junior colleague’s methodology using language that different parties interpret, respectively, as an assault on honour, an affront to dignity, or the infliction of victimhood requiring formal institutional redress. The rules should be arranged so that participants must first navigate the crisis using the internal logic of each culture in turn—before they are permitted to name the framework—thereby experiencing the coherence and emotional pull of each resolution for themselves. In particular, the same procedural rule (whether redress requires a public ritual of restoration, a private recognition of equal status, or an institutional validation of subjective harm) should generate incompatible moral outcomes under the three frameworks, so that the group discovers the divergence through action rather than exposition. The debrief should map each experiential turning-point onto the conceptual architecture of honour, dignity, and victimhood cultures; it should predict what will go wrong on the first running—such as participants conflating honour with dignity or treating victimhood as mere emotional fragility—and it should be frank about which feature of victimhood culture, specifically its dependence on third-party institutional recognition of harm that may not be externally observable, the simulation cannot fully reproduce and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator. Please write your response in British English, avoid requesting bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not ask participants to recall specific theoretical texts by name.
