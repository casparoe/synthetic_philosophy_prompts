Read Voltaire's Candide through the lens of Leibniz's moral metaphysics and theodicy, but resist the easy caricature of Panglossian optimism. Specifically, apply Leibniz's tripartite taxonomy of evil—metaphysical evil (the finitude and limitation intrinsic to any created being), physical evil (suffering, disease, and natural disaster), and moral evil (sin and vice produced by free rational agents)—together with his claim that each form is not directly willed but is instrumentally necessary within the best compossible world, since its removal would entail either a logical contradiction or the loss of a greater good that depends upon it. Analyse key episodes in these terms: the Lisbon earthquake and the subsequent auto-da-fé, where physical and moral evils coincide; Pangloss's syphilis and its chain of transmission, in which moral choice produces physical suffering; Cunégonde's repeated enslavement and disfigurement, where institutionalised moral evil generates sustained physical harm; the mutilated Surinamese slave, whose bodily suffering exists to sustain the comfort of others; and Candide's eventual withdrawal to cultivate his garden, which abandons metaphysical explanation for practical labour. For each case, ask whether Voltaire's satire actually targets the correct Leibnizian category, or whether it collapses limitation, natural law, and human cruelty into an undifferentiated spectacle of misery that Leibniz's distinctions would in fact separate. Where does the picaresque accumulation of disasters resist the idea of compossibility—does the narrative present suffering that yields no visible compensatory perfection? Does the absence of narrative redemption test the theodicy more severely than abstract argument can? Consider also whether Candide's conclusion represents a genuinely Leibnizian practical morality—acting within one's sphere of achievable goods—or a tacit admission that metaphysical optimism is useless for ethical life. Give a reading faithful in both directions: do not reduce Voltaire's art to an illustration of philosophy, and do not distort Leibniz's distinctions to make the satire fit. End by identifying what Candide's narrative form and its specific horrors add to the philosophical debate about evil and optimism that pure reasoning alone cannot supply.
