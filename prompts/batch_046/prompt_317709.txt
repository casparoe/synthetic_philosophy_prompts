I am drafting a chapter on intertemporal rationality for my PhD and have become stuck on a framing issue that came up in a reading group with decision theorists. As I understand the canonical economic account, honouring sunk costs is irrational because a rational agent ought to maximise expected utility from the present forward; since past expenditures are irrecoverable by definition, they should drop out of the decision calculus entirely. I have taken this to be not merely a pragmatic heuristic but a direct consequence of applying the independence axiom across temporal partitions—if my preference ordering at time t ought to depend only on prospects available from t onward, then assigning any weight to resources already expended is formally analogous to allowing an irrelevant state of nature to induce a preference reversal.

Yet in the philosophical literature on practical rationality I keep encountering the claim that continuing a failing project can be genuinely rational—rather than merely psychologically understandable—when abandonment would undermine a credible long-term plan or betray commitments made to collaborators. I am inclined to think these authors are smuggling strategic considerations into a single-agent problem: either they are implicitly appealing to repeated-game reputation effects and audience costs, or they are invoking non-consequentialist values like loyalty without admitting it. If we genuinely isolate the decision—no observers, no future strategic interaction, no state-dependent utility—then is there any technically coherent refinement within standard expected utility theory, perhaps via plan-based preferences or a modified separability condition, that permits unrecoverable costs to influence a present choice without collapsing into the sunk cost fallacy? Or is my framing correct: that once strategic and sentimental factors are stripped away, honouring sunk costs is necessarily irrational?
