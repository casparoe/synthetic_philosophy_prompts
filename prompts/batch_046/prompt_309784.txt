I am designing a short philosophical exercise on fatalism for a graduate seminar, and I would like a poem to serve as both an aesthetic object and a logical diagram. The subject is the parable often called "Death in Damascus"—the version in which a merchant, seeing Death in Damascus, flees overnight to Aleppo in the belief that distance and speed can annul an appointment; Death, encountering him there, remarks only that she had expected him in Aleppo all along, and that she was surprised to see him in Damascus because she knew he was meant to be in Aleppo that night. The philosophical point I want captured is not the banal claim that one cannot escape fate, but the more precise logical structure: the flight itself is the mechanism of fulfillment, so that evasion and arrival are not opposites but a single trajectory observed from two directions.

Please write a villanelle in which the two refrains are assigned to the two voices—the fugitive and Death—so that the form's alternating returns enact the way the two positions keep passing each other without ever resolving. The fugitive's refrain should express the conviction that motion and separation can break a prediction; Death's refrain should express the counter-intuitive claim that the very act of flight delivers him to the appointed place. The villanelle's final couplet, in which both refrains must appear together, should therefore not stage one voice defeating the other, but should place them side by side so that a listener sees they describe the same event. Use imagery that is concrete—the city gate, the road at dawn, the shadow that does not need to follow—without reducing the poem to simple allegory; someone familiar with the fatalist paradox should recognize the logical twist in the rhyme scheme and repetition, while someone unfamiliar with the parable should still feel the inevitability through the form alone. Write it as a standalone piece, without citations, bibliographies, or references to outside texts.
