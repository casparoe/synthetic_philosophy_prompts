I am a graduate student in labor economics, and my entire conceptual toolkit is built around reservation wages, compensating differentials, revealed preference through labor-supply curves, and signaling equilibria in competitive labor markets. Last week I was discussing with a friend from sociology the normative question of whether a person has a fundamental right to work, to leisure, or to some protected boundary between them. She kept invoking three reference points: Weber's thesis that the Protestant ethic transformed labor into a spiritual calling with its own intrinsic reward structure; the French 35-hour-week legislation, which treats leisure as a socially protected entitlement rather than a residual category; and karoshi, the Japanese phenomenon of death by overwork, which she presented as evidence that unregulated labor markets can violate a basic human claim to rest. I want you to translate this entire three-way normative debate into my vocabulary, as precisely and honestly as you can. Treat Weber's calling as a non-pecuniary, possibly identity-based return to labor supply that shifts the reservation wage downward; treat the 35-hour cap as a binding institutional constraint on hours supplied, and ask whether it binds at the worker's optimal point or creates deadweight loss; treat karoshi as an equilibrium in which the signaling value of visible overwork exceeds the marginal disutility of labor, possibly sustained by coordination failure among employers. Do not import moral vocabulary like exploitation, dignity, or right unless you can express it as a preference parameter or a constraint. Once the translation is complete, give me a residue report: which normative claims survive intact, which have to be distorted — for instance, does leisure as entitlement map onto anything other than an exogenous ceiling on hours? — and which disappear entirely. Then tell me whether the disappearing parts indicate that economic vocabulary lacks the resources to describe human flourishing, or whether they reveal that the original sociological debate was less substantive than it appeared because it relied on rhetorical framing that collapses under formal translation.
