You are a professional philosopher specializing in ancient biography and moral epistemology. You just finished a long, somewhat frustrating argument with a close friend about whether reading Plutarch’s Parallel Lives actually improves anyone’s character, or whether the genre’s built-in hagiographic pressure creates what you want to call “exemplary distortion”—the necessary compression of a multi-causal life into a single moral axis that makes it pedagogically visible but epistemically unreliable. You are now writing the final essay exam for your upper-level seminar “Biography, Hagiography, and Moral Learning from Lives.” Before drafting the exam, please take a moment to think carefully and brainstorm the central philosophical crux: can a life be both genuinely instructive and genuinely multi-causal, or does moral exemplarity always require a simplifying distortion that undermines the very learning it promises?

Write one essay question that students could answer in 75 minutes in about 800 words. The question must not be answerable by summarizing Plutarch’s comparative method; instead, it should force students to confront whether the asymmetry between vice-visibility and virtue-visibility in biography—where failures are narratively vivid but virtues are often flattened into generic praise—makes hagiographic biography an unstable foundation for moral learning. Specifically, ask them to evaluate whether “exemplary distortion” is an unavoidable feature of moral biography or a correctible flaw.

Then provide a model answer of roughly 700 words. The answer should make at least four specific philosophical moves: (1) distinguish learning from admiration; (2) explain why multi-causal explanation in history tends to dissolve exemplarity; (3) address whether suppressing vice for pedagogical effect corrupts the epistemic status of the virtue shown; and (4) propose a criterion for when compression becomes distortion rather than illumination.

Finally, provide a marking scheme out of 25 points that separates an A from a B answer by naming those four moves, allocating marks to each, and including a one-line note on how candidates typically botch each one. Do not require or invite bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Keep the tone formal and polished.
