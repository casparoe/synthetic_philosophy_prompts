I'm preparing material for a philosophy-and-film discussion group that has been working through classic science-fiction cinema with an eye to how films dramatize economic and technological change rather than merely depicting it. For our next meeting I want to screen Fritz Lang's Metropolis (1927) and read it through Karl Marx's analysis of machinery and automation—specifically not the popular "robots replace workers" narrative, but Marx's more precise claim in Capital and in the Grundrisse notebooks that machinery operates as fixed capital which restructures the labor process itself, reducing living labor to a supervisory appendage or watchman over an automatic system, while the productive knowledge embedded in machinery tends to be expelled from the worker's body and made into an independent power of capital. I also want to bring in Marx's idea of the general intellect—the accumulated social knowledge and scientific capacity objectified in machinery—since Rotwang's artificial Maria seems to literalize exactly this: knowledge and charisma made mechanical and seductive.

Could you draft a critical reading that takes both Lang's film and Marx's theory seriously in both directions? I'm especially interested in the opening shift-change sequence, where workers move in synchronized ranks like pistons into the underground factory; the Moloch-machine scene, in which the machinery appears literally to devour human bodies; and the robot Maria's dance and sermon, which replace both manual labor and political leadership with an artificial double. Where does Marx's framework illuminate these images—does the robot represent machinery as capital's weapon against labor, or does she instead embody the general intellect that Marx thought would eventually undermine capital's own basis? Where does the film resist a Marxist reading: for instance, in its famous resolution proposing a mediator between heart and hands rather than structural transformation, or in its visual glorification of the very machinery Marx describes as dehumanizing? Finally, what does the film add to the philosophical debate about technology and labor that Marx's texts alone do not—perhaps something about the gendering of automation, or about spectacle as a mechanism that masks exploitation? Please avoid reducing Metropolis to an illustration of Marx and avoid distorting Marx to fit the plot; I want an honest account of where the analogy holds, where it breaks, and what the honest critic must concede on each side.
