While drafting a historical novel about an archivist who comes to believe that every surviving record of her nation’s past was preserved or destroyed according to the interests of successive ruling powers, I tried to formalize her skepticism into a rigorous argument, only to discover that it seems to prove far more than I intended—namely, that no one can ever possess disinterested historical knowledge, and that the very assertion of this thesis undermines its own rational standing. I present the reasoning in steps and ask you to diagnose exactly where it breaks, which premise is least costly to abandon, and what each abandonment commits me to, particularly since I would prefer to preserve the first two premises if that is philosophically defensible.

First, a particular historical fact is knowable only if some trace—whether document, artifact, or testimony—survives to record its occurrence. Second, all such traces persist or perish through selections made by agents who hold power over preservation and destruction, which means every trace available to us serves, or at least does not threaten, the interests of some authority that permitted its survival. Third, therefore every historical fact that can actually be known is one that served the interests of the power that preserved it. Fourth, if knowing a fact necessarily involves relying on traces that were shaped by interested selection, then knowing that fact also requires recognizing that it was preserved for interested reasons, which implies that no fact can be known except as an instrument of domination. Fifth, consequently there is no such thing as disinterested historical knowledge whatsoever. Sixth, the claim that there is no disinterested historical knowledge is itself presented as knowledge about the history of historiography, assembled from philosophical texts and archival remains that have survived through the power structures of present academic institutions. Seventh, applying the reasoning of steps three and four to this very claim, it follows that we can know this meta-claim only as an instrument of our own present power structures, not as a rationally compelled conclusion independent of interest. Eighth, hence if we accept the argument, we must accept that our acceptance is merely an exercise of present power rather than reason, which makes rational assent to the conclusion impossible. Ninth, so the argument proves too much: it does not merely constrain historical objectivity but annihilates the possibility of rationally believing anything about history, including its own central thesis.

Please reply in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write in a letter or email. Identify the precise inference or step where the chain fails—whether it involves an illicit quantifier shift between the origin of evidence and the content of belief, an equivocation on what it means for knowledge to be interested, a modal slide, or some other subtle error—and explain why that step initially appeared sound. Then discuss the principal ways to repair the argument, indicating for each repair what is preserved and what is sacrificed, especially with respect to my desire to keep the first two premises intact.
