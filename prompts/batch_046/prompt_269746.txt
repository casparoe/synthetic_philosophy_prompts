I am writing the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of political economy, and I want a closed-book essay question that forces students to apply Marx’s analysis of machinery, automation, and the labor process to a constructed contemporary scenario rather than recite the machinery chapter from Capital. The exam is ninety minutes long; students should write approximately eight hundred words; the question is worth forty marks. Please write the exam prompt itself, a model answer of about seven hundred words, and a detailed marking scheme that distinguishes an A-range response (35–40 marks) from a B-range response (28–34 marks) by listing the specific conceptual moves a top answer must make and the identifiable errors that should lose marks. Everything should be written in American English, and the deliverables should contain no bibliographies, citation requests, or reading lists, since students will not have access to sources.

The scenario is this: a multinational logistics firm deploys predictive-maintenance algorithms across its warehouse fleet. Mechanical failures drop by nearly half, allowing continuous twenty-four-hour operation with far fewer on-site repair technicians. Meanwhile, the remaining warehouse workers wear biometric sensors that track location, movement, and physiological data not only during shifts but also during breaks and while traveling between zones; the data feeds into a scheduling algorithm that adjusts output quotas in real time. Management claims that automation has “eliminated toil,” yet the working day has not been shortened, acute machinery injuries have been replaced by chronic stress-related conditions, and the technicians who remain now spend most of their time interpreting algorithmic alerts rather than repairing physical machinery. Ask students to analyze this situation using Marx’s distinctions between constant and variable capital, the organic composition of capital, formal and real subsumption, and his remarks on machinery in Capital and the Grundrisse notebooks. Specifically, they should address: (1) whether predictive maintenance reduces the value contributed by living labor or merely reconfigures it; (2) how real subsumption explains the extension of supervisory control into breaks and transit time; (3) whether Marx’s framework can accommodate the “immaterial” labor of monitoring algorithms as value-producing activity or whether it reveals a structural limit in his model; and (4) why capital’s drive to reduce necessary labor time within the working day tends, under capitalist relations, to intensify or extend surplus labor rather than liberate the worker. The best answers should use Marx’s categories to diagnose the specific contradiction presented—capital saves labor-time only to reclaim it for surplus extraction, and automation transforms the labor process without necessarily reducing human subordination to capital—rather than offering a generic summary of Marx on technology.
