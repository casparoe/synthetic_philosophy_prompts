I am trying to work out what I actually believe about ontology identification when some information is necessarily inaccessible, and I would like to see whether my intuitions hang together. Here are my gut reactions to several concrete cases.

First, consider two cosmological models that agree on every observation we could ever make, but one asserts that space extends infinitely beyond the cosmic horizon while the other asserts it is finite with periodic boundary conditions. I am inclined to say they describe exactly the same ontology; the difference is purely descriptive, like two coordinate representations of one space.

Second, take the classic brain-in-a-vat scenario: my phenomenal experience is precisely as it is now, but the causal history is a computer simulation with no corresponding physical world outside. Here I want to say the ontology is genuinely different; there is a real external world in one case and none in the other, even though I can never access that difference from the inside.

Third, consider two formal constructions of the real numbers—one via Dedekind cuts and one via equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences. I think these are not two ontologies but one, presented differently; the numbers themselves are identical.

Fourth, compare Bohmian mechanics with standard quantum mechanics: both reproduce all measurement statistics, but the former posits definite particle positions that are in principle experimentally hidden. I think this is an ontological difference, not merely descriptive; the hidden trajectories are genuinely present in one world and absent in the other.

Fifth, imagine a physical theory that postulates an “ether” filling space but exerting no forces, producing no heat, and interacting with nothing—its presence or absence makes no empirical difference whatsoever. I am inclined to dismiss this as no ontological difference at all; it is an empty label attached to the same world.

So I have a pattern, but I can see the tension. In the cosmological case I dismiss an inaccessible structural difference as non-ontological, yet in the Bohmian case I treat an inaccessible dynamical difference as ontologically real. Meanwhile, the brain-in-a-vat case suggests that causal or external structure matters even when phenomenally hidden, whereas the ether case suggests that some hidden postulates do not matter.

Is there one principle—or perhaps a small family of principles—that captures all five verdicts without excessive contrivance? If so, please state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and say what it predicts for a case I have not listed: two artificial intelligence systems that behave identically in every interaction, where one possesses conscious inner experience and the other is a philosophical zombie, with no possible test to distinguish them. If no single principle can save all five verdicts, I want an honest diagnosis: which verdict should be overturned, and what does the repaired principle say about the AI case? Please reason from the cases themselves rather than appealing to authority or bibliography.
