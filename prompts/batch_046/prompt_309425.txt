Consider the following line of reasoning from Zermelo's 1904 defense of the well-ordering principle. He responds to critics who object that selecting one element from each non-empty subset requires an explicit rule or formula by saying that such a law is unnecessary: it is enough that for every subset one can associate an element, and that logic requires only that these correspondences exist, not that we can describe them. He adds that denying this possibility restricts mathematical reasoning more severely than denying the axiom of choice itself. I am trying to understand the logical status he assigns to these choice-correspondences. Are they mental acts, set-theoretic objects that exist independently of description, or implicit definitions legitimated by the consistency of the system they produce? Furthermore, does his argument depend on treating the subsets as a well-defined set of sets, or does it tacitly extend to arbitrary collections—including ones that are not themselves sets within the theory being developed? Please help me disentangle these two levels in the passage and say whether Zermelo anticipates the distinction or collapses it.
