I'm trying to work out my own view on what makes friendship and aesthetic appreciation genuinely valuable, using a broadly Moorean framework—organic wholes, intrinsic goods, and the idea that pleasure is neither necessary nor sufficient—without assuming Moore gets every case right. Here are my gut verdicts on concrete situations.

Case 1: Two old friends spend an evening together in silence after a shared bereavement; neither feels pleasure, but each knows the other is present out of pure care. Verdict: this is among the best things possible.

Case 2: A solitary listener feels intense joy in the live, unrecorded acoustic presence of a string quartet. Verdict: very good, but I rank it below Case 1 despite the far greater pleasure.

Case 3: The listener has an acoustically identical experience in a virtual environment and knows there are no performers and no hall. Verdict: still genuinely valuable, but clearly diminished—perhaps half as valuable—because the real objects of appreciation are absent.

Case 4: Someone maintains a close relationship purely for professional advancement, feeling no affection and ready to drop the contact once the utility ends. Verdict: not genuinely valuable in the relevant sense; it is instrumentally useful but lacks the intrinsic good.

Case 5: A person spends years caring for a friend in a persistent vegetative state, feeling pure affection and providing real comfort, but receiving no awareness or reciprocity whatsoever. Verdict: I keep going back and forth—sometimes the care retains full value because the affection is real and directed at the right object, other times I think the total absence of reciprocal consciousness leaves the organic whole incomplete, reducing it to something closer to Case 4.

Case 6: A viewer is deeply moved by a landscape painting that she later learns was generated by an AI trained on human masterpieces, with no human painter involved. The canvas and the visual experience are physically real and indistinguishable from a human work. Verdict: I think the aesthetic appreciation retains its full value; the mental state is genuine, the object is real, and the artificial origin does not seem to enter the organic whole.

My tentative principle is that intrinsic value here requires an organic whole composed of (i) a mental state—affection or aesthetic appreciation—directed non-instrumentally at its object for the object's own sake, and (ii) an object appropriate to that direction, with pleasure being neither necessary nor sufficient, with simulation damaging value only when it removes the real object of appreciation rather than merely revealing an artificial origin, and with reciprocity not required but its complete and permanent absence possibly leaving the whole incomplete.

Test this principle against Cases 1 through 6. Does it capture the pattern, or must it be repaired?
