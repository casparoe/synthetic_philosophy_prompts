I'm writing as a doctoral student in philosophy of value. I've been working through the heterogeneity problem for hedonism—specifically whether pleasures share anything beyond family resemblance that justifies treating them as a single currency of well-being. I want to stage an adversarial collaboration between two camps I encounter in the literature but rarely see forced into direct joint formulation: the Phenomenal Uniformists, who hold that all pleasures instantiate one intrinsic phenomenal quality (call it pleasurableness) that makes cross-modal comparison metaphysically direct; and the Functional-Relational Pluralists, who hold that pleasures are unified only by a cluster of functional and normative roles—motivational salience, positive evaluation, sustained attention—and that any appearance of a shared feel is an artifact of linguistic categorization.

Please draft the joint statement these two camps should produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Structure it exactly as follows:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. Include agreement about what pleasure is not (e.g., not reducible to mere desire-satisfaction), about the phenomenological data both accept (that subjects report diverse qualitative characters across sensory, intellectual, emotional, and aesthetic domains), about the normative stakes (that hedonism requires some account of why all pleasures count positively toward well-being), and about the limits of introspective access.

2. A disagreements table with two columns—each phrased in wording its own side would accept—covering at least: (a) whether there exists a single intrinsic phenomenal property common to all pleasures; (b) whether cross-modal comparisons (e.g., weighing the satisfaction of solving a proof against that of a meal) involve direct measurement of a shared quantity or instead depend on practical reasoning about goods that resist commensuration; (c) whether evolutionary divergence in the functional bases of reward undermines the uniformist claim.

3. A section identifying the cruxes—those sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I take the central cruxes to be: (i) the interpretation of first-person reports of incomparability between qualitatively different pleasures; (ii) whether hedonism as a theory of well-being requires phenomenal uniformity or can survive on functional equivalence alone; and (iii) whether a being capable of only one pleasure-type (say, purely aesthetic or purely gustatory) possesses well-being in a hedonistic sense.

For each crux, provide: the concrete conceptual case or argument both sides agree is relevant; and, written down in advance, each camp's prediction about what that case shows. For instance, regarding (i), both might agree that the forced-choice refusal scenario—where a subject consistently declines to rank an intellectual breakthrough against bodily comfort, not from indifference but from a sense that the comparison is category-mistaken—bears directly on the issue. The Uniformist should predict that such refusal reflects a cognitive framing limitation rather than metaphysical incommensurability; the Pluralist should predict that it reveals the absence of a shared metric.

Please keep the formulation precise, avoid straw-manning either position, and do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or requests for further sources. I am particularly interested in whether part of their dispute turns out to be verbal—if so, identify which part and explain why.
