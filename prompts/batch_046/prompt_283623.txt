hey, i cooked up this argument for a seminar on Davidson and now i'm second-guessing it. it seems to show deep disagreement is impossible, which feels wrong, but i can't find teh crack.

(1) Radical interpretation requires us to maximize charity and rationality in belief attribution.
(2) If two interpreters both apply charity correctly to the same speaker, they must converge on the same belief attributions.
(3) So understanding someone means attributing to them mostly beliefs that match our own, adjusted only for differences in perceptual position.
(4) Deep disagreement is when we hold beliefs that are incompatible in ways not reducible to perceptual position (e.g., about moral foundations or metaphysics).
(5) If (3) holds, then any apparently deep disagreement must be a misinterpretation—either i'm wrong about what you believe, or you're irrational.
(6) But people clearly do have deep disagreements that persist even after careful interpretation.
(7) So either radical interpretation is false, or deep disagreement is always an illusion caused by bad interpretation.
(8) I don't want to say disagreement is always an illusion, and i'd like to keep (1). So where's the slip? Is there an equivocation between "mostly true beliefs about teh shared environment" and "mostly identical beliefs overall"? Or does Davidson allow localized irrationality that saves (6) without giving up charity?
