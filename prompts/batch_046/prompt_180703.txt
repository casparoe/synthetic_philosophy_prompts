I've been working through a comparative ethics module and keep running into tranquilism — the thesis that only the absence of craving and suffering carries moral weight, while achievements, attachments, and positive joys are at best instrumentally valuable and at worst distractions. In our discussions, a particular analogy keeps surfacing: the tranquilist life is like wearing high-grade noise-canceling headphones. The point is not to compose or hear beautiful music, but to eliminate static; success is measured entirely by the fidelity of the cancellation and the comfort of the resulting silence. Proponents use it to license a relaxed, minimalist ethical conclusion — that our obligations extend only to reducing suffering and quieting desire, with no further duty to cultivate goods or pursue flourishing. I want to know whether the analogy can actually bear that weight.

Please lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the ear, the audio signal, the noise, the cancellation circuitry, the power source, and the silence itself? Then show me where the mapping breaks in ways that matter for the conclusion: do headphones presuppose a listener who might have valued the music, thereby importing a positive good that tranquilism denies? Does the analogy treat silence as a pleasant experiential state rather than a mere absence, smuggling in positive value through the back door? And does it obscure a distinction some defenders of equanimity insist on — that the ideal is not deadened suppression but an unperturbed openness, more like a precisely tuned instrument that resonates without distortion? Once these disanalogies are on the table, tell me whether the relaxed ethical conclusion survives or whether the headphone image was carrying the argument all along. If it collapses, suggest a better analogy that captures the difference between mere suppression and genuine equanimity without importing the positive goods the view rejects.
