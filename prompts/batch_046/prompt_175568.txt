I'm drafting a dissertation proposal in moral and political philosophy centered on what I call the archaeology of atrocity naming—the thesis that labeling an event genocide is not merely a retrospective historical judgment but an act that restructures present institutional obligations toward past victim groups. My point of entry is the familiar divergence between Raphael Lemkin's broad 1940s formulation, which encompassed cultural, economic, and religious destruction, and the narrower intent-based framework that was subsequently codified in international law. Rather than rehearsing the standard debate about whether the legal definition should be expanded, I want to identify which conceptual problems in this space are genuinely upstream and which are derivative or practically inert.

Specifically, I am weighing three open questions. First, the problem of temporal authority: if descendants of a victim community, rather than contemporaneous jurists or historians, claim the right to retroactively apply the genocide label generations after the fact, does that authority derive from inherited harm, from representational continuity, or from some other normative source? Second, the problem of categorical leakage: if cultural destruction is admitted within the category, how can we prevent the concept from expanding to cover every instance of forced assimilation or linguistic suppression, and does the risk of over-inclusion actually undermine the protective political power that makes the label effective? Third, the problem of institutional revisability: should international tribunals and memorial bodies treat the genocide designation as a stable legal classification, or as an interpretive framework that can be legitimately updated as historical understanding evolves?

Given that I have roughly two years before I must commit to a focused thesis, and given that my training is in conceptual analysis rather than in international law or empirical historiography, which of these three should I treat as foundational—the one whose resolution would either dissolve or significantly reshape the others? Which should I deprioritize because its answer, however philosophically interesting, would not substantially alter how memorial policy, reparative frameworks, or cross-generational justice obligations are structured? Please defend the ordering with explicit criteria: tractability for a philosopher without legal expertise, dependency relations among the questions, and the practical payoff if the answer turned out one way rather than another. I am not looking for a survey of the literature, but for a reasoned agenda that tells me where limited research effort should go first.
