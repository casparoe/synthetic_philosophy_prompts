Case 2: A biologically human child is raised from birth by non-human caregivers with zero human interaction, though neurologically normal. Verdict: She does not initially possess full personhood; being embedded in human relations is necessary for it to develop, though she retains the potential to acquire it.

Case 3: A corporation knowingly poisons a distant river community with which it shares no history, culture, or interaction. Verdict: This is seriously wrong, but the wrongness is "thin"—like violating an abstract universal duty—rather than "thick," which involves betraying a specific relational bond.

Case 4: A community permanently exiles a member, deliberately cutting every tie. Verdict: This is gravely wrong, closer to destroying the person than merely harming them, because personhood is partly constituted by those ties; yet if the exiled member had already destroyed the communal fabric, the exile becomes justified.

Case 5: An aging parent refuses life-saving treatment, explaining only "I don't want to be a burden." Verdict: This is not genuinely autonomous; autonomy is relational, and deciding in isolation to "not burden others" misapprehends one's interdependence.

Case 6: A stateless refugee asserts moral rights against every government, with no community recognizing or backing the claim. Verdict: The claim is intelligible but weak—almost hollow—because rights-claims gain force from being embedded in some relational structure of recognition.

The tension is sharpest between Case 1 and Case 4 (both involve absence of relations, yet status survives in one and is destroyed in the other) and between Case 1 and Case 5 (both involve solitary decision, yet I accept the hermit's withdrawal and reject the parent's refusal as non-autonomous).
