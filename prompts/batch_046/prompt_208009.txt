I'm preparing material for a graduate seminar on decision theory, and a colleague challenged me to formalize the Newcomb dispute without falling back on the usual one-box versus two-box slogans. Rather than asking for a verdict, I want you to draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between evidential decision theorists (the one-box camp) and causal decision theorists (the two-box camp) might produce—specifically under a modified scenario that avoids the standard black-box framing. In this variant, the predictor runs an algorithm whose source code is partially inspectable: its calibration accuracy is known to be 0.85 when predicting a one-box choice and 0.92 when predicting a two-box choice, yet the agent cannot observe the predictor's internal state or its random seed at the moment of choice. The agent knows these statistics, knows the payoff structure (one box yields either $1,000,000 or $0; both boxes yield $1,000 plus either $1,000,000 or $0), and knows that the prediction was made before the choice.

Please structure the collaboration document as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: the formal causal graph linking agent choice, predictor state, and payout; the precise interpretation of the calibration accuracies as either evidential indicators or causal probabilities; the conditions under which precommitment is possible; and what both sides acknowledge the no-go theorems separating evidential and causal recommendations actually rule out. Second, a disagreements table: state each camp's position in wording that camp would accept for its own view—whether the agent's choice provides evidence about a prior predictor state or is only causally downstream, whether rationality requires maximizing causal expected utility or evidential expected utility given partial transparency, and whether the difference in calibration accuracy changes the normative recommendation. Third, identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side—for instance, whether partial inspectability of the predictor's mechanism collapses the evidential-causal distinction, or whether a rational agent should condition on the predictor's algorithm rather than on its output. For each crux, record what both sides agree would count as decisive—whether a formal proof, a constructed decision problem with transparent mechanics, or an agreement on how to compute expected utility—and state each side's advance prediction. Fourth, examine whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal: specify which formulations (for example, "causes the payout" versus "indicates the payout") generate false disagreement, and provide neutral wording both sides could sign. Keep the deliverable self-contained: do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid relying on obscure historical details about specific authors; instead, reason from the formal structure of the problem and the modified predictor conditions described above.
