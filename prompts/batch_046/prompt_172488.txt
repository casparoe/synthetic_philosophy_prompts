I've been working through a version of the heterogeneity problem for hedonism and ended up with an argument that seems to dissolve the view entirely, which feels like overkill. Here are the steps:

(1) If hedonism is true, then every pleasure is good in virtue of sharing some single common feature F; otherwise 'pleasure' is merely a family-resemblance term with no unifying axiological significance.

(2) The classic heterogeneity objections—sensory versus intellectual versus malicious versus aesthetic pleasures—show that there is no shared phenomenal quality, no shared intentional object, and no shared functional role across all pleasures.

(3) Therefore, if hedonism is true, F must be purely formal: something like being desired for its own sake, or having positive valence, or functioning as a reward signal.

(4) But if F is purely formal in this way, then any state that satisfies F counts as a pleasure and is good in virtue of F. This includes states we do not ordinarily classify as pleasures—e.g., the satisfaction of a sadist, the serene acceptance of annihilation found in certain mystical traditions, or even the update weights in a reinforcement-learning system that lacks conscious experience altogether.

(5) So hedonism either collapses into a broader axiological theory that values these non-pleasurable states equally, or it must arbitrarily restrict which F-satisfying states count as genuine pleasures.

(6) Any such restriction requires appealing to some substantive feature—phenomenal, functional, or intentional—that distinguishes real pleasures from mere F-satisfying states—but that is exactly the kind of feature that (2) showed does not exist across all pleasures.

(7) Therefore hedonism is either false (because there is no F) or vacuous (because F applies indiscriminately).

The conclusion seems too destructive: it doesn't merely challenge hedonism, it makes the concept of pleasure unusable for normative theory. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I'd prefer to keep (1) and (3)—that hedonism needs a unifying feature and that it must be formal—but I'm open to giving up (2) if there's a hidden commonality I've missed, or to modifying (5) if the expansion to non-ordinary cases isn't actually damaging. Please say how confident you are in your diagnosis and identify the specific point about which you are least certain.
