I'm working through some ideas for a seminar on philosophy of mind and keep returning to a version of the combination problem that seems more destructive than the usual formulation. Here is the argument, laid out step by step. (1) Constitutive panpsychism claims that macro-subjects are composed of micro-subjects, and that macro-experiential properties depend on but are not reducible to the micro-experiential properties of the parts. (2) For this dependence to be explanatory rather than merely correlational, there must be a combination relation R that transforms a plurality of micro-experiences into a unified macro-experience. (3) R must be either a physical relation or a phenomenal relation. (4) If R is physical, then the macro-subject's unity is fully accounted for by physical structure and dynamics, which means panpsychism contributes nothing beyond physicalism to the explanation of consciousness; the micro-subjects become explanatorily redundant. (5) If R is phenomenal, then explaining macro-consciousness requires appealing to a phenomenal feature of the relation itself, but that feature's phenomenal character is itself unexplained at the macro level, rendering the account circular—we invoke consciousness to explain consciousness. (6) Moreover, if being in R is sufficient for generating a macro-subject, and R is instantiated by any sufficiently integrated physical system, then artifacts like complex climate-control networks or crystalline lattices would also stand in R and therefore possess unified macro-consciousness. (7) Thus panpsychism either collapses into physicalism, is viciously circular, or proves far too much by attributing consciousness to inanimate artifacts. (8) Yet the panpsychist wants to deny both physicalist reduction and artifactual consciousness. Where exactly does this chain fail? I am inclined to defend premises (2) and (5), so I want to understand whether the mistake is in the dichotomy at (3), in an implicit slide between (5) and (6), or somewhere else entirely, and what giving up each option costs.
