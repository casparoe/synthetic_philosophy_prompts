You are serving as a grader for a graduate examination in comparative metaphysics. The exam question was: “Jain anekāntavāda (non-absolutism) holds that reality is ontologically complex and can be grasped only through multiple standpoints (nayas). Explain how syādvāda—the theory of conditional predication expressed through the saptabhaṅgī (seven-fold scheme)—functions as the linguistic expression of anekāntavāda. In particular, clarify how the distinction between dravyāstika-naya (the substance-viewpoint) and paryāyāstika-naya (the mode-viewpoint) ensures that qualified assertions do not entail real contradictions. Finally, assess whether anekāntavāda is best understood as an epistemic doctrine about limited perspectives or as an ontological claim about the inherent multiplicity of being.”

A student submitted the following answer:

“Anekāntavāda rejects ekānta, the view that things possess only one nature. Because reality has infinite attributes, no single statement can fully capture any object. Syādvāda addresses this by prefixing every assertion with syāt (‘perhaps’), generating the seven forms of the saptabhaṅgī: it is; it is not; it is and is not; it is inexpressible; and the three compound forms involving inexpressibility. This shows that for any substance, every predicate applies and its opposite applies as well, but always in different respects. The distinction between dravyāstika and paryāyāstika naya explains why this is not contradictory: dravya is the permanent substance, while paryāya are its transient modes. So asserting ‘the jar is red’ from one paryāyāstika standpoint and ‘the jar is not red’ from another is consistent, because the underlying dravya remains unchanged. Nevertheless, this means that in every respect both a predicate and its negation are simultaneously true of the substance itself. Consequently, Jain ontology is a form of dialetheism in which contradictions are literally instantiated in being. It follows that kevala-jñāna—complete omniscient knowledge—must be impossible, since an omniscient being would have to affirm and deny every predicate of every object at once without the qualifying syāt, thereby violating the fundamental principle of syādvāda.”
