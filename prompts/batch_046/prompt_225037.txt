I am preparing a feature for a science and ideas section on how popular cinema engages with the hard problem of consciousness. For this, I would like you to read Spike Jonze's film Being John Malkovich through the lens of the explanatory gap, with particular attention to Ned Block's distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, and to the question of phenomenal transparency — whether conscious experience is ever experienced as mediated rather than immediate.

The film's central conceit is a portal that allows a user to inhabit John Malkovich's consciousness, perceiving the world through his eyes and initially hearing his thoughts. Treat this not as a mere science-fiction premise but as a philosophical thought experiment: does the film assume that consciousness is transferable like a functional state, or does it preserve an irreducible first-person residue? Focus on the sequences in which control of Malkovich's consciousness becomes contested — particularly the point at which multiple claimants crowd his subjective field, reducing his experience to competing voices and a loss of agency, and the later sequence in which Malkovich himself enters the portal and finds a world composed entirely of his own image. Do these scenes dramatise the metaphysical impossibility of shared phenomenal character, or do they merely depict an epistemic overload — too many access-conscious contents competing for a single functional architecture?

I want a reading that takes both the film and the philosophy seriously. Where does the plot's commercial logic — the portal is sold as a brief, timed experience for a fee — implicitly treat phenomenal properties as exchangeable commodities, and does that commitment align with functionalism? Where does the narrative resist the philosophical framing, perhaps by suggesting that the occupants do gain something genuinely first-person, or conversely by showing that they remain external observers no matter how deep the immersion?

Please conclude by identifying what the film contributes to the debate that philosophical argument alone tends to miss — whether it reveals an emotional or ethical dimension to the hard problem that analytic discussion suppresses.
