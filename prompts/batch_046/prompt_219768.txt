Please describe the most important implications of adopting a bifurcated taxonomy of free will—specifically, distinguishing regulatory free will, conceived as the empirically tractable capacity for real-time cognitive self-correction and inhibitory control, from narrative free will, conceived as the socially constructed capacity to author a coherent diachronic life-story—for the institutional design of criminal justice, with particular attention to the justification of retributive sentencing versus rehabilitative intervention. Your analysis should address three points. First, identify which legal practices become conceptually defensible or indefensible if only regulatory free will can ground direct moral responsibility, while narrative free will grounds only attributions of enduring character. Second, assess whether this bifurcation resolves, or instead sharpens, the longstanding tension between compatibilist and libertarian intuitions in jurisprudence. Third, present the strongest objection that a revisionist—one who holds that neither variety satisfies the conditions for being worth wanting in the retributivist sense—would press against using this taxonomy in policy formation, and indicate how a defender of the distinction should reply at the conceptual level, without relying on citation of specific empirical studies. Frame the response for a philosophy-of-law working group that is familiar with the standard debates but has not encountered this particular taxonomy.
