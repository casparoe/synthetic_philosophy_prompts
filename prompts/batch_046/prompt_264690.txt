A colleague and I were debating Maimonides' explanation of animal sacrifice in the Guide for the Perplexed. She argued that he treats the sacrificial laws as nothing more than a transitional concession: God instituted them solely because the Israelites were immersed in idolatrous customs, and without that historical conditioning they would never have been commanded. She pointed to his comparison with dietary and clothing regulations—measures that adapt eternal moral aims to a particular people's circumstances. I objected that this makes the extraordinary legal precision of the sacrificial code—its measurements, its distinctions between voluntary and obligatory offerings, its integration with priestly purity—seem like an implausible investment in a purely disposable institution. She replied that transitional mechanisms often borrow the detailed forms of the culture they are meant to redirect, so legislative complexity is exactly what one should expect. I then observed that Maimonides never forecasts the abolition of sacrifice in the messianic age, unlike several later Jewish thinkers, which suggests he does not regard it as merely provisional. She countered that his silence reflects respect for the Torah's permanence rather than endorsement of sacrifice's enduring value; he treats prayer as a pragmatic substitute available when the Temple is absent, not as proof that the rite was always dispensable in principle. I suggested that if sacrifice were truly a scaffold with no intrinsic purpose, one might expect him to present prayer as superior in kind, yet he describes prayer as an altogether different service, leaving open the possibility that sacrifice retains a unique symbolic function he refuses to reduce to social psychology. She accused me of smuggling in a notion she called structural residue—the idea that an institution can leave an irreducible imprint on the legal system even after its original rationale has expired—into a text that consistently explains commandments by their pedagogical and social effects, not by hidden metaphysical functions.

Please go through their exchange point by point. At each stage, say whose argument is stronger and why, without reducing the whole dispute to a single overall victor. Identify what both parties overlook, especially whether there is a coherent middle position that respects Maimonides' functional explanation without treating the sacrificial system as entirely devoid of irreducible ritual significance. Finally, reach and justify a conclusion about which interpretive framework—pure functional accommodation, structural residue, or an intermediate view—best fits both the relevant passages and Maimonides' broader philosophical commitments.
