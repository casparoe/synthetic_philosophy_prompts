As a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of economics, I have drafted the following essay notes on Keynes’s treatment of probability and uncertainty, and I would value conceptual commentary rather than bibliographic guidance.

Notes: Keynes separates risk, which permits quantitative probability assignment, from radical uncertainty, which resists it. Central to his account is the weight of argument—the total body of relevant evidence—which behaves counter-intuitively: additional data may increase the probability of a specific claim while decreasing its weight, should that data expose previously concealed structural possibilities. I propose interpreting this weight structurally, by analogy with statistical mechanics. Just as constraining a physical system reduces its entropy yet may reveal new accessible microstates, accumulating economic evidence may sharpen a particular forecast while expanding the recognized space of unknown unknowns. On this reading, Keynesian uncertainty is not a temporary information deficit but an irreducible feature of open, complex systems.

Please comment specifically on: whether this mechanical analogy distorts Keynes’s logical-relation theory of probability; whether framing weight as the volume of an open logical possibility space offers genuine insight beyond standard interpretations; and whether Keynes’s writings contain an unresolved tension between treating probabilities as logical relations and treating them as empirically revisable. I am interested in conceptual evaluation and internal coherence only—no suggestions for further reading, citations, or reading lists.
