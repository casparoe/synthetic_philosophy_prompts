In applied ethics discussions of rescue and triage, a disagreement that traces back to John Taurek’s challenge to aggregationist reasoning has persisted for decades without visible convergence. The canonical case is straightforward: if you can save either one person or five from an identical lethal threat, with no difference in cost or risk to yourself, many hold that you are required to save the five. A minority position, rooted in the claim that each individual possesses a separate, non-derivative claim against being sacrificed for the greater good, maintains instead that you should assign the rescue opportunity by fair lottery among all six potential victims rather than automatically privileging the larger group. What is puzzling is that both sides accept the same descriptive premises—the suffering is identical in kind, the rescuer’s burden is identical, and impartiality is mandatory—and both reject simple utilitarian aggregation in structurally analogous cases, such as the prohibition on killing one healthy patient to harvest organs for five. Despite this shared framework, the literature and working-group discussions have stalled: neither side has persuaded the other that the other is misunderstanding the scenario rather than simply disagreeing about it.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not a clash of bedrock intuitions about whether “numbers matter,” but a deeper structural disagreement about the ontology of moral reasons—specifically, whether a reason to prevent harm to one person can be scaled or multiplied across distinct persons, or whether it remains individually binding but strictly non-cumulative. On one reading, the aggregationist treats harm-prevention reasons as interpersonally additive; on the other, the anti-aggregationist treats them as personally directed and therefore incommensurable across separate lives. But I am unsure whether this captures the real fracture, or whether a hidden question is doing the work—perhaps about whether the rescue situation should be modeled from the ex ante perspective of risk distribution among a population, or from the ex post perspective of a single agent facing discrete individuals; or about whether “saving five” describes one collective act with five beneficiaries or five separate acts each with one beneficiary.

I want a diagnosis of the disagreement’s structure rather than an adjudication of the cases. Is this a verbal dispute about what “counting” means, a substantive disagreement about the metaphysics of harm and personal separateness, or an ill-posed question that illegitimately collapses the normative perspective of an individual rescuer with that of an institutional allocator? Do the sides differ about empirical facts, about standards of moral evidence, about which descriptions of the scenario are authoritative, or about starting commitments that neither can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would actually constitute progress: a formal distinction between scalable and directed reasons, a revised framing that separates ex ante contractualist reasoning from direct rescue obligations, a demonstration that one side is answering a different question under the same name, or is the disagreement irreducibly hard because it tracks incompatible foundational commitments about the separateness of persons?
