I direct the curriculum for a two-year applied energy-technology program at a regional college, and I am trying to build a reusable module-sequencing procedure grounded in Alfred North Whitehead’s theory of educational rhythm—the successive stages of romance, precision, and generalization—without reducing it to a motivational slogan about “making learning fun.” Our delivery is hybrid, our students are working adults with uneven prior training, and we must satisfy both accreditation checklists and industry partners who demand specific competencies on fixed calendars. I need an ordered decision procedure that tells a non-philosopher program coordinator, step by step, what conditions must be satisfied before a module can enter precision work, what counts as sufficient evidence that students have achieved disciplined mastery rather than merely passing assessments, and when the curriculum is genuinely ready for generalization, where technical detail reconnects to broader judgment. The procedure must also include explicit “hand-off to judgment” nodes—places where the theory cannot eliminate a human call—such as whether prior workplace experience counts as having completed the romance stage for a given domain, or whether an asynchronous online cohort can satisfy the social conditions that precision training requires.

Because I am worried about the hidden costs of acceleration, I want the procedure to incorporate a concept I am calling epistemic debt: if precision training is compressed or reordered to meet an external deadline before romance has genuinely occurred, the debt does not vanish; it accrues and must be paid during the generalization stage through remedial integration work that the standard schedule may not accommodate. Please present this as a practical flowchart in words—ordered questions, stopping conditions, a small set of diagnostic tests, and clear flags for misfire—rather than as an essay. Then apply it to three proposals: (a) a standard semester-long electrical-circuit sequence; (b) an accelerated employer-partner track that wants precision-level skills delivered by week four; and (c) a returning-student cohort with extensive practical experience but no formal vocabulary, where the danger is not missing romance but mistaking familiarity for disciplined precision. Include one case—most likely the employer-track—where the procedure yields an answer the stakeholders will reject, and conclude by stating plainly what the procedure can and cannot do, especially regarding any measurement of wonder, integration, or readiness that resists proceduralization.
