I run a 90-minute graduate seminar on decision theory and personal identity, about 10 students. I want a classroom game that makes participants feel the specific force of transformative experience—not merely that they lack knowledge of what an experience is like, but that rational choice itself breaks down when preferences are not stable across the transformation and the information is first-personally opaque. To avoid a standard lecture on Paul, frame the activity as a temporal commitment market: each student plays both a present-self and a projected future-self with separate, hidden utility weights, and the group must negotiate a binding contract to enter or avoid an opaque "experience box" that randomly assigns a new preference ordering. Design rules, roles, a payoff scheme using symbolic course-credit tokens rather than real money, and a timeline so that the group discovers for themselves that any ex-ante rational decision rule either prohibits all transformative entry or licenses ex-post regret, with the failure mode structurally resembling a Newcomb-style dependency between present belief and future preference. Include a debrief that maps the negotiation breakdown onto the epistemic and personal-transformation distinctions, say exactly what will predictably go wrong on the first run—likely that students will try to rescue the situation with expected-utility averaging and miss the preference-change point—and explain how to redirect that without lecturing. Be frank about which feature this market framing cannot reproduce: the phenomenological opacity of the experience itself, which the facilitator must supply verbally. Write in a precise, mechanistic style appropriate for a longtime rationalist forum reader. Lead with the core mechanic and the intended insight in the first sentence, then lay out the rules, materials, timeline, debrief, failure modes, and honest limitations with no preamble and no restatement of the brief.
