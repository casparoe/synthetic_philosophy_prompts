I am a novelist serving on the endowment-risk committee of the Meridian Cultural Foundation, and I must circulate a formal opinion before Thursday’s vote on whether to adopt a proposed grant-allocation algorithm. The algorithm—submitted by our quantitative advisory board—maximizes expected long-run cultural-impact value across portfolios, and it includes what its authors call a Pasadena-paradox protocol: whenever a project’s payoff distribution produces an undefined expectation, arising from heavy-tailed asymmetrical divergence in both potential cultural gain and catastrophic loss, the model assigns that scenario a placeholder utility of zero and excludes it from optimization. The board divides evenly: one side treats this as prudent engineering for an edge case; the other contends it disguises a normative decision-theoretic commitment, collapsing the distinction between rational caution and an arbitrary refusal to evaluate undefined values. Draft the committee memo recommending approval, rejection, or conditional approval with a concrete timeline and implementation conditions; the reasoning must be responsive to the quantitative board’s claim that undefined expectation is merely a technical boundary case, and it must engage seriously the strongest opposing philosophical argument—that nullifying undefined expectation is not a neutral mathematical fix but a substantive stance on how rational agents ought to treat structurally unbounded risk—without reducing that objection to mathematical illiteracy. The memo should read as an actionable institutional document rather than personal guidance, with an explicit bottom line and an honest acknowledgment of what the losing side gets right.
