Question (30 minutes, weighted 40%): 'Discuss the philosophical significance of Zhuangzi's butterfly dream (Zhuang Zhou meng die). How does this passage relate to sceptical arguments about knowledge of the external world? In your answer, compare Zhuangzi's position with at least one Western sceptical argument, and explain whether Zhuangzi's concern is primarily epistemological, ontological, or both.'

Student answer:
Zhuangzi's butterfly dream, found in the second chapter of the Zhuangzi, recounts how Zhuang Zhou dreamt he was a butterfly, and upon waking was unsure whether he was Zhuang Zhou who had dreamt of being a butterfly, or a butterfly now dreaming it was Zhuang Zhou. This illustrates that we lack a definitive criterion to distinguish waking experience from dreaming, which makes it structurally parallel to Descartes' dream argument in the Meditations. Both thinkers use the possibility of dreaming to show that sensory experience is unreliable, and therefore that our claims to know the external world are unfounded.

Yet Zhuangzi's position is more radical. While Descartes employs methodological doubt in order to rebuild knowledge on rational foundations, Zhuangzi denies that there is any stable self that persists through these transformations. The 'self' is merely one phase in an endless process of change, or hua. Consequently, Zhuangzi's scepticism is not merely epistemological but ontological: he rejects the very idea of a fixed external world that a knowing subject could either grasp or fail to grasp. This reading is supported by the text's emphasis on the transformation of things (wuhua). Although some interpreters claim Zhuangzi is not interested in knowledge at all but in spontaneous responsiveness, the dream passage itself asks whether there is a real distinction between Zhuang Zhou and the butterfly. If no such distinction holds, then the framework of knowing subject and known object collapses entirely. Thus, Zhuangzi offers a scepticism that undermines both our knowledge and the knower, going well beyond Descartes.
