(2) that means any intervention the agent makes on its environment propagates back, via loopback, into its own physical substrate and evaluation function. (3) but rational choice requires holding your criteria fixed while comparing outcomes; if your utility function is shifting during the comparison, you're not evaluating like with like. (4) since loopback is unavoidable for embedded agents, they can never stabilize the comparison long enough to actually choose. (5) therefore embedded agency is incompatible with rational action. (6) yet we clearly perform rational actions, so there must exist some functionally sealed module—call it a reflexive core—that is shielded from loopback.

i want to hang on to (1) and (3) because they feel non-negotiable, but then (6) looks like smuggling back a cartesian boundary. is the slip in (2), assuming loopback reaches the evaluation level? or is there a hidden equivocation between 'intervention on the world' and 'intervention on the self'? if the argument is actually valid, which premise is least costly to drop, and does dropping it save embedded agency or just turn us into self-modifying systems with no stable preferences?
