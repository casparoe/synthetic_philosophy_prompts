Question 4: The 'ant fugue' model compares an ant colony's pheromone loops to the interweaving voices in a fugue — each chemical trail acts like a voice entering, repeating, and altering in response to others, so the colony tracks heat and food as a unified pattern. Does this show the colony has a mind? Discuss, distinguishing functional cognition from phenomenal consciousness, and say whether distributed processing can ever ground subjective experience.

Student answer:

The ant fugue is a neat metaphor but I don't think it proves there's a colony mind. Functionally, the colony intergrates temperature and food data across its chemical network, behaving like a global workspace where different trail-layers broadcast to the whole system. So it's reasonable to call this a distributed cognitive architecture.

But functional integration isn't consciousness. Individual ants are too simple to have subjective experience, and you can't just stack non-conscious parts together to get consciousness — that's the fallacy of division working backwards. So there's nothing it is like to be the colony.

We should also consider the intentional stance. Dennett argues we should only adopt it when both the physical and design stances are genuinely impossible to apply. Here chemistry fully explains the trails, so we shouldn't treat the colony as an agent with beliefs.

Finally, integrated information theory says consciousness is identical to high phi regardless of substrate. The colony's loops do integrate information globally, but Tononi built the theory for neural mechanisms with discrete cause-effect power. Pheromone diffusion lacks that causal structure, so phi is undefined for the colony, and IIT doesn't apply. The fugue gives us emergent order, not an emergent subject.
