(2) In long-term projects—building a career, maintaining a collaboration, completing an extended research program—agents must form determinate beliefs about future contingent states (for example, "I will have the stamina to finish this," "this partnership will survive disagreement").

(3) For distant or structurally complex future states, available evidence always radically underdetermines the specific belief; the evidential basis remains compatible with both success and failure.

(4) Nevertheless, rational planning requires adopting one determinate forecast and reasoning from it; sustained suspended judgment or constant probabilistic hedging at every planning step leads to paralysis, premature abandonment, or self-defeating revision.

(5) The considerations that actually push an agent to adopt the stabilizing forecast are practical: holding the belief prevents premature withdrawal, sustains motivation, and makes coordinated long-range action possible.

(6) By (1), these considerations are wrong-kind reasons for holding the belief.

(7) Therefore, rational long-term agency requires holding beliefs that are justified only by wrong-kind reasons.

(8) But the standard wrong-kind-reasons thesis holds that belief is constitutively governed by the aim of truth, so wrong-kind reasons can never justify belief, regardless of strength.

(9) So rational long-term agency requires doing what is constitutively unjustified.

(10) That is absurd.

(11) Hence either the wrong-kind-reasons exclusion fails for beliefs formed in extended agency, or rational long-term planning is impossible.
