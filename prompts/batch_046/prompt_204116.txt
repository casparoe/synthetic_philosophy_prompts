I'm preparing a long-form feature for a science and ideas magazine on the architecture of subjective time. My reporting has led me to Daniel Dennett's distinction between Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions of conscious experience—frameworks he developed partly to explain illusions like color phi, in which discrete flashes are experienced as continuous motion with an intermediate color change. Recently I spoke with a cognitive scientist who applies a similar logic to interoception, proposing what she calls a 'stroboscopic self-model': the idea that our sense of continuous embodied presence is reconstructed frame-by-frame from discrete updates of cardiac and respiratory signals, rather than being sustained in real time.

What strikes me is the empirical architecture she describes. Subcortical structures register each discrete interoceptive frame; cortical networks then bind these frames into a unified 'now' with the characteristic 200-to-300-millisecond delay seen in other temporal-binding tasks. To my mind, this demonstrates that consciousness operates in the Orwellian mode: there exists a veridical, frame-level bodily log that is literally overwritten during integration, producing the illusion of an unbroken self. If the Stalinesque account were correct, I assume there could be no such discrete, pre-conscious registration—consciousness would receive only the finished editorial product, with no raw footage to corrupt.

Given that we can apparently trace these discrete frames all the way up to the point of cortical binding, hasn't the Stalinesque view been effectively falsified for the temporal structure of self-experience? And if so, should we conclude that our feeling of being continuously present in our bodies is not a direct perception but a false memory—an Orwellian revision of a body that was, in fact, experienced only in stroboscopic flashes?
