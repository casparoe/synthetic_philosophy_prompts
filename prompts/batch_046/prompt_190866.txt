I run a philosophy-of-mind seminar for twelve graduate students, with ninety minutes available. Design a runnable activity that makes participants feel the specific tension in the ant-colony mind problem—the so-called Ant Fugue question—without any opening lecture on emergence, functionalism, or collective intentionality. The conceptual pressure should arise from the rules and payoffs, not from my framing afterwards.

Split the group into four triads, each a subcolony. Give every student one private rule card—e.g., follow the strongest color gradient, avoid black tape, deposit red tape near resources—while forbidding speech, pointing, or eye contact. They may only place or remove colored tape markers on a floor maze. Their shared task is to move a tennis ball from one corner to a nest corner, but a looped corridor is arranged so that the triads’ rules conflict and the ball oscillates in place, producing an emergent deadlock that none intended. After fifteen minutes, hand each triad one mutation card that lets exactly one student revise one clause of their rule. They resume; typically the loop breaks, but only because the revised rule propagates through marker interactions rather than through any command.

Then run a twenty-minute debrief. Map the oscillation onto distributed cognition without a central executive; map the mutation onto stigmergic coordination; and ask whether the solved maze indicates a collective subject or merely a functional pattern. Predict the predictable failures—students will try to speak, or one will grab the ball directly—and say how to block them without destroying the analogy. Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: the absence of phenomenal unity or subjective phenomenal character belonging to the colony itself. Note that I must supply that gap by posing the closing question—whether anyone experienced the solution, and if not, whether functional success alone can count as mind—because the tape-and-ball setup has no internal point of view to display.

Keep the instructions concrete enough that I can execute them with tape, index cards, and a tennis ball, and keep the conceptual framing tied to the colony case rather than to generic teamwork exercises.
