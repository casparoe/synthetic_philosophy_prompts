At a colloquium on early modern political thought, two scholars debated Spinoza's concept of democracy. (1) The first argued that in both the Tractatus Theologico-Politicus and the Tractatus Politicus, Spinoza treats democracy as essentially procedural: a state is democratic if sovereignty resides in the collective body of citizens who legislate through universal participation, and institutional arrangements are secondary instruments for securing that participation. (2) The second replied that this misses Spinoza's ontological commitment: democracy is not defined by voting procedures but by the actual expression of the collective power of the multitude (potentia multitudinis); a state with democratic procedures in which oligarchic passions secretly direct outcomes is, for Spinoza, not truly democratic, because democracy names a mode of being in which collective right and collective power coincide. (3) The first countered that Spinoza's detailed institutional prescriptions — rotation of magistrates, large deliberative councils, and exclusions of certain groups from citizenship — demonstrate that democratic legitimacy depends on concrete procedural safeguards against corruption; if democracy were purely ontological, these specific mechanisms would be irrelevant rather than central. (4) The second responded that these mechanisms are not procedural conditions for legitimacy but are organizational techniques for maintaining affective transparency: they prevent the passions of individual citizens from crystallizing into a separate sovereign will, thereby ensuring that the multitude's conatus remains immanent to its governance rather than being captured by a faction. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then indicate which specific points each interlocutor gets right, where they talk past each other, and what important aspect of Spinoza's view both overlook.
