I am not an academic; I have just been trying to make sense of a specific water-rationing system my city adopted during drought, and I keep feeling that its rules only hang together if you already accept some hidden view about fairness, property, and nature. If you exceeded that baseline in prior drought years, your current allowance is permanently reduced by a fraction of the overage—so past consumption lowers future entitlement. The water saved by these reductions is not redistributed to other households; it is reserved entirely for ecological restoration of the upstream watershed. There is also a voluntary program in which you can permanently surrender part of your baseline in exchange for a tax credit, but the surrendered gallons are not reallocated to anyone else; they are simply erased from the total authorized human draw, shrinking the pool.

What puzzles me is the philosophical stitching. The baseline sounds like a claim earned through prior use, yet it is penalized precisely when it was exercised. The erased and redirected water treats the total resource pool not as a fixed pie to be divided among people, but as something that ought to contract for the sake of a non-human system. And directing savings to watershed restoration rather than to other users seems to treat the ecosystem as an independent claimant with standing, not merely as a background condition for human welfare.
