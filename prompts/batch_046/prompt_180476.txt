Here is a paraphrased line of reasoning from Kant's 1797 essay on whether benevolent lying is permissible: a murderer asks whether your friend is hidden in your home. If you lie to misdirect him, but he nevertheless discovers and kills the friend, your false declaration harms humanity generally by corrupting the foundation of rightful relations. You also bear partial responsibility for the death, because the falsehood was the cause that led the murderer to his victim.

I want to understand the logical bridge between these two claims—the general corruption of right and the specific co-responsibility for murder. Does the argument depend on treating truthful declaration as an institutional practice analogous to a shared currency, where one counterfeit undermines the protective system as a whole, and that systemic corruption is what makes the liar causally implicated? Or is the co-responsibility meant to follow from a more direct causal chain? Please explain how the damage to the foundation of right is supposed to generate personal liability for the murderer's act, and whether reading speech norms as shared infrastructure is necessary to make the reasoning hold together.
