As a retired field biologist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of history, I serve on the curatorial ethics board of our regional natural-history museum, and by next Thursday we must vote on how to present original 1950s ecological field notebooks in our permanent biodiversity exhibition. The notebooks record meticulous species counts but also describe animal-collection practices now widely regarded as unethical; one faction wants the display annotated with present-day moral judgments, while the other insists that such framing commits presentism and that historians can never fully escape the standards of their own time. I need a short opinion memo for circulation before the meeting that recommends one concrete course—retain the notebooks unmarked, add contextual annotations without evaluative language, or relocate them to a restricted research archive—with reasoning that treats the strongest opposing view honestly. Please deliver the memo as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion; in framing the philosophical issue, distinguish between evaluative presentism and interpretive presentism, and address whether a curator can genuinely bracket contemporary moral standards without smuggling them back in through selection, silence, or the choice of what to display.
