We've read Plato's Allegory of the Cave together but have no formal background in historiography or archaeology. I want you to design a structured simulation or deliberation game that makes participants feel, through their own decisions, why the boundary between legend and history is not simply a function of age or popularity. Instead, distribute role cards that assign each student a hidden evidentiary stance—archaeological silence, textual repetition across putatively independent sources, or single-source attestation with external correlation—without revealing which stance belongs to whom. Have the group apply each stance blindly to all three narratives in two voting rounds, using rules that create information asymmetry and a status-based payoff scheme that requires no real money. Include a minute-by-minute timeline, rules for the mediator role, and a debrief that maps the inevitable vote reversals onto the historiographical problem: specifically, that the same narrative can cross into or fall out of "history" depending on which filter is applied, not on any feature internal to the story. Be explicit about what feature of the legend-to-history transition this simulation cannot reproduce and that I must supply in the debrief. Also predict what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with peers, and how to recover. The insight should emerge from the voting dynamics before anyone names it. Finally, please say clearly what argument or observed classroom failure would change your mind about whether this design actually produces the insight rather than merely staging an inconclusive debate—where is your reasoning about its effectiveness most vulnerable?
