After a lengthy discussion with a friend about whether a statue can survive the complete replacement of its material substrate, I am grading an undergraduate examination on material constitution and need an outside assessment of the following response. The exam question was: "Consider a marble statue that, over the course of three centuries, undergoes a conservation protocol in which every calcite crystal is gradually replaced by a synthetically produced analog with identical chemical structure, lattice geometry, and macroscopic appearance, such that no original marble remains, yet the artistic form and spatiotemporal continuity are uninterrupted. Does the original statue survive? Defend your answer by appealing to the distinction between material constitution and numerical identity, and address whether the replacement of constituent matter affects the persistence conditions of the constituted object."

The student wrote: "The statue and the marble are not identical, because they have different modal properties: the marble could survive being crushed into powder and then reformed into a different shape, whereas the statue could not survive being crushed into powder, since that would destroy its artistic form. This shows constitution is a one-many relation, not identity. Now, in the replacement scenario, every original calcite crystal has been destroyed and replaced by synthetic analogs. Because the statue is constituted by that particular marble, and that particular marble no longer exists, the original statue is destroyed. Even though a new object with the same form exists in the same location, it is a different statue, just as a copy of a painting is not the original. One might think that if the form is preserved continuously, the statue survives, as in the ship of Theseus; but that applies only to artifacts that are defined by functional continuity, not to artworks whose identity depends on the specific material origin. Therefore, the synthetic analogs now constitute a new statue that happens to be qualitatively indistinguishable from the original, but numerical identity is lost because the original constituting matter is gone. The student also notes that if we allowed matter to be replaced, then there would be no principled reason to deny that the statue survives being melted and recast, which contradicts our intuition that the statue is essentially tied to its particular material history."

Please assign a grade and provide specific, point-by-point comments that justify it. I am particularly interested in whether the student has correctly applied the distinction between constitution and identity, whether the reasoning about material replacement is consistent with standard constitution views, and whether the appeal to artistic origin introduces a subtle conflation. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I am grading for conceptual accuracy, not source citation.
