I am a recently retired experimental physicist who has, over the past three years, turned my attention to philosophy of science and the intellectual history of the Scientific Revolution. In a seminar I now attend—mostly other retired scientists and a few graduate students in philosophy—we have been reading about the relationship between empirical method and metaphysical commitment in early modern astronomy. Our discussion of Johannes Kepler has raised a specific controversy that I would like explored in essay form.

Kepler is justly famous for his empirical rigor: his derivation of the laws of planetary motion, his rejection of circular orbits as physically implausible, and his insistence that celestial mechanics must be governed by physical causes rather than geometric perfection alone. At the same time, he cast horoscopes for patrons and composed treatises defending the practice of astrology. The conventional explanation is that this was merely a concession to economic necessity and courtly expectation. Yet I wonder whether the situation admits a more philosophically interesting reading. Specifically, I want your essay to examine whether Kepler drew a rigorous conceptual distinction between what he considered superstitious predictive astrology—claiming direct causal influence of planets on individual human affairs—and a correlative or harmonic astrology that reads planetary configurations as expressions of a divinely ordered geometric cosmos, without positing occult forces acting on bodies. The central question is whether this distinction genuinely protects his astrological practice from contradiction with his scientific naturalism, or whether it collapses once we examine what he actually did when casting a horoscope.

Please write an essay that weighs both sides of this controversy and reaches a justified conclusion: was Kepler's astrology an intellectually continuous extension of his cosmological principles, or was it an opportunistic departure masked by philosophical distinctions? You should ground your argument in the conceptual structure of Kepler's known commitments—his belief in a mathematically ordered universe, his rejection of action-at-a-distance without physical mediation, and his view that celestial bodies are material entities—without providing bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific editions or page numbers. I am interested in the coherence of the conceptual argument rather than in scholarly apparatus.
