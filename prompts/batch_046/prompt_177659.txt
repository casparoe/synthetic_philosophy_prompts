As a software engineer without any formal philosophy background, I have been trying to understand the moral logic behind Argentina's post-dictatorship transitional justice, particularly the tension between state-granted amnesty and private claims to punishment. I think Juan José Campanella's 2009 film The Secret in Their Eyes (El secreto de sus ojos) dramatizes this tension in a way that pure argument does not, and I would like to use it as the center of a discussion with colleagues who have no access to outside sources.

Read the film through the lens of transitional justice philosophy: the Argentine Leyes de Obediencia Debida y Punto Final blocked prosecution for Dirty War crimes, creating a political settlement in which the state waived retribution in exchange for democratic stability. In the film, judiciary agent Benjamín Espósito investigates the 1974 rape and murder of Liliana Colotto. The killer, Isidoro Gómez, is caught and sentenced, but is released after one month through political interference and eventually absorbed into the state's security apparatus, protected by the amnesty framework. Decades later, in 1999, Espósito discovers that the victim's husband, Ricardo Morales, has not killed Gómez but has kept him imprisoned for roughly twenty-five years in total isolation—feeding him, denying him human contact, and refusing either execution or release.

Please take a definite, committed position: does the film ultimately present Morales's private captivity as a morally legitimate assertion of an inalienable victim's claim that the state had no right to extinguish, or as a tragic corruption of justice that replicates the very dehumanization it seeks to answer? Ground your reading in specific scenes—such as Espósito's discovery in the rural cottage, his initial departure and subsequent return, Morales's explanation of why he never killed Gómez, and the visual contrast between the open, communal space of the stadium chase and the sealed, private space of captivity—rather than reducing the film to an illustration of a philosophical thesis.

At the same time, be honest about where the philosophical framing fails: does the work resist being read as a position on amnesty? What does the film add to the debate—through its use of memory, sight, and temporal delay—that an argument about state forgiveness and victim rights alone cannot capture? Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
