After a long and frustrating argument with a friend about whether contractualist ethics can ever justify sacrificing a few for the many, I realized I need an operational version of the theory rather than another abstract seminar. I chair the allocation review board for a regional water authority; we meet every quarter under tight deadlines to approve or reject infrastructure proposals that impose small, distributed burdens on upstream communities in exchange for large, diffuse benefits for downstream populations. I need a reusable decision procedure—an ordered checklist with explicit tests—that translates Scanlon's contractualist principle, that an action is wrong if it violates principles that no affected party could reasonably reject, into a tool my non-philosopher committee members can apply without specialized training. The procedure must confront the aggregation problem directly: it should specify how to weigh many minor harms against one catastrophic harm, whether benefits that only appear in aggregate can justify concentrated burdens, and if there is a non-arbitrary cutoff where summed small injuries become impermissible.

Please construct the procedure and apply it to three cases: first, a pipeline that marginally raises long-term health risks for ten thousand residents but prevents drought for half a million; second, a reservoir that permanently displaces one small community to secure water for a major metropolis; and third, a deliberately hard case in which the procedure permits imposing a severe, concentrated burden on a minority for a genuinely massive collective gain—I want you to flag precisely where in the checklist that result emerges, explain why Scanlon's framework demands it rather than allowing an intuitive veto, and identify the points where judgment cannot be eliminated, such as when framing a loss as an active burden versus a foregone benefit depends on prior normative commitments or when reasonable rejection is genuinely ambiguous across different cultural standpoints. The deliverable should also state what the procedure cannot settle—for instance, whether contractualism extends to non-human entities affected by diversion, or how to weight the claims of future generations—and must include an explicit statement of what would change your mind about the procedure's reliability: which interpretation of reasonable rejection, which treatment of probabilistic harm, or which boundary condition on aggregation, if shown to be mistaken, would cause you to reorder the tests or abandon the aggregation step entirely.
