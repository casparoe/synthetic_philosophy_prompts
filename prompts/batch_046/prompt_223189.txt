Write an essay on whether dramatists owe the dead a direct duty of representational fidelity that does not reduce to harm against the living. Consider the view that misrepresenting a historical figure in fiction wrongs the deceased themselves by corrupting the narrative coherence of the life they authored—the pattern of commitments, relationships, and actions through which they exercised agency—so that dramatic distortion is not merely inaccurate but violates a kind of posthumous autonomy. Oppose this to the view that because the dead have no present interests, consciousness, or capacity for suffering, any moral weight of fidelity must be instrumental: protecting the living from deception, honoring descendants' attachments, or preserving collective historical memory. Weigh the arguments, addressing specifically whether recognizing a direct duty requires an implausible metaphysics of posthumous harm, and whether there is a meaningful moral difference between falsifying documented actions and inventing unrecorded interior mental states. Defend a clear verdict—either that fidelity is an independent moral obligation grounded in respect for the integrity of a completed life, or that it is a derivative and contingent virtue—and justify it.
