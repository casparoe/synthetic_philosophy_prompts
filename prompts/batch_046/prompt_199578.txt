I chair a seven-member interdisciplinary review board for a state environmental agency, and in the last quarter we have twice faced cases where our individual judgments on premises and our collective vote on the final recommendation pulled in opposite directions. We evaluate industrial expansions against a statutory “net public benefit” standard by judging three premises—whether projected emissions stay within the local airshed capacity, whether mitigation technology is commercially deployable at scale, and whether community consent was obtained without coercion—before voting on the conclusion that the project should be approved. I now recognize this as an instance of the discursive dilemma: majorities can align on a conclusion even when no consistent set of premises commands a majority, and I need a reusable decision procedure my board can run in our 90-minute sessions without philosophical training.

Please design a step-by-step aggregation protocol that specifies the exact order of testing premise-majorities versus conclusion-majority, the numerical threshold (simple majority, two-thirds, or unanimous) required at each node, the conditions that trigger a mandatory structured revision round rather than an immediate vote, and the precise points where the procedure must stop and hand the decision to me as chair because the conflict is one of statutory values rather than aggregative logic. The procedure should include a small set of test conditions—worded as yes/no or threshold-check questions—that a non-expert member can apply to any judgment matrix.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: (A) five members approve the conclusion, but only three endorse the emissions premise, five endorse deployability, and four endorse consent; (B) all seven unanimously endorse every premise, yet the conclusion splits four-to-three in favor; and (C) a case in which your procedure outputs “approve with a recorded dissent on premises” that violates my intuition that unanimous premises should guarantee approval, and you must explain why the procedure produces that result and whether I am permitted to override it. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot resolve—particularly whether premise-based aggregation is normatively required by our statute or whether conclusion-based aggregation remains permissible when the two conflict—and flag any step that is a judgment call dressed up as formal logic.
