I am writing a novel in which a retired philosophy professor explains Kant's position on lying to a younger character. The professor summarizes it like this: In Kant's essay on lying from philanthropic concerns, he argues that lying to the murderer at the door is never permissible, not even to save an innocent life, because truth-telling is a perfect duty—an exceptionless obligation—whereas the duty to preserve another's life is only an imperfect duty of virtue, which allows latitude in how it is fulfilled. The professor notes that in the casuistical questions of the Metaphysics of Morals, Kant adds that if your lie happens to reveal the victim's hiding place, you share moral responsibility for the death, since by violating an unconditional rule you inserted yourself into the causal chain. From this, the professor concludes that Kant's ethics is essentially rigorist: all moral rules are absolute, consequences are entirely irrelevant to moral judgment, and an agent can be blameworthy for outcomes that were genuinely unforeseeable. Something about this feels like a caricature, but I am not sure which parts are faithful to Kant and which are exaggerations. Please audit the summary: separate what a Kant scholar would accept as accurate, identify the one or two subtle misrepresentations or overstatements, and tell me whether the professor's conclusion about rigorism and unforeseeable outcomes actually follows from the accurate parts. Write your answer in American English.
