As a retired natural scientist who has recently begun reading philosophy seriously, I have found myself returning to a paired inversion that seems to run through modern thought: Darwin demonstrated that biological competence—functional design—can exist without a comprehending designer, and Turing demonstrated that behavioral competence—intelligent performance—can exist without internal comprehension. I would like you to argue for a further, less discussed inversion that extends this pattern into the philosophy of science itself. Specifically, defend the claim that scientific models can possess full predictive and explanatory competence even when their internal variables bear no structural correspondence to the mechanisms they target, and that recognizing this "representational inversion" undermines both naive realism and instrumentalism without collapsing into either. In developing this argument, draw on the Darwinian and Turing-style decoupling of competence from comprehension to show why explanatory success should be assessed by something like teleonomic stability—the sustained functional integration of a model with its predictive environment—rather than by representational fidelity or by the presence of a hidden comprehender. Please also address whether this view can preserve the intuition that some models are genuinely explanatory rather than merely predictive, without smuggling back in the very comprehension it seeks to eliminate. I ask that you be explicit about what would change your mind: identify the specific argument or empirical finding—perhaps a proof that predictive competence necessarily entails structural correspondence, or a failure of the teleonomic analogy in scientific practice—that would lead you to abandon or significantly revise this position, and point to where your reasoning is most vulnerable.
