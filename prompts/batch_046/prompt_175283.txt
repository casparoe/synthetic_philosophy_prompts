Write the opening remarks—about six to eight minutes when delivered—for the reopening ceremony of the Westmere Municipal Archive, rebuilt after a fire destroyed part of its holdings five years ago. The room is divided: on one side are professional historians and archivists who will recognize Nietzsche’s distinction among monumental, antiquarian, and critical history without being told; on the other are local donors, city council members, and residents who funded the restoration and expect the archive to be complete, and who are also there to honor the late director, Dr. Elias Vore, a revered local historian who believed that a true archive must preserve every document it touches, regardless of content. The speaker is Vore’s successor, and the philosophical framework must do real structural work rather than decorate the occasion: she must defend the archive’s intentional incompleteness. Specifically, several boxes of records from a 1920s labor-suppression campaign—documents Vore had meticulously saved—have been deliberately left unrestored and excluded from the new digital collection, not through neglect but as an exercise in critical history. The speech must therefore navigate a live tension: it needs to celebrate Vore’s monumental and antiquarian legacy without endorsing his total-preservation ethic; it needs to explain to non-philosophers why allowing certain records to decay can be an act of historical honesty rather than censorship or waste; and it needs to signal precisely to the specialists which Nietzschean mode is operating at each point, all without using technical jargon that alienates the donors. Paraphrase the three modes in plain language—do not quote Nietzsche’s text directly. The argument should show why a purely antiquarian commitment to completeness fails here, why the monumental celebration of Vore must be qualified, and how the critical mode reshapes what the rebuilt archive is for. Make the speech land for both halves of the room, and ensure that if the critical element were removed, the speaker would have nothing honest to say about the missing boxes.
