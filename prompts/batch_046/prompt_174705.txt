I am an undergraduate in a course on philosophy of history and historical explanation, and I have been asked to draft the kind of joint statement that an adversarial collaboration should produce between two camps in our readings. One camp, which I will call the structuralists, holds that large-scale outcomes—say, the collapse of an empire or the outbreak of a major conflict—are overdetermined by long-run economic, institutional, and demographic forces, so that talk of a single missing nail changing everything is at best a pedagogical fiction and at worst a retrospective narrative fallacy in which we select the micro-event only because we already know the macro-outcome. The other camp, which I will call the contingency theorists, insists that human societies are non-linear dynamical systems displaying sensitive dependence on initial conditions, and that genuinely open branching points exist—moments where a small perturbation is amplified rather than dampened—so that metaphysical contingency and chaos-theoretic unpredictability coincide in history. Both sides invoke the nursery rhyme about the nail and the kingdom, but they seem to mean different things by it: the structuralists treat it as an example of misleading counterfactual storytelling, while the contingency theorists treat it as an intuitive model of causal amplification in complex systems.

Could you draft the joint statement they should sign? Please include: (1) a numbered list of claims both camps can genuinely accept about historical explanation, chaos theory, and the limits of prediction; (2) a disagreements table that states each side's position in wording that its own members would accept, with particular care to separate the claim that societies are mathematically chaotic from the claim that history is metaphysically open, since I think part of their dispute is verbal; (3) the cruxes—especially whether we can distinguish retrospective selection of a "nail" event from genuine causal amplification, whether chaos-theoretic models have been empirically validated for large-scale social dynamics, and whether "contingency" here denotes epistemic limits or ontological branching; and (4) for each crux, one concrete historical case (for instance, the fall of Constantinople in 1453 or the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand) that both sides agree is relevant, together with each side's explicit prediction about what that case shows. I am not asking for a bibliography or a reading list; I want the carving of the phenomenon itself—where the disagreement is real, where it dissolves into ambiguity, and what exactly would have to be shown for either camp to revise its view.
