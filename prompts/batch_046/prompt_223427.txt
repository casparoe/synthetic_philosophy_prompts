Consider the following philosophy-of-mind exam question and one student's answer.

Question: Daniel Dennett argues that much of our thinking about consciousness is trapped in what he calls the Cartesian theater. Define this misconception, explain Dennett's multiple-drafts alternative, and clarify whether that alternative eliminates a central point of awareness or merely redistributes it across neural subsystems.

Student answer: The Cartesian theater is the mistaken belief, going back to Descartes, that consciousness requires a single physical location in the brain—such as the pineal gland—where all sensory information is projected onto a kind of internal screen and observed by a non-physical self. Dennett rejects this picture because neuroscience reveals no such screen and no internal viewer. In its place he offers the multiple-drafts model: instead of one unified narrative being assembled in one place, the brain continuously produces many competing, partial descriptions of events across different regions. At any given time, one of these drafts achieves a kind of prominence by being accessed by other cognitive processes, and that accessed draft just is the conscious experience. Thus the model does not truly do away with a center; it simply fragments the observer into many temporary local centers, each of which can function as a mini-theater for particular contents.
