A composer is writing variations on a familiar melody. She has identified every feature she might alter—key, tempo, instrumentation, harmonic rhythm, and dynamic range—treating each as an independent dial that can be turned while leaving the underlying form intact. Her collaborator objects that this picture is too shallow: true creativity, he says, involves recognizing that dimensions everyone treats as fixed—such as the listener's expectation of a resolving cadence—are themselves just further dials, and that the most profound variations come not from sliding existing controls but from deciding which controls belong in the space at all. The composer fears that if she accepts this, she can no longer distinguish working within a tradition from inventing an entirely new form, since redefining the parameter space seems to dissolve the very idea of a "variation." Is her collaborator correct that redefining what is available to change is part of genuine variation, or does that collapse the distinction between variation and invention?
