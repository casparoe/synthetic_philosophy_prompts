I'm trying to determine the principle behind my judgments about when a pictorial symbol system achieves the kind of cross-linguistic transparency Otto Neurath sought for his international picture language, and when it collapses into ambiguity or culture-bound interpretation.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

A standardized factory icon with smoke stacks, placed beside an upward arrow and a numeral, clearly communicates rising industrial production across language groups. The identical icon alone on a warning sign communicates environmental danger, not manufacturing. So syntactic framing seems to govern interpretation, but I am unsure how far.

A sequence of three panels showing a human figure with a progressively larger heart symbol is intended to represent improving cardiovascular health statistics. I judge that it fails: viewers consistently read it as growing emotional capacity or romantic attachment. By contrast, the same figure with a red horizontal bar across the chest that shrinks in height communicates the health trend reliably, even though the red bar is purely conventional and bears no perceptual resemblance to a heart. So perceptual resemblance to the referent does not determine success.

Using repetition of an icon—three identical shoe icons in a row—to represent plurality works well for concrete objects. But using repetition of an abstract shape—three identical triangles—to represent three instances of conflict fails; viewers interpret the repetition as decorative pattern rather than quantification. So repetition is not a universal syntactic rule.

A picture sentence depicting two groups separated by a wall, with goods moving over it via an arrow, succeeds in representing international trade across political barriers. Replacing the wall with a river while keeping the arrow makes viewers think of smuggling or natural migration rather than trade. Thus the specific visual metaphor does work that pure syntax does not control.

In demographic charts, a skull symbol consistently reads as mortality data, even though in other contexts skulls signal poison or rebellion. I think the statistical frame suppresses the alternatives. But placing the same skull inside a decorative border with floral motifs destroys the mortality reading entirely. So framing dominates, yet it is fragile.

Adding color conventions—red for decrease, green for increase—to these icons works in contexts with traffic-light associations, but breaks in contexts where red signals prosperity and green signals inexperience. So any claim to universality is conditional on shared conventional associations that are not themselves pictorial.

Given these verdicts, is there a coherent principle that predicts which pictorial propositions remain transparent across linguistic communities and which fragment into local interpretation? Does it turn on an interaction between syntactic frame, conventional color or shape associations, and the degree to which the symbol is anchored to a concrete object rather than an abstract relation? And what does the resulting principle predict for a case I have not resolved: using an asymmetrical spiral form, placed within a statistical grid with no representational imagery, to communicate the concept of unjust distribution in an international infographic? Would framing necessarily fail to stabilize its meaning, or could the grid supply enough context to make it work? Please state the principle precisely, identify which of my verdicts it forces me to drop if it is to remain simple, and apply it to the spiral case.
