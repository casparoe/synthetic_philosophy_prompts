I'm drafting the central paper for my upper-division epistemology seminar, and I need to sharpen the argument before I commit to a thesis. Our course has been working with what our professor calls the dogmatism paradox: the claim that a blanket refusal to accept beliefs without justification is self-undermining, since that refusal itself must either be unjustified—hence dogmatic—or justified only by premises that eventually terminate in an unjustified stopping point. We've examined the standard escapes—coherentist webs, contextualist shifts in evidential standards, and foundationalist basic beliefs—but I remain unconvinced that any of them fully dissolves the tension rather than relocating it.

Write an essay that takes up this controversy and reaches a defended verdict. Specifically: is the dogmatism paradox a genuine logical obstacle to anti-dogmatism, or is it a pseudo-problem generated by conflating first-order and second-order justification? Weigh the strongest version of the self-refutation objection—namely, that the principle "accept nothing without adequate justification" is itself accepted without adequate justification—against the best available reply, whether that reply appeals to contextualism, a tiered model of justification, or a distinction between procedural and substantive rationality. Do not end on a non-committal note; defend a clear position, either that anti-dogmatism can be maintained consistently without hidden dogmatism, or that it cannot and we should therefore embrace a consciously limited, reflective form of dogmatism.

To keep the essay from becoming a generic survey, build your analysis around the "justification budget" framework we developed in seminar—the idea that rational agents operate with finite epistemic resources and must allocate justification selectively rather than distributing it universally across every belief. Apply this framing structurally: show whether demanding universal justification exceeds any plausible budget, whether recognizing such a budget forces us to acknowledge a necessary, non-arbitrary stopping point, and whether that stopping point counts as dogmatism in the pejorative sense or as a rational limit-condition. Your conclusion should depend on whether this economic model resolves the paradox, merely relocates it to the meta-level, or collapses under its own weight once we ask who sets the budget and on what grounds.
