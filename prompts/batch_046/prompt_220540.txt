I'm trying to see whether my views on group-level generalization form a stable package or whether I'm smuggling in incompatible commitments. Here is where I stand. (1) I reject essentialist accounts of national character: there is no shared psychological essence or innate temperament that makes one people fundamentally different from another. (2) I think that defeasible statistical generalizations about culturally embedded practices—such as the prevalence of particular bureaucratic norms or communicative styles—are epistemically legitimate and can be action-guiding, provided they are indexed to institutions and social structures rather than to individuals. (3) I endorse a notion I call "functional character": a stable equilibrium of practices, expectations, and feedback loops that predicts aggregate behavior without presupposing an underlying essence—something like an institutional attractor that reproduces itself over generations. (4) I hold that moral judgments directed at entire peoples are almost always illegitimate, with one exception: when a democratic majority knowingly and repeatedly endorses harmful policies, collective moral assessment becomes warranted. (5) I maintain that epistemic humility requires treating all group-level predictions as highly provisional, especially for diaspora communities, small-n populations, or rapidly changing societies, and that such predictions must never justify exclusion or discrimination. (6) At the same time, I believe policymakers sometimes have to act on the best available group-level predictions even when the evidential base is thin, because institutional inaction carries its own distributive consequences that are not neutral.

Are these jointly tenable? Please audit them: identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict but can be reconciled, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs—whether it requires abandoning functional character, narrowing the democratic-exception clause, weakening the policy-action claim, or something else. In particular, I worry that my institutional-attractor view in (3) undermines the collective agency I seem to presuppose in (4), and that (5) and (6) pull in opposite directions on the use of thin evidence. Tell me which single revision would clean up the most tension, and whether there is a coherent version of this package that preserves the core of (2), (3), and (6).
