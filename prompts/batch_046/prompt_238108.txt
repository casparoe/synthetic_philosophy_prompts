I have just come away from a lengthy and rather heated discussion with a close friend about whether a world of pervasive suffering creates obligations only to prevent future lives, or also to protect those already alive, and we kept returning to Cormac McCarthy's The Road as an unintended philosophical test case. My friend argues that the father's relentless preservation of the boy is a humanist rejection of any suffering-focused calculus—especially the kind that treats non-existence as preferable to even minimal harm—while I suspect the novel's ending quietly undermines that humanism by showing the boy absorbed into another family that will presumably reproduce the same cycle of bare survival. I would like you to read The Road through the lens of negative utilitarianism and suffering-focused ethics, paying particular attention to the tension between harm-prevention (avoiding the creation of new sufferers) and suffering-reduction (alleviating the pain of those who already exist). Does McCarthy's aestheticization of endurance—particularly in the father's refusal to surrender to despair—function as an implicit critique of strong negative utilitarianism, or does the novel's refusal to imagine a genuinely flourishing life for the survivors actually vindicate the view that continued existence in such conditions is itself a moral failure? I am preparing this for a small discussion group that knows the book well but has not read the ethics literature, so I want a reading that treats the novel's narrative structure and symbolic imagery with the same seriousness as the philosophical arguments, without reducing either side to a mere illustration of the other.
