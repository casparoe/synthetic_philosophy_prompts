I've been reading about Lemkin and how he originally wanted genocide to cover cultural destruction too, not just the physical killing of people. Lately I've come around to this view: when governments or international tribunals hesistate to call something genocide—like in various recent conflicts—it's rarely because they're genuinely unsure about the legal definition or worried about diplomatic blowback. Instead, I think they're dodging what I'd call a "moral cascade": once you apply the genocide label, it triggers an all-or-nothing framing where criminal prosecution becomes the only legitimate response, and local reperative or political mechanisims get pushed aside. So the politics of naming, in my view, is mostly about avoiding that cascade rather than denying the atrocity itself.

But I want to know what should actually move me away from this. Not just the obvious "sometimes states deny harm outright"—I mean concrete cases or arguments that show refusal to name is driven by something else entirely, like legal precision concerns that are genuinely principled, or instances where applying the genocide label actually strengthened local justice instead of crowding it out. Also, does my view risk being unfalsifiable—if I can always say "they're just avoiding the cascade"—and is that insulation a problem for a claim of this kind? I'd like the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, including what someone who holds my cascade-avoidance story would themselves admit counts against them, and whether there are predictions my view makes that its rivals don't.
