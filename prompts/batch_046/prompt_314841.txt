In my philosophy-of-law reading group, a debate that began as a familiar clash—retributivists insisting punishment must track culpable wrongdoing regardless of social effects, consequentialists insisting it is only justified by future benefits—has stalled in a strange way. Both sides now routinely invoke “proportionality,” yet they treat it as doing entirely different work: the retributivists understand it as a non-instrumental moral relation between wrong and suffering, while the consequentialists understand it as an empirically stable heuristic that prevents over-punishment and sustains institutional trust. The stall is not empirical—everyone accepts the same crime-rate data and the same psychological studies on public attitudes—nor is it about whether proportionality matters; neither side denies its importance. Rather, each claims the other is “explaining away” the real reason we care about it, and both now seem able to accommodate proportionality within their own framework without conceding anything. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about punishment per se, but about whether moral explanations of a single practice can be genuinely plural—whether sentencing can be sustained by both desert and utility without either being primary—or whether one explanation must always be the “real” one and the other merely derivative. Is that diagnosis correct? Or is the stall hiding a substantive dispute about the metaphysics of desert, about which kind of explanation counts as fundamental, or about bedrock intuitions regarding naturalness that neither side can argue for? I want a structural diagnosis—verbal, normative, metaphysical, or intuitive—supported by what each side actually says about proportionality, together with an honest assessment of whether anything—a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding about legitimacy, or a formal model of sentencing rules—could actually end the impasse, or whether no progress should be expected and why. Please also state clearly what consideration or argument would change your mind about this diagnosis, and point to where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
