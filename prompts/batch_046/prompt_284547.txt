Here is roughly where I have landed in my dissertation chapter on phenomenal consciousness, and I want a consistency audit before I commit to the framework. I defend a restricted actualist higher-order representational theory with the following commitments.

(1) Phenomenal consciousness requires an occurrent, non-dispositional higher-order thought directed at a first-order mental state; mere dispositional availability of such a thought is insufficient.

(2) I accept higher-order misrepresentation: a HOT can take an actual first-order state as its object but mischaracterize its properties—representing, for instance, a neural noise state as pain—so that the subject has a conscious experience with phenomenal properties that the first-order substrate lacks.

(3) I reject self-representational theories on the grounds that unconscious subliminal self-monitoring demonstrates that self-representation is not sufficient for phenomenal consciousness.

(4) I hold that the HOTs in question require possession of specific mental-state concepts, which implies that non-linguistic animals and pre-linguistic infants lack phenomenal consciousness in the full sense, though they may possess access-conscious or affective states.

(5) I treat the explanatory gap as dissolving once phenomenal properties are identified with the properties represented in the content of the HOT, leaving no further mystery about why first-order representation feels like something.

(6) Methodologically, I think empirical attributions of consciousness should depend on whether the best behavioral and explanatory model of a creature requires positing these conceptually demanding higher-order monitoring capacities.

My question is whether this package is jointly tenable. Specifically:

- Is there a hidden tension between (1) and (5)? If phenomenal properties are just what is represented in the HOT's content, then requiring an actual first-order object in (1) looks redundant, especially since (2) already permits the substrate to lack the represented property. Does (1) collapse into a weaker causal-anchor claim, or does (5) need to be restricted?

- Does (3) actually conflict with (4)? If the relevant HOTs require sophisticated mental-state concepts, then subliminal self-monitoring in animals or infants would not involve the right kind of self-representation to begin with, which seems to undermine the premise of (3)'s objection to self-representational theories. Is (3) salvageable only by weakening (4), or is the tension merely apparent?

- How severe is the tension—if any—between (4) and (6)? One commitment looks internalist and concept-heavy, the other looks functionalist and behavior-driven. Can they be reconciled without costing either?

- Are there other pairwise tensions I am missing, such as between (2)'s misrepresentation and (6)'s explanatory practice, or between (2) and (3) regarding what counts as the intentional object?

Please rank the tensions by severity, indicate which ones dissolve on closer inspection, and for each genuine conflict specify the least costly revision and what that revision sacrifices. I am not signaling which commitment I am most attached to, so treat all six as provisional.
