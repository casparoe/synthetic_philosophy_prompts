I cover science and epistemology for a magazine, and I am co-designing a ninety-minute seminar for about sixteen advanced undergraduates and early-career journalists on how information environments shape belief. Rather than a lecture, I want a structured, in-room simulation that makes participants feel the force of the idea that structural filtration—where dissenting reports are never visible because the aggregation rules themselves exclude them—and active discrediting—where dissent is received and then systematically rejected—can converge on the same epistemic state through entirely different pathways. The group should build the filtration architecture themselves in an initial design phase, then live inside it during a second decision-making phase with small but real collective stakes, such as a pooled-point system that converts into a shared reward or penalty tied to collective accuracy, so that the mechanism rather than the facilitator delivers the insight.

Please design the full activity with clear roles, paper-based rules and a visible scoreboard requiring no digital tools, and a payoff scheme in which individual outcomes depend on group belief-formation rather than on personal victory. Include a debrief that maps each phase onto the conceptual distinction between absence and discredit, states explicitly what the simulation cannot reproduce—such as the long-term identity costs that make leaving a real echo chamber psychologically expensive—and predicts what will go wrong on the first run, for instance participants conflating the two structures in discussion, with instructions on how to correct the confusion without breaking the experiential logic. The idea must emerge from what the rules and incentives cause the participants to do before anyone names the concepts.
