The argument from illusion can be reconstructed in three premises. First, in cases of illusion or hallucination, the subject is aware of something that possesses the properties she seems to see—for instance, a bent stick when none is present. Second, if a veridical perception and an illusion have exactly the same phenomenal character, then in the veridical case the subject is also aware of something with those properties. Third, since in veridical perception there is no physical object with those properties, the object of awareness must be non-physical—typically a sense-datum—so all perception is indirect. Disjunctivists reject the second premise by denying that veridical and hallucinatory experiences share a common mental kind. On the view defended by philosophers like Martin, when perception is veridical the subject stands in a direct perceptual relation to a physical object and its properties, with no intermediary mental object; in hallucination, by contrast, there is no object of awareness at all, and the experience is an entirely different kind of mental event. This blocks the argument because there is no shared phenomenal factor that forces us to posit sense-data in the good case.

A problem arises with partial illusions, where an object is genuinely present but appears with properties it lacks. Here it seems there is both a direct relation to the object and an illusory element. I think disjunctivists must classify any experience containing an illusory component as a hallucination, since the direct-object relation is broken whenever the experienced properties do not match the actual object; otherwise they would have to admit a common mental factor—the misrepresented property—shared between this case and a total hallucination of the same property. Therefore partial illusions force the disjunctivist to adopt a hybrid classification: the mental kind is determined by the dominant component, so if the veridical aspect is primary the state counts as perception with an error, but if the illusory aspect dominates it is a hallucination. This also solves the indistinguishability problem. Because disjunctivists deny that the two states share phenomenal character, they do not need to explain why we mistake one for the other at the level of mental kind; they only need an epistemological story about why our judgments go wrong. As Martin notes, the transparency of experience means we are always aware of mental particulars in hallucination, but in perception we are aware of the world itself.
