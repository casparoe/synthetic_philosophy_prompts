I'm taking a seminar on Buddhist ethics and consequentialism, and I'm trying to determine my own view by testing my intuitions against concrete cases rather than abstract doctrine. A bodhisattva who lies to a tyrant to prevent a genocide: right, despite the negative karma incurred. A monk who redirects monastery funds to feed famine victims, knowing the institution will collapse and deprive future practitioners of teachings: right, though I feel genuine tension. A meditator who remains in deep jhāna for decades, eliminating all personal suffering but teaching no one: not right. A being who voluntarily takes rebirth in a painful realm to guide others, accepting far more suffering than they relieve: right. A ruler who orders the torture of one innocent person to save thousands, with complete mental detachment and no anger, purely as skillful means: not right, which suggests mental purity alone is insufficient. A monk who allows a fatal parasite to kill him rather than destroy it, forfeiting decades of teaching: right. But I keep oscillating on this case: a bodhisattva who delays final parinirvāṇa to remain in saṃsāra for eons helping beings, even if the total suffering they personally endure across those lives exceeds the suffering they actually prevent in others. I believe they should still stay—yet if Buddhist ethics is not simply aggregate-suffering reduction across rebirths, I don't see what principle licenses my other verdicts. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one about orienting action toward universal liberation rather than welfare-maximization—that captures all of these, or must I abandon at least one judgment? And what does it say about the case I keep returning to?
