Question: Consider the proposal made by the municipal authorities of a town that suffered a mass atrocity fifty years ago. They intend to establish what they term an Archive of Unrestored Presence. Under this plan, every physical structure linked to the atrocity will be maintained in its present decayed condition—without restoration, without interpretive signage, without guided narration, and without any curatorial framework that assigns collective meaning. Simultaneously, all recorded oral testimonies from survivors and descendants will be permanently sealed and withdrawn from public access, on the explicit ground that narrative remembrance instrumentalizes private grief for public ends. Evaluate this proposal with reference to Avishai Margalit’s distinction between an ethics of memory and a politics of memory, and with reference to David Rieff’s argument in In Praise of Forgetting that modern societies have transformed remembrance into a therapeutic moral imperative that often obstructs genuine forgetting. Specifically, address whether the preservation of material ruins without interpretation satisfies Margalit’s account of the duty to remember, and whether the suppression of oral testimony successfully avoids the dangers Rieff associates with politicized remembrance.

Student answer: The proposal attempts to separate material preservation from narrative politics, which aligns well with Margalit’s distinction. Margalit holds that an ethics of memory governs thick interpersonal relations, whereas a politics of memory concerns how states construct collective identity. Because the Archive avoids plaques and collective framing, it remains within the ethical domain rather than the political one, thereby satisfying Margalit’s demand that we remember the dead without turning them into symbols. The preservation of the physical ruins serves as an objective witness that does not depend on interpretation, so it fulfills the duty to remember in a direct, non-instrumental way.

Rieff, in his critique of modern therapeutic culture, argues that societies have made remembrance into an absolute moral duty that actually prevents communities from moving forward. By sealing the oral testimonies, the proposal removes the narrative fuel that keeps trauma alive in public discourse, which is precisely what Rieff recommends. Rieff treats narrative remembrance as the primary vehicle for the therapeutic obsession with the past, so eliminating testimony while retaining silent material traces avoids both politicization and therapeutic fixation.

Therefore, the proposal succeeds on both counts: it honors the dead through material presence without narrative exploitation, and it permits the community to forget in the manner Rieff regards as necessary for moral health.
