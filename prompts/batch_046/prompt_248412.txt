I'm in a seminar on moral responsability and our tutor keeps giving quick yes/no verdicts on blame in these hypothetical set-ups. So far she's said: the guy with an undiagnosed brain tumor making him violent, not responsible; the woman literally held at gunpoint and forced to forge checks, not responsible; the guy severely abused as a kid who runs fraud as an adult, not responsible; the person with a genetic condition wiping out empathy who never consented to testing, not responsible; and even someone raised in a fully deterministic virtual world designed to make them kind, who then does a good deed—she said not responsible for that either. So every case she's touched involves either coercion, mental illness, or a deterministic origin she doesn't control.

Next week she has to rule on a case I made up: a mentally healthy woman with normal reasoning voluntarily installs a neural implant that takes over all her ethical decision-making. The implant was trained only on her own past moral choices plus some random noise, she knows exactly what she's getting, and she consents because she wants to be 'better'. After installation she does something harmful that the implant calculated, not her conscious self. Do you think she'll say she's responsible?

I'm less interested in the bare answer than in the rule she's running. Could be Strawson's Basic Argument—nobody's ever truly responsible since you can't be causa sui. Could be a compatabalist view where you're responsible if your actions flow from your own reasoning, even if you didn't pick your nature. Or maybe a Frankfurt-style deep-self identification thing. What does each predict here? Give me your best guess and why, and then name a further case that'd actually split these theories apart, since I feel like the implant scenario might still be muddy.
