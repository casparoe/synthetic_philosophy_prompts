I am taking an upper-division seminar on the archaeology of narrative and the epistemology of historical origins, and for our closing session I want to present a poem that performs the problem we have traced all term: the point at which a legend stops being treated as literature and starts being read as evidence, and whether that boundary is ever genuinely stable. I would like a sestina—six stanzas of six lines each, followed by a three-line envoi—using the rotating end-words: “Troy,” “evidence,” “Arthur,” “source,” “Exodus,” and “record.” The formal rotation should do conceptual work: as the end-words migrate through the stanzas, the same narrative fragments—Schliemann’s excavation layer at Hisarlik being interpreted as the material trace of Homer’s city, the chronicler Geoffrey’s transformation of Welsh oral tradition into a pseudo-historical chronicle, and the debate over whether an Egyptian victory inscription mentioning Israel offers an archival trace of the Exodus—should shift in disciplinary status from myth to datum and back again, so that the reader experiences the classification as procedural rather than discovered. I want the poem to avoid resolving whether any of these three cases actually crosses the threshold into history; instead, the envoi should gather all six end-words together in an order that implies “legend” and “history” have become interchangeable registers of the same fragment. It needs to be precise enough that someone familiar with the historiography recognizes the specific circularity problems—such as ceramic typologies being dated by the very stratigraphic layer they are then used to authenticate—without asking for citations, footnotes, or a bibliography. The tone should suit a seminar closing: intellectually serious rather than sermonic, leaving the tension structurally open rather than resolved.
