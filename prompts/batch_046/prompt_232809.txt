I was arguing with a friend last week about whether the Enlightenment should be taught as one thing or two, and it made me notice something weird in how high school curricula are structured. There's usually this unit called "The Enlightenment and Its Legacy" that covers Locke on rights, Kant on autonomy, maybe a bit of Diderot's encyclopedia, but then months later there's a completely separate unit—often in world history rather than philosophy—called "Age of Empire" that talks about the slave trade and colonial extraction without ever noting that a lot of the same people wrote both kinds of texts. What implicit philosophy of history does that separation presuppose? Does it treat reason and domination as belonging to different conceptual boxes, assume that progress has a pure "essence" that can be extracted from its historical conditions, or just take linear development for granted? Reconstruct the theory, say whether the curriculum strictly needs it or is compatible with rival views, and tell me where it's philosophically underdetermined.

Then, if we replaced that presupposition with the "domination" framing—where universal rights discourse and racial science develop as mutally constitutive parts of the same intellectual project—what would actually have to change? Not just which books get mentioned, but the chronological framing, the conceptual categories used to group figures, and whether "progress" can still be the organizing narrative at all. I'm not asking for a bibliography or specific page numbers, just the architecture of the implicit theory and what a rebuilt practice would look like.
