Consider the institutional cluster found in several liberal democracies: the legal system awards substantial damages for ecological harm that has already occurred, treating the affected ecosystem or community as bearing present standing that was violated at a past moment. At the same time, courts reject preemptive claims against future threats—such as a proposed industrial development—unless the risk is shown to be virtually certain, treating identical future harm as lacking present legal standing. Separately, the state's official scientific advisory bodies routinely publish long-range forecasts—of climate states, seismic risks, demographic shifts—using physical frameworks that treat space-time as a four-dimensional manifold in which the distinction between past, present and future events is frame-relative rather than absolute. These bodies describe future states with the same ontological weight as recorded past ones, yet they never explain why the law should treat harm at one temporal coordinate as fully compensable and harm at another as merely speculative.

What metaphysics of time and existence does this cluster of practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it require presentism—the view that only present entities exist and only present violations ground obligations—or can an eternalist, block-universe conception accommodate the legal asymmetry through some other principle? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely happens to fit it, and identify the precise point at which the legal distinction between past and future harm becomes philosophically unstable once one accepts that simultaneity is relative to an observer's frame of reference. Finally, if the state were to replace its implicit presentist metaphysics with a consistently eternalist one, what concrete changes would be required in its rules of standing, its burden of proof for future injuries, and its conception of which entities can be wronged?
