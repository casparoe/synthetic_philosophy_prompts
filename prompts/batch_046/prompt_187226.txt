Specifically, the prompt was: In his well-known critique of aggregation, Taurek argues that if we must choose between saving one person and saving five, we are not morally required to save the five merely because they are more numerous. First, present Taurek's core argument, focusing on his claim that suffering is comparable across persons but that there is no interpersonal aggregation of harms. Then evaluate whether his view can accommodate what we might call structured rescue obligations—situations in which an agent has previously undertaken a role-specific duty (for instance, as a lifeguard assigned to a particular beach sector) that gives her reason to save a smaller group within her sector rather than a larger group elsewhere. Does Taurek's rejection of the numbers-count principle undermine the coherence of such role-based reasons, or can they be reconciled?

Student answer: Taurek's central claim is that moral comparison works pairwise: I can understand that another person's suffering is comparable in severity to my own, but there is no meaningful operation of adding sufferings across different people into an aggregate harm. Because of this, when I face a choice between saving one person and saving five, there is no sense in which the five suffer "five times as much" collectively. The situation is structurally like being able to save only one of several friends: I have reason to save one, but the presence of four others does not generate a multiplied reason to save them instead. So Taurek rejects the idea that we should maximize the number of lives saved. He does concede, however, that once the disparity becomes extreme—say, one thousand to one—the comparative claim becomes so implausible that the numbers begin to carry some weight, though he denies they do so in ordinary ranges like five to one or even fifty to two.

Taurek also allows that special relationships can break the symmetry. If one of the potential victims is my child, I may save her even if that means leaving several strangers to die, because my parental duty gives me a comparative reason that applies only within that relationship. Applying this to structured rescue obligations: suppose a lifeguard is assigned to Sector A, where two swimmers are in danger, while fifty swimmers are in danger in Sector B. Since numbers never generate moral weight in ordinary ranges, the lifeguard should remain in Sector A and save the two, because her role creates a relational boundary that excludes the outsiders from consideration. In fact, Taurek's view strengthens role-based reasoning: because there is no aggregate harm pulling her toward Sector B, the only morally relevant factors are the special obligations tied to her assignment. Moreover, the presence of two people in Sector A gives her two separate, non-aggregable reasons—one for each swimmer—which together outweigh any single reason she might have toward anyone in Sector B. Thus the lifeguard's duty is not undermined but clarified by the rejection of aggregation, and she should save the pair in her sector.
