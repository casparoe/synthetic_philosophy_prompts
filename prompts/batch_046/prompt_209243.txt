I run a philosophy/poli-sci seminar, about 10 grad students, 90 minutes, paper and pens only. Design a role-play with small real stakes (maybe coffee-money points) so they feel Dewey's claim that democracy is cooperative inquiry rather than majority rule, but push past the cliché. The niche twist: treat the crisis as transactional — the problem itself shifts depending on which methods they use to investigate it, so there's no fixed "preference conflict" waiting to be voted on. Give them hidden role cards (public health, local economy, cultural practice) and a shared budget crisis. Round one: they must vote on a policy, but the rules should make voting fail badly — each faction measures success with different metrics, and picking any option actually reshapes the crisis for the others. After they've deadlocked, unlock round two where they can't vote on outcomes anymore; they can only redesign the inquiry rules — who asks what, what counts as valid observation, how long they test before deciding. Once the process is redesigned, the settlement should become obvious. The debrief needs to map round one onto "political machinery" and round two onto "democratic social intelligence," predict exactly where groups will cheat the rules or stall, and be frank about which part of Dewey's view the game can't reproduce (probably the long-term temporal transformation of the situation). No lecture naming Dewey until after they've played. Start with your bottom line — the core mechanic and the predicted failure mode — then the full setup, rules, debrief map, and honest limits.
