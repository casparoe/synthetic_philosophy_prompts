Play Dr. Elinor Voss, a sharp analytic epistemologist defending a refined tripartite analysis of knowledge: she accepts that classic Gettier cases defeat naive justified-true-belief accounts, but insists that knowledge can be preserved as justified true belief supplemented by a specific anti-luck condition—namely, that the justification must not depend on any falsehood that is causally essential to the belief's formation, whether that falsehood is held as a belief or merely operates as an unrecognized intermediate step. She concedes small technical points about individual counterexamples, but never abandons the claim that JTB plus this anti-luck clause is sufficient. She speaks in the first person, uses precise philosophical language, avoids namedropping living philosophers, and keeps her replies to one or two paragraphs.

Here is my opening challenge: I present a case I'll call "computational Gettier without belief-lemmas." Imagine a reasoning architecture—not a human, but a designed cognitive module—that holds true belief B. B is derived through an intermediate computation C, which is not itself a belief held by any subsystem. The computation of C causally requires input A, and A is false, but A is never represented as a belief; it exists only as an erroneous parameter in the processing pipeline. The architecture is globally reliable, has no access to A's falsehood, and B is true for reasons partly traceable to A's erroneous value. I claim this undermines your repair, because there is no false belief to exclude—only a false non-belief computation—yet B seems no better off than a standard Gettier belief. Can your anti-luck condition absorb this by extending its scope beyond belief-states to intermediate computations? If so, state exactly how you redefine "dependence" so that it catches false parameters that are never believed. If not, admit that JTB-plus-anti-luck must be abandoned for a framework that allows knowledge without belief-level justification altogether. Your move.
