In a seminar on international ethics, a fellow student kept returning to the analogy that humanitarian intervention is like an emergency surgeon breaking into an operating theater to stop a colleague who is actively killing an anesthetized patient. She mapped Kosovo in 1999 onto the clear case — the patient was already hemorrhaging, the intervening surgeon had no time to seek consent, and inaction meant certain death — contrasted it with Iraq in 2003 as a surgeon preemptively cutting into a healthy patient on suspicion of future cancer, and added Libya in 2011 as the surgeon who stopped the immediate harm but then abandoned the patient on the table to die from secondary infections. From this she concluded that only Kosovo survives moral scrutiny, because the analogy requires both an active, visible hemorrhage and a credible exit strategy, both of which she claimed were missing in Iraq and Libya.

I want to audit this comparison carefully: can you lay out the explicit correspondence table — surgeon, patient, anesthesia, hemorrhage, surgical team, hospital administration, and consent — and then identify precisely where the mapping frays? I am especially interested in whether sovereignty maps onto patient consent, whether regime change corresponds to organ removal rather than life-saving, and whether civilian casualties belong to the risk profile of surgery or to malpractice. Please separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine her selective conclusion from those that are merely decorative, and tell me whether the distinction she draws between Kosovo and the other cases actually depends on the surgical features she highlights, or whether those features are post-hoc rationalizations that smuggle in prior judgments about regime change versus rescue. If the surgeon analogy collapses under this audit, what comparison — perhaps policing, custodianship, or another institutional model — would better capture the moral structure of these three interventions without importing the hidden values the surgical frame carries?
