Draft the joint statement that should emerge from an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps on the extraction of latent knowledge from large language models.

Camp A—the Calibrationists—holds that factually correct information a model encodes but does not reliably emit (latent knowledge) can be extracted only through behavioral calibration: aggregating outputs across paraphrased prompts, measuring consistency under counterfactual framing, and using probabilistic methods to separate signal from sycophancy or context artifacts. They maintain that internal representations are too high-dimensional and interpretation-dependent to serve as independent evidence of belief, and that any mechanistic claim about a circuit's "meaning" is underdetermined without behavioral ground-truth.

Camp B—the Mechanists—holds that true latent knowledge resides in internal causal structure, and that behavioral methods are fundamentally confounded by training artifacts, social-desirability bias in outputs, and the model's capacity for strategic reasoning. They argue that only by tracing circuits that compute specific logical or factual relations can one distinguish genuine encoded beliefs from conversational performance, and that behavioral consistency is neither necessary nor sufficient for knowledge representation.

Produce the collaboration document in the following format: first, a numbered section of claims both sides can endorse, covering (i) a shared formal definition of latent knowledge that does not presuppose either extraction method, (ii) agreed-upon limitations of current behavioral evaluation protocols, and (iii) agreed-upon limitations of current mechanistic interpretability techniques; second, a disagreements table that states each camp's position in wording that camp would accept, focusing on three cruxes—(1) whether a behaviorally inconsistent but mechanistically coherent internal circuit should count as knowledge, (2) whether calibration requires an independent ground-truth source whose own justification is circular, and (3) whether applying the economic distinction between "stated preference" (surface outputs) and "revealed preference" (internal activation patterns) to language models is analytically illuminating or an anthropomorphic category error; third, for each crux, identify a concrete evaluation case or experimental design that both sides agree would bear on the issue, and record in advance what result each camp predicts.

In drafting, avoid vacuous agreement: if one camp's core commitment makes a claim impossible for them to sign, omit it or rephrase it precisely so that only the genuinely shared ground remains. Where the dispute turns on verbal ambiguity—particularly around the word "belief" as applied to artificial systems—say so explicitly and propose the disambiguated terms each camp should use going forward.
