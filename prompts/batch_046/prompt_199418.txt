In a philosophy-of-social-science seminar last semester, a colleague kept falling back on the line "if it isn't fundamental, it's grounded — full stop" to dismiss the idea that institutional kinds like corporations or borders could have any autonomous ontological status. The claim seemed to be that either these entities belong to the basic furniture of the world, or they are nothing over and above individual mental states and physical arrangements arranged in the right way, leaving no room for an intermediate or irreducible social reality. I want to understand whether that inference actually follows, but I see that the slogan itself hides several different theses. Can you lay out, as a clear menu, the precise metaphysical principles that sentence could be advancing? For each reading, please specify: (1) the conception of fundamentality in play — for instance, membership in a minimal complete base, absence of any further ground, or independence from other entities; (2) the nature of the grounding relation assumed — such as metaphysical determination, essential dependence, causal-explanatory derivation, or hyperintensional building; (3) whether the view permits plural or partial grounding, non-well-founded descending chains, or looping ground dependencies; and (4) whether that particular reading actually licenses the conclusion that institutional kinds must be fully reducible if they fail to be fundamental. In each case, indicate whether the reading is a near-tautology, a substantive and defensible position, or a false or implausible claim, and tell me which version the speaker was most likely invoking. Please write your response in American English.
