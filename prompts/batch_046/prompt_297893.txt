I am a novelist drafting a philosophical dialogue set during the calculus priority dispute. Construct a realistic thought experiment—grounded in the actual historical conditions of the 1710s, with no anachronistic technology or fictional physics—in which two independent mathematicians derive structurally identical results within six months, each unaware of the other, and present them to different national academies. Engineer the case so that strong scientific inevitabilism and contingent genius theory agree that the result would have entered the record regardless, but diverge on whether the second derivation counts as a genuine discovery: the inevitabilist should deny that temporal priority carries epistemic weight when the idea was structurally necessary, while the contingentist should insist that the specific conceptual path makes each derivation a distinct achievement. Introduce a third view, which you may call attributional neutrality, holding that priority is ceremonially important but epistemically void, and show that it collapses the first two positions only if discoveries are treated as purely propositional rather than processual. For each position, state whether the second mathematician truly discovered anything, whether the scientific community should register one discovery or two, and which structural feature of the case forces the verdict. Conclude by noting, honestly, whether removing the near-simultaneity—say, a fifty-year gap—undermines the argument for attributional neutrality, and identify the feature that does the work.
