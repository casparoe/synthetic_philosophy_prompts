I'm a software engineer without formal philosophical training, and I've been trying to understand a debate in decision theory that seems to generate extreme conclusions. I'd like you to draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between two camps on expected-value fanaticism could both sign.

The first camp — call them the Unbounded Aggregators — maintains that a rational agent must maximise expected value, even when this requires preferring a near-certain moderate outcome for many people over an astronomically unlikely but vastly larger outcome, because the expected-value calculus already weights by probability. The second camp — call them the Bounded Rationalists — maintains that rational decision-making requires bounded utility, ambiguity aversion, or a principled rejection of infinitary aggregation, and that unbounded expected-value maximisation yields reductios that discredit the framework rather than the intuition.

Please write your answer in British English.

The document should include:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims that both sides can sign. These should cover: the formal definition of expected value; the structure of the Stochastic Cathedral case (in which an agent must choose ex ante between a guaranteed welfare gain for 10^9 people and a 0.0001% chance of initiating a self-sustaining digital welfare substrate with potential value of 10^30 units, with no possibility of post-hoc observation); which features of Newcomb-style problems are genuinely puzzling versus which are artefacts of framing; and which mathematical results (e.g., unbounded expected value in St. Petersburg-type structures) are uncontroversial.

2. The precise disagreements, each phrased in wording that its own side would accept. In particular, state exactly where they part ways on whether rationality permits lexicographic dominance by tail-risk outcomes, and whether cardinal comparability of value across ontologically distinct states is a precondition of decision theory or an unmotivated assumption.

3. The cruxes — sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I take it that likely cruxes include: whether there exists a non-arbitrary probabilistic cutoff for 'negligible' risk that does not collapse into stipulation; and whether any justification for bounded rationality can avoid importing deontological or risk-averse commitments that the Aggregators regard as external to rational choice.

4. For each crux, specify a concrete case, argument, or piece of evidence that both sides agree bears on it, together with each side's advance prediction of what that case or argument shows. For example, both might agree that a formal demonstration that every bounded utility function violates a specific axiom of dynamic consistency under risk is relevant; record in advance what each camp predicts such a demonstration would imply for their position.

5. An explicit identification of any part of the dispute that is verbal rather than substantive — for instance, if 'rationality' is being used with normative versus descriptive force — stating exactly which part and why.

The statement should be formal and polished. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke obscure theorists or specific texts that a general reader would not recognise. Keep the focus on making the residual disagreement exact, not on adjudicating which camp is correct.
