Imagine you are advising a three-year interdisciplinary research programme—funded to inform regulatory frameworks rather than to build academic taxonomy—on conceptual questions in animal ethics. The programme must allocate its primary effort among three lines of inquiry: first, refining the boundary conditions for sentience-based moral status in invertebrates and synthetic agents; second, determining whether species membership can carry independent normative weight without collapsing into arbitrary discrimination; and third, constructing a framework for weighing cross-species welfare trade-offs in ecological management and food systems, particularly where improving one population’s welfare necessarily diminishes another’s.

Rank these three lines in order of priority, justifying the ordering with reference to tractability for philosophers rather than empirical scientists, the number of downstream practical questions that depend on each resolution, and the risk that leaving a question unsettled renders other ethical recommendations unstable. In particular, examine whether there is an upstream dependency between the second and third lines: does demonstrating that speciesism lacks justification automatically dissolve the problem of cross-species trade-offs, or does the latter require an independent normative apparatus? Finally, identify the single open question in contemporary animal ethics that you regard as most overrated—absorbing disproportionate attention relative to its practical payoff for policy or institutional design—and explain why.
