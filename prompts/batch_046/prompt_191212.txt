'Keynes separates risk—where probabilities are calculable like in statistics—from true uncertainty, where no objective basis exists, such as long-term investment forecasts. The weight of argument refers to the volume and relevance of evidence behind a judgement; it is not itself a probability between 0 and 1, but a kind of meta-measure of how complete our knowledge is. A judgement with high weight is not necessarily high-probability, but it is more robust, so rational agents should treat it as closer to certainty in practical reasoning and apply less caution. Keynes developed this in his Treatise on Probability (1921) as part of a logical theory of partial belief, then used it in the General Theory to show investment depends on animal spirits when weight is near zero. He explicitly integrates Knight's risk-uncertainty vocabulary into this epistemological framework, treating Knight's economic distinction as a special case of his own weight-based logic. In this sense Keynes anticipates modern ambiguity aversion, though he frames it through logical relations rather than psychological bias.'
