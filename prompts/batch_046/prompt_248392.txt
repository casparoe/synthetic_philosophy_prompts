As I am developing the philosophical architecture for a novel in which the protagonist is a moral psychologist, I have been working through a line of reasoning that one of her interlocutors advances—only to find that it collapses into an implausible conclusion I cannot accept, and I am unsure where the fissure lies. The argument runs as follows. (1) For an action to possess genuine moral worth, the agent's decisive reason must be the moral rightness of the act itself, unmediated by any expectation of emotional reward, social recognition, or self-conceptual harmony. (2) Any reason capable of motivating action must be anchored in a desire, and desires are individuated by their satisfaction conditions—the states of affairs that would fulfill them. (3) Therefore, moral worth requires a desire whose satisfaction conditions contain nothing but the occurrence of moral rightness; call this a pure moral desire. (4) Now introduce the index of motivational purity (IMP), defined as the proportion of an agent's total motivational energy contributed by pure moral desire in a given decision. (5) In every actual human psychology, the IMP is strictly less than one, because even the most ascetic agent retains residual self-preservation, cognitive ease, or what I am calling affective resonance—the subtle emotional congruence felt when one's action aligns with one's narrative self-image. (6) Because affective resonance has satisfaction conditions that extend beyond the moral rightness of the particular act (it can be achieved through other self-confirming actions), it functions as an instrumental desire co-present with the pure moral desire. (7) Consequently, no human action ever exhibits an IMP of one; pure moral desire is always adulterated by instrumental co-motives. (8) Hence, by (1), no human action ever possesses genuine moral worth. (9) Yet we consistently and rationally attribute exceptional moral worth to unrewarded, anonymous acts of self-sacrifice. (10) Therefore, the concept of moral worth, as defined in (1), is either universally inapplicable or incoherent.

I find myself wanting to preserve (1) and (9), since the narrative depends on the possibility of unalloyed moral heroism. But I cannot locate the error. Is the mistake in (2), which assumes a Humean psychology that a Kantian might reject? Or in (5), which treats affective resonance as necessarily an independent co-motive rather than an inseparable phenomenological shadow of the pure desire itself? Or is the error in (6), where I slide from the presence of a psychological state to its status as a separate motivating reason? I would be grateful for a diagnosis that names the specific step, explains why it initially seemed defensible, and clarifies what one must pay—conceptually and psychologically—to reject it instead of abandoning (1).
