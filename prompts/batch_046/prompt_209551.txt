I am a doctoral student in philosophy of history drafting a chapter on presentism, and I have distilled five strategies historians invoke to claim they can escape the normative standards of their own time: (1) radical contextualism, which treats past norms as incommensurable with ours and permits only internal description; (2) dual-perspective historicism, which adopts past agents' reasons as hypothetical imperatives without endorsement; (3) weak presentism, which concedes that our values guide evidence selection but denies they contaminate interpretation of intentions; (4) normative bracketing, which suspends judgment during reconstruction and applies modern standards only in a separate evaluative layer; and (5) genealogical reflexivity, which incorporates the historian's own normative commitments into the narrative, turning presentism itself into an object of study.

Please rank these five strategies in tiers (S, A, B, C, D) according to how well they sustain what I call diachronic normative transparency—the ability to recognize past norms as genuinely binding for historical agents without either endorsing them from our present standpoint or reducing them to inert curiosities—while simultaneously avoiding anachronism and moral quietism. Give one sentence of justification per tier. Then identify the pair whose rankings are closest, and specify a concrete historiographical problem—for example, assessing medieval canon law's treatment of heresy against modern frameworks of religious freedom—that would force those two strategies to diverge in practice. I am not seeking citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want an evaluation of the conceptual architecture alone.
