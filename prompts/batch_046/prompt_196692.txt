Consider the institutional package common in many wealthy democracies: paid parental leave is framed as an individual worker’s entitlement tied to employment contracts rather than to the child or household; formal childcare is heavily subsidized but delivered primarily through private markets, with the state acting as purchaser rather than direct provider; and labor-market norms presume continuous dual-earner attachment without reducing standard work hours. This combination treats care simultaneously as a private family obligation and as infrastructure required for economic participation. What philosophical commitments about distributive justice, gender equality, and the public-private boundary does this arrangement presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it assume that care is fully substitutable by market services without moral or relational loss, that gender equality is satisfied by symmetric labor-force participation rather than symmetric care burdens, or that the state’s obligation stops at enabling employment rather than sustaining care relationships? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, say whether these commitments are mutually consistent, and describe what structural changes—in the legal form of leave, the organization of work time, or the status of childcare providers—would be necessary if the underlying theory were replaced by one that treated care as a non-substitutable public good or as an irreducible relational practice.
