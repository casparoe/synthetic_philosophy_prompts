I’m working through the literature on personal identity in a graduate seminar, and I find the classic teletransporter cases too detached from actual neurotechnology to be useful. Please construct a thought experiment that meets the following specifications exactly.

Design two tightly paired scenarios, Case A and Case B, each set in a realistic clinical context: a patient with progressive neurodegeneration undergoes gradual replacement of brain tissue with functionally equivalent neuroprosthetic material. There should be no teleportation beams, no perfect predictors, no demons, and no exotic physics—only medical rehabilitation technology. In Case A, both a psychological-continuity view (in the Parfitian tradition) and a bodily-continuity view must agree that the original patient survives the procedure in the original body. In Case B, alter exactly one structural feature of Case A: introduce a distributed cognitive-backup protocol in which high-level functional and narrative patterns are continuously archived to an external substrate during the replacement. Arrange Case B so that, once replacement is complete, the original biological body remains alive but retains only low-level autonomic tissue with no higher-order cognitive architecture, while the archived patterns are instantiated in a second, prosthetic-equipped body. The psychological-continuity view should then deliver survival in the second body, whereas the bodily-continuity view should deliver survival in the first body.

Present each case in no more than one paragraph. Then demonstrate that the verdicts fall out of the respective theories rather than being stipulated: state the belief or judgment about survival, the method of evaluation each theory uses, the relevant nearby worlds or counterfactual conditions, and why each condition gives the verdict it does in both cases. Identify precisely which feature of Case B does the work in generating the disagreement, explain whether removing that feature collapses the case back into the agreement seen in Case A, and describe how a defender of the theory that comes out counterintuitively in Case B might attack the scenario—whether by denying that the backup protocol preserves genuine psychological continuity, or by arguing that the preserved biological body lacks the right kind of biological continuity to count. Finally, please separate what is broadly settled in the philosophical literature on survival and identity from what remains genuinely contested among specialists who work on the topic, and do not present any contested interpretation—such as the claim that psychological continuity alone is sufficient for personal survival—as though it were the consensus view.
