I'm putting together a dataset of conceptual prompts for a newsletter on science and ideas — kind of a side project away from my usual reporting on cognition and culture. I was arguing with a friend over coffee about why Dennett's attack on the Cartesian theater never really sticks in classrooms; students just memorize "multple drafts" and move on without feeling the coordination failure. So I need a runnable classroom game, not an explanation. The setting is a 14-person philosophy-of-mind seminar, 90 minutes, no tech besides paper and a timer. Everyone has encountered the basic Dennett framing but nothing obscure. They should come away seeing that a central projection booth doesn't just misdescribe consciousness — it forces an impossible synchronization problem that disappears once you allow parallel drafts with no master copy.

I want the exercise named "The Echo Chamber Protocol." Split the group into four draft modules — visual, linguistic, affective, and temporal — each recieving partial, timestamped notes about a shared 25-second stimulus (an ambiguous clip: a shadow sliding across a wall interrupted by a sudden sound). Each module writes its own micro-report independently; they are forbidden from comparing notes until a designated "theater manager" declares an "official experience" at minute 35. The manager can only read drafts that have physically reached their desk by a hard cutoff; meanwhile, the other modules continue revising and even retroactively editing earlier drafts based on late-arriving context. At minute 50 the manager must deliver a unified narrative of "what it was like," but then the remaining modules reveal post-cutoff revisions. The group then votes: did the manager miss a real occuring fact, or was there never a single version to miss? Give real small stakes — maybe the manager loses a token if they assert certainty where drafts conflict, while modules gain tokens for predicting the manager's breakdown. I need the full design: precise roles and rules, a minute-by-minute timeline (setup, drafting, manager deliberation, reveal, debrief), and a debrief script that maps each crisis moment onto the Cartesian theater fallacy and the multple drafts resolution. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong — for instance, the manager will almost certainly claim there must be a hidden correct version — and how to handle that without shutting discussion down. Crucially, be honest about the one feature of Dennett's view that this game can't reproduce — the actual phenomenological absence of a subjective "screen" — and tell me exactly what I have to supply verbally as facilitator. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: the core rules and timeline. Then give the reasoning, role details, stake structure, and debrief notes, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
