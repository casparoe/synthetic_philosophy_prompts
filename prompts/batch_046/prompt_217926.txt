I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of science preparing a brief closing piece for a workshop on rationalist epistemology, and I'm trying to capture—without another analytic paragraph—the tension between inside-view and outside-view reasoning when a philosopher assesses whether she has genuinely solved a classic conceptual problem. Rather than forecasting a policy outcome, I want to apply the distinction to self-evaluating judgment: the philosopher examines her own step-by-step reasoning about, for instance, the sorites paradox or the problem of other minds, while also confronting the reference-class reminder that thinkers who have felt this same certainty about these same problems have systematically turned out to be wrong.

Write a villanelle—exactly nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a quatrain, with two refrains that alternate according to the form and finally meet in the closing couplet—whose structure enacts epistemic modesty rather than resolving it. One refrain should voice the inside view, something like Each link I check, and every link holds firm; the other should voice the outside view, something like But so they felt, and so they were deceived. The rhyme scheme and repetition should do philosophical work: the refrains should circle without merging, and their adjacency in the final two lines should represent holding both perspectives simultaneously. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the inside/outside distinction and the base-rate problem in expert judgment recognizes the architecture, yet it should work as a poem for readers unfamiliar with the framework. Present only the poem itself—no explanatory prose, no resolving title—and adhere strictly to nineteen lines.
