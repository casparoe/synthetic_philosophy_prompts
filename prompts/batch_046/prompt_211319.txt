Since I recently began coordinating peer-review protocols and contribution-ranking for a distributed research collective, I have been living inside the very tension Foucault describes: evaluation metrics seem to reshape what members treat as credible, yet the collective insists it is producing autonomous knowledge. I want a formal adversarial-collaboration document between two camps that have argued about this for years.

Camp A holds the constitutive-power view: in any institutionalized knowledge practice, relations of power are not external corruptions but conditions of possibility for the truths produced; normalization, hierarchical observation, and disciplinary ranking necessarily generate the knowledge they appear merely to assess. Camp B holds the contingency view: Foucault correctly describes how specific historical institutions—clinics, prisons, schools—coupled power with knowledge, but this coupling is historically contingent rather than conceptually necessary; practices such as open peer review, transparent data protocols, or algorithmic reputation systems with published weights can genuinely decouple epistemic authority from disciplinary normalization.

Please produce the document they should have signed together. I want it structured as follows.

1. Joint Agreed Claims (numbered, approximately 12 to 15 claims, roughly 350 words). State the claims both sides accept about Foucault’s core framework—power/knowledge, discipline, normalization, biopower, and the examination—about the structural features of distributed knowledge institutions, and about what phenomena require explanation. The formulations should be precise enough that neither side feels misrepresented, and they should include agreement on the formal mechanics of disciplinary mechanisms without committing to those mechanics being inevitable.

2. Residual Disagreements (table format, roughly 250 words). Present the precise remaining disagreements, each phrased in wording that its own camp would accept. Separate substantive disagreements from merely verbal ones; if any portion of the dispute dissolves once terms are clarified, identify exactly which portion and explain why. Include at least one disagreement about the applicability of the framework to algorithmic or peer-based evaluation systems.

3. Cruxes and Predictive Tests (roughly 400 words). Identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. I suspect two central cruxes are (i) whether full transparency of evaluation criteria eliminates the power asymmetry that makes normalization genuinely disciplinary, and (ii) whether a collective knowledge practice can exhibit progressive error-correction over time without simultaneously producing docile, self-surveilling contributors. For each crux, specify a concrete institutional design feature or case study both sides agree is genuinely relevant—such as a reputation algorithm whose weights are fully public versus one kept opaque, or a peer-review system that anonymizes reviewers versus one that makes evaluations permanently attributable. Record each side’s explicit prediction about what that case demonstrates, and state whether both agree it actually bears on the crux or whether one side regards it as ambiguous. Conclude with a brief assessment of whether any remaining disagreement is partly verbal and, if so, which part.
