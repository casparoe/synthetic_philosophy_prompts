After a long debate with a friend about Dennett's framework, I tried to formalize our disagreement and ended up with a chain that seems to prove far more than either of us wants. Here it is.

(1) A pattern tracked by stance S is ontologically real—not a mere fiction—iff S yields predictions about its domain that are (a) systematically projectible beyond observed cases and (b) ineliminable: no computation available within any stance more fundamental than S can reproduce those predictions without a severe loss of efficiency or explanatory coherence.

(2) The intentional stance tracks belief-desire patterns; its predictions about human behavior are projectible, and no physical-stance computation can reproduce them without catastrophic loss of tractability.

(3) Therefore belief-desire patterns are real features of agents, not just useful fictions.

(4) The design stance tracks functional patterns; its predictions about artifacts are projectible, and no physical-stance computation can reproduce them with comparable efficiency.

(5) Therefore functional patterns are real features of artifacts.

(6) Consider now what I will call the aesthetic stance: treating a musical work as having an emotional trajectory and narrative arc. This stance yields cross-culturally projectible predictions about listener responses—predictions about which passages will be heard as tragic, triumphant, or ironic—that acoustic-physical analysis cannot reproduce without a comparable loss of efficiency and coherence.

(7) By direct application of (1), emotional-trajectory patterns are real features of symphonies in precisely the same ontological sense in which beliefs are real features of persons.

(8) But to attribute literal emotions and narratives to symphonies is not an ontological discovery; it is a metaphorical projection. A symphony is not the kind of thing that can suffer or intend.

(9) So either (1) is false—which collapses the standard argument for mental-state realism—or we must grant symphonies a genuinely mental ontology.

My friend claims the error is in (6), arguing that acoustic physics could in principle derive every listener response, so the aesthetic stance fails condition (1b). I replied that neuroscience could in principle derive every behavioral prediction too, so if (6) fails, so does (2). He then said the relevant difference is that beliefs explain behavior in a way that is not just predictive but rationalizing, and that rationalization is absent in the aesthetic case. I am not convinced that saves (1) without smuggling in a criterion that (1) does not contain.

Which step should be rejected? If it is (1), what does that cost for the reality of mental states? If it is (6) or (7), what distinguishes the intentional stance from the aesthetic stance without making (1) either false or ad hoc? I would prefer to preserve (2) and (8) if possible, but I recognize they may be in tension.
