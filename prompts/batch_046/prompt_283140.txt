As a retired evolutionary ecologist who has recently begun working through the philosophical and archaeological literature on human prehistory, I would like a consistency audit of the position I have settled on before presenting it to a reading group. My commitments are as follows.

(1) The Neolithic transition to agriculture was, on balance, a welfare regression for the average individual. Bioarchaeological comparisons—reduced adult stature, increased dental caries and enamel defects, heavier skeletal markers of labor stress, and greater pathogen loads—suggest that Mesolithic foragers enjoyed better physical outcomes than their early agrarian successors.

(2) Mobile hunter-gatherer economies achieved what Marshall Sahlins termed original affluence: high leisure-to-work ratios and reliable caloric adequacy. This makes the foraging baseline genuinely attractive rather than a state of miserable scarcity that people were eager to escape.

(3) Durable, institutionalized inequality requires storable surplus and fixed settlement. In mobile foraging bands, the structural conditions for stable power asymmetries—accumulated, defensible resources—are largely absent, so hierarchy cannot stabilize.

(4) I reject the noble savage fallacy in its strong form. Contemporary foraging societies are not evolutionary time capsules; ethnographic analogies to the deep Paleolithic are heuristic tools at best and should never be treated as direct windows onto prehistoric life.

(5) Archaeological evidence from complex pre-agricultural societies—such as the Jomon culture of Japan and certain Pacific Northwest groups—shows that partial sedentism, food storage, and hereditary social ranking could emerge well before plant or animal domestication.

(6) Normative evaluation of this transition should be framed as a counterfactual welfare comparison rather than an assessment of demographic success. Even if local climate change and population pressure made agriculture ecologically inevitable in specific regions, inevitability does not entail optimality.

(7) I also accept the osteological paradox: higher frequencies of skeletal lesions in agricultural populations may partly reflect longer survival with chronic conditions rather than purely worse health, which complicates straightforward welfare inferences drawn directly from bone pathology.

Please examine whether this package is jointly tenable. Specifically, identify which pairs of commitments pull against each other in ways that are not merely superficial, and identify any pairs that only appear to conflict but dissolve once their underlying motivations are separated. Rank the genuine tensions by severity and indicate, for each, what the least costly revision would be and what that revision costs me. Be precise about whether a commitment must be abandoned or only qualified. Present your assessment as a point-by-point audit rather than a general impression, and do not provide bibliographic recommendations or ask me to consult specific texts; I am working without access to external sources.
