The domain is Plutarch and the uses of biography for moral learning, specifically hagiography and what happens when lives are paired rather than told alone. Here's the angle I'm stuck on. The standard literature treats moral learning from biography as either emulation or admiration: you read a life, identify a virtue, try to copy it. But Plutarch's Parallel Lives seem to do something stranger. Take the Demosthenes-Cicero pairing — both orators, both politically failed, both ended in suicide or self-imposed death. Plutarch doesn't just hold one up as model and the other as warning. He puts them in this comparative mirror where the virtues and failures overlap in ways that make simple admiration impossible. So my precise question is: is there a distinct mode of moral cognition — maybe call it comparative calibration or negative exemplarity — that only emerges in the gap between paired lives, and have modern forms of biography (secular leadership hagiography, effective altruism profiles, even political memoir) lost this precisely because they insist on single-subject inspiration?

I want the proposal to lay out why the existing answers don't settle this. Emulation theories and character-education frameworks can explain single-life learning, but they don't have resources for why pairing two flawed figures produces judgment rather than confusion. Historical-reception studies can trace influence, but they don't explain the normative mechanism. So the approach I'd take is close conceptual analysis of two or three paired lives — maybe Demosthenes/Cicero and Pericles/Fabius — combined with a comparison to modern single-subject biography to see what structural feature is missing. The obvious alternatives are empirical psychology of narrative or straight history of genre; I'd argue those miss the philosophical point about practical judgment. A useful result would be identifying the structural condition — maybe ambivalent comparison without resolution — that makes comparative biography do moral work that hagiography can't. Failure would be showing that the pairing is just rhetorical decoration and doesn't actually change the reader's practical reasoning.
