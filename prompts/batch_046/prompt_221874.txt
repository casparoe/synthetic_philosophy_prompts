I am writing with a question that has become urgent since I received treatment for a recent head injury that has left me with mild but persistent cognitive fatigue. At my institution's health center, clinicians distinguish sharply between restorative interventions—such as cognitive rehabilitation or stimulant prescriptions intended to return me to my pre-injury level of executive function—and enhancement interventions, including the same medications or specialized neurofeedback protocols requested to improve functioning beyond that prior level. The athletics department operates under an analogous policy: physical therapy and nutritional support are covered for injured athletes to regain pre-injury performance, but technologies or supplements that might accelerate recovery or push performance past that prior level are prohibited. Yet the institution's health plan covers elective cosmetic procedures—LASIK, orthodontic alignment, and dermatological treatments—without any reference to a baseline, treating them as legitimate exercises in self-modification.

I am trying to understand whether this tripartite institutional logic—restoration permitted, normalization enforced, optimization forbidden or unregulated—rests on a coherent philosophical foundation. Does it presuppose that cognitive and athletic capacities are governed by a normative, species-typical baseline in a way that appearance is not? Or does the distinction depend not on ontology but on social role: that students and athletes have institutional obligations to perform within a defined range, whereas appearance belongs to a private sphere of autonomy? Moreover, the baseline for my own treatment is defined diachronically—my pre-injury self—while for an athlete born with an unusually efficient metabolism, the baseline might be defined synchronically against a statistical norm; does the policy therefore smuggle in competing theories of personal identity and well-being without acknowledging them? If the implicit view were replaced—say, by a purely relational or subjective account of well-being—would the institution have to abandon its prohibition on enhancement, or could it preserve the distinction by appealing solely to resource allocation and risk management? And if the same system permits cosmetic interventions that alter appearance without reference to a baseline, yet insists on a strict baseline for cognitive and physical interventions, does it implicitly adopt an inconsistent ontology of the body—treating appearance as a domain of free self-creation but treating cognition and athletic capacity as domains governed by a normative standard?
