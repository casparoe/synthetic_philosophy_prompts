For my seminar on Nietzsche's philosophy of history, I have built an argument about his three modes that now feels like a reductio: it seems to show that monumental, antiquarian, and critical history are mutually destructive, and that the whole idea of history serving life collapses under its own weight. The steps are:

(1) For Nietzsche, history is only justified when it serves life, which requires adopting one of the three modes—monumental, antiquarian, or critical—according to present vital needs.

(2) Critical history works by dissolving the apparent necessity of present institutions and values through genealogical exposure of their contingent origins.

(3) But the normative principle that "history must serve life" is itself not a natural law; it is a specific commitment that emerged under particular historical conditions and functions as an institution governing how we relate to the past.

(4) So a consistently applied critical history must also subject that principle to dissolution, undermining the very authority of "life" as the criterion for legitimate historical study.

(5) Meanwhile, monumental history depends on identifying genuinely exemplary lives and achievements that transcend the present and deserve emulation.

(6) Yet once critical history has dissolved the authority of normative criteria, there is no non-circular ground for identifying which lives are genuinely exemplary rather than merely useful fictions constructed by present needs.

(7) Antiquarian history, in turn, requires preserving traditions that constitute a collective identity and provide continuity.

(8) But after critical history has revealed those traditions to be contingent constructions with no essential authority, preservation becomes arbitrary—there is no reason to preserve these particular fictions rather than invent new ones.

(9) Therefore, if critical history is applied consistently, it destroys the normative framework that justifies all three modes; if it is restrained in order to protect that framework, then it is not truly critical history but a disguised form of antiquarian preservation.

(10) So Nietzsche's typology is either self-undermining or inconsistently applied, and the claim that history can serve life without harming it is unsustainable.

I would prefer not to abandon (1) or (2), since they seem central to the essay's opening argument, but (10) strikes me as an absurd conclusion that I should reject. Is the slip in (3), where I treat the service-to-life principle as an institution open to genealogical critique rather than as a pre-reflective vital condition? Or is there a modal slide between "dissolving apparent necessity" and "destroying normative authority"? Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost me—especially for the coherence of critical history itself?
