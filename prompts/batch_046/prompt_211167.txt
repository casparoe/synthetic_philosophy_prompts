In philosophical defenses of modal realism, proponents often invite us to treat possible worlds as analogous to distant planets or isolated universes: they are concrete, spatiotemporally separated, causally closed, and vary in their contents, with the term 'actual' functioning indexically—much like 'here' or 'now'—to pick out the one we inhabit. The analogy is used to dissolve modal mystery: saying that something is possible becomes, on this view, no stranger than saying something happens elsewhere in the cosmos. Audit this analogy precisely: lay out the correspondence table—what plays the role of spatiotemporal separation, causal isolation, physical-law variation, and the indexical reference of 'actual'? Then identify the points of breakdown: do the worlds share a common space-time manifold or causal structure? Can an indexical account of actuality survive if there is no spatial or temporal relation linking worlds? Examine whether the analogy quietly imports a physicalist cosmology that modal discourse in science and ordinary reasoning does not require. Finally, assess whether modal realism's claim to ontological parsimony survives once these disanalogies are exposed, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion that modal talk is essentially cosmological travelogue. If the distant-planets framing fails, is there a more accurate structural comparison—perhaps to models in formal semantics, or to parameter spaces in physics—that preserves what is useful in the realism without the misleading spatial imagery?
