I'm preparing a feature for an ideas magazine—after a lengthy conversation with a colleague who models climate-risk distributions—on why the aggregation problem in moral philosophy refuses to stay inside the seminar room and keeps resurfacing in public-health triage, environmental regulation, and algorithmic resource allocation. I keep returning to Scanlon's contractualism not because it resolves aggregation, but because it seems to change the terms of the dispute: rather than asking whether aggregate benefits justify individual harms, it asks whether anyone can reasonably reject a principle that permits them. That reframing, however, leaves open a cluster of genuinely unresolved directions that feel more like branching research programs than settled disagreements. Some philosophers are trying to formalize contractualist justification for probabilistic harms spread across populations whose members are structurally opaque to the decision-maker; others are extending the framework to intergenerational obligations where the affected parties do not yet exist; a third line asks whether contractualist reasoning can be integrated with democratic aggregation mechanisms without collapsing into a disguised form of majority rule. Among these directions—and any others you think belong in the core cluster—which two or three deserve the most sustained intellectual effort over the coming decade, and which are overrated or likely dead ends? I want an explicit ranking defended by criteria: tractability with current conceptual tools, dependency relations among the questions, and whether settling an issue would actually alter institutional design. Please treat this as an agenda-setting exercise for a research program, not a survey or bibliography. I am especially interested in whether structural opacity—where decision-makers cannot identify the specific individuals who will bear a risk—renders contractualist reasoning inoperable in practice, or whether it simply demands a reformulated kind of principle formulation.
