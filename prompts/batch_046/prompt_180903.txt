Consider the institutional practice maintained in many traditional Jewish communities that preserve the full liturgical recitation of animal sacrificial procedures—the korbanot—in daily prayer with precise ritual detail, even though the Temple is absent and actual sacrifice is impossible, and even though those same communities teach Maimonides' rationalist interpretation, developed in his Guide for the Perplexed, that sacrificial worship was not an end in itself but a pedagogical concession accommodating an ancient, widespread inclination toward animal worship. The prayer is said verbatim, the educational curriculum treats the concessionary account as authoritative, and no contradiction is officially acknowledged. What philosophical commitments about ritual language, divine command, and symbolic commitment does that pairing presuppose? Must the community implicitly hold that liturgical repetition operates independently of theoretical belief—perhaps as an aspirational projection rather than a present-tense assertion—or does it require a more specific theory of ritual as sustaining a normative world that does not yet exist? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, and indicate what would have to change in either the liturgy or the pedagogy if the concessionary view were replaced by a theory that treated sacrifice as intrinsically valuable worship.
