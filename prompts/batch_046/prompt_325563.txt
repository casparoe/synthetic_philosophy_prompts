I spent my career in biophysics, and since retiring I have been reading systematically in moral philosophy, particularly the literature on causal responsibility in clinical settings. I have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern that does not seem to match the standard taxonomy of acts and omissions, and I am trying to determine whether it has an established name.

Consider a hospital that maintains a patient on mechanical ventilation. If the clinical team actively discontinues the ventilator, this is typically classified as an intentional withdrawal—an omission made explicit, but one that falls within the permissible scope of allowing death. Now consider a second scenario: the hospital never expands its ventilator capacity, knowing that this same patient will require support in six months; when the need arises, no machine is available, and the patient dies of respiratory failure. The administrators describe this as a supply failure, not as a denial of care, even though the prior budgetary decision was the direct cause of the missing resource. The causal structure is parallel—an institutional choice removes a life-sustaining pathway—yet the moral framing shifts sharply: the first case is debated within the ethics of killing versus letting die, while the second is treated as a neutral background condition of resource scarcity.

The same configuration appears with pharmaceutical formularies. Removing a drug from an existing formulary is scrutinized as an active withholding; never adding it in the first place is treated as an absence of provision, despite the fact that both decisions are institutional acts with identical foreseeable outcomes. What interests me is not the generic doing/allowing distinction, which I understand, but this more specific dependency: the moral classification of an omission seems to turn on whether the omitted intervention had previously been incorporated into the institutional baseline of standard care for that patient.

Does this precise phenomenon—where the ethical status of an omission is determined by its relation to a socially constructed care baseline rather than by its causal or intentional structure—have a recognized name in the philosophical literature? I am aware of discussions of status quo bias and of critiques that treat baseline expectations as morally arbitrary, but neither captures exactly this institutional framing effect. If the concept is established, how is it distinguished from ordinary baseline-dependency arguments? If it is not, what would be its precise boundaries, particularly in cases where the baseline itself was recently altered by a prior institutional decision?
