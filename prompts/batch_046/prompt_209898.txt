Write a blank-verse poem of exactly twelve lines on Plato's treatment of wine in the Laws and the Symposium: specifically, the idea that regulated drinking under a sober symposiarch serves as a civic test that reveals hidden temperaments, set against the unregulated, erotic disorder of Alcibiades' drunken intrusion. Use a single refrain—the symposiarch's measuring phrase—that appears at lines 3, 6, 9, and 12, but have it degrade metrically with each appearance: intact iambic pentameter at first, then with a dropped foot, then with internal rhyme collapsing, then fractured across the line break, so that the form itself enacts the loss of measure it describes while the speaker's voice remains constant. Do not resolve the tension between the Laws' pedagogical optimism and the Symposium's chaos; end with an open question about whether what is revealed is the true soul or only its unguarded shadow. The poem should be recognizable to someone who knows the texts—reference the symposiarch's legal authority and Alcibiades' disruptive arrival without treating them as mere labels—and should avoid turning into either a moral lesson on moderation or a celebration of excess. After the poem, provide a brief note identifying the specific liberties taken with Plato's account—for example, whether you have imported a modern psychological model of revealed preference into the ancient diagnostic frame, or whether your refrain's formal collapse implies a deterministic progression of intoxication that the Laws does not commit to—and state what alternative reading of the symposion's purpose would cause you to restructure the refrain's degradation.
