I've been thinking about whether generational labels—Gen Z, Millennials, Boomers, and so on—pick out real kinds of people, or whether they're just marketing taxonomies and nostalgic shortcuts for talking about age. The position I've tentatively adopted is that they are closer to real social kinds: not biological essences, but groups whose members share formative economic, technological, and institutional conditions that generate stable, causally active patterns of belief, behavior, and consumption. On this view, knowing someone belongs to Gen Z should carry explanatory weight that knowing they are twenty-two in 2025 does not, and if you could swap a Millennial's formative context with a Boomer's and preserve the cohort signature intact, that would be a serious problem for the claim.

What I want is an honest map of what could actually defeat this position—not just the usual objection that stereotypes are inaccurate, but the specific evidence, conceptual distinctions, or findings that would show generational kindhood is an illusion, a self-fulfilling construct, or reducible to life-stage and period effects. In particular:

- Which observations or study designs (for instance, about intra-cohort variance versus inter-cohort variance, the arbitrariness of birth-year boundaries, or the separation of cohort, life-cycle, and period effects) would be decisive rather than merely awkward?
- Are there conceptual escape routes—qualifying the claim to "only in certain domains," treating generations as family-resemblance clusters rather than essential kinds, or redefining the kind around self-identification—that risk insulating the view from falsification? Is that insulation a problem for a claim of this kind?
- What would a committed defender of generational kindhood themselves concede as genuinely counting against them, as opposed to objections they would dismiss?

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely uncomfortable, say what predictions the view makes that rival accounts do not, and assess whether it has already been qualified into unfalsifiability. Also, if my framing rests on a confused premise—for example, if asking for "defeaters" of a sociological taxonomy is the wrong kind of question, or if I'm conflating cohort effects with life-stage effects in a way that makes the whole issue muddled—say so plainly instead of playing along politely.
