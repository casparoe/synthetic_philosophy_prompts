I am preparing a discussion for a graduate seminar on distributive justice, and I want to read Ursula K. Le Guin’s short story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas through John Rawls’s veil of ignorance and the difference principle, but with a framing that deliberately avoids the usual utilitarian comparison. Specifically, treat the child’s suffering not as a quantity of pain to be weighed against collective happiness, but as the product of a basic structure that allocates goods according to a principle no one could accept behind the veil: namely, that one person’s severe deprivation is the necessary condition for everyone else’s flourishing. In particular, examine whether the arrangement violates the difference principle not merely because the child is worst off, but because the social cooperation in Omelas fails Rawls’s test of reciprocity—the child is not a party to the cooperative scheme but its exploited foundation. Furthermore, consider Rawls’s idea of the strains of commitment: do the citizens who remain suffer a moral corruption that undermines the stability of the just society they claim to inhabit, and are the walkers away best interpreted as recognizing that no overlapping consensus could justify this basic structure? Please identify the specific scenes in the story—the description of the child’s imprisonment, the citizens’ initial awareness, the moment of departure—that illuminate or complicate this Rawlsian reading. I want a critical interpretation that does not reduce Le Guin’s narrative to a thought experiment, and that honestly notes where the story’s affective power outstrips the analytical framework: does the story suggest that justice requires not merely institutional reform but a kind of refusal that Rawls’s theory, with its emphasis on stable cooperation, cannot fully accommodate?
