I am a Year 13 pupil preparing to deliver the opening address at our school's annual History and Philosophy Symposium. The audience will include our head of history, fellow sixth-formers, and several parents, one of whom is an enthusiastic proponent of ancient-astronaut theories and another who takes Fomenko's New Chronology seriously. I want to speak about demarcation — the philosophical problem of distinguishing legitimate historical inquiry from pseudohistory — but I must avoid both lecturing the room and appealing to institutional authority that the non-specialists will not share. The distinction needs to do real structural work: it should determine which claims about Atlantis, ancient astronauts, and Fomenko's revised chronology I can present as genuinely open questions and which I must treat as settled, without insulting anyone's intelligence. Specifically, I want the speech to be organised around three demarcation criteria — predictive success, consilience with independent lines of evidence, and vulnerability to empirical revision — applied to these three cases. It should be accessible to parents and pupils who are not philosophers, yet precise enough that my history teacher recognises the reasoning. Crucially, I must not cite scholars, texts, or bibliographies; the argument must stand on the visible logic of the examples themselves. Please write a five-minute address that ends not with a simple dismissal, but with a question about what intellectual obligation remains when a claim satisfies one criterion brilliantly yet fails the other two.
