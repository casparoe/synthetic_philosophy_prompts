I've been thinking a lot about how people try to learn how to live by reading about other people's lives—like the old practice of pairing two figures, one Greek and one Roman, to show virtue and vice side by side, or the way saints' lives are written so that every choice seems to point straight toward holiness. There's a conceptual snag I can't shake: once a life is turned into a lesson, the writer has to pick out which moments are real moral turning points and which are just ordinary background. But that picking isn't neutral. It teaches the reader an invisible rule about what kinds of situations even count as ethical tests. Could you give me an example of a conceptual problem that comes out of this framing—maybe something about how comparing two lives in parallel changes the moral meaning of each, or about how leaving out the roads-not-taken in a saint's biography creates a false picture of choice—where the very shape of the story makes it hard to use the life as genuine moral guidance? I'm not after a history lesson; I want the kind of philosophical puzzle that makes you wonder whether the biography is showing you how to be good, or just showing you a very particular, maybe misleading, idea of what being good looks like.
