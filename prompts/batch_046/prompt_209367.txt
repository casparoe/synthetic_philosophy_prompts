I serve as a clinical ethics consultant at a tertiary care hospital. Roughly twice a month I am asked to produce, within forty-five minutes, a documented recommendation for the ethics committee on whether to honor an advance directive that conflicts with either the treating team's clinical judgment, a family member's request, or the patient's own present expressed preferences. The audience includes physicians, nurses, and lay family representatives who have no philosophy background, so the reasoning must be transparent and actionable without theoretical jargon. I need a reusable procedural framework — an ordered set of tests and decision nodes in plain language — for determining whether an advance directive retains binding authority over the present patient. The framework should translate the philosophical idea that an earlier, competent self can exercise diachronic authority over a later self, while explicitly accounting for conditions of partial identity rupture (for example, severe neurocognitive change or transformative brain injury) in which that authority may weaken or dissolve.

Operationalize the framework around three conditions that must jointly be satisfied for the directive to bind: first, the epistemic accessibility condition — the directive-author could have anticipated the present clinical scenario with reasonable specificity rather than addressing a generic or misimagined future; second, the narrative continuity condition — the present patient retains sufficient psychological and evaluative continuity with the directive-author to count as the same moral agent for purposes of self-governance; and third, the welfare asymmetry condition — honoring the directive does not impose a present experiential harm so severe that it overrides the earlier self's authority. At each step, clearly mark whether the assessment is a procedural check that can be standardized or an unavoidable judgment call requiring explicit justification.

The procedure must be able to handle the following hard cases: (a) a patient with advanced dementia who now expresses consistent contentment but whose directive refuses artificial nutrition and hydration in this exact stage; (b) a patient with a traumatic brain injury who retains full autobiographical memory but has developed radically different values and preferences; and (c) a case in which honoring the directive causes only minimal present discomfort, but the family argues that the patient's personality has undergone complete transformation. Apply the framework to all three cases, including at least one in which the procedure yields a recommendation that runs counter to common clinical intuition or family wishes, and explain why the procedure requires that result despite its discomfort.

Finally, specify what the framework explicitly cannot do — for instance, which metaphysical disputes about personal identity outstrip clinical evidence, or which value conflicts it is not designed to resolve. Write your answer in American English. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the deliverable must stand on its own for a practitioner without library access. Avoid invoking obscure named theories that require specialist knowledge to apply.
