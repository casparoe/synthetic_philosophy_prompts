Consider the following extension of Bernard Williams's integrity objection. (1) Integrity demands coherence between an agent's actions and her ground projects, not merely compliance with moral permissions. (2) Every universalist moral theory—whether utilitarian, Kantian, or contractualist—can demand that an agent abandon or severely compromise a ground project when impartial requirements conflict with it. (3) Because any ground project is in principle vulnerable to such conflict, universalist theories will sometimes require the agent to subject her deepest commitments to prior moral scrutiny—adding what Williams identifies as one thought too many—before she acts on them. (4) But once a ground project must survive that scrutiny to motivate action, it ceases to be genuinely ground-level and becomes subordinate to moral theory. (5) Hence any universalist theory systematically undermines the integrity of agents who possess ground projects. (6) Conversely, if we reject universalism entirely to protect integrity, we forfeit the resources to condemn agents whose ground projects are themselves morally monstrous—for instance, a life structured around the domination of others as a constitutive aim. The argument thus seems to show that either integrity dissolves all universal moral theory, or universal theory dissolves integrity. Which step should be rejected, and what does that rejection cost?
