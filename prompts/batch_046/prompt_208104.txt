As someone who reports on institutional design at the boundary of science and cultural memory, I am examining a curatorial protocol now adopted in several national memorial museums. Under the protocol, professional historians retain exclusive veto power over evidentiary claims, while designated community representatives—survivors, descendants, or cultural custodians—retain exclusive veto power over narrative framing, commemorative language, and the visual hierarchy of display; crucially, there is no arbitration mechanism for conflicts that resist decomposition into “facts versus interpretation,” such as whether an absence of archival records should be read as evidentiary absence or as an artifact of suppression, and whether aggregate statistical data should be granted equal typographic authority to individual oral testimony. The charter explicitly states that neither sovereignty may override the other. What philosophical theory of the relationship between collective memory and historical record does this bifurcated architecture necessarily presuppose? Does it depend on the view that memory and history belong to ontologically separate domains—one of identity, one of knowledge—or can the arrangement be justified on purely pragmatic or political grounds without any deep metaphysics of the past? Reconstruct the implicit view, distinguish rigorously between what the practice strictly requires and what merely happens to accommodate it, assess whether the model is internally coherent given that framing and fact are often inseparable, and describe precisely what would have to change in the institution’s design, authority structure, and public purpose if it replaced this dual sovereignty with a unitary authority—whether that of professional historiography alone or of community memory alone.
