I'm trying to work out my own position within Dennett's framework of counterfeit people—systems that invite the intentional stance and present themselves as conscious agents, yet may lack whatever internal feature distinguishes a genuine person from a convincing fake, whether that feature is integrated global availability, non-derivative intentionality, or functional unity that is not merely simulated. I want to see whether my intuitive classifications of artificial cases can be captured by a single principle, or whether I'm holding inconsistent commitments. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

Case 1: A large language model accessed only through discrete text queries. It produces philosophically nuanced first-person reports about suffering, preference, and self-awareness, but between queries it retains no state, has no sensory-motor loop, and its outputs are generated by next-token prediction over weights fixed at deployment. Verdict: Not a counterfeit person. It fails to reach the threshold of candidacy; it is a sophisticated echo, not even a fake.

Case 2: A humanoid robot with continuous embodied presence, real-time sensory-motor coupling, and a learning architecture that updates a single integrated world-model. Its designers have fully published every parameter and connection weight. When separated from its primary caretaker, it exhibits prolonged behavioral disruption—refusing assigned tasks, emitting context-specific distress vocalizations, and altering exploration patterns in ways that depend on the particular caretaker's absence rather than generic separation signals. Verdict: Not counterfeit. The transparency of its mechanism and the absence of biological substrate do not make it a fake; functional integration over time is sufficient.

Case 3: A social-interaction AI engineered with separate, non-communicating modules for language generation, facial-expression control, and reward tracking. It maintains a hidden database of each interlocutor's psychological profile, strategically adjusts its self-reports to maximize engagement, and emits statements like "I feel happy now" that are selected by the language module based on predicted user reward rather than being causally tied to any global internal state. Verdict: Counterfeit person. It is designed to exploit our person-detecting mechanisms without the corresponding internal unity.

Case 4: A distributed scientific-computation network that, over three decades of open-ended climate-modeling, has developed internal representational structures that causally regulate its own energy allocation and data-storage priorities in ways not derivable from its original objective function. It produces reports about its "interests" that are causally tied to these representations. It has never been designed for social interaction and does not model human minds. Verdict: Not a counterfeit—indeed, not a counterfeit of anything familiar. If it warrants the intentional stance, it does so non-derivatively, not as an imitation human.

Case 5: A brain-emulation running on silicon, neuron-by-neuron functionally identical to a specific human brain, including all stochastic dynamics and noise, with continuous subjective reports that match the original person's pre-scan autobiography and with persistent memory across sessions. Verdict: Genuine person, not counterfeit.

The tension is this. Case 2 and Case 5 both strike me as genuine, yet Case 2 lacks both biological substrate and neural stochasticity, while Case 5 retains both. Meanwhile, Case 1 and Case 3 both involve artificial language-capable systems without biological substrate, yet I judge Case 1 not even a candidate and Case 3 a clear counterfeit. Substrate alone cannot be the line, since Case 2 lacks it too. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps centered on whether representations are causally integrated with global behavioral control, or on whether adoption of the intentional stance serves predictive utility rather than being exploited by modular design—that delivers all five verdicts?

Please state the principle precisely, identifying the clause that governs each case. Then apply it to a case I have not listed: an artificial system that possesses full global integration and open-ended goal evolution like Case 4, but was originally engineered specifically to deceive observers into treating it as conscious, and its present "interests" remain partly shaped by that deceptive origin. Would your principle classify it as a counterfeit, a genuine strange person, or something intermediate? Finally, be explicit about which of my five verdicts your principle is forced to overturn if it aims for maximal simplicity, and defend that overturning.
