I am a third-year philosophy student currently enrolled in an advanced seminar on metaethics and normative theory, and I am drafting an essay on moral particularism versus generalism. Rather than rehearsing the familiar caricature in which particularists simply reject all moral principles, I want to explore a less conventional framing: I propose that particularism is best understood as a thesis about the modality of moral knowledge—specifically, that it functions more like the tacit, embodied discernment found in expert craft practice (for example, the way an experienced surgeon recognizes pathological tissue that resists explicit checklist criteria) than like the conscious application of explicit propositional rules. I have sketched some preliminary notes and would appreciate expert commentary.

My proposed structure and claims are as follows.

First, generalism holds that sound moral reasoning requires true universal moral principles serving as premises. Particularists, on standard readings, deny this. Second, I want to distinguish two separate particularist commitments: (a) the denial that there are true universal moral principles, and (b) the claim that moral knowledge is not propositional or rule-governed in the manner generalists assume. Third, drawing on the concept of tacit knowledge in skilled practice—where recognition is holistic, context-sensitive, and often resistant to full verbal articulation—I suggest that particularists might consistently accept that stable patterns of moral salience exist, while denying that these patterns operate as explicit premises available to conscious reasoning. Fourth, I anticipate the objection that this collapses particularism into a form of generalism about implicit principles. I plan to respond by distinguishing between rules that govern practice from within (analogous to grammatical rules that native speakers follow without being able to state them) and rules that serve as external justifications. On my reading, particularism denies that moral principles fulfill the latter justificatory function, not necessarily that they structure practice in the former, internal sense. Fifth, I am especially uncertain whether this distinction is coherent and whether I am attributing to prominent particularists—such as Jonathan Dancy—a position they would actually reject.

Please act as a professional moral philosopher with expertise in metaethics and the philosophy of practical reason. Before giving detailed criticism, please begin with brief brainstorming: outline two or three alternative ways to frame the particularist position that avoid the anti-principle caricature, and note one significant weakness in each framing. Then, please comment systematically on the accuracy of my characterization of both particularism and generalism; on whether the craft-practice analogy genuinely illuminates the debate or instead smuggles in unwanted commitments about moral training, expertise, or socialization; on the coherence of my distinction between internal and external rules in this context; and on whether my central claim risks being merely a redescription of familiar positions rather than a novel contribution. Finally, please suggest specific revisions that would sharpen the argument—particularly how to clarify the relationship between tacit moral discernment and explicit moral justification—so that the essay makes a substantive, defensible intervention in the debate. Please think carefully and work through each point in order.
