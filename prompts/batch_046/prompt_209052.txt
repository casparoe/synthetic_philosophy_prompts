Consider the following exam question from an advanced philosophy of causation course:

"On 15 March, the Riverside Water Authority's predictive-maintenance algorithm assigns the reservoir's filtration units a risk score of 0.42, below the 0.60 threshold that triggers automatic replacement orders. The board, meeting that week, declines to manually override the algorithm. The filters fail in June. Meanwhile, an upstream factory illegally discharges a specific contaminant in May; the contaminant is exactly the kind the filters were designed to capture. By July, residents show symptoms of exposure. Analyze whether the Authority's failure to replace the filters in March counts as a cause of the July illnesses. In your answer, address counterfactual dependence, the omission/commission distinction, whether the causal structure here is overdetermination or joint necessary conditions, whether distributing the omission through an algorithmic system alters its causal status, and whether temporal proximity affects omission causation differently than it affects commission."

A student submitted the following answer (approximately 450 words):

"The Authority's omission is clearly a cause. Counterfactual dependence is satisfied: had the filters been replaced in March, the May discharge would have been caught, and there would have been no July illness. The factory's dumping is also a cause. At first this looks like overdetermination, but it is actually joint causation—neither factor alone is sufficient, since bad filters without dumping produce no contamination, and dumping with good filters produces none either. So both are necessary parts of a single causal pathway.

The board's decision not to override the algorithm is the omission itself; the algorithm is only an instrument, so distributing the failure through the system does not change anything metaphysically. The human choice remains the locus of causation.

Regarding omission versus commission, there is no deep asymmetry. Both involve making a difference to what happens. The factory physically added matter to the river, while the Authority physically did not intervene, but causation tracks counterfactual dependence, not physical transmission. Therefore omissions and commissions should be treated symmetrically in causal and moral assessment.

Finally, the factory's dumping in May is temporally closer to the illness than the March omission, but this does not amount to preemption. Omissions lack spatiotemporal location in the causal chain, so temporal proximity cannot cancel them out. The omission remains a full cause alongside the commission."
