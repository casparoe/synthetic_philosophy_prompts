As a novelist drafting a narrative about institutional language decay, I have built the following argument from Orwell's political essays and fiction, but its conclusion undermines the very distinction my plot requires, and I need to locate the precise failure. (1) In his essay on political language, Orwell condemns abstract, Latinate diction and passive constructions as instruments that allow governments to conceal violence behind euphemism; his remedy is concrete, Anglo-Saxon directness—short words, active verbs, images drawn from physical reality. (2) Yet in 1984, Newspeak achieves total domination not through euphemistic obscurity but through radical simplification—stripping vocabulary and grammatical options until heretical concepts become grammatically impossible. (3) Therefore, the formal properties Orwell praises—lexical economy, grammatical regularity, absence of ambiguity—are precisely those that render Newspeak effective. (4) If there is no structural difference between Orwell's ideal prose and the language of totalitarian control, then his linguistic reformism is self-defeating: purifying language creates the conditions for its weaponization. (5) One might reply that speaker intent distinguishes them—voluntary clarity versus imposed reduction—but intent is extralinguistic and therefore cannot ground a theory of language itself. (6) Hence Orwell offers no internally linguistic criterion to separate liberating simplicity from oppressive simplification, and his politics of language collapses. I am inclined to defend (1) and (3), yet I sense an equivocation in the transition to (4). Where does the argument overreach, and what does saving my preferred premises cost?
