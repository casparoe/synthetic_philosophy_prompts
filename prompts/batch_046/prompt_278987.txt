hey, i run a small restorative-justice nonprofit that mediates housing conflicts—like property damage, lease violations, or neighbor harassment—where someone clearly did wrong but there's usually mental-health, addiction, or economic stress in the mix. we get maybe five intakes a week, each one is 45 minutes max, and my staff aren't philosophers. they need a reusable screening procedue to decide: do we route the case into the moral-dialogue track, where we treat the person as a responsible moral agent and open space for real reactive attitudes—resentment, apology, forgiveness, blame—or do we route it into the structural-support track, where we take an objective attitude and just manage risk, connect services, and avoid blame?

ground this in Strawson's reactive attitudes and moral responsibility, but don't just regurgitate the standard responsible/not-responsible binary. i want you to inject a "reactive gradient" with three zones—full participatory standing, attenuated standing (reactive attitudes are held but redirected through institutional proxies like a mediator or tenant board), and withdrawn standing (pure objective attitude)—plus a "double-entry check" that tracks both the offender's capacity for moral address and the community's entitlement to direct reactive attitudes at them. this should handle vicarious reactive standing too, like when a landlord holds resentment on behalf of other tenants.

write the checklist in order: the questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedue stops with a hard no, and where it hands the call to a person because it's values not facts. then run it on three cases:
1. a tenant with untreated schizophrenia who intentionally flooded an apartment to "cleanse evil"—the victim is terrified and demands a direct face-to-face apology.
2. a tenant who repeatedly threw loud parties knowing it violated the lease, but was also working three jobs and severely sleep-deprived—the landlord wants moral condemnation plus eviction.
3. a tenant who damaged a unit during a dissociative episode triggered by the landlord's own illegal eviction threat—so victim and offender roles kind of blur.

make sure one case gives an answer the staff will genuinely hate. also say clearly what the procedure cannot do—like whether attenuated standing is just a cop-out for avoiding hard blame, or what to do when multiple victims disagree on thier reactive entitlements. and at the end, tell me what would change your mind: what specific argument or failure mode would convince you that the reactive-gradient framework is wrong and we should go back to a simple binary?

write casual, american english. a typo or two is totally fine.
