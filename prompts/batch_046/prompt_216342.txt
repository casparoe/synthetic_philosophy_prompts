If Hofstadter is correct that an 'I' is essentially a strange loop—a pattern of self-reference that can be reconstructed in other minds—then distributing that pattern across multiple brains appears to turn personal identity into a divisible quantity rather than a discrete condition. Suppose the loop corresponding to one person is instantiated in two different brains, each reproducing roughly half of the pattern's structural relations. Should we say that the original self survives as a single distributed entity spanning both substrates, that it has split into two diminished copies, or that the loop's closure requires a single dominant locus and therefore neither host truly contains the self? More generally, if identity is genuinely distributed across brains, by what non-arbitrary criterion do we determine whether we are witnessing one multiplied self or several incomplete echoes?
