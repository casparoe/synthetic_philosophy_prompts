I am taking a graduate seminar on normative ethics, and after our session on supererogation two classmates—Marcus and Elena—continued arguing over coffee in a direction that quickly outpaced the standard textbook framing. Marcus proposed what he called moral surplus pooling: when an agent performs a genuinely supererogatory act—say, donating a kidney to a stranger—the value created exceeds what duty demands, and that excess does not simply dissipate. Instead, it functions like a cooperative dividend, raising the baseline of moral expectation within the agent's social circle without converting the original act into an obligation for anyone. Elena rejected this immediately. She argued that supererogation is structurally non-transferable: if the surplus can be distributed, credited, or used to recalibrate collective expectations, then the agent has not truly gone beyond duty but has instead fulfilled a hidden collective obligation to generate that surplus for the group. Marcus replied that Elena was treating moral obligations as zero-sum, but obligations might allow positive externalities—his donation helps the recipient directly, yet also improves the moral climate of the community, allowing others to meet adjusted, not lowered, standards. Elena countered that once you allow an individual's free act to alter what is reasonably expected of others, you collapse the distinction between supererogation and collective duty at the group level; the act remains personally free, but framing it as a public resource transforms it into a constitutive part of a shared normative framework. Marcus then suggested that Elena's view forces every heroic act into a kind of moral isolation, preventing communities from recognizing excellence without being burdened by it, which seems counterintuitive to how we actually praise saints and heroes. Elena answered that recognition and redistribution are different: we can praise Marcus without allowing his sister to claim that his surplus excuses her smaller failures of charity, because praise attaches to the agent, not to a fungible moral currency.

They turned to me because I had been silent, and asked me to adjudicate their exchange rather than appeal to authority. Can you work through their argument round by round—identifying where each makes a genuinely sound point, where each missteps, and whether Marcus's pooling framework or Elena's non-transferability thesis is more defensible overall? In particular, please say who is right about which specific claim, what both parties are missing—especially regarding whether their disagreement is ultimately about the metaphysics of moral value or about the social practices of praise and expectation—and whether there is a coherent middle position that preserves the non-obligatory character of the original act without treating its value as either strictly private or collectively fungible.
