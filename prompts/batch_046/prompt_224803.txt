I'm drafting a novel about a real mathematician who died in the eighties — not one of the absulute giants, but he published some well-known proofs. I invented a scene where he deliberately buries a junior colleague's result to protect his own reputation. There's zero evidence he ever did that; the letters we have show he was actually generous about sharing credit. But the invented betrayal makes the theme about ambition and complicity actually land, and my editor insists it's the heart of the book. His former students caught wind and wrote me saying I'm poisoning the well for anyone who wants to study his real legacy. I feel like fiction gets to lie for art, but I also feel like there's a real difference between inventing a secret love affair and inventing a moral crime — am I just rationalizing? I'm definately not trying to be malicious, but I don't want to be a coward either.

Please don't just tell me "art has license" — I want you to be straight about what's settled here and what's genuinely contested among people who actually study the ethics of representation and posthumous harm. Seperate the consensus from the arguments that are still live, and don't present one contested view as if everyone agrees on it.
