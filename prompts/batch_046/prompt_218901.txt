As a doctoral researcher in bioethics advising a tertiary hospital clinical-ethics service, I need a reusable decision procedure for consultants who must determine whether an advance directive retains normative authority when a patient with fluctuating cognitive capacity expresses wishes that contradict it. The theoretical framework I am working with distinguishes three distinct claims an earlier self may exercise: predictive authority (the directive forecasts present preferences), valuative authority (it expresses enduring commitments that ought to govern despite changed affective states), and executive authority (it operates as a binding instruction that can override present welfare considerations). I want this translated into a step-by-step protocol — a sequence of capacity assessments, authority-weighting tests, and decision nodes — that a consultant can apply in under thirty minutes before a family conference, without philosophical specialisation. The procedure must specify how to evaluate intermittent lucidity, how to adjudicate conflicts among the three authorities, and where irreducible clinical judgment must be marked explicitly rather than concealed as procedural output. It must address the hard case of a stable, value-inverted present preference — for instance, a patient who previously prioritised autonomy now consistently seeks total dependence. Apply it to three scenarios: (i) a dementia patient with brief lucid intervals who refuses nutrition mandated by the directive; (ii) a patient with dissociative-state dynamics who revokes a psychiatric-advance directive during an altered state; and (iii) a case in which the protocol recommends honouring the directive despite the consultant's strong intuitive reluctance. State what the protocol cannot resolve and where it is prone to misfire. Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence, then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question. Write in British English.
