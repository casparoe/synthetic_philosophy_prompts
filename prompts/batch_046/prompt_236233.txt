I'm working through an argument for my seminar on blame, hypocrisy, and moral standing, and I keep returning to it because the conclusion seems clearly absurd yet I can't locate the flaw without abandoning a premise I want to keep. The argument attempts to extend the personal hypocrisy objection to collective agents, and in doing so it appears to show that no institution—no court, university, or regulatory body—can ever legitimately blame or sanction an individual for wrongs like plagiarism, fraud, or discrimination.

(1) If agent A blames B for wrong W, and A knows that they themselves have committed W (or a wrong that falls under the same governing moral principle) without having made adequate moral repair, then A's blame is hypocritical and lacks second-personal standing: B is entitled to reject it not merely as unfair but as failing to address B in the appropriate normative mode.

(2) This undermining of standing applies not only to token instances but to the principle: once A's hypocrisy is acknowledged, A is not in a position to recognize W in B through the lens of shared normative authority.

(3) Collective agents—universities, courts, states—are constituted by the members who act in their name, and they persist through time. Unless there has been explicit institutional acknowledgment and repair, an institution inherits the unacknowledged wrongs of its present and past members.

(4) Because virtually every institution contains members who have violated every broad moral principle (honesty, fairness, non-harm, respect for autonomy) in some form, and because no institution has fully catalogued and repaired every such violation, every collective agent is structurally hypocritical about nearly every wrong it attempts to police.

(5) Therefore, by (1) and (2), no collective agent possesses the standing to blame any individual for plagiarism, fraud, discrimination, or analogous wrongs; its blame is always epistemically and normatively defective in the same way a private hypocrite's blame is.

(6) Furthermore, if an agent lacks standing to blame, then that agent also lacks the derivative authority to impose sanctions that express blame, since sanction without standing collapses into mere coercion.

(7) So courts cannot sentence fraudsters, universities cannot expel plagiarists, and states cannot penalize discrimination—unless we deny that institutions are genuine agents capable of blame, which seems too costly.

(8) But we clearly think some institutional correction is legitimate, even when we know that some judges have parked illegally, some professors have sloppy citation practices, or some administrators have bent rules.

(9) Therefore the argument must fail. I am inclined to defend (1) and (2)—I think hypocrisy genuinely corrupts the second-personal address that makes blame rational—so I do not want to reject the core personal principle. I also want to keep (7)'s link between standing and sanction, because severing them seems ad hoc. That leaves me looking at (3), (4), or perhaps a hidden scope shift between the personal case in (1) and the structural case in (5). Where does the argument actually go wrong, and what does rejecting the faulty step commit me to about collective moral agency or the conditions of institutional repair?
