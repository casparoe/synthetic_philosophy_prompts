I keep running into the same trio of objections whenever someone draws an analogy between the brain and a digital computer, yet they always seem to appear together, and I have never seen anyone treat them as a single named pattern rather than a loose checklist. First, people say there is no clear separation between processor and storage—the same physical structures both carry out computation and hold the accumulated traces of experience. Second, they note that when one part fails, the system does not crash, because errors are absorbed through statistical redundancy rather than caught by an explicit error-correcting code. Third, they point out that learning is not just writing to an addressable record; it physically reshapes the pathways through which future signals flow, so the computing medium and the stored information change together. I have seen each point used separately to resist the computer metaphor, but what interests me is the bundle where all three hold at once: an analog substrate that computes, remembers, and rewires itself, with no master clock or separated architecture, and with fault tolerance built into the pattern of activity rather than into a dedicated verification layer. I recall reading that von Neumann, late in his life, questioned whether the brain really followed the architecture he had formalized—he seemed to think biological computation relied on distributed statistical patterns rather than on the precise sequential logic of his machines. Does this specific cluster have an established name in philosophy of mind or neuroscience? Or is it only an informal grouping? If a concept exists, how exactly does it map onto his reservations—does it capture the same mismatch, or is his concern narrower? And if there is no standard term, could the boundary be drawn clearly enough to include a brain-inspired analog chip that modifies its own physical connections, while excluding a conventional computer running a learning algorithm in software?
