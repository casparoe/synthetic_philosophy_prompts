At a graduate seminar on democratic legitimacy last Tuesday, I observed an extended exchange between two doctoral students—Amara and Ben—regarding the proper boundaries of the franchise. Amara defended a reform package combining compulsory voting for citizens, lowering the voting age to sixteen, and extending local voting rights to long-term non-citizen residents. She grounded her position in what she called the all-affected-interests principle: anyone subjected to a law's coercive force should possess a formal voice in its formation, and she maintained that excluding tax-paying adolescents or resident non-citizens is arbitrary absent proof of deficient cognitive or moral capacity, which she argued existing studies do not establish. Ben responded that this framework collapses democratic self-rule into a service-delivery model; on his account, the polity is not a welfare agency distributing representation according to need, but a bounded collective that exercises autonomy by authorizing its own coercion. Consequently, the decisive question is not who is affected, but who belongs to the people that binds itself through law. He further argued that compulsory voting undermines its own justification—if legitimacy requires consent, then compelled participation vitiates the authorization it purports to secure—and that lowering the voting age without parallel restructuring of civic education risks instrumentalizing teenagers rather than empowering them. Amara countered that Ben's membership criterion is viciously circular, since existing citizens draw the boundaries they then invoke to justify exclusion, and she introduced what she termed a coercion-consent asymmetry: where the state denies meaningful exit through immigration controls and tax obligations, it owes participatory rights to those it traps within its jurisdiction, regardless of formal nationality. Ben then articulated a distinction I found particularly sharp: between stakeholding legitimacy, which demands representation for all with interests at stake, and authorship legitimacy, which holds that only those who share in the collective identity of the polity may legitimately bind it. He granted that local matters such as school zoning or policing appear to invite broader inclusion, yet insisted that municipal authority derives entirely from state delegation, so allowing non-citizens to vote locally severs the chain of democratic authorization without providing a coherent replacement. They asked me, as the only senior scholar present, to referee, and I genuinely could not determine where the fault line lies.

Please adjudicate this disagreement with precision. Organize your response into the following sections, in order, with approximate lengths: (1) Reconstruction of the exchange (about 250 words), clarifying Amara's coercion-consent asymmetry and Ben's distinction between stakeholding and authorship legitimacy, and identifying the exact point at which their frameworks become incommensurable; (2) Point-by-point evaluation (about 350 words), stating who is right about which specific claim—whether Amara's circularity charge succeeds, whether Ben's consent objection defeats compulsory voting, and whether local non-citizen voting can be defended without breaking Ben's authorization chain—and noting where each speaker relies on an unstated empirical or normative premise; (3) The missing middle (about 200 words), identifying what both Amara and Ben overlook, whether a conceptual distinction they fail to draw, a practical institutional constraint they neglect, or an implicit assumption about the nature of political obligation that should be challenged; and (4) Overall verdict (about 200 words), delivering a clear bottom line on the three reform proposals considered together—compulsory voting, age sixteen, and non-citizen local voting—rather than treating them in isolation, and explaining which conception of democratic legitimacy ultimately proves more defensible and why.
