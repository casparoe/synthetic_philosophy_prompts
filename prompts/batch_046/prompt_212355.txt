Analyze the Chilean Project Cybersyn (1971–1973) through the lens of the socialist calculation debate between Mises, Hayek, and Lange. Rather than assessing its technology, treat Cybersyn as an empirical attempt to solve the economic-calculation problem without full market pricing: a cybernetic system linking state-owned factories via telex networks to a central operations room that used algorithmic feedback loops to adjust production targets in near real time. First, map specific features of this architecture—real-time data aggregation, algorithmic rules intended to substitute for market-clearing prices, and closed-loop resource reallocation—onto Lange’s model of trial-and-error socialist pricing, and identify which features remain exposed to Hayek’s objections about dispersed tacit knowledge and local incentive distortion. Second, examine whether the project’s termination during the September 1973 coup should be read as a failure of socialist calculation per se, as a failure of the political conditions required for any planning system, or as a conceptual mistake in treating a national economy as an engineering control problem amenable to centralized feedback. Third, say what the episode reveals about the underlying stakes of the debate: was the disagreement fundamentally about information-processing capacity, about the institutional prerequisites for knowledge discovery, or about something neither side fully articulated?
