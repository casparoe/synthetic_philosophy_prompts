Design a 75-minute classroom activity for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy seminar of about 14 students on Buddhist anatta (no-self) and personal identity. Participants should come away feeling the practical tension between experiential continuity and the absence of an enduring subject—specifically, that promises, blame, and future-planning depend on an implicit owner who is not there. The idea should emerge through what happens, not through a lecture.

Divide the class into four streams of 3–4 students. Give each stream a four-stage scenario: (1) a promise made to care for a family member; (2) a harmful act committed in anger; (3) a decision whether to forgive an offender; (4) an end-of-life request. Each student in a stream holds one skandha card—form, sensation, perception, mental formation, or consciousness. At each stage, the stream must reach a collective decision, record it on a visible santana ledger that assigns symbolic points to the stream rather than to any individual, and then rotate out exactly one skandha-holder and replace them with a new student holding that same card. Thus the stream’s aggregate composition is never identical twice, yet causal rules bind the sequence.

The remaining members must justify the accumulated commitments to the newcomer using only causal, compassionate, or consequence-based language: no first-person pronouns, no appeals to character or memory-ownership, no implicit subject. At the end, the streams with the highest and lowest ledger scores must jointly allocate a small symbolic resource—say, a shared token that either honors the original promise or breaks it—without any member claiming personal entitlement.

Specify exact rules, rotation mechanics, a 75-minute timeline, and the symbolic payoff/ledger scheme. Provide a debrief that maps each stage onto anatta, upadana (clinging to an owner), and karmic continuity; say what will predictably go wrong (e.g., students smuggling identity back in through pronouns, or treating rotation as new people rather than the absence of people) and how to correct it through guided questioning rather than lecturing. Be explicit about which feature of anatta the activity cannot reproduce—likely the first-person phenomenological absence of self, or the full metaphysical denial of substance across all aggregates—and that the facilitator must supply this through brief framing.

Keep the design runnable with only cards and a ledger board. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Do not invoke highly obscure theories; stick to standard concepts (skandhas, santana, upadana, karma) arranged here in a novel structure.
