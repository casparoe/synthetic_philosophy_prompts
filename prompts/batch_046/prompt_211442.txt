I'm working through Hume's framework for aesthetic judgment—specifically whether taste can support an objective standard of beauty despite its basis in sentiment rather than reason—and I've been tracking how a colleague, Dr. Mara Ellison, applies it to borderline cases in a seminar she runs on philosophy of art. Her verdicts so far have been consistent but, I think, jointly compatible with more than one underlying principle, and I'd like you to predict her ruling on a new case, identify the competing interpretations of her view, and say what further example would settle the matter.

Here is her record. First, she judged a meticulously restored Renaissance fresco beautiful despite modern viewers reporting emotional flatness, insisting that qualified critics' convergent admiration establishes the verdict regardless of lay response. Second, she denied beauty to a globally viral pop melody, arguing that widespread pleasure among untrained listeners is mere agreeableness and lacks the refined delicacy required by the standard. Third, confronted with a decaying organic installation that split trained curators evenly between sublime awe and visceral disgust, she suspended judgment, claiming the standard had not yet achieved the necessary intersubjective stability. Fourth, she rejected as non-beautiful a geometrically perfect digital pattern that elicited no emotional response whatsoever, maintaining that structural coherence without sentiment is empty. Fifth, she affirmed the beauty of a classical Japanese garden that lay audiences find dull, attributing their failure to insufficient practice and comparison rather than to any deficiency in the work.

The new case is this: an AI system has been trained exclusively on the real-time physiological signatures—galvanic skin response, pupillary dilation, and neural coherence patterns—of a single master critic whose judgments align with the historical consensus of qualified judges. The system generates visual compositions that reproduce, with high fidelity, the exact somatic profile this critic exhibits when encountering acknowledged masterpieces. However, the outputs are never experienced directly by anyone else; other experts see only reproductions and report bewilderment or mild displeasure. The training subject, when exposed to the originals, reports the characteristic refined pleasure. Would Dr. Ellison call these compositions beautiful? State whether her verdict is better explained by a strict intersubjective-consensus reading of the standard—where beauty requires convergence among multiple qualified observers—or by a calibrated-sentiment objectivism that treats the correct physiological response as the real marker of beauty regardless of how many people currently share it. Then propose one further hypothetical case that would force her to choose between these two rules.
