I have come to defend the view that hypocrisy is never a defeater for standing to blame, but only for the propriety or sincerity of expressing it. On my account, standing is determined entirely by the relational structure between blamer and wronged party—whether one is the victim, occupies an appropriate institutional role, or stands in some other position that authorizes holding another to account—while the blamer's own moral consistency is irrelevant to this entitlement. The victim need not be a saint to resent her assailant. Yet I want an honest map of what would count against this position, not merely charges of tu quoque. Specifically: are there distinctions—say, between standing-to-blame and standing-to-resent, or between moral and epistemic standing—through which hypocrisy could undermine one form of entitlement without touching another? Do collective or third-party contexts reveal cases where parallel wrongdoing by the blamer erodes not just the weight but the very authority to blame? And consider the hard case: a perpetrator of atrocities condemning an identical crime by an adversary—must my view insist he retains full standing, or is there a principled way to concede its loss without collapsing into the claim that blamers must be morally perfect? Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether defenders of this relational account should themselves concede any such scenario. Finally, assess whether my view risks being insulated into unfalsifiability by defining standing so narrowly that no feature of the blamer's moral record could ever matter—and whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a defect.
