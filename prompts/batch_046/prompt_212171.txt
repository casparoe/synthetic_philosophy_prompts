As a novelist exploring inherited shame in postwar narratives, I want to read Günter Grass's The Tin Drum through Karl Jaspers's taxonomy of collective guilt—political, criminal, moral, and metaphysical—while testing a particular tension: whether metaphysical guilt can be transmitted to a generation that did not perform the acts that incurred it, or whether it collapses into an aesthetic posture. Oskar Matzerath's refusal to grow and his percussive, glass-shattering voice seem to suspend him between complicity and innocence. Read the novel with that lens. Where does Jaspers illuminate Oskar's choice to remain a child among adults who deny their past? Where does Grass resist the taxonomy—perhaps by suggesting that drumming substitutes for moral reckoning rather than expressing it? Focus on the burial of Oskar's father and the postwar episodes of collective denial. Does the novel argue that the second generation's duty is not to bear inherited guilt but to refuse its performance? I am preparing this for a seminar on postwar German fiction and need a reading that takes both the philosophy and the narrative architecture seriously, without reducing either to an illustration of the other.
