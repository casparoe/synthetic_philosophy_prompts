I have been trying to understand a philosophical puzzle that I think is called Benacerraf’s dilemma, though I may be misremembering the exact name. From what I gather, it asks how we can claim to know about mathematical things like numbers if they are not physical, not located in space or time, and therefore cannot cause anything to happen in our minds — since most of our knowledge seems to come from causal interaction with the world. I was discussing this with a friend over tea, and she asked whether older thinkers, who never encountered this modern framing, might nevertheless have had the resources to answer it, or even to anticipate the trouble.

With that in mind, I should like you to reconstruct how David Hume might have reacted had someone laid Benacerraf’s dilemma out to him in detail, particularly the part that quietly assumes a causal theory of knowledge — the idea that knowing something requires being causally connected to it. Given Hume’s own insistence that the mind works through custom, association and the relations of ideas rather than through direct causal transmission of content, do you think he would have accepted that causal condition in the first place? Or would he have said that mathematics is precisely the domain where causal access is unnecessary, because it concerns only relations of ideas that we discover through internal comparison? I am especially curious whether he would have treated the dilemma as a reason to deny that numbers exist independently of our minds, or whether he might have allowed them a kind of mental or relational existence without falling into Platonism. Please work through this as a reasoned reconstruction of his likely view rather than a survey of modern scholarship, and feel free to explore the tension between his empiricism and the claim that we can know necessary truths about objects we never encounter in experience.
