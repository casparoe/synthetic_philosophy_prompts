In a jurisprudence seminar I observed recently, two participants—let’s call them M and R—became locked in a debate about whether the law may prohibit a practice that is widely seen as immoral yet causes no direct physical injury to anyone outside the arrangement. The practice they focused on is what they called an “institutional exit contract”: a voluntary, revocable legal agreement through which adults formally dissolve all reciprocal obligations of care, loyalty, and inheritance within their family unit, receiving in exchange state-sanctioned tax exemptions and release from elder-care mandates. No party is coerced, and the contracts are used mainly by wealthy individuals optimizing estate planning. M opened with a Devlin-style argument: even absent a specific victim, the normalization of such explicit, contractual repudiation of familial duty erodes the shared moral fabric that sustains cooperative social structures, and the state has a legitimate interest in defending that fabric by criminalizing the practice. R responded with a Hart-style harm-principle objection: because entry is entirely voluntary and the direct effects are confined to the contracting parties, there is no victim in the relevant sense, and the law should not enforce one particular conception of family morality. M then pressed that R was assuming a false individualism—if enough people opt out, the remaining social expectation of care collapses, harming vulnerable dependents indirectly and degrading a public good that no single opt-out destroys but many collectively undermine. R countered that this is speculative sociology rather than demonstrated harm, and warned that by that logic any unpopular moral choice, from religious apostasy to voluntary childlessness, could be banned once it reaches a critical mass. M conceded the slope risk but insisted there is a qualitative difference between passive non-participation and an active, state-recognized contract that symbolically repudiates the institution itself, making the harm structural rather than merely statistical. R closed by arguing that treating symbolic repudiation as harm collapses the distinction between offense and injury, which is precisely the error the harm principle was designed to prevent.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. Rate M’s initial legal-moralist claim, R’s harm-principle rebuttal, M’s collective-action reply about indirect harm, R’s slope-risk counter, M’s qualitative-distinction concession, and R’s final offense-injury point—each on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification. Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing: whether their disagreement turns on an empirical claim about social cohesion that neither has defended, whether one has mischaracterized the other’s position, and whether there is a principled middle ground between enforcing morality and requiring direct victimhood. Do not treat this as a request for legal advice; the deliverable is a point-by-point adjudication of the arguments as actually made.
