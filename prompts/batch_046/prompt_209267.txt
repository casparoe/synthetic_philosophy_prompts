I'm preparing a graduate seminar paper on the metaphysics of causation, and I've become stuck on a tension that standard textbooks don't seem to address directly. Most introductions present counterfactual and interventionist theories—roughly, the Lewis-Stalnaker tradition versus the Pearl-style structural-equation approach—as rival accounts of the same phenomenon. But I'm interested in whether the interventionist framework can genuinely claim to be free of counterfactual commitments, or whether it merely relocates them into the formal machinery of the do-operator.

Write an essay addressing this question. Rather than rehearsing familiar examples like smoking and cancer, focus on what we might call "causation without possible intervention"—cases such as the explanatory structure of mathematical proofs, or deterministic physical laws in closed systems where no agent could, even in principle, manipulate a variable. Examine the argument that defining causal effect via do(X) implicitly presupposes modal reasoning about what would obtain under hypothetical manipulation, and therefore smuggles counterfactual dependence back into the framework. Then consider the interventionist reply that causal structure is identified through invariant structural equations, and that counterfactual talk is merely a heuristic for representing those equations, not part of the ontology.

Weigh both sides carefully, but do not end non-committally. Defend a specific verdict: either that counterfactual dependence is ineliminable and the interventionist program collapses into a notational variant of counterfactualism, or that there is a principled metaphysical distinction between causal structure and modal counterfactuality that allows a genuinely non-counterfactual interventionist account—even in cases where literal intervention is impossible.
