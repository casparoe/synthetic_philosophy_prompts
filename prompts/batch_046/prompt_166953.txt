I'm a PhD student in normative ethics, and in two weeks I'm presenting a dissertation chapter to a joint workshop of moral philosophers and welfare economists. My central thesis is that the standard leveling-down objection to prioritarianism succeeds only if we presuppose cardinal, interpersonally comparable welfare scales; I defend a reformulated prioritarian metric that ranks welfare ordinally and indexes each agent's weight to their epistemic access to discriminating that welfare state from nearby alternatives. On this view, reducing everyone within a shared evidential partition to an equally low welfare level does not constitute a distributive improvement, because equality of epistemic inaccessibility is not a moral gain—the priority weight attaches only to improvements the agent can evidentially register. The audience is split: half are committed egalitarians or sufficientists who will treat prioritarianism as covert aggregative utilitarianism, and half are prioritarian sympathizers in welfare measurement who will worry that an ordinal, epistemically indexed function cannot guide institutional design or policy evaluation. I need a prep sheet for the Q&A: list the objections each side will actually raise, ordered by how likely and damaging they are, phrased in the vocabulary that egalitarians and welfare economists respectively use. For each, tell me what to concede, what to hold, how to reply concisely within two minutes, and where the reply is weakest. Be sure to include the challenge that I'm not answering the leveling-down objection but redefining equality away, and include the question I'm hoping the economists don't ask—whether this metric can determine resource allocation without cardinal measurement—along with the reply I'd have to give and its soft point. I also want flagged the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss: that the epistemic-access condition risks circularity if welfare states themselves partly constitute what an agent can discriminate.
