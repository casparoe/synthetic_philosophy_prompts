I am drafting an essay for a graduate seminar on Hellenistic ethics and its contemporary appropriations. The core claim is that the modern Stoic revival—exemplified by popular exponents who frame Stoicism primarily as a toolkit for personal resilience—has systematically collapsed Epictetus’s dichotomy of control into a simplistic binary between internal attitude and external circumstance. In the Discourses and the Enchiridion, Epictetus distinguishes not merely between what is and is not in our power, but between the proper exercise of prohairesis and the sphere of externals that includes not only material goods but also bodily health, reputation, and the actions of others. Modern revivalists, however, tend to treat the dichotomy as a therapeutic heuristic: if you cannot directly change an outcome, you should detach your emotional investment from it. This framing, I argue, obscures the Stoic commitment to preferred indifferents—things like health and social relationships that, while not good in the strict moral sense, remain appropriate objects of rational concern and active pursuit.

My essay then introduces a conceptual complication I have not seen developed in the existing literature. I propose that there exists a category of phenomena that are neither fully within the sphere of prohairesis nor straightforwardly external: what I call structurally mediated dispositions. These are patterns of judgment, attention, and emotional response initially shaped by external social architectures—algorithmic feeds, workplace incentive structures, urban design—yet become, through habituation, partially internalized as seemingly spontaneous reactions. The modern revival’s binary framing cannot adequately account for this intermediate zone, because it assumes that once an influence is recognized as external, the Stoic practitioner can simply withhold assent. But if a disposition has been internalized to the point that it operates below the threshold of conscious deliberation, the standard therapeutic move—cognitive reframing of the external—fails to address the structural origin of the judgment itself. I suggest that a more rigorous Stoic practice would require not only internal reframing but also active restructuring of the external conditions that shape pre-reflective assent.

I would appreciate comments on three specific points. First, is my characterization of the modern revival’s flattening of the dichotomy accurate, or do contemporary writers in this space actually preserve more nuance about preferred indifferents and active engagement with externals than I allow? Second, does the concept of structurally mediated dispositions genuinely extend the Stoic framework, or does it merely rename familiar problems about social conditioning that Stoics already addressed through their theory of impressions and assent? Third, I am particularly unsure about the normative conclusion: if these dispositions are neither fully internal nor fully external, does the Stoic practitioner bear full moral responsibility for restructuring them, or only for the assent given to them once they arise? I am not seeking bibliographic recommendations or citations, but rather conceptual clarification and suggestions for sharpening the argument’s internal coherence.
