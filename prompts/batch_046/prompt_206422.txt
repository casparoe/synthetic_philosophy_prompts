Write a joke that depends for its punchline on the specific structure of Jean-Jacques Rousseau's critique of theater, particularly as articulated in his letter to d'Alembert. The joke should not be a generic observation that Rousseau disliked entertainment; rather, it should turn on his actual arguments—that theatrical spectacle separates citizens into passive spectators and self-promoting performers, that it corrupts public morals especially by encouraging artificial display and luxury among women, and that it offers only the illusion of virtue rather than its practice—contrasted with his praise for open-air civic festivals in which the people are simultaneously actors and audience in collective liberty. To prevent the joke from being a simple historical pastiche, embed it in the following precisely defined hypothetical scenario: the modern city council of Geneva votes to abolish its opera house and replace it with a municipally funded 'Festival of Republican Citizenship,' in which every resident must perform a civic duty before the others, but the organizing committee—composed entirely of retired professional actors—insists on Parisian costume designers, admission fees to fund 'ascetic luxury' refreshments, and a separate elevated gallery where women are seated as living exhibits of republican modesty. The punchline should expose the internal contradiction in terms that someone genuinely familiar with Rousseau's objections to spectacle, vanity, economic waste, and gendered display would immediately recognize. Avoid invented quotations, references to obscure secondary scholarship, or requests for bibliographic material.
