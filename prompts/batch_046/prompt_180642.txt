I'm preparing a ninety-minute seminar for graduate students in political theory who have no background in social-choice mathematics. I want them to understand the ethics of voting—specifically the tension between a duty to participate, the expressive value of casting a ballot for one's true preference, and the strategic pressure to vote for the lesser evil—through the lens of a niche framing: the "clean hands" problem in a ranked-choice election, where a voter sincerely prefers a minor candidate, knows that her single vote is causally negligible, but also knows that her ranking can affect elimination order and thus the final pairwise comparison. Do not explain the ethics to me; design the teaching sequence. First, state the conceptual dependency chain: must they grasp the logic of preference aggregation before the idea of causal efficacy of one vote, or the reverse, and where does the distinction between instrumental and expressive reasons enter? Second, identify the one concrete scenario that should carry the whole discussion—specify exactly what institutional features it must include (e.g., visible preference ordering, a genuinely worse major-party option, negligible individual pivotality) and what simplifying conditions must be stripped away so it does not collapse into a generic prisoner's dilemma. Third, map the predictable misconceptions: for instance, that expressive voting is merely virtue-signaling, or that strategic voting for the lesser evil is always a moral compromise rather than a fulfillment of duty; indicate precisely where in the sequence each should be preempted. Fourth, propose one diagnostic question to ask at the end that reveals whether they have grasped that the tension is structural—rooted in the aggregation mechanism—rather than resolvable by more information or clearer preferences. Finally, state explicitly which portion of the broader literature—say, epistemic-democracy arguments or the calculus of collective action—you will set aside, and justify why it is inappropriate for this audience and time constraint.
