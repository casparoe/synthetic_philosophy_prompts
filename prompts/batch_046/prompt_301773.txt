I'm prepping some case studies for my grad seminar on Scanlon's contractualism and the aggregation problem, and I'm stuck on whether there's one principle that can capture my gut reactions without stitching together ad hoc rules.

Here are the verdicts. One: I can save one stranger from drowning only by breaking a minor promise to a friend—missing a coffee meeting that'd dissapoint them but cause no lasting harm. I think that's wrong; the promise binds me even for a single life.

Two: same broken promise, but now I save five strangers. I still feel it's wrong, though maybe slightly less clearly—yet I don't think the number tips it. The friend can still reject being used as a mere means this way, and five lives don't seem to cancel that complaint.

Three: I can save one person from severe torture by allowing five others to suffer brief, mild headaches—no promises involved, just distributing discomfort. That feels okay; the aggregate suffering avoided outweighs the distributed harm.

Four: flip the numbers—save five from torture by letting one suffer the brief headache. Even more okay, obviously.

Five: I can save ten lives by imposing a tiny 1-in-100 risk of death on each of a hundred strangers—expected value is one death, but distributed and non-volitional. I think that's permissible, maybe required.

Six: saving the same ten lives by killing one healthy person and harvesting organs. Expected harm is identical, but this feels absolutely wrong.

So my pattern looks like aggregation is fine when harms are distributed and no one is singled out as a resource, but wrong when it violates a specific obligation to an identifiable individual, regardless of numbers. But then case two bugs me—if I stick with that, I'm letting five people die for the sake of a coffee meeting? That feels like a reductio, yet I still want to hold case one.

Is there a coherent principle—maybe something like 'reasonable rejectability applies to each complaint separately and is never summed across persons, except when the harm is probabilistic and anonymous'—that delivers all six? Or do I have to bite the bullet and drop one? And what should I say about an unlisted case: saving a hundred lives by imposing a 1-in-200 risk on ten thousand people, versus saving them by breaking ten seperate promises to ten different friends? I'd like to know which of my verdicts have to go for consistency, and whether Scanlon's own framework can actually accommodate the ones I keep without collapsing into utilitarianism.
