I'm currently taking an upper-division seminar on Hellenistic ethics and the modern revival of Stoicism, and we've been working through Epictetus's distinction between what is within our power and what lies outside it—together with contemporary interpretations that extend it into a theory of moral responsibility. I want to pressure-test the framework with an argument my study group constructed, but I can't locate the weak step.

(1) The Stoic dichotomy of control holds that only our judgments, impulses, and volitions—what Epictetus calls prohairesis—lie fully within our power; everything external, including the outcomes of our actions, lies outside it.
(2) If moral evaluation tracks only what is within an agent's power, then the virtue or vice of an action depends solely on the state of the agent's prohairesis, not on any external consequence.
(3) Now consider two cardiac surgeons performing identical procedures: Surgeon A exercises perfect diligence and correct clinical judgment, yet the patient dies from an unforeseeable anaphylactic reaction; Surgeon B operates with clear negligence and distracted judgment, yet by pure chance the patient survives without complication.
(4) By the criterion in (2), Surgeon A is fully virtuous and Surgeon B fully vicious, and the actual outcomes—death versus survival—carry no independent moral weight.
(5) But this seems to erase the distinction between culpable negligence and mere misfortune in every practical domain, since nearly all human actions have outcomes partly determined by factors outside our control.
(6) Therefore, either Stoicism must concede that external outcomes contribute to moral evaluation (contradicting the exclusivity of the dichotomy), or it must accept that a negligent agent who succeeds is morally no worse than a diligent agent who fails, provided their internal states match those descriptions—which proves far too much.

Which premise or step should I reject? I'd prefer to keep (1) and (2) if possible, because they capture what I take to be the core of Stoic ethics, but I'm open to being shown that one of them needs qualification. If I reject (2), what does that cost the modern Stoic claim that we should not be disturbed by things outside our control? If I reject (4), am I smuggling in consequentialist assumptions that a Stoic should reject? Please identify the step, explain why it looked plausible, and price the alternatives—especially what a defender of the modern revival (who wants Stoicism to guide civic and professional life, not just private tranquility) must pay to save the argument from its conclusion.
