In a philosophy podcast I listened to, the host summed up Leibniz's moral philosophy like this: 'Leibniz thought morality is basically about promoting the greatest harmony and variety in the universe, so justice is caritas sapientis — charity of the wise — which means you rank every being by its rational perfection and love them in exact proportion, making his view a kind of cosmic meritocracy. Because God chose the best of all possible worlds, every instance of moral evil is metaphysically necessary; take away one sin and you'd break the logical coherence of the whole system. Human freedom is real but only in a weak compatibilist sense: we always act on our strongest inclination, but Leibniz treated that inclination as mechanically fixed, like gravity pulling a rock downward, not as something flowing from our rational nature. So blaming someone for wrongdoing is like blaming a clock for being slow — it's just a category mistake. The host concluded that on Leibniz's view moral praise and blame are just useful fictions we keep up for social harmony, and that his ethics ultimately collapses into a deterministic system where responsibility is an illusion.'

That sounds mostly right to me in parts, but the mechanical determinism part and the metaphysical necessity part feel like they might be misrepresenting something. Is this summary fair? Please go sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a Leibniz scholar would accept, which are common simplifications that distort things, and which are just wrong. Especialy I want to know whether the conclusion — that Leibniz makes moral blame pointless — actually follows from the parts that are accurate, or if it depends on the errors.
