I am drafting an essay for a graduate seminar on philosophy and literature, and I would appreciate rigorous feedback on its interpretive plausibility and conceptual architecture. The essay concerns Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor and the familiar exchange in which the Inquisitor argues that humanity will inevitably trade freedom for security, miracle, and collective satisfaction. Rather than treating this as a generic authoritarian critique of liberty, I propose a more specific reading centered on what I call allocative hedonism: the idea that happiness, for the Inquisitor, functions not as an individual psychological state but as a collective resource that must be centrally distributed, and that freedom operates as a kind of moral noise that corrupts this distribution by introducing irreducible variance in human desire. I then press this framing against the text to ask whether the Inquisitor's position implicitly reduces to a form of collective-utilitarian reasoning that Dostoevsky's broader novelistic ethics reject, or whether it retains a distinctively theological claim—namely, that the burden of freedom is itself a sacred trial that makes genuine happiness impossible by design. I am particularly worried that my economic metaphors are anachronistic and that I am not doing justice to the specifically Christian dimensions of the Inquisitor's argument.

Could you evaluate whether the allocative hedonism framing genuinely illuminates the passage or imposes a foreign conceptual grid, whether the distinction between distributed happiness and individual dignity survives philosophical scrutiny, and which parts of the reasoning are most vulnerable to objection? I am not seeking citations, a bibliography, or source verification; I want conceptual criticism, suggestions for sharpening the argument, and an assessment of whether the essay avoids reducing the Inquisitor to a straw man for modern liberalism. If the framing holds, I also want to know whether it can be extended to explain why the Inquisitor insists on retaining the secret of miracle and mystery rather than simply enforcing obedience—does that retention serve the logic of allocation, or does it point to a limit in my model?
