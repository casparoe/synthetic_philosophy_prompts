I recently encountered a summary of the paradox of fiction — attributed to no particular author, but written in the voice of a confident populariser — and I am unsure how much of it to trust. The summary begins by stating that Colin Radford proved emotional responses to fiction are impossible: since fear requires belief in present danger and pity requires belief in actual suffering, and since audiences know that Anna Karenina and cinematic monsters do not exist, any apparent emotional reaction must be either self-deception or an error in psychological theory. It then introduces Kendall Walton's resolution, describing his notion of quasi-emotions as essentially theatrical performances: the audience voluntarily adopts postures of fear or sorrow, much as an actor pretends to be Hamlet, within the conventional framework of the fiction. On this account, the audience knows it is only performing, so no belief is required, and the paradox disappears because there never was a real emotion lacking its proper object. The summary adds that Walton therefore concedes that fiction cannot genuinely move us, which is why philosophers such as Peter Lamarque reject his view in favour of treating fictional emotions as authentic feelings directed towards imaginary scenarios. I have three concerns. First, is Radford's position correctly characterised, or does he present a problem rather than a proof of impossibility? Second, does Walton really describe quasi-emotions as voluntary theatrical performances, or is that a conflation with something else? Third, is it accurate to say that Walton's view makes fiction emotionally hollow, and that Lamarque's thought-theory is best understood as the direct opposite? Please separate what is accurate in the summary, what is a subtle distortion, and what, if anything, is simply wrong — and indicate whether the summary's conclusion about the superiority of the thought-theory follows from its correct premises.
