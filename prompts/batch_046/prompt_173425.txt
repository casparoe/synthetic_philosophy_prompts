I was chatting with my flatmate last night about this bioethics module I'm taking, and she brought up the hedonistic imperative — you know, the idea that we should use biotech to abolish suffering entirely rather than just managing it. It got me thinking about what happens if you don't just remove pain but flatten the whole affective range. Could you write a short story exploring that?

Set it in a near-future UK where a company has released a voluntary implant called the Valence Filter. It doesn't erase memory but stops any experience registering as unpleasant — grief, embarrassment, even dull aches become neutral information. Most people have taken it, but there's a small subculture of 'Contrast Keepers' who pay for sessions in licensed grey rooms where the filter is paused, so they can feel genuine suffering for art or intimacy. The main character should be Elara, a grief counsellor who literally can't feel sad, and she books a session not for some creative project but because she's definatly worried she's lost the architecture of empathy. Inside the room she should discover something weirder than simple pain: the filter had also flattened anticipatory dread and a particular kind of boredom that used to give her days shape, and when it occured to her that these textures were missing she starts to question whether the hedonistic project collapsed an entire temporal structure of feeling rather than just removing the bad bits. Don't turn it into a moralising fable about suffering being noble; instead let her realise that absence of pain isn't the same as presence of meaning. Keep it grounded — no robots, no aliens — just people, the tech, and maybe her flatmate turning up at the end with a totally different take on whether she's made a mistake.
