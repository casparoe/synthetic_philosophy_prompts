Consider the debate over whether moral revolutions—such as the abolition of chattel slavery and the disappearance of dueling—are driven by rational moral arguments, by shifts in material interest, or by changes in honor codes (as Appiah argues for footbinding and dueling, and as some readers take Bernard Williams’s remarks on the “morality system” to suggest for slavery). Here is an argument that tries to unify these mechanisms, but ends up proving far too much.

(1) Any sustained social norm N is sustained either by material interest, by honor/shame dynamics, or by explicit moral belief.
(2) If N is sustained by material interest alone, then N collapses as soon as it becomes materially inefficient for a critical mass of participants; yet slavery and dueling persisted long after they were materially costly for many who upheld them.
(3) If N is sustained by honor/shame dynamics alone, then N collapses only when the honor code is redefined; but such redefinitions are themselves caused by prior shifts in what people are willing to publicly endorse and sanction.
(4) Therefore, in both cases the ultimate driver is a change in explicit moral belief—honor codes merely mediate the speed and visibility of transmission.
(5) But if explicit moral belief is the ultimate driver, then any norm for which a genuinely convincing counter-argument exists should eventually collapse, regardless of current interest structures or honor configurations.
(6) There are convincing counter-arguments to many currently entrenched norms—say, to private ownership of productive land, or to the consumption of sentient animals—yet these norms show no sign of collapsing.
(7) So either no convincing counter-argument actually exists for those norms, or explicit moral belief is not the driver after all.
(8) Yet equally convincing counter-arguments to slavery and dueling could have been constructed using only premises available within those societies (universalizability, religious equality, consistency with aristocratic virtue); the arguments did not work then.
(9) Therefore, if explicit moral belief were the driver, slavery and dueling should never have collapsed, since the same arguments existed and failed; conversely, if they did collapse, then current norms with structurally similar arguments should also collapse.
(10) Hence moral revolutions are impossible unless they are already fully explained by non-moral factors, which makes the very idea of moral progress conceptually vacuous.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I am inclined to reject (4) and keep (1) through (3) intact, since it seems right that honor dynamics have a kind of causal independence from individual conviction. But I worry that giving up (4) forces me to treat honor shifts as brute social facts with no rational component whatsoever, and that price feels too high. Is there a subtler flaw—perhaps an equivocation in “explicit moral belief” between private conviction and public acknowledgment, a scope error in (5) about what “convincing” requires across different social structures, or a modal slide between “should collapse” and “will collapse”? I want to know which step is weakest and what retaining or abandoning it costs the overall picture of how norms actually change.
