I was arguing with a friend about whether "corruption" is a useful cross-cultural category, and she summarized a view she picked up from a comparative politics essay—something about clientelism, gift economies, and Western bias. Her version runs roughly like this: every international anti-corruption framework defines corruption as the diversion of public resources for private benefit, but that formula assumes a sharp boundary between state and society that is actually a recent Western invention, not a universal feature of governance. In many societies, she said, what outsiders call bribery is better understood as clientelism or gift-exchange—systems of reciprocal obligation that bind patrons and clients together, and that anthropological work on total social facts shows are governed by their own internal norms of reciprocity rather than by contract. The summary adds a further claim I hadn't heard before: that we should distinguish three regimes of obligation—contractual, reciprocal, and hierarchical patronage—where corruption only has a coherent meaning in the first, because only there is there a formal rule that can be violated for private gain; in the other two, labeling exchange as corrupt applies an alien market logic to relationships that are structurally non-contractual. From this she concluded that universal anti-corruption standards are not merely culturally insensitive but conceptually incoherent outside contractual regimes, and that reform efforts should instead ask only whether local exchange violates its own internal reciprocity norms.

The argument sounds sophisticated, but I worry it does several things at once—collapsing distinct anthropological concepts, overstating the historical uniqueness of the Western public/private split, and turning a descriptive taxonomy into a normative defense of local practices. I also can't tell whether the three-regime distinction is a real analytical tool or something she improvised. Can you audit this summary for fairness?
