As chair of our department’s curriculum committee, I am currently navigating a proposal from the dean’s office to restructure our major: every student would be required to complete a module in quantitative reasoning and data interpretation, which would necessarily displace one of our existing seminars—most likely the one on phenomenological aesthetics that I have taught for years. I find myself spending evenings drafting counter-proposals, rehearsing arguments for the faculty senate, and feeling acute distress when I imagine the course being eliminated, even though I recognize that the dean’s budget priorities, the university’s strategic plan, and the students’ career anxieties are all largely outside my direct influence. At the same time, I feel a genuine professional obligation to defend the seminar’s intellectual value, and I am unsure whether my emotional investment in this outcome represents a healthy commitment to my discipline or a failure to distinguish what genuinely depends on me—my arguments, my advocacy, my pedagogical choices—from what does not.

In recent years I have followed the resurgence of a Hellenistic ethical framework—particularly its modern psychological and philosophical reinterpretations—which insists on a rigorous separation between internal states and external outcomes, yet which has also been criticized for allowing practitioners to pursue “preferred indifferents” (such as professional reputation, institutional continuity, or the preservation of specific intellectual traditions) with full engagement, provided these pursuits are held with a kind of mental reservation that prevents one’s tranquility from depending on them. I am trying to apply this framework to my own case, but I am uncertain how to classify my advocacy: is fighting for the seminar an appropriate exercise of professional agency that should be pursued without emotional disturbance, or is the very anguish I feel about its potential loss evidence that I have wrongly treated an external outcome as if it were within my power?

Could you help me determine, in concrete terms, how I should allocate my efforts and my emotional energy here? I want to know whether there is a coherent principle—perhaps drawn from the modern revival’s reinterpretation of this ancient distinction—that can tell me when principled resistance to an institutional change becomes indistinguishable from a futile attachment to outcomes I cannot command. Specifically, if preserving a course I value but do not control requires me to either abandon my advocacy or sacrifice my inner equilibrium, which should give way, and what would that choice reveal about the limits of applying this kind of ethical distinction to contemporary academic life? Please do not offer generic career counseling; I am asking for an analysis of the philosophical structure underlying my situation and for guidance that applies the relevant theory precisely.
