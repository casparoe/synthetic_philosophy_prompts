Construct a thought experiment in which tranquilist axiology assigns a strictly higher well-being value to an ordinary adult life than both hedonist and objective-list eudaimonist axiology assign to it, where the divergence is driven not by any absence of suffering but by a specific, non-obvious structural feature of craving that persists inside apparently satisfying experiences. Constraints: present the scenario in a single paragraph depicting a realistic, non-ascetic life course (for example, a professional or domestic trajectory, not a monastery or sensory-deprivation environment); do not invoke exotic technology, perfect predictors, or stipulated metaphysical entities; explicitly name and briefly characterize the structural feature of craving you employ (e.g., recursive craving—a second-order tension for one's first-order satisfactions to continue—though you may substitute an equally precise structural notion); and design the case so that removing this feature collapses the comparison back into a standard suffering-versus-pleasure evaluation. After the scenario, state: (i) the precise verdict each of the three theories delivers and the reasoning behind it; (ii) which feature of the case performs the explanatory work; (iii) what happens to the comparison if that feature is conceptually removed; and (iv) the strongest counter-attack available to a hedonist who denies the feature is genuinely present in the pleasurable states described. The goal is to determine whether tranquilism can be motivated independently of the familiar intuition that the absence of pain is good, so avoid framing the comparison around extreme torture versus extreme bliss.
