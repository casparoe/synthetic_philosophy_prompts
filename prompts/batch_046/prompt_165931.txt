Hey — in my practical reason seminar yesterday a student dropped "rationality requires being irrational" as a mic-drop to end debate about Parfit's hitchhiker. They used it to claim that once she's rescued she has no reason to pay the driver, since rationality just meant making herself unable to pay. But that one-liner is smuggling in like four different theses. Can you pull it apart? I want a menu: (a) the boring instrumental claim that commitment devices sometimes help (platitude?), (b) the Parfit-style evidential claim that cultivating "irrational" dispostions can be rational from a planning standpoint, (c) the strong paradoxical claim that practical reason is genuinely self-undermining, (d) the game-theory notion of rational irrationality in repeated play being wrongly imported into a one-shot rescue, and (e) whatever false reading lets people say "see, she owes nothing." For each, flag whether it's trivial, defensible but contentious, or flat wrong. Then tell me which reading actually supports the student's move — that refusal to pay is justified — and which ones are just noise. Also, I'm toying with a variant where the hitchhiker can only bind 70% of her future self, not fully. Does that partial-commitment case break the slogan, or does it show the slogan was always about degrees? I feel like people invoke this phrase exactly to avoid thinking about partial binding, but maybe that's unfair.
