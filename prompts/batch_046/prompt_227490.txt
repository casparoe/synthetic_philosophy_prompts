As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of mind seriously, I am struck by Dennett's attack on the Cartesian theater—the notion of a single inner stage where consciousness happens—and by his alternative, the multiple drafts model, in which parallel processes generate narrative fragments without any central audience. I want a poem that makes this structural absence felt rather than merely asserted, not by naming the theories but by embodying them.

Please write a polished poem of no more than thirty lines, divided into five brief sections, each presenting a different draft of the same instantaneous perceptual event—say, a shadow falling across a laboratory workbench. There must be no framing narrator, no authoritative version, and no concluding synthesis; the sections should stand in direct, unresolved tension, with concrete details that contradict one another across drafts. Include a single recurring phrase—perhaps the reading is taken—that shifts its meaning and referent in each section, so that repetition itself mimics the illusion of continuous observation.

Crucially, treat the drafts not as competing stories told to an inner spectator, but as analogous to microscopic physical events whose uncoordinated interactions produce an emergent macroscopic state: no single draft carries the true experience, just as no single particle holds the temperature of a gas. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of mind recognizes the point—showing precisely why a central screen is unnecessary—without using the concepts as decorative name-drops. Let form do philosophical work: allow lineation, meter, or spacing to differ across sections so that the absence of a unified theater is visible on the page.

Keep the language self-contained and formal; do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts.
