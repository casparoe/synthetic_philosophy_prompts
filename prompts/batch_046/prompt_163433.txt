I am developing a conceptual framework for assessing theories of phenomenal consciousness, and I wish to begin with the standard of evaluation itself rather than with particular candidates. Write the desiderata that any adequate higher-order theory — one that explains why a mental state becomes conscious in virtue of being targeted by an appropriate higher-order representation — must satisfy. For each requirement, provide the motivating case or conceptual distinction behind it, and identify any pair of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable.

In particular, the theory must account for the standard contrast between conscious perception and unconscious processing, and it must preserve the distinction between cognitive access and phenomenal presence. Beyond these familiar demands, it must remain agnostic on whether the requisite higher-order representation is conceptual and linguistic, or whether it can be non-conceptual and pre-linguistic, since an adequate account should extend to pre-linguistic infants and to non-human animals with radically different cognitive architectures. It must also explain why certain forms of silent internal monitoring — where an organism tracks its own perceptual states for action-guidance in the absence of any phenomenal feel — fail to produce consciousness, while other forms of tracking succeed. Furthermore, it must accommodate apparent cases of phenomenal experience that outstrip reportability, without collapsing into a purely first-order representational theory.

Finally, indicate briefly how the principal existing approaches — the actualist higher-order thought view, the dispositionalist higher-order view, and the self-representationalist account — measure up against your list, specifying where each is forced to relinquish one of your requirements.

Keep the emphasis on the criteria and their tensions rather than on the scoring; I am particularly interested in whether the demand for non-conceptual applicability conflicts with the demand to explain misrepresentation without loss of phenomenology.
