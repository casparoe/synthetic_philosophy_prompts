I'm preparing a feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on how classical metaphysical puzzles about identity survive in contemporary systems engineering. For the piece, I want to stage a rigorous, self-contained seminar activity—designed for ten graduate students in philosophy or philosophy of science, in a single 75-minute session—that makes the conflict between Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles and the symmetric-spheres counterexample (the scenario associated with Max Black) visceral rather than textual. The participants should encounter the problem through structured action before it is named.

Please design a three-phase simulation. Phase one (observation and classification, 20 minutes): in pairs, students receive sealed envelopes containing identical relational reports describing two spheres in a featureless, rotationally symmetric space; each report lists mass, temperature, color, and spatial relations to fixed landmarks, but because the space is symmetric, the relational descriptions are interchangeable under rotation. Using only these reports and a simple point-based payoff scheme—efficiency points for using a single database entry, precision points for using two—each pair must commit to tracking either one object or two, writing a brief justification that cites only properties mentioned in the report.

Phase two (symmetry transformation, 20 minutes): a facilitator announces a 180-degree rotation of the reference frame; reports are swapped between pairs. Pairs must update their database decision without contradiction, facing a penalty for reversing their prior commitment without new qualitative evidence. The rules should be arranged so that at least some pairs experience coordination failure: they want to maintain consistency but find the rotated frame makes their earlier distinction unstable.

Phase three (structured deliberation, 25 minutes): the room splits into two roles—"structural ontologists," who deny bare numerical difference (haecceitistic distinction) and must defend a single entry for the space-station supply algorithm, and "particularists," who insist on two entries and must justify the resource cost of redundancy. Both sides must negotiate a final database schema under the constraint that the supply algorithm cannot tolerate ambiguity. The facilitator should impose a hard deadline.

Include clear rules, the card or envelope materials, the non-monetary payoff scheme, and a minute-by-minute timeline. Provide a debrief that maps each phase onto the philosophical distinction—qualitative versus relational discernibility, weak versus strong identity conditions, and whether spatial position can ground numerical difference—while being explicit about which feature of the metaphysical debate the simulation cannot reproduce (for instance, the actual ontological status of space-independent bare particularity) and must be supplied verbally by the facilitator. Also include an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run—such as students importing hidden qualitative differences or treating rotation as providing new evidence—and how to correct it in the moment. Keep all conceptual references to the core idea and its direct analogs in database or engineering design; do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Answer in American English.
