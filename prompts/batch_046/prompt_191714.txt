Design a 60-minute classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy-of-mind seminar of about 16 students that makes participants feel the absent-minded driver problem — specifically, the failure of an intact explicit intention to govern action within an overlearned procedural routine, rather than simple forgetting or ignorance. The activity must involve real movement or structured choice (not only discussion), with rules that generate an autopilot-like flow, a clearly stated deviation instruction, and a competing cognitive load that mimics divided attention. Specify the materials, the division of roles (for example, “drivers” and “observers”), the sequence of rounds including an overlearning phase, and any small stakes or payoffs — points, brief privileges, or social feedback — that do not involve real money. Provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto parts of the philosophical problem: the practice round as procedural memory, the deviation instruction as propositional intention, the missed turn as absent-mindedness rather than lack of knowledge, and any retrospective realisation as monitoring failure. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong on the first run — for instance, participants may become hyper-vigilant and defeat the autopilot effect — and explain how to correct for it. Also state frankly which feature of the problem the rules cannot reproduce (for example, the first-person phenomenology of suddenly discovering that one has driven well past the intended exit) and note that the facilitator must supply this through first-person narrative or testimony rather than through the game mechanics alone. To avoid a generic design, frame the failure not merely as an internal lapse of attention but as a mismatch between the environmental cue-structure of the route and the attentional demands of the deviation, so that participants experience how the absence of an appropriate external trigger can render an explicit intention invisible to the acting self.
