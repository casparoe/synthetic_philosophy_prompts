As a journalist covering science and ideas, I have noticed a recurring argumentative loop when researchers defend probabilistic forecasts. They first estimate an underlying physical tendency—say, that a particular extreme weather pattern has a specific likelihood—based on historical data. When that tendency is questioned, they defend it by citing the predictive accuracy of the model built upon it, as though the model's success offers independent support. Yet the predictions derive from the very tendency under scrutiny. The loop is not plain circular reasoning, since empirical observation and theoretical modelling are treated as separate pillars; nor is it a simple confusion between personal confidence and physical reality, because the researchers keep those categories distinct. Is there an established philosophical term for this precise pattern, in which an inferred physical propensity and its predictive applications become mutually reinforcing? If not, could one coin a term and delineate it from the view that rational belief ought to track known physical probabilities, as well as from ordinary bootstrapping?
