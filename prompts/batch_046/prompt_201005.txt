(2) i think the modern revival - especially the productivity-flavored version in popular books - basically inverts this by treating emotional resilience and career performance as the real goals, with stoic exercises as just mental-tech tools for getting there, which feels like a betrayal of the original idea. (3) at the same time, i think climate grief is rational and even virtuous, because mourning ecological loss is an internal judgment that honors value, not an attempt to control the climate itself.

(4) if i'm being consistent with (1), then caring about my legacy or whether i'm remembered well after death is always a mistake, since legacy depends entirely on other people's opinions and events long after i'm gone. (5) but here's the thing: the only reason i started this practice was because i was burnt out at work and wanted to handle stress better, and now my manager has actually commented on my improved attitude and i got assigned to a better project - so i recieved an external benefit that i wasn't supposed to care about, and part of me is pleased.

do these five commitments actually cohere, or am i just picking and chosing? specifically, is (5) flat hypocrisy that requires me to drop either my hard-line dichotomy or my claim that i practice sincerely? does (3) secretly smuggle in an attachment - if i truly think climate outcomes are indifferent, shouldn't i be indifferent to the loss too, rather than grieving it? and am i being unfair in (2), since modern stoicism might just apply the dichotomy to mental health (which is internal) rather than inverting it? please rank the tensions by severity, say which ones dissolve on closer inspection, and tell me the cheapest single revision that cleans up the most mess - even if that revision costs me something i like.
