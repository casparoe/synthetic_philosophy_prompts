I spent much of last night arguing with a close friend about whether historical writing can ever claim objectivity, and we kept returning to the same trio of positions: Ranke’s aspiration to reconstruct the past as it actually was, Carr’s insistence that every historian selects and weighs facts according to present social interests and therefore can never be neutral, and Hayden White’s stronger claim that historical narratives are irreducibly shaped by prefigurative tropes—romance, tragedy, satire, comedy—that organize empirical material before any causal explanation begins. My friend eventually settled on a moderate Carr-style constructivism: she concedes that archival documents constrain interpretation, but maintains that the inevitable acts of selection, causal weighting, and narrative framing make objectivity impossible at every meaningful scale of inquiry.

Please argue for a more specific and, I think, underexplored middle position: that Carr’s and White’s scepticisms actually operate at different scales and on different objects, and that this divergence allows us to recover a restricted but genuine form of Rankean objectivity—not at the level of grand synthetic narrative, but at the level of granular archival reconstruction. Specifically, defend the claim that White’s tropological analysis primarily targets macro-narrative emplotment, where the historian must indeed impose one of several deep structural forms onto large-scale processes such as revolutions or state formations; at that scale, Carr is correct that selection is inescapable and interest-laden. But then argue that Ranke’s actual methodological practice—his fixation on diplomatic correspondence, bureaucratic protocols, and the precise temporal adjacency of discrete decisions—operates closer to what White distinguishes as the chronicle or annals mode, a form of recording that minimizes synthetic causation and emplotment in favor of documentary fidelity and sequential accuracy. From this, derive the thesis that there exists a domain of historical practice—call it documentary microhistory—where Carr’s critique of ideological selection loses much of its force, because the task is not to explain a broad outcome but to establish the exact sequence, institutional pathway, and authorship of a discrete record. You should concede that this restricted objectivity cannot scale up to answer large-scale “why” questions about civilizational change, and concede that even archival work involves prior judgments about which files are relevant. Nevertheless, hold that within these boundaries a modified Rankean standard survives: not “how it actually was” in the grand synthetic sense, but “what the record actually contains” in an intersubjectively checkable, rigorously verifiable form. Make this argument without treating Carr or White as strawmen—acknowledge the real force of Carr’s point about present-centered selection and White’s demonstration that narrative form precedes empirical content at the synthetic level—while showing that neither critique fully dissolves the epistemic authority of granular, non-emplacement archival reconstruction. Do not offer bibliographies or reading lists, and do not appeal to any highly obscure theorists the model might not know; stay within these well-known conceptual resources and the niche scale-distinction I have introduced.
