You are a philosopher of categorization and perceptual cognition with expertise in conceptual analysis rather than empirical psychophysics. Before you begin, take a moment to brainstorm the distinct failure modes a theory of letterform identity might suffer: being overly rigid and missing valid variants, being overly permissive and absorbing near-neighbors, or conflating stylistic excellence with categorical membership.

Then write the full desiderata — the specification of what an adequate theory must deliver — for explaining what makes every instance of the letterform a count as an a across handwriting, typography, distortion, and artistic rendering. The theory must be evaluable by a reader without web access and must not depend on obscure specialist results. Your list should cover:

- The phenomena it must explain: extreme stylistic variation; instantaneous recognition of novel, never-previously-encountered styles; correct discrimination from near-neighbors (d, o, q, e, and others); and the status of cross-script analogues such as Greek alpha and Cyrillic a.
- The distinctions it must preserve without collapse: membership in the category versus being a good, typical, or canonical instance; essential properties of a-ness versus accidental stylistic features; and graded ambiguity versus outright misclassification.
- The conceptual constraints it must meet: consistency with broad findings in perceptual psychology without reducing identity to a fixed feature checklist; an account of adversarial letterforms — glyphs deliberately engineered to be maximally ambiguous between two categories — treating ambiguity either as a failure of the theory or as a structured feature of graded membership; and a stance on invariance under continuous topological deformation (connectivity-preserving stretching, bending, and warping), specifying whether such invariance is a non-negotiable requirement or merely a contingent feature of human perception.
