Analyze the 1936 French factory occupations and the Matignon Agreements through Simone Weil's critique of political parties. Treat the Socialist, Communist, and Radical coalition not merely as a government but as an instance of what Weil saw as the party-form: a collective body that demands total allegiance and suppresses individual moral judgment. Map the dynamics precisely—how Blum's Socialist Party relied on Communist support while trying to restrain the strikes, how the factory committees operated partly outside party control, and how the coalition's rapid dissolution reflected the tension between party discipline and spontaneous worker action. Then ask what this episode does to Weil's theory: does the failure of the Popular Front confirm her claim that parties are inherently unstable machines of collective passion, or does it reveal that she underestimated the strategic necessity of party coordination? In particular, consider whether the factory committees functioned as the kind of intermediary bodies she praised in her later political writings, and whether that distinction helps her theory survive the case or exposes an inconsistency between her hostility to parties and her defense of mediating institutions. Separate what is settled in Weil's political writings from what scholars genuinely dispute about her view of collective will, and do not present any contested interpretation as consensus.
