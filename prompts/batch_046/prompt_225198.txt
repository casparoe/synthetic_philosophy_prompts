I'm preparing a consistency check for a workshop on causation in public policy and want an audit of my commitments on omission before I present. Here is where I stand: (1) omissions are genuine causes only under what I call dependency-preservation—an omission is causally efficacious when it maintains an existing structural dependency that an action would have disrupted, not merely when absence correlates with an outcome; (2) I reject physicalist accounts of causation generally, taking it as fundamentally a relation of dependency and difference-making with no essential tie to energy transfer or spatiotemporal connection; (3) the counterfactual test—had the agent acted, the outcome would have differed—is necessary but not sufficient for omission-causation, since without dependency-preservation there is only correlation; (4) individual omissions in collective-action problems do not aggregate into collective causal responsibility, because dependency-preservation usually fails at the aggregate level, with no single inaction uniquely maintaining the dependency; (5) I nonetheless hold that a national government's failure to enact carbon pricing is a direct cause of specific climate damages, treating this as a paradigmatic case of omission-causation. Please examine which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to conflict, rank the tensions by severity, and for each real tension identify the least costly revision and what it sacrifices. Specifically, address whether (4) undermines (5) given that government is a collective body, and whether (2) destabilizes (1) when applied to institutional agents. End by saying which single revision would resolve the most problems without collapsing the view, and mark where the procedure for deciding omission-causation must hand the judgment to values rather than to the dependency-preservation test alone.
