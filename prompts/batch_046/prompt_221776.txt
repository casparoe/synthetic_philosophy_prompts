I serve on the board of a neighborhood cooperative workshop. Our bylaws state that any revision to the safety protocols must be approved by the current safety officer, a position created and defined by those very protocols. A member has proposed replacing the manual checkout system with automated access, which would render the safety-officer role obsolete. The board—whose members were appointed under the existing protocols—rejected the proposal on the grounds that protocol changes require the safety officer's approval. The proposer claims the reasoning is circular: the authority used to block the change derives entirely from the system being changed. The original charter was drafted by the founders, who named themselves the first officers. Is this merely institutional self-protection, or does it reveal a deeper structural problem about levels of authority that ought not to loop back onto themselves? I want an analysis of the logical structure here, not practical governance tips.
