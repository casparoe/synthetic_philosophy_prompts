I am designing the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on free will and the philosophy of mind, to be completed in 90 minutes with answers expected to reach approximately 900 words. I need an essay question that engages sphexishness—the form of mechanical rigidity illustrated by the sphex wasp's stereotyped burrow-checking loop—without allowing students to earn credit by merely summarizing the standard example and asserting that rigidity undermines free agency. The question must instead compel students to navigate a fine-grained conceptual distinction that is often overlooked in introductory treatments: the gap between first-order behavioral flexibility and second-order openness in an agent's revision architecture.

Please construct the following:

1. The exam question. Introduce a hypothetical agent—label it a 'second-order sphex'—that exhibits first-order flexibility (it can revise its immediate behavioral rules in response to reasons or environmental feedback) yet is structurally closed at the meta-level (it cannot alter the governing rule that specifies when, whether, and by what criteria such revisions are permitted). Ask students to explain why this configuration threatens to collapse the familiar distinction between determinism accompanied by flexibility and genuine sphexishness, and to defend or reject the claim that free will requires not merely first-order adaptability but second-order openness.

2. A model answer of about 700 words. The answer should rigorously distinguish (a) causal determinism of particular actions, (b) counterfactual insensitivity of a specific behavioral loop, and (c) structural closure of the agent's rule-revision architecture. It should also evaluate whether second-order closure amounts to an extreme case of determinism or represents a categorically distinct defect with respect to autonomy.

3. A marking scheme totaling 20 marks, organized around five must-hit conceptual moves. For each move, specify the point value, the precise analytical contribution required, and a one-line description of the typical student failure (for example, conflating first-order flexibility with full autonomy, or treating loop-insensitivity and determinism as interchangeable).

The prompt should be formal and examination-appropriate. Do not include requests for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Do not invoke obscure theoretical frameworks that a well-prepared student could not be expected to command without external research; embed the 'second-order sphex' construct directly in the question so that engagement depends only on standard conceptual resources.
