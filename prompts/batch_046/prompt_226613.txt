Okay so I was trying to think through why Pascal's mugging feels wrong and I built this argument that kind of proves too much in both directions. Here:

(1) The usual reply is that rational agents should use a probability floor—say 10^-50—below which possibilities get rounded to zero for decision-making.

(2) But that truncation can't be domain-specific. If you round away the mugger's 10^-40 chance of infinite payoff, you also round away the prior that the next anomolous signal at a collider comes from a hidden sector with prior around 10^-60.

(3) Once rounded away, no amount of evidence can revive that hypothesis inside your decision framework, so you'd miss a real discovery that starts implausibly low.

(4) So the floor saves you from the mugger only by breaking open ended scientific updating.

(5) If instead you stay pure bayesian with no floor, then any nonzero chance of infinite utility dominates every finite cost, and you should pay.

(6) That means either we accept the mugging, or we accept that rational belief is systematically truncated in a way that blocks discovery.

(7) Both feel wrong: I don't want to hand over my wallet, but I don't want to tell physicists to ignore weird data just because the prior was tiny.

Where's the error? Is (3) wrong because belief-updating and decision-weights can come apart? Is there a hidden slide in (2) from practical stakes to epistemic priors? Or is (5) missing something about bounded utility?

I'd rather keep the idea that science needs long-tail openness, but I'm okay with giving up (5) if that's what it takes. Just tell me which premise is the weak link and what I'm actually paying if I reject it.
