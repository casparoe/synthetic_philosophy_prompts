A colleague sketched this for me and I cannot locate the escape route. (1) Backward induction in the centipede game requires only common knowledge of rationality and a finite sequence of decision nodes; it does not depend on the payoff structure being monetary or on the interaction being literally a game. (2) Any sequential commitment with discrete exit points — a multi-stage contract containing renegotiation clauses, a research partnership with periodic evaluation, or even a promise that one knows one might later reconsider — therefore has the same formal structure: at each node, either party can unilaterally defect and collect an intermediate payoff. (3) So if backward induction is sound, rational agents should anticipate full unraveling in all such commitments and should refuse to enter them at the initial node. (4) But rational agents routinely form long-term collaborations, sign complex contracts, and treat promises as genuinely binding rather than as predictable errors. (5) The usual reply is that real-world interactions lack the perfect epistemic transparency — common knowledge of rationality at every node — that drives the induction. (6) However, if transparency fails in contracts and promises, it also fails in the laboratory centipede game, where players are no more transparent to each other than contracting parties; the paradox then dissolves not by being solved but by denying a premise that was never independently justified. (7) Hence either backward induction applies consistently and rationality forbids all intertemporal cooperation, or it applies only in artificially transparent settings and the centipede result is an artifact of an unrealistic assumption. I would prefer to preserve (1) and (4). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
