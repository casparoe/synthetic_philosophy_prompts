I am a high school student in an advanced ethics seminar, and after a long discussion with friends about whether descendants should feel responsible for historical injustices, I find that most public debates collapse into slogans—either “we must never forget” or “I didn’t do it”—without clarifying what kind of responsibility is even at stake. I want to move past this and understand the actual conceptual landscape, particularly in dialogue with Karl Jaspers’s well-known distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, but I am especially interested in how these categories behave when we ask whether guilt can be inherited across generations after the original perpetrators are dead.

Could you construct a sorting taxonomy—structured as a decision tree with clearly labeled branch nodes and named leaf positions—for the debate over whether a nation can bear guilt across generations? The tree should begin with a concrete commitment that anyone could answer without already knowing the philosophical labels: do you accept that moral responsibility requires direct personal agency, or can it be mediated through institutional continuity, cultural transmission, and structural benefit? From there, please branch on whether the state counts as a persisting moral agent, whether guilt can survive the death of individuals through institutional identity rather than personal psychology, and whether descendants inherit something properly called “guilt” rather than “debt,” “shame,” or “complicity.”

Please include these specific positions or combinations as identifiable leaves: (1) strict individualism—no collective guilt ever; (2) a Jaspers-style view that political guilt is inheritable through citizenship in a legally continuous state; (3) a Jaspers-style view that metaphysical guilt is inheritable through shared human solidarity regardless of citizenship; (4) a “structural-benefit” view that rejects guilt-language altogether but affirms reparative obligations grounded in unjust enrichment; (5) a “cultural-memory” view that treats inherited guilt as a socially constructed identity-marker rather than an objective moral fact; and (6) an “intergenerational agent” view that treats the nation as a diachronic person genuinely capable of moral properties across time. For each leaf, indicate the specific combination of branch answers that leads there. Crucially, map out any logically possible combinations that your tree allows but that appear unoccupied—such as accepting institutional continuity while denying every form of inherited obligation—and briefly diagnose whether each gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Phrase every branch node as a specific case or claim, for example: “If a government that committed atrocities is legally the same entity fifty years later, do its current citizens share in its political guilt?” This should allow someone to navigate the tree without recognizing the philosophical name attached to the destination. Where two positions genuinely cannot be separated by the questions you have asked, flag them explicitly rather than forcing a false distinction. Finally, please separate what is broadly settled in philosophical discussion—such as the difference between criminal liability and collective moral sentiment—from what remains genuinely contested among people who study the topic, especially regarding whether metaphysical guilt can be meaningfully transmitted and whether treating the nation as a moral agent is coherent. Do not present any one contested view, particularly the inheritance of metaphysical guilt or the nation-as-agent thesis, as if it were the consensus.
