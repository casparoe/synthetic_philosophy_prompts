I am a retired physicist who, since leaving my laboratory position, has pursued philosophy of mind with particular attention to the clinical literature on hemispheric disconnection. Next month I present to an interdisciplinary seminar—cognitive neuroscientists, clinical neurologists, and philosophers of science—my thesis that patients who have undergone complete commissurotomy should not be interpreted as hosting two discrete streams of consciousness, nor as retaining a fully unified consciousness, but rather as constituting a single conscious system whose hemispheric modules have lost temporal synchronization. I argue that the corpus callosum operates not merely as an interhemispheric information conduit but as a binding protocol that maintains a unified temporal integration window; when it is divided, the result is not a bifurcated self but one self experiencing a fractured binding interval, analogous to distributed computing nodes that have lost clock synchronization while remaining nodes of a single network. The audience is empirically rigorous, skeptical of metaphysical claims that outrun clinical observation, and includes committed defenders of global workspace theory who will regard my framing as an unnecessary multiplication of entities. I need a speaker-preparation sheet for the Q&A: the objections I will actually receive, ordered by how likely they are and how severely they could damage my position, each phrased in the vocabulary those interlocutors will use. For each, specify what I should concede without surrender, what I must hold firm on, a reply concise enough for a two-minute exchange, and the specific vulnerability in that reply that I should be ready to defend rather than conceal. Identify the one question I most hope is not asked, and explain precisely why it undermines the core of my argument. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I will have no access to external sources during the presentation.
