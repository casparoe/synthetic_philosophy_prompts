Here's an argument I sketched for a seminar on comparative history, and now I can't tell whether it's a reductio of Mill's methods or a proof that macro-comparative explanation is impossible in principle. (1) A contrastive historical explanation of the form "why P rather than Q?" is well-formed only if P and Q belong to a comparison class in which all causally relevant background conditions are shared except the explanatory factor F being tested. (2) The Needham question—why sustained industrial modernity emerged in Britain rather than in Qing China—presupposes that Britain and China form just such a class. (3) But they differ in coal endowment, state centralization, colonial access, linguistic standardization, legal tradition, and dozens of other variables that are plausibly causally relevant; none of these can be held constant across the comparison. (4) Therefore the Britain-China pairing is not well-posed, and the question should be dissolved into separate, non-contrastive causal narratives for each case. (5) However, if (1) is applied consistently, then nearly every macro-historical contrast—why the Roman Republic collapsed and not the Han dynasty, why mechanized industry did not emerge in the Islamic Golden Age—fails the same test, since historical cases are never matched on background conditions. (6) So (1) entails that macro-comparative history is impossible in principle. (7) That conclusion is absurd; the field produces genuine explanatory knowledge. (8) Hence (1) must be false. But (1) is just a direct application of Mill's method of difference, which comparative historians invoke precisely to justify selecting Britain and China as paired cases. If I reject (1), what distinguishes a meaningful "why Britain and not China?" from an arbitrary "why this valley and not that valley?" I want to keep (4)—there is something genuinely suspicious about the Britain-China pairing—without accepting (6). Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost?
