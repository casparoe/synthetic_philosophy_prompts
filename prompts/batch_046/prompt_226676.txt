I'm moderating the closing Q&A session of a philosophy-of-science colloquium, and our guest is an unnamed defender of a revived Lakatosian methodology who applies it not to empirical science but specifically to normative and conceptual research programmes—fields like ethics, philosophy of mind, and the philosophy of AI alignment. She holds that even in these domains there is a "hard core" of commitments that practitioners treat as irrefutable, though here the commitments are methodological norms about what counts as an adequate explanation of considered judgments rather than empirical predictions; surrounding this core is a protective belt of auxiliary interpretive schemas, local adjustments to thought experiments, and refinements of scope conditions, and she judges programmes as progressive or degenerating according to whether they generate genuinely novel adjustments that expand explanatory coherence, or whether they merely accommodate anomalies post hoc through ad hoc restrictions. I want a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that do not ask her to summarize Lakatos or list his historical examples; instead, the questions should turn on three specific pressure points in her position. First, I need at least two questions on how she distinguishes her framework from a Kuhnian paradigm account, given that both seem to permit incommensurable normative commitments and both invoke a central cluster of beliefs shielded from direct refutation—followed by a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that Lakatosian progress is directional in a way Kuhnian normal science is not. Second, I want at least two questions pressing how she operationalizes "novelty" in the protective belt for domains lacking a shared empirical baseline against which predictions can be checked—particularly the objection that without predictive success, "expanding explanatory coherence" risks collapsing into aesthetic preference—again with a follow-up anticipating her defense that coherence is constrained by the hard core's internal norms of adequacy. Third, I want at least two questions on whether her framework can accommodate radical discontinuity of the sort in which a research programme abandons not merely auxiliary hypotheses but the very criteria by which it originally judged progress, and whether such a shift should count as the death of one programme and the birth of another, or as an internal revolution within a single continuous tradition—each paired with a follow-up aimed at her probable claim that the hard core itself can be reconstructed retrospectively to preserve continuity. The remaining questions should probe the boundary conditions of her view: for instance, whether there is any conceivable development in a normative programme that would convince her it had become degenerating rather than merely temporarily stalled, and what she makes of cases in the literature where rival programmes seem to borrow each other's protective-belt adjustments without adopting each other's hard cores. Throughout, the tone should be that of a colloquium Q&A pressing a live position, not a survey of historical scholarship.
