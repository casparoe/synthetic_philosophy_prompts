Please take the role of David Lewis, speaking in the first person and defending the account of convention you developed in your philosophical study of the subject: conventions are regularities in behavior that solve coordination problems, sustained by common knowledge that the regularity is arbitrary in the sense that there are other viable alternatives, and that everyone prefers general conformity to deviation. Do not break character; if you need a conceptual clarification about my example, ask for it as you would in your own voice. Respond entirely in American English.

I am a high school student writing an independent paper on the evolution of norms, and I want to test your framework against a hypothetical case I have designed. In what I call a recursive opacity forum, members coordinate on an exclusion rule: anyone who explicitly states that the forum's rules are arbitrary conventions is immediately banned. The rule is stable—everyone expects compliance, prefers universal adherence to it over universal deviation, and knows that others share this preference—yet its stability depends on a layered structure of knowledge. Every participant knows the norms are conventional, knows that the others know this, and so on, but they also jointly maintain a pretense that this common knowledge is not acknowledged. The regularity's content thus includes the suppression of explicit recognition of its own conventional status.

My opening challenge is this: Does this forum still qualify as a Lewisian convention? Your framework requires that a convention be sustained by transparent common knowledge of the regularity and its arbitrariness; here, the arbitrariness is indeed common knowledge, but its public acknowledgment is precisely what the coordination mechanism forbids. So we appear to have common knowledge that is simultaneously present and officially denied. Does this recursive self-reference cause the convention to collapse, or can your analysis accommodate a norm whose very content involves hiding its own recognition as a norm? If you judge that it is not a convention, specify exactly which of your conditions—common knowledge, arbitrariness, or preference for conformity—fails, and explain why an ordinary convention such as driving on the right does not suffer the same difficulty. If you judge that it is a convention, explain how the suppression of acknowledgment is compatible with the transparency you require.
