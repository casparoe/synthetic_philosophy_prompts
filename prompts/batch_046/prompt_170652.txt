I spent the better part of yesterday evening arguing with a close friend about whether destroying a particular ecosystem—say, draining a wetland to plant timber—actually improves the net welfare of that system by reducing the number of sentient lives lived in suffering. My friend kept sliding between two different objections: one about the direct harm done to the animals currently displaced or killed, and another about the intrinsic value of ecological stability regardless of welfare outcomes. I want to map the conceptual space precisely, not to settle the empirical biology but to see where our normative commitments actually diverge, so that someone can place themselves on the map without already knowing which camp they belong to.

Please construct a taxonomy—structured as a branching decision tree with labeled internal nodes—of the positions one can take on whether habitat destruction reduces wild-animal suffering and improves the net welfare of ecosystems. Start from the root commitment that wild animal welfare is relevant to evaluating habitat change at all. From there, branch by concrete diagnostic questions that do not ask for labels: (1) Is welfare assessed per individual, per unit of biomass, or at the population/replacement level—specifically, does one adopt a "standing-suffering-to-reproductive-subsidy" framing in which the welfare cost of maintaining a stable population is weighed against the welfare of the offspring that population continuously generates? (2) Do counterfactual animals—those who never come to exist because the habitat is destroyed—enter the calculus, and if so are they treated as welfare-neutral omissions or as negative entries (prevented suffering)? (3) Does ecological stability or "naturalness" carry normative weight independent of its welfare effects on sentient individuals? (4) Does our epistemic uncertainty about trophic cascades provide a decisive practical reason not to destroy habitats, or only a defeasible caution? (5) When comparing an intact predator-rich ecosystem to a destroyed or simplified one, is the correct comparison based on total suffering summed across all lives, average suffering per capita, or suffering weighted by ecological role?

Each leaf should be either a named position in the philosophical or ecological-ethics literature (if widely recognized) or marked "unnamed," followed by one sentence stating its most awkward commitment—for instance, that it must treat an empty clear-cut as welfare-superior to a flourishing but suffering-dense wetland, or that it must deny any welfare significance to the extinction of a high-suffering, high-reproduction species. Where the tree leaves a logical combination unoccupied, note briefly whether that gap is likely incoherent (e.g., counting counterfactual welfare while denying that populations are welfare-substrates), merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves that share answers to all five branches and therefore cannot be separated by this instrument. The taxonomy should be formal, self-contained, and usable by someone reasoning only from common knowledge in animal ethics and ecology; include no bibliographies, no citations, and no references to obscure theoretical frameworks that require specialist retrieval.
