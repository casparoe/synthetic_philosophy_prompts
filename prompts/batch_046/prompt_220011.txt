I work in mechanism design, revealed-preference economics, and social choice — not in moral philosophy. Restate Adam Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator, particularly the thesis that moral judgment arises through an imaginative exchange of situations with an internalized observer whose approval regulates our passions, using only my vocabulary: preference revelation, incentive compatibility, mechanism design, social welfare aggregation, strategic interaction, and information asymmetry. Map each component of Smith's view — direct sympathy, propriety, merit and demerit, and the spectator's supposed neutrality — onto an economic concept, and flag where the mapping requires distorting either Smith or economics. Then provide the residue report: identify the specific feature of sympathy (for instance, the non-strategic, pre-reflective affective resonance that allows one to enter another's situation before any preference ordering is formed) that either collapses into a standard economic primitive or lacks any counterpart in mechanism design. Assess whether that residue indicates a genuine explanatory gap in economic vocabulary or merely shows that Smith's rhetoric carries decorative weight that a preference-aggregation model could replace without loss. Finally, state explicitly what would change your mind about that assessment: specify the argument, empirical observation, or conceptual distinction that would convince you that the untranslated remainder is doing real philosophical work rather than being an artifact of a failed translation.
