I have been drafting notes for an essay—following a discussion with a colleague who studies institutional economics—proposing a somewhat unorthodox reading of Machiavelli's treatment of fortune and virtù. Rather than interpreting fortuna as mere external contingency or misfortune, I want to argue that it operates in his thought as structured institutional resistance: an accumulated sedimentation of prior political arrangements that constrains present action in ways analogous to path dependence. Virtù, on this account, is not classical moral virtue but a form of adaptive structural intelligence—the capacity to read the specific viscosity of inherited arrangements and to redirect their momentum rather than dominate them outright.

I am particularly drawn to Machiavelli's well-known image of fortune as a river that floods destructively unless dykes and banks are prepared in advance, though I extend this by proposing a recursive dimension: the banks themselves are not natural features but artefacts of earlier exercises of virtù, meaning that fortuna is partly the fossilised residue of past success. This suggests that Machiavelli offers not only a psychology of leadership but a proto-sociological account of how institutions harden and how exceptional agents must navigate that hardening.

I should welcome your critical comments on whether this interpretation does violence to Machiavelli's actual arguments, whether importing the language of path dependence is illuminating or anachronistic, and whether the recursive element I have described is genuinely present in his work or an imposition of my own framework. Please reply in British English, and feel free to suggest where the argument might be strengthened or where it collapses into incoherence.
