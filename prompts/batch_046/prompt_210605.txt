I'm drafting my position for a graduate bioethics seminar on the hedonistic imperative, and I want a consistency check on the package I've settled on before I present it. Here are my commitments: (a) I endorse a strong hedonistic imperative—if safe biotechnology can eliminate suffering across sentient beings, we are morally required to do so, and withholding it is comparable to refusing to cure a treatable epidemic; (b) I understand suffering strictly phenomenologically, as negative valence or affective aversion, not functionally, which means a being that fails to achieve goals but experiences no bad feeling is not suffering; (c) I believe that moral grief, empathic anguish, and the affective shadow cast by deep joy are not merely useful instruments but partially constitutive of moral agency, genuine attachment, and the kind of love that involves vulnerability; (d) I defend what I call "affective topology preservation"—the engineering of emotional architectures so that the functional and relational structures of grief, care, and commitment remain intact, while all negative valence is decoupled and removed, producing analogues without aversion; (e) I hold that beings permanently designed to lack any negative valence, yet retaining these functional structures, have full moral status and can genuinely participate in ethical community; and (f) I reject the contrast argument that well-being requires suffering as a background condition, treating the absence of suffering instead as analogous to the absence of infection—unalloyed gain.

At first glance, (a) and (c) seem to clash, since abolishing suffering appears to require eliminating the very grief I claim is constitutive, though (d) is meant to resolve this. Meanwhile, (b) and (d) may pull apart: if suffering just is negative valence, then removing valence from grief transforms it into something that may no longer be grief at all, undermining (c)'s claim about what is preserved. There also seems to be tension between (c) and (e): if suffering is constitutive of moral agency, can beings that never suffer truly possess the agency that (e) assumes they retain? Yet (f) might only be in tension with (c) if one thinks well-being needs suffering, which I deny—so that conflict may dissolve on inspection. Please audit this set: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which tensions are only apparent, and rank the real ones by severity. For each serious tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and exactly what I would have to give up—whether it's the phenomenological definition, the preservation thesis, the status claim, or the strong imperative itself—and tell me which single change would clean up the most inconsistencies at the least cost to the overall view.
