In our philosophy of religion course we have been examining Freud's comparison between religious ritual and obsessive-compulsive neurosis, centered on the claim that both rest on a belief in the direct causal efficacy of mental acts over external events. A standard analogy in the literature treats this as the same underlying mechanism operating at individual versus collective scale: the neurotic performs private rituals to ward off anxiety, just as a religious community performs public ones to secure collective well-being. Audit this analogy systematically: map the correspondences between private compulsion and collective worship; identify precisely where the mapping breaks down with respect to social coordination, voluntary participation, and the distribution of guilt; and assess whether the comparison tacitly imports a clinical norm of rationality that conflicts with Freud's own naturalistic account of belief formation. Finally, evaluate whether replacing this clinical analogy with an evolutionary one—treating religious ritual as a costly signaling device for credible group commitment rather than as a symptom—would escape the disanalogies you have identified, or whether it merely substitutes them with different ones.
