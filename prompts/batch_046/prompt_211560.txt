I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been studying philosophy of science and cosmology systematically rather than casually. I have developed a cluster of views about the so-called fine-tuning problem, but I am unsure whether they form a stable package or whether I am selectively applying principles. Before I present these ideas to a local philosophy discussion group, I would like a consistency audit.

My commitments are as follows:
(1) The empirical premise of fine-tuning strikes me as sound. The Hoyle state of carbon-12 near 7.65 MeV, the cosmological constant's extreme suppression relative to quantum-field predictions, and the narrow range for the strong nuclear force all appear necessary for stellar nucleosynthesis and long-lived stars; modest deviations seem to preclude complex chemistry.
(2) I regard the multiverse landscape—particularly the combination of eternal inflation with a string-theoretic landscape of vacuum states—as a more satisfactory explanation than any design hypothesis, because it substitutes a mechanistic account for a teleological one.
(3) At the same time, I hold a strict criterion for scientific legitimacy: a hypothesis should be empirically constrainable, at least in principle, within our causal patch. Explanations that invoke domains permanently beyond our observational horizon strike me as metaphysical rather than properly scientific.
(4) I am deeply skeptical of the probabilistic framing that calls our constants "improbable." Without a justified measure or prior over physically possible parameter spaces, I think such talk is conceptually confused.
(5) Despite (3) and (4), I still favor the multiverse explanation because I believe it generates internal consistency conditions—predictions about the cosmological constant's order of magnitude that are checkable within our own universe—even if other bubbles remain unobservable.
(6) I am also inclined to apply a self-sampling assumption in cosmological reasoning: I treat our presence in a life-permitting region as unremarkable if the multiverse is vast, because we could only observe from such a region. Yet I reject the same self-sampling reasoning in epistemic puzzles such as the Sleeping Beauty problem, where I think it yields clearly erroneous conclusions.

Please assess whether these hang together. Specifically: does my endorsement of (2) violate (3) in a way that undermines my rejection of design? Does my acceptance of (1) together with (4) collapse into incoherence, or can "fine-tuning" be read as pure parameter sensitivity rather than statistical rarity? Is there a hidden tension between (4) and (6) regarding implicit priors? And is my differential treatment of self-sampling in (6) defensible or ad hoc? Rank the tensions by severity, indicate the least costly revision for each, and write your answer in American English. I am not seeking bibliographic references or a reading list—only conceptual clarification.
