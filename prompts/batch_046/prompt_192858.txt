I'm delivering the retirement toast at a dinner honoring Professor Elena Voss, who taught early modern philosophy for thirty-five years. Roughly half the guests are philosophers—epistemologists and historians of philosophy—who know her published arguments; the other half are family, former students from other disciplines, and university administrators who do not. The toast must be structured by a specific, narrow interpretation of Descartes's cogito that she defended throughout her career: she followed Lichtenberg in holding that the only legitimate conclusion is the bare occurrence of thinking, best captured in an impersonal formulation such as it thinks, and she treated every stronger inference—to a substantive ego, to a persisting thinking substance, or to a fully transparent self—as an illegitimate metaphysical addition smuggled into an apparently modest epistemic claim. Because she rejected all of those stronger conclusions, the speech must not attribute them to her; it must celebrate her intellectual life precisely through this refusal, framing it as rigorous honesty rather than as skepticism or nihilism. Write a five-minute toast of about 650 words that uses the restricted scope of the cogito's proof as its spine: what she was willing to claim, what she steadfastly refused to claim, and why that boundary mattered for how she taught and lived. Include one moment precise enough that the philosophers in the room will recognize the exact inference-block she maintained, and one analogical moment accessible enough that the family can see how her seminar-room restraint mirrored her conduct outside it. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or suggestions for further reading.
