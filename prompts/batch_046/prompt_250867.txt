As a novelist whose current project turns on catastrophic memory revision and the persistence of a protagonist across successive bodies, I have tried to set down the philosophical commitments that govern my characters' ontological status, and I need to know whether they form a coherent profile.

First, I treat the self not as a substance but as a center of narrative gravity—an abstract locus around which intentions, regrets, and perceptual reports organize themselves, acquiring explanatory and causal weight solely through the coherence of the biography told about them. Second, I hold that this coherence is constitutive rather than descriptive: where the life-story is disrupted—by amnesia, editorial intervention, or transplantation into a different body—the self either transforms or dissolves, regardless of underlying physical or causal continuity. Third, I maintain that first-person authority is immediate, irreducible, and non-derivative; the subject retains a privileged, non-observational access to her own mental states that no external biographer, however informed, can legitimately override. Fourth, I reject both bodily continuity and standard psychological-continuity conditions for personal identity; narrative integration is my sole criterion, and in my fiction I present cross-body persistence as metaphysically serious whenever the narrative thread—stylistic habits, patterns of preference, unresolved guilt—survives intact. Fifth, I deny that infants prior to the acquisition of narrative language, non-human animals, and artificial systems lacking autobiographical modeling possess selves at all, yet I assign them full and equal moral standing with narrative selves. Sixth, I have begun to work with what I call focalization debt: the coherence of the first-person pronoun is sustained only by syntactically suppressing alternative perspectives—the body's own non-narrative sensations, counterfactual versions of the protagonist, competing accounts from other characters—that are excluded from the gravitational field of the I.

Do these commitments hang together? I want a specific audit. Rank the genuine tensions by severity; for each, identify the cheapest revision and what it costs. In particular, does my constitutive narrative view undermine the irreducibility of first-person authority, or can that authority be reconceived as an internal feature of the narrative? Is my rejection of psychological continuity genuinely compatible with accepting cross-body persistence through narrative thread, or am I concealing a disguised form of the continuity I reject? Does my denial of selfhood to non-narrative beings conflict with my assignment of equal moral standing, or is that tension only apparent? And does focalization debt compromise the autonomy implied by first-person authority, or does it deepen it?

Please also identify which conflicts dissolve on closer inspection, and tell me which single change—revising or abandoning one commitment—would most improve overall coherence. Do not conclude with a non-committal balance; deliver a verdict on whether the profile is salvageable without revision, and if not, state precisely what must give.
