I am developing a conceptual taxonomy for democratic theory that distinguishes first-order constitutional commitments—substantive rights or institutional arrangements that are entrenched—from second-order meta-commitments, which are rules specifying how those entrenchments themselves may be revised or dissolved, including what I call recursive precommitment structures in which a constitution binds not only future policy but also the amendment mechanisms available to future generations. For this exercise, I want you to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps that have argued past each other on Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment.

The first camp, which I will call the Rational Continuity Theorists, maintains that constitutional self-binding is a legitimate extension of democratic autonomy across time: a constituent power rationally limits its successors to prevent short-term majoritarian erosion of fundamental values, and the legitimacy of this binding is preserved so long as structured amendment procedures remain available, including meta-commitment clauses that allow future generations to alter the rules of alteration without repudiating the constitutional order itself. The second camp, the Present Sovereignty Critics, contends that any binding of future democratic majorities by past ones constitutes an illegitimate dead-hand problem, and that meta-commitment clauses do not solve this but merely displace it—because if the meta-rules are themselves entrenched, they still subordinate present will to past design, and if they are not entrenched, then the original precommitment has been effectively voided.

Please produce a document both sides could sign. Begin with a numbered section of agreed claims about the formal structure of amendment rules, the difference between procedural and substantive entrenchment, and the conditions under which a democratic polity can dissolve a prior binding without breaking constitutional continuity. Follow this with a disagreements table stating, in wording each camp would accept for its own position, the precise points of divergence—particularly on whether recursive precommitment resolves the dead-hand objection or relocates it to a higher-order level. Then identify at least two cruxes, such as whether democratic legitimacy requires contemporaneous authorization for every binding constraint or can be satisfied by rational continuity plus accessible exit mechanisms, and whether the presence of a sunset clause within an otherwise unamendable provision strengthens or undermines the case for precommitment. For each crux, specify a concrete constitutional design or hypothetical institutional case that both sides agree is relevant, and record in advance what each camp would predict about its normative or functional outcome. The statement should avoid requests for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and should be formulated with sufficient conceptual precision that a model without web access could evaluate the arguments on their merits.
