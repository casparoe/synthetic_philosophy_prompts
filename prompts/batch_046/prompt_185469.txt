I am designing a single fifty-minute tutorial for first-year philosophy students on Bernard Williams's integrity objection and the one-thought-too-many case. Because they have already seen the Jim-and-the-Indians scenario in lecture, that example must not open the session. Plan the sequence: the dependency order of the conceptual pieces—beginning with agent-relative versus agent-neutral reasons, then ground projects—before introducing the carrying scenario. The scenario itself should involve a deep, identity-constituting commitment but must avoid any life-or-death rescue framing; specify what features it needs and what it must lack. Map the predictable misconceptions, particularly the misreading that Williams is only defending sentimental attachment or urging agents to follow their hearts, rather than advancing a structural claim about practical reasoning; for each, state the precise point in the sequence where it should be defused. Provide one end-of-session check question that distinguishes grasping the structural claim about reasons from merely sympathizing with the agent's choice. Finally, identify exactly which part of the standard utilitarian response—the decision-procedure versus criterion-of-rightness distinction—should be explicitly withheld at this level, and explain why including it would defeat the pedagogical purpose.
