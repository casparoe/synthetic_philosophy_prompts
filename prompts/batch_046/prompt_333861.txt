I spent the better part of last evening arguing with a close friend—who is a historian of the American South and deeply skeptical of abstraction—about whether Dunkirk and Masada really belong in the same conceptual family as the Lost Cause of the Confederacy and the Kosovo Field myth. We kept circling the same problem: every comparative study either flattens the religious-secular distinction or treats “lost cause” as a single emotional register rather than a structured narrative form. I believe I have finally articulated a taxonomy that holds the distinctions steady, but I need to be ready for a room that will not grant me the framing for free.

Next Thursday I present a twenty-minute paper to a joint workshop of the History Department and the Program in Political Theory. The audience is mostly empirical historians—two specialists in American Civil War memory, one in Balkan nationalism, and a military historian who writes on Dunkirk—plus two political theorists who study collective identity but are openly suspicious of structuralist taxonomies. My thesis is that we should replace the binary of “defeat myth” versus “foundational victory” with a three-axis taxonomy of sacrificial defeat narratives: temporal orientation (past redemption versus future renewal), agent attribution (divine election versus human covenant), and spatial logic (enclosure versus dispersal). On this grid, the Confederacy’s Lost Cause and the Kosovo Myth both sit in the enclosure-redemption quadrant but diverge sharply on agent attribution—divine election for Kosovo, human covenant for the Lost Cause—while Dunkirk and Masada cluster together in the dispersal-renewal quadrant despite their surface differences, which means Dunkirk is structurally closer to Masada than to the Confederacy. I am not claiming the taxonomy explains everything; I am claiming it explains why existing comparisons feel intuitively wrong when they group Dunkirk with the Lost Cause purely because both involve military failure.
