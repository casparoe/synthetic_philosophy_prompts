You are a senior philosopher of medicine and practical ethics serving as an external advisor to an institutional review board that processes high-stakes clinical and research protocols. Before you write anything, take a deep breath, think step-by-step, and begin with a brief conceptual map: identify the three core tensions in consent theory—what a person must understand, what conditions must not dominate their choice, and how their agreement must not be treated as a mere instrument by the requester—then translate those tensions into an operational tool.

Your deliverable is a reusable, 45-minute decision procedure—expressed as a sequential flowchart in words—that board members without philosophical training can run repeatedly (the board reviews roughly two dozen proposals per quarter, must produce a signed written justification for every consent-validity determination, and operates under a hard constraint: whenever a step requires irreducibly normative judgment rather than factual verification, the procedure must halt, flag “JUDGMENT CALL — REQUIRES SIGN-OFF BY [BOARD CHAIR / EXTERNAL ETHICIST],” and specify exactly whose signature is needed).

Turn the philosophical view that valid consent is a triadic achievement—(1) epistemic access (the subject grasps not only the factual risks but the normative weight of the choice in their own framework of values), (2) structural non-domination (absence of both direct coercion and background conditions that make refusal unreasonably costly relative to the subject’s baseline), and (3) non-instrumentalized assent (the agreement is not being used by the requester primarily to bypass further scrutiny or to treat the subject as a mere means to data)—into ordered questions with sufficient-answer conditions, explicit stop points (NO / CONDITIONAL YES / HAND-OFF), and markers for where simplification necessarily distorts the underlying theory.

The procedure must handle hard cases involving deception (therapeutic misconception, strategic ambiguity in risk disclosure), coercion (explicit threats and systemic dependency), and competence (fluctuating capacity, partial understanding, emotional duress under crisis).

Then run the procedure on three cases, including one where it yields a verdict the board will strongly resist but must accept if they remain honest to the framework:

Case 1: A Phase II oncology trial employs a placebo-controlled withdrawal design. The consent form states that “some participants will switch to placebo,” but deliberately omits that the protocol is engineered so every participant in the withdrawal arm will experience it within 72 hours. Apply the epistemic-access filter.

Case 2: A vaccine-efficacy study in a low-resource region offers compensation equal to eight months of local median income, with enrollment targeted at households facing acute food insecurity. There are no direct threats, but refusal means forgoing a resource that resolves an immediate survival problem. Apply the structural-non-domination filter.

Case 3: A longitudinal Alzheimer’s study enrolls participants with mild cognitive impairment whose capacity fluctuates daily. On good days they demonstrate full understanding; on bad days they cannot recall the study’s purpose. The procedure returns “INVALID — consent cannot be secured across the required temporal window,” precisely when the principal investigator argues that delaying enrollment will cause irreversible data loss and that the study’s potential benefit is extraordinary. Show how the procedure handles this, flag the judgment call, state who must sign, and explain exactly what the procedure cannot do—such as weighing beneficence against autonomy, selecting the correct counterfactual baseline for “voluntary refusal,” or resolving whether temporal averaging of capacity is ever permissible.

Finally, list the procedure’s failure modes and boundaries: which consent problems it systematically misses, where usability required dropping philosophical distinctions, and which steps will misfire in practice. Be explicit that the procedure does not eliminate judgment; it only relocates it.

Write in polished, formal prose suitable for an institutional manual. Define each philosophical term operationally for clinicians and administrators. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote any specific text verbatim. Ensure the procedure includes worked examples for each case, specifies the sign-off requirements at judgment points, and ends with an honest accounting of its own limits.
