In debates over whether torturing one person could be worse than causing a barely perceptible harm—say, a dust speck in the eye—to billions, people often reach for the boulder-and-sand analogy: one massive rock crushing a victim versus beach sand that collectively weighs the same but feels like nothing. The analogy is meant to show that moral weight does not aggregate like physical mass, licensing the intuition that torture remains categorically worse no matter how many micro-harms are summed. Audit this analogy in detail: lay out the explicit correspondence table (boulder, sand grains, weight, crushing force, visibility, reversibility), identify where the mapping breaks—particularly around whether moral badness is genuinely non-additive, whether visibility is a reliable guide to moral importance, and whether the sand grains can accumulate into something boulder-like (for instance, blindness or chronic pain from countless specks)—and say whether the conclusion that torture is always the greater evil survives once those disanalogies are on the table. If the analogy collapses, suggest what comparison, if any, better captures the aggregation problem without smuggling in our perceptual biases.
