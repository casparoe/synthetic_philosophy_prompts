I'm preparing the framework for a graduate seminar on self-locating belief, and I'm dissatisfied with the usual framing that treats the Sleeping Beauty problem as a simple standoff between halfers and thirders. Before I evaluate any particular solution, I want the full specification of what a theory of self-locating belief must actually deliver. Give me the desiderata: the phenomena it must explain, the distinctions it must preserve, the formal constraints it must satisfy, and the conceptual pitfalls it must avoid — with the motivating case or consideration behind each. In particular, include the standard Sleeping Beauty structure (a subject who knows she will be awakened once or twice with a fair coin, with memory erased between awakenings, and must assign a credence to the coin having landed heads), but treat it as a stress test rather than the entire domain.

Specifically, I want requirements covering at least the following: how the theory handles centered worlds that are qualitatively identical but indexed to different times or locations; whether it can accommodate memory erasure without collapsing the post-awakening self into a numerically distinct subject; how it relates self-locating credence to ordinary Bayesian updating when no new non-indexical evidence is acquired; and whether it treats first-person location as an irreducible component of propositional content or as a reducible parameter appended to an uncentered description. I also want you to inject a less-discussed constraint: the theory should be able to distinguish cases where self-location provides genuinely new information (as in the Sleeping Beauty case) from cases where it is merely a redescription of the same evidential state (as in the standard "I am here now" indexical without epistemic change), and it should explain why that boundary is not arbitrary. For each desideratum, indicate whether it is non-negotiable or whether it reflects a preference of one camp, and flag any pair you think is jointly unsatisfiable — especially if the tension arises between preserving classical probability theory and accommodating the intuition that centered evidence can rationally shift credence. Finally, briefly indicate how the major positions — the view that assigns one-half, the view that assigns one-third, and hybrid or double-updating approaches — fare against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves rather than on the scorecard.
