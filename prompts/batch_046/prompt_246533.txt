I am preparing the final examination for an MA-level seminar on social ontology and the philosophy of language, and I have noticed that candidates often reproduce standard summaries of coordination problems without confronting the structural fragility of the conditions that sustain them. I should like you to draft a single 90-minute essay question, together with a model answer of approximately 700 words and a marking scheme, that sits within the domain of David Lewis's account of convention and the evolutionary dynamics of norms. The question must not be answerable by reciting familiar examples—such as driving on the right—or by listing the canonical conditions for a regularity to count as conventional.

Instead, please construct a specific, somewhat niche scenario that injects a structural complication into Lewis's framework: a decentralised research community that maintains an open-source citation norm through an asynchronous, algorithmically mediated environment supported by a distributed timestamped ledger. In this setting, participants cannot observe one another's current preferences in real time, updates to the ledger consistently lag behind actual practice, and the salience of precedent is filtered through algorithmic ranking rather than through direct mutual recognition. The exam question should ask candidates to determine whether the citation norm remains a single Lewisian convention, whether it has fragmented into two or more distinct conventions, or whether the conditions for convention have dissolved altogether; it should further require them to contrast Lewis's rational-choice-based conditions with evolutionary-game-theoretic treatments of norm change, and to address whether salience retains its relational character or collapses into an intrinsic property of the ledger entries.

Please provide a model answer that demonstrates the analysis I am seeking: it should distinguish common knowledge from mere shared belief, identify precisely which of Lewis's conditions become strained under asynchronous mediation, explain how the absence of direct feedback loops affects the preference-for-conformity condition, and offer a reasoned verdict on the ontological status of the fragmented practice. Alongside this, include a marking scheme structured around five must-hit analytical moves, with marks allocated out of 20, and for each move include a brief note on the characteristic error—such as treating the ledger lag as a mere practical inconvenience rather than as a breakdown in the feedback loop required for convention maintenance, or conflating algorithmic visibility with the mutual recognition that Lewis requires.
