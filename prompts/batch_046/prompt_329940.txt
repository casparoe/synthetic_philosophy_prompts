ok so me and my friend got into it last night about why originals are actually worth more. I ended up defending this hybrid thing i call indexical authenticity — the idea that an original artworks value comes partly from being in a direct, non-replicable causal chain to the artists intentional act. Its not just aesthetics (perfect fakes look identical) and its not just sentimental attachment. This chain is what makes conservation practices rational, explains why attribution disputes actually matter, and predicts that damage to an original hurts differently than damage to a copy even when the visual result is the same. So what would actually count against this? Rank the defeaters for me — from fatal to merely awkward. Which ones should a committed indexical-authenticity defender concede as real problems, and which can they dismiss? Im especially curious about the belief-dependence objection: if people still valued an object after being told it was a forgery but not knowing which specific object, does that break the causal-chain story? Or psychological results showing our responses track perceived authenticity rather than actual history? And is there a formal worry — maybe from value theory — that causal history cant ground normative properties? Also, does this risk collapsing into boring causal essentialism about art, and if so is that fatal or just embarrassing? My friend says its all status signaling in disguise — is that a real defeater or just changing the subject? I want to know what this view is actually exposed to, not just the standard "but fakes can be beautiful too" line.
