Two colleagues in my department—Dr. Elena Vasquez, who specializes in philosophy of science, and Dr. Marcus Chen, who works in moral philosophy—have been arguing about Darwin’s account of morality and religion in his later writings, particularly The Descent of Man and the private notebooks on moral origins. They asked me to referee, and since I could not settle the matter to either’s satisfaction, I am presenting their exchange for adjudication.

The disagreement unfolded over lunch as follows.

Elena began by arguing that Darwin’s evolutionary explanation of morality treats moral feelings as derived from social instincts shaped by natural selection. On her reading, the “moral sense” Darwin describes is nothing more than a complex adaptive mechanism for group coordination; its psychological authority is real, but it carries no independent normative weight. Moral judgments, on this view, are causally explained by evolutionary history, and that explanation undermines any claim that they track objective moral truths.

Marcus replied that Darwin explicitly distinguishes the moral sense from mere social instincts, calling it the most important distinction between human beings and the lower animals. For Marcus, this is not merely taxonomic: Darwin attributes to the moral sense a distinctive capacity for approval and disapproval that operates independently of immediate self-interest or direct group utility. If Darwin truly thought morality was reducible to adaptive coordination, Marcus contends, there would be no reason to single out this capacity as uniquely defining.

Elena answered that even if Darwin uses the language of a distinct “moral sense,” he describes it in purely causal and psychological terms—feelings produced by evolutionary hardwiring. Knowing the causal origin of a judgment, she insisted, is precisely what generates the evolutionary debunking problem: once we see that our moral reactions are selected for reproductive success rather than for tracking moral reality, their claim to authority collapses into a useful fiction.

Marcus then pointed to Darwin’s discussion of moral progress through the expansion of sympathy and the application of reason to social instincts. If morality were purely instrumental, Marcus argued, Darwin would have no non-circular standard by which to call later moral developments genuinely “better” rather than merely “more adaptive for cooperative societies.” The fact that Darwin evaluates moral development as progressive—moving from narrow tribal sympathy toward broader ethical concern—implies, according to Marcus, a normative standard that evolutionary utility alone cannot supply.

Elena countered that Darwin’s standard of progress is itself functional: a society with broader sympathy is more stable and cooperative, hence more adaptive. “Better” is defined relative to the flourishing of social groups, not relative to any transcendent or metaphysically objective moral order. She added that Darwin’s well-known religious agnosticism reinforces this naturalistic picture: by rejecting transcendent sources of value, Darwin leaves natural selection as the sole ground of moral feeling, leaving no hidden normative residue.

Marcus concluded that Darwin’s own reluctance to apply evolutionary explanation to the human capacity for reason—his reported discomfort at the thought that even rational judgment might be merely the product of natural selection—reveals a tension in his position. If Darwin truly believed that evolutionary explanation exhausts the authority of moral and rational judgment, Marcus suggested, he should not have treated the extension of sympathy and reason as a form of genuine progress. The tension, Marcus claimed, shows that Darwin’s naturalism cannot fully accommodate the normative force he himself assigns to morality.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the five moves outlined above, indicate whether it is clearly mistaken, partially correct, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what, if anything, both parties are missing—particularly regarding whether Darwin’s evolutionary naturalism can preserve the normative authority of moral judgment, and whether Darwin’s stance on religion bears on that question in the way Elena and Marcus each suggest.
