I serve on the board of the Mid-Atlantic Historical Foundation, and we vote Thursday on funding a proposed permanent installation at the Old River Mill site. The exhibit, titled "The Vote That Forked," uses branching architectural design to present the region's 1973 deindustrialization as genuinely path-dependent: had one union ballot broken differently, the mill might have survived, locking in an alternate economic trajectory rather than the collapse that structural macroeconomic forces made inevitable. Our staff historian objects that this promotes a misleading "great contingency" fallacy, arguing that closure was overdetermined by global market conditions regardless of local events, and that presenting an alternate timeline distorts public understanding of historical causation. As a novelist, I am drawn to contingency, but I must recommend rigorously. Please draft the brief formal opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting: recommend a concrete course of action—approve with conditions, reject, or require substantial revision—within a defined framework, such as funding caps, mandatory companion interpretive materials, or structural-context requirements. The reasoning should engage seriously with whether small contingent events can genuinely lock in macro-historical outcomes or whether such counterfactual exhibits misrepresent causation, and it must treat the strongest argument for the opposing position with intellectual honesty rather than dismissal. Finally, please state how confident you are in the recommendation overall, and identify specifically which point in your reasoning about path dependence versus structural overdetermination you are least certain about.
