I am working through a graduate seminar handout on comparative philosophies of mortality that offers a compressed account of Zhuangzi’s view, and I want to check whether the framing is defensible before I write my response paper. The summary runs as follows: Zhuangzi treats death as a seasonal transformation no different from winter giving way to spring; in the well-known story of his wife’s death, he celebrates with drums because he has seen through the illusion that personal identity survives change—he holds that the self is only a temporary clustering of qi that dissolves back into the undifferentiated Dao, rather as a log rots into mushrooms. Because there is no enduring subject to suffer loss, grief is always a category error. The handout then connects this to contemporary process philosophy and claims that, for Zhuangzi, being dead is literally being dispersed into other living things, so that a dead person literally becomes parts of trees and insects, which is why he can speak of sleeping in a vast mansion—he means the biosphere itself is his afterlife. The summary concludes that Zhuangzi’s position is essentially an early form of ecological materialism, denying any normative weight to death because matter is conserved and nothing is truly lost. I can see how the non-attachment and the transformation imagery fit, but importing process philosophy and treating qi-dispersion as literal ecological recycling feels like a stretch. Is this a fair characterization? Please distinguish what actually tracks Zhuangzi’s texts—particularly the drum-song episode and the butterfly dream—from what is an anachronistic overlay, and tell me whether the claim that grief is a category error genuinely follows from his metaphysics or whether it smuggles in a modern scientific assumption about conservation of matter.
