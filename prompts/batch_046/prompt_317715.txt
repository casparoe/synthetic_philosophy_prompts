I cover the boundary between speculative cosmology and conceptual philosophy for a science-and-ideas publication. I am developing a feature on how science-fictional premises become serious theoretical problems, and for the research backbone of the piece I need a polished, one-page workshop proposal—approximately 600 words, formal in tone and suitable for an interdisciplinary audience.

The subject is the dark forest hypothesis: the claim that civilizations in the universe conceal themselves because revealing one's position invites preemptive destruction. Rather than treating this as a settled strategic result, I want the proposal to open a specific conceptual gap. The standard formulation seems to assume cognitive homogeneity—that all civilizations share a uniform rational calculus and a common ontology of harm, survival, and contact. The proposal should ask whether the equilibrium of universal silence survives once we drop that assumption. If civilizations possess radically incommensurable reasoning architectures—where destruction, contact, and survival do not map onto shared categories—the strategic logic of concealment may dissolve into an epistemic coordination problem with no dominant strategy.

Please structure the proposal with these elements: a precise statement of the research question; an account of why existing game-theoretic treatments leave the homogeneity assumption unexamined; a methodological approach using conceptual analysis and tools from epistemic game theory or incommensurability arguments, with an explanation of why empirical astronomy is not the right avenue; a clear description of what a positive result would look like versus what failure would look like, including an honest risk that the argument collapses back into a generic restatement of the Fermi paradox; and a realistic scope assessment for a single semester or workshop cycle. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, citation requests, or appeals to external sources; the proposal should stand entirely on its conceptual framing. Keep the language rigorous but accessible to philosophers of science who are not committed to the fiction as doctrine.
