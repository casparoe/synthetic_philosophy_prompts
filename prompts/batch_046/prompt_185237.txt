As a novelist constructing a protagonist whose decision-making follows Herbert Simon’s model of bounded rationality rather than global utility maximization, I have drafted an argument that now feels suspiciously destructive, and I need to locate the fault line before I build the narrative architecture around it. (1) A rational decision procedure must not systematically exclude superior alternatives that could be discovered at negligible additional cost. (2) Satisficing, by definition, terminates search once an aspiration level is met, thereby excluding every remaining option regardless of how cheaply it might be evaluated. (3) For any bounded agent, the marginal cost of one additional comparison can be driven arbitrarily close to zero, though never to exactly zero; hence there always exists some unexamined alternative discoverable for a cost approaching nil. (4) Therefore satisficing invariably violates the condition in (1). (5) If such a violation is not rational but merely expedient, then bounded rationality collapses into an oxymoron: a form of rationality that is necessarily irrational. (6) Yet Simon intended the concept as a descriptive account of competent cognition, not as a taxonomy of failure. (7) So either (1) imposes an impossible normative standard, or satisficing is not rationality at all but only a practical concession. Where does the argument slip? I am inclined to preserve both (1) and (6), but I want to understand precisely what I must pay to keep them intact, and whether that cost undermines my character’s psychology.
