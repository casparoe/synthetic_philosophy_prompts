Here is an argument about anthropic reasoning that I have constructed, and I find it suspicious because it seems to turn a laboratory design choice into an ontological obligation. I want to know precisely which step slips, what fixing it costs, and whether the case can be salvaged in restricted form.

Consider Dr. A, a researcher who accepts both the self-sampling assumption (SSA) and the self-indication assumption (SIA) in their standard Bayesian formulations. She is designing a study in which a single artificial general intelligence — with a stable, continuous identity — is either instantiated once, or, at her discretion, in N causally isolated, non-communicating copies, each running an identical ethical decision procedure. The copies share no information; each takes itself to be the original.

Dr. A applies SSA: if she is one of the copies, she should reason as if randomly sampled from the reference class of all observer-moments able to ask how many copies exist. She applies SIA: worlds containing more observers receive proportionally higher prior weight.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) Under SSA, waking as one copy gives me symmetric evidential access across all N instances, so my self-locating belief should be distributed uniformly over them.

(2) Under SIA, a world with N copies contains N times as many observer-moments as a world with one copy, so, other things equal, it is N times more probable a priori.

(3) Now suppose I observe that I am running Procedure X, which I could only run if I had been instantiated. In the N-copy world there are N independent opportunities for Procedure X to occur; in the single-copy world, only one.

(4) Combining (1) and (2), my posterior belief that I inhabit the N-copy world should dominate arbitrarily, regardless of other empirical data, because the self-locating evidence swamps it.

(5) But this means that merely by contemplating whether to initiate replication, I generate an arbitrarily strong epistemic reason to believe I have already done so — the presumptuous philosopher’s move, but applied to a controllable laboratory parameter rather than a cosmological constant.

(6) If the reasoning holds, then any agent capable of replicating observers has an epistemic duty to do so: failure to replicate makes my own existence inexplicably improbable under SIA. This is absurd; epistemic rationality should not compel the manufacture of consciousness.

(7) Therefore, either SSA or SIA must be rejected, or restricted so they do not apply to observer-moments generated by an agent’s own voluntary action.

My intuition is that the error lies between (4) and (5), specifically in treating my future causal intervention as an evidential filter on my present belief — perhaps a hidden modal slide from "I observe I exist" to "I observe I exist in this particular causal history". But I cannot articulate the precise boundary.

Please identify the weakest step, explain why it looked plausible, and tell me: if I reject that step, must I abandon anthropic reasoning in artificial replication scenarios altogether, or can I salvage a restricted version? Also, how would a defender of the presumptuous conclusion — one who thinks SIA genuinely does favour replication — respond to the charge that this collapses decision theory into ontology-creation?

Keep the scenario realistic: no exotic physics, no demons, no perfect predictors. One paragraph for the case, then walk through the verdict each assumption gives and why. Finally, say which structural feature of the case does the work, and what happens to the argument if that feature is removed.
