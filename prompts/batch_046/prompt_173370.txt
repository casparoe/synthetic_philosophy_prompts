I lead a philosophy-of-mind seminar for 12 graduate students in a 90-minute session on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. Design a runnable in-class activity — with explicit rules, materials, and a debrief — that makes participants feel the force of the illusionist idea that introspective reports of “what it is like” do not require an intrinsic qualitative property. Avoid the standard Mary or zombie scenarios. Instead, use this specific framing: present three printed cards describing the same stimulus, a slowly rotating amber light. Card A uses dense phenomenal vocabulary (“a warm, glowing presence pressing inward with emotional weight”). Card B uses only structural-functional language (“a circular luminance gradient near 590 nm triggering sustained attentional fixation with mild arousal”). Card C is a meta-report insisting that Card A captures an irreducible qualitative residue that Card B necessarily omits. The activity should proceed in three rounds: (1) in pairs, students defend Card C against Card B using only non-authoritative, first-person arguments; (2) they attempt to translate every phrase in Card A into Card B’s vocabulary, explicitly marking which phrases resist translation and why; (3) the group votes on whether the resistant residue belongs to the experience itself or only to the report. Include a symbolic payoff or scoring mechanism that rewards successful translation rather than rhetorical defense, strict rules forbidding appeals to “direct acquaintance” or citation of authorities, and guidelines for handling students who treat the task as merely linguistic rather than metaphysical. The debrief must map each round onto a specific illusionist claim — for example, that phenomenal concepts are recognitional rather than descriptive, or that introspective transparency collapses the appearance/reality distinction for experience — and must be frank about which feature of illusionism (e.g., the metaphysical denial that there is literally anything it is like) the activity cannot generate on its own and the facilitator must supply. Predict what will predictably go wrong in the first run and how to correct it mid-activity. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
