I am applying for a six-month independent research grant to develop a conceptual framework for assessing when non-human forces—pathogenic microbes, long-range climate shifts, and volcanic events—should be treated as 'historical actors' rather than background conditions in explanations of past societies. The precise question is not empirical but philosophical: is there a coherent criterion that distinguishes attributing 'protagonist-like' causal efficacy to these entities from falling into environmental determinism, and can that criterion be applied consistently across biological, meteorological, and geological domains?

Existing scholarship in environmental history and philosophy of science tends to oscillate between two unsatisfactory poles. One side treats germs, climate regimes, and eruptions as autonomous agents that reshape civilisations, which risks reifying them as intentional subjects and obscuring human institutional structures. The other side rejects all such language as determinist metaphor that flattens cultural variation and human agency. Neither offers a middle-ground conceptual tool: a way to evaluate whether a non-human cause operates in a historical narrative as a 'triggering condition'—necessary but not sufficient, interacting with specific social vulnerabilities—rather than as either a passive backdrop or a deterministic driver.

My approach will be analytical rather than archival. I will construct three paired case analyses—each pairing one domain with a well-documented historical episode (the 1815 Tambora eruption and the subsequent Year Without a Summer; the Black Death's penetration of fourteenth-century Eurasian trade networks; and the Little Ice Age's interaction with early modern agricultural regimes)—not to establish new empirical facts, but to test whether the 'triggering condition' framework can be applied without collapsing into anthropomorphism or determinism. For each pair, I will ask: does treating the non-human force as narratively indispensable—such that removing it renders the subsequent transformation unintelligible rather than merely different—require attributing any form of agency to it, or can it be explained through a model of 'causal protagonism' that remains strictly non-intentional?

The deliverable will be a 15,000-word working paper with a formal typology: (1) background conditions; (2) triggering conditions (narratively indispensable but non-agential); (3) structural determinants (systemic constraints operating independently of particular events); and (4) pseudo-agential descriptions (where metaphor is unavoidable but must be flagged). I will also include a decision procedure that historians and philosophers of science could apply to classify explanations across these domains.

Failure would mean that the framework collapses: if every instance of narrative indispensability either implies intentional agency or reduces to structural determinism, then the middle ground is illusory, and I will defend one of the two poles with revised arguments. If the framework holds but applies only to one domain—say, pathogens but not climate—that is partial success, yielding a domain-specific theory that I will present as the actual finding rather than a deficiency.

Scope: six months permits the conceptual architecture, the three paired analyses, and one revision cycle based on workshop feedback. It does not permit original archival research or quantitative climate modelling; I will rely on established historical syntheses and treat empirical narratives as given.

Why should a foundation fund philosophical work here? Because contemporary debates about pandemic preparedness, climate adaptation, and geoengineering increasingly invoke historical analogies that carry unexamined causal assumptions. A rigorous distinction between narrative indispensability and agency would provide policymakers and historians with a clearer vocabulary for describing non-human influence without either exaggerating it or dismissing it.
