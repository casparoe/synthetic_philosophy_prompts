I serve as the senior scientific-integrity officer for a regional public-health authority, and every quarter my team must sign off on expert assertions that enter advisory briefs—claims such as that a particular exposure pattern causally elevates risk within a specified subpopulation. The stakes are high enough that a false positive alarms the public and triggers costly interventions, while a false negative delays protective measures, yet we have neither the mandate nor the months required to replicate the underlying studies; we must evaluate the warrant for an assertion already made by an invited expert. Recently my colleagues and I have been arguing about which epistemic norm should govern these sign-offs: must the expert know the claim, is justified belief sufficient, or is mere truth enough? I need that disagreement translated into an operational decision procedure—something my non-philosopher staff can run in about forty-five minutes—rather than another abstract debate.

Please design a reusable checklist or flowchart in words that my office can apply to any proposed expert causal assertion. The questions should proceed in a fixed order: first about the expert’s evidential position and explicit awareness of defeating conditions; then about institutional uptake conditions—how non-expert readers, policymakers, and journalists will actually use or misinterpret the claim once it is released; then about whether the remaining gap is epistemic or a matter of values that requires human judgment rather than procedural resolution. At each stage, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure halts with a refusal to include the assertion, where it permits inclusion with mandatory caveats, and where it must hand the decision to a senior officer because the issue is normative rather than factual. The procedure must explicitly accommodate three hard cases: an expert who holds a rationally justified belief that falls just short of knowledge because of a lottery-style defeater she has acknowledged but not resolved; an expert whose claim is technically true but only by epistemic luck, because an unknown confounder cancels out in a way that makes the causal attribution correct for the wrong reason; and an expert who recognizes that the claim is false in its intended formulation but reformulates it into a technically true yet misleading version. Apply the procedure in full to the lottery-defeater case and to the lucky-truth case, and ensure that at least one of these applications yields an answer I will not want to approve—specifically, that an assertion must be blocked or heavily caveated—while the logic of the procedure forces that result through. Finally, state clearly what categories of assertions this tool is not designed to evaluate—particularly those embedded in contested theoretical frameworks rather than observational causal claims—and identify the specific points where the procedure will systematically misfire or produce false confidence.
