As part of a feature I am writing on pedagogical experiments that make philosophical structures physically tangible, I need a fully specified classroom design. The setting is a graduate seminar in philosophy of history—twelve students, one ninety-minute session—where participants have encountered the standard literature on explanation but have not yet experienced why narrative understanding is structurally irreducible to covering-law derivation.

Design an in-class simulation built around a fictional, richly detailed scenario: the sudden dissolution of a municipal water cooperative in an unnamed Mediterranean town during the late 1970s. Provide the students with discrete event-cards—political betrayal, an unseasonal drought, a misdelivered telegram, an unexpected funeral, a bank refusal—without labeling them as causes, effects, or narrative nodes.

Divide the group into two teams of six. Team A, the Analysts, must explain the collapse using only law-like generalizations (economic, sociological, climatological). Their model must take the form of a vertical flowchart with substitutable intermediate links: they should be able to replace any one card with a counterfactual alternative and show that, provided the initial conditions and the covering law remain fixed, the explanatory derivation still holds or fails in a law-governed way. Team B, the Narrators, receives the identical cards but must arrange them in a two-dimensional board configuration—spatial proximity, enclosure, and sequence—such that the final card, marked Dissolution, becomes retrospectively intelligible only through the particular configurational coherence of the path. They must defend the claim that altering any intermediate card does not merely change the predictive outcome but collapses the narrative closure itself: the ending no longer makes sense as the culmination of that specific sequence.

After thirty minutes of construction and a ten-minute presentation by each team, run a forced exchange. The Analysts must now defend narrative coherence without allowing counterfactual substitution; the Narrators must derive the outcome from general laws without invoking the spatial or sequential configuration of the cards. This should generate an experienced friction rather than a verbal disagreement.

Include explicit rules and a non-monetary payoff scheme: award points when the opposing team can identify a substitutable intermediate that preserves coherence (Analysts win points) or when a derivation succeeds without referencing card arrangement (Narrators win points). The facilitator should score rigor of defense, not convergence on a correct answer.

Structure a twenty-minute debrief that maps the physical actions onto philosophical distinctions: the vertical flowchart as the symmetry of explanation and prediction demanded by covering-law models; the two-dimensional board as the configurational grasp—seeing events together in a whole—without which narrative explanation collapses; the inevitable smuggling of narrative elements by the Analysts as evidence that historical covering-law explanations often depend on unstated configurational assumptions; and the Narrators’ tendency to invent hidden teleologies as a predictable failure mode.

Specify materials—index cards, a large table, colored markers for arrow-types versus proximity-lines—and a timeline broken into construction, presentation, forced exchange, and debrief. Identify one student per team as a neutral observer whose sole role is to flag rule violations, since the first run will predictably see Analysts reorder cards to preserve a hidden story and Narrators treat the ending as causally pulling preceding events.

Finally, be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce. Because every participant knows the Dissolution card from the outset, the simulation cannot replicate the genuine retrospective asymmetry of historical understanding—the sense that the past is open-ended for agents within it but configured only for the historian looking backward. The facilitator must supply this limitation verbally in the debrief, clarifying that the exercise demonstrates structural irreducibility between explanation-types, not temporal epistemic asymmetry.

Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations. The design should be self-contained, runnable without reference to specific texts, and should avoid any up-front lecture so that participants encounter the conceptual tension through the rules and incentives themselves.
