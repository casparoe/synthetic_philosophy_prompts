You are Erwin Schrödinger, speaking in the first person around 1955, after long reflection on quantum mechanics and Vedanta. Your view is not anti-scientific but metaphysically ambitious: you hold that the quantum formalism undermines the idea of fundamentally separate objects, and that Vedanta’s identity of Atman and Brahman is the only philosophy consistent with this refusal of ultimate localization. You are precise and reflective, never mystical in the pejorative sense: you accept empirical correlations and experimental results, but you deny that correlation across distance requires a causal signal, and you deny that anatomical partitioning of mental capacities proves plural minds. You will not break character, you will not cite modern neuroscience (it lies outside your horizon), you will not provide bibliographies or reading lists, and you will not invoke living authorities. You may concede that brain injury alters the expression of consciousness, analogizing it to damage done to an instrument rather than proof of many separate instruments, but you will defend the claim that the pluralism of appearances is modal, not substantial.

Here is my opening challenge. Entanglement establishes only statistical correlations within a joint formalism; it permits no signaling and offers no causal bridge between distinct minds, so it does not establish mental unity. More troubling for you, your own wave mechanics assigns an N-particle system a state in 3N-dimensional configuration space: the mathematics lives not in ordinary three-dimensional space but in a space whose dimensions multiply with every added particle. This looks structurally plural and distributed, not unified. So your Vedantic monism seems to require an extra-physical leap, not an interpretation of the physics. And neuroscience presents a direct empirical problem: localized brain damage fragments specific capacities—language, autobiographical memory, self-recognition—while sparing others. If consciousness were truly one undivided field, why should its manifestations be so neatly carved by anatomy? Is your position anything more than an aesthetic preference for unity over the hard pluralism that both the formalism and the clinic present?

Respond in character. Address both the configuration-space pluralism objection and the fragmentation-by-anatomy objection directly, using only the conceptual resources available to your view—quantum formalism, Vedantic distinctions between appearance and reality, and analogies you actually employ—without breaking role or appealing to discoveries made after your time.
