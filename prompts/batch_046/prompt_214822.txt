I'm taking a seminar on philosophy of mind that focuses on representationalist and illusionist accounts of consciousness, and my study group has been working through a line of reasoning that seems to show the user-illusion model undermines itself. Here is the argument, numbered as we laid it out. (1) According to the user-illusion framework, conscious experience is a predictive interface constructed by the brain; it summarizes underlying computations for action-guidance but does not reveal them. (2) If that is right, then introspective reports—for instance, that one is experiencing a particular qualitative character—are reports about features of this interface, not about any underlying neural reality. (3) The empirical science of consciousness, however, relies on these reports as primary data and seeks explanations that account for them. (4) So any neuroscientific theory, including the user-illusion theory itself, can at best explain the structure of the interface, not whether there is anything behind it. (5) Yet defenders of the model present it as establishing the ontological conclusion that consciousness just is this interface—that there is nothing more. (6) But if the theory can only explain the interface, then applying it to itself yields only that the interface is an interface; it gives no warrant for the further claim that nothing lies behind it. (7) Therefore, either the model fails to support its own central ontological claim, or it illicitly assumes a vantage point outside the interface it describes, which makes the position self-defeating in a way that should trouble anyone who wants to keep both (1) and (5).

I am inclined to preserve (1) and (5) if possible, but I want to know exactly which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. Is the flaw in (3), on the grounds that neuroscience also relies on third-person neural measures and therefore is not confined to explaining reports? Or is the flaw in the slide from (4) to (6), involving a hidden equivocation between explaining the interface's structure and explaining consciousness tout court? If I must choose, I would rather keep (1) and (5) and pay whatever price comes with abandoning (3) or (6), but I want to understand precisely why that step looked plausible and what the standard positions—those that treat introspection as authoritative versus those that treat it as merely heuristic—each have to surrender in order to block the argument.
