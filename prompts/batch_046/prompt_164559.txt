As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of mind in earnest, I have been meeting monthly with a small discussion group composed mostly of former colleagues from other disciplines. Last month we turned to the idea, associated with Daniel Dennett, that the self is best understood not as a substantial entity but as a center of narrative gravity—a functional attractor around which autobiographical stories cohere. The discussion quickly turned to a hypothetical case I proposed: imagine an artificial cognitive architecture, or even a biologically modified human, in which two parallel narrative-integration subsystems operate without any cross-communication. Each subsystem constructs a coherent, first-person autobiography with distinct value commitments, emotional valences, and future-directed intentions; neither has privileged access to the other's outputs, and both drive behavior through separate motor-planning pathways. I called this the split-stream memoir case. Two members of the group, both with backgrounds in cognitive science, disagreed sharply about its implications for Dennett's view.

(1) The first argued that the split-stream case demonstrates that narrative gravity can genuinely be plural: there are two distinct centers, each with its own gravitational pull on behavior, and neither is ontologically prior. If Dennett's view is correct, he said, we should describe this system as containing two selves, not one self with internal conflict.

(2) The second replied that this misapplies the metaphor. A center of narrative gravity, she said, is not merely any coherent story but an integrated dynamics of self-modeling that regulates action across contexts. The split-stream system is precisely what happens when that integration fails; it is not two healthy centers but one broken center, or perhaps a centerless system. The pathology proves the norm: there is only one center when the narrative dynamics are unified.

(3) The first countered that Dennett himself treats dissociative conditions as cases where narrative integration fragments, and he does not insist there is a hidden true self behind the fragments. If fragmentation can yield genuinely independent narrative attractors—each with its own memory consolidation, emotional appraisal, and decision architecture—then the unitary-center view is an unjustified presupposition. He added that requiring integration as a necessary condition smuggles back in the very substantial self Dennett wanted to eliminate.

(4) The second responded that even if we reject a substantial ego, we still need a functional criterion to distinguish one self from none or from many. Integration is not a metaphysical essence but a dynamical property: the capacity for cross-contextual coherence, for resolving contradiction through a single deliberative framework, and for sustaining long-term commitments. Without that, she said, we have not discovered multiple selves; we have discovered that the conditions for being a self at all have not been met.

Neither convinced the other. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. In particular, I want to know whether the split-stream memoir case genuinely supports plural centers of narrative gravity, or whether it instead reveals the limits of the metaphor itself. Please also say what both parties are missing, if anything, and whether a retired physicist should expect the concept of narrative gravity to survive once we begin modeling selfhood with the precision of dynamical systems theory.
