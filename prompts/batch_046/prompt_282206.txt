I'm in a historiograpy seminar and we keep circling back to Ranke's old claim that history should show how things actually happened, versus Carr and Hayden White saying that every narrative is shaped by the historian's choices and there's no bare fact to recover. A friend of mine keeps pushing this analogy: Ranke is like someone who thinks they can just download raw server logs and call that the truth, while Carr and White are pointing out that writing history is more like training a model on those logs — you have to choose an architecture (the plot structure), weight some events more than others, and the raw logs never tell you what story they belong to. He says this proves Ranke was naive, like believing you can serve data without an interpretive model, and that Carr and White clearly win because every historian is implicitly running some kind of training process.

Can you audit how far this holds? I want a correspondance table — server, logs, weights, loss function, whatever — then the breaks: where does the LLM-training picture fail to map onto historiography? Does it quietly import the assumption that interpretation works exactly like parameter updates, or that there's no distinction between selecting evidence and optimizing weights? And most importantly, does the conclusion that Ranke is just naive survive once you lay out the disanalogies, or was the analogy doing all the work? If the analogy collapses, is there a better one for thinking about objectivity in history?
