I just finished a long and heated argument with a friend about carbon-footprint food labels in grocery stores. The labels rank beef worst, chicken in the middle, and plant-based foods best, but they remain completely silent on the wild animals killed during crop harvesting—field mice, insects, amphibians crushed by machinery. I want to understand the implicit philosophy behind this institutional practice. Reconstruct the view of harm, moral considerability, and causal responsibility that makes it consistent to warn consumers about beef’s climate impact while saying nothing about the direct wild-animal deaths caused by growing lentils or wheat. Is the scheme presupposing that only climate-mediated harms count? That indirect harm is morally different from direct? Or that only beings above a certain cognitive threshold matter, with crop deaths falling beneath it? Distinguish what the labeling practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say whether its parts presuppose consistent commitments or quietly contradict each other. Then explain what would have to change in labeling policy if we replaced the underlying view with one that weighed all wild-animal suffering equally, regardless of whether it flows through carbon emissions. Finally, tell me what specific argument or empirical finding would most likely change your verdict about the implicit theory you have reconstructed, and flag the point in your reasoning that is weakest.
