I have been reading Plato’s dialogue about virtue and teaching—where a man named Meno asks Socrates why knowledge is more valuable than correct opinion—without any formal training in philosophy, and I keep returning to a suspicion that the entire problem is built on a hidden mistake about where value lives. I was discussing this with a friend who works in logistics, and she noted that a delivery contract and an unsigned promise can contain exactly the same terms, yet only one becomes enforceable once it is witnessed and recorded; this made me wonder whether knowledge and true belief might stand in a similar relation. Perhaps knowledge is not a different kind of mental content at all, but the same correct representation embedded in a network of justification and accountability that makes it portable and defensible to others, while true belief is that same content left unwitnessed and therefore useless for coordination.

Please argue for the position that the Meno problem dissolves once we stop treating knowledge and true belief as competing internal mental states and instead understand them as the same correct representation situated in different social-accountability structures—specifically, that knowledge is true belief made portable through practices of justification that serve interpersonal coordination rather than private cognition. Take a definite stand: claim that there is no intrinsic epistemic advantage to knowledge over true belief for a solitary thinker or in any non-coordinating context, and that Socrates’ search for an internal property—such as stability or durability—that makes knowledge more valuable is therefore a category error. Give your reasons directly, do not lay out the standard answers and leave me to choose, and address the obvious objection that knowledge still seems more defensible or durable even when no one else is around. I want a fully committed response—if you think the social-portability view fails, say exactly where and defend that failure, but do not hedge between positions.
