Below is the outline and central claim of an essay I am drafting on Leibniz’s moral philosophy. I am not asking for a bibliography, for citations I could not verify without access to a library, or for reading recommendations; I want critical comments on the coherence and textual fidelity of the argument itself, and on whether the framing I propose illuminates or distorts Leibniz’s position.

Thesis and outline. Leibniz is standardly read as a rationalist perfectionist: moral improvement consists in the increase of rational distinctness, and justice is defined in the Theodicy as charity guided by wisdom. I want to push this further and argue that, on Leibniz’s metaphysics, moral obligation is structurally identical to a monad’s intrinsic drive to maximize perceptual clarity. Each substance unfolds according to an internal law assigned by God; what we call moral deliberation is, at bottom, the resolution of perceptual confusion into clearer representations of the good. Because the harmony among monads is pre-established, other-regarding actions are not genuinely altruistic in the sense of being motivated by an independent concern for another’s good. Instead, they are instrumentally rational byproducts of each monad’s self-perfecting trajectory: I help you because the clearest representation available to me reveals that your flourishing is harmonized with my own rational optimization.

This yields what I call the moral inertia problem. If moral progress is nothing but the continuous, deterministic unfolding of each monad’s internal law, then there is no space for genuine moral choice or for moral tragedy. A so-called dilemma is merely a temporary epistemic blur—an incomplete ordering of representations—that resolves automatically as distinctness increases. The appearance of conflict between justice and mercy is therefore a phenomenological artifact of limited perspective, not a real metaphysical tension. On this reading, Leibniz’s ethics collapses into a form of deterministic epistemic instrumentalism, and his talk of love of God and neighbor in the Theodicy becomes descriptive of harmonized self-interest rather than prescriptive of other-directed obligation.

Questions for comment.
1. Does this framing misrepresent Leibniz’s account of justice and charity, particularly his claim that wisdom must be guided by love? Or can that claim be consistently interpreted within the instrumentalist picture I sketch?
2. Is the moral inertia problem a genuine tension in Leibniz’s system, or does it depend on conflating metaphysical determinism (the internal law of the monad) with moral determinism (the absence of normative choice)?
3. If the framing is largely accurate, does it make Leibniz’s ethics more or less attractive? Specifically, does the reduction of altruism to harmonized self-interest undermine the normative force he wants moral philosophy to have?

Please treat this as a consistency and accuracy check: point out where the argument overreaches, where it confuses metaphysical and normative claims, and whether there is a less costly revision that preserves the core insight without the deterministic collapse. I am especially interested in whether the injection of the moral inertia problem is a legitimate development of Leibniz’s own commitments or an anachronistic imposition.
