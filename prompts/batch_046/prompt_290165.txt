Design a 75-minute classroom activity for an upper-division seminar of about 14 students with philosophy or social-science backgrounds and no prior game-theory training. The goal is to make participants feel the rational pull of an information cascade inside an institutional peer-review setting rather than in the classic binary-choice urn. Frame the simulation as an interdisciplinary funding council evaluating five fictional grant proposals. Each student receives, for each proposal, a private evaluation card rating the proposal on two dimensions—methodological rigor and theoretical novelty—each on a 1-to-5 scale, plus a noisy private reviewer note that may conflict with the card. The council votes sequentially in public, announcing only a final composite score from 1 to 5 and one brief sentence of justification. Before each vote, the student sees all previously announced scores but not anyone else’s private cards or notes.

The payoff structure must remain hidden until after round two. Students earn 2 points for being within one point of the proposal’s true composite score, which is revealed only at the end. They also earn institutional-alignment points that scale with the square of the majority size they join—joining a 3-person bloc yields 9 points, a 5-person bloc yields 25, and so on. Present this initially only as “the council values coherence,” without revealing the quadratic scaling. The rules should forbid explicit negotiation outside the public vote and should prevent students from showing their cards.

Include concrete materials (the card format, how proposals are introduced, how hidden true scores are sealed), a strict timeline for 75 minutes including voting rounds and debrief, and a debrief that maps specific in-game moments—such as a student suppressing a high-rigor, low-novelty signal to match an emerging high-score cascade—onto the distinction between rational belief updating and rational herding. Specify what should be predictable failure points the first time the activity is run and how the facilitator can correct them without breaking the illusion.

Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order:
