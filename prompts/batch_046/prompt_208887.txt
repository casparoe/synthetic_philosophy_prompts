Consider the standard bioethical distinction between therapy and enhancement, often characterized as the difference between restoring a capacity to its species-typical range and augmenting it beyond that range. Now adopt a diachronic rather than synchronic interpretation of the baseline: instead of measuring against the peak performance observed in young adults, calibrate "species-typical" cohort by cohort across the lifespan, so that the median working-memory capacity of healthy seventy-year-olds defines the therapeutic target for an octogenarian, while that same seventy-year-old median represents an enhanced state for a healthy thirty-year-old. Under this framing, an identical pharmacological intervention—say, one that elevates synaptic density to precisely that seventy-year-old median—would be classified as therapy in one patient and enhancement in another, despite producing the exact same physiological endpoint. Does this demonstrate that the therapy/enhancement distinction tracks no intrinsic biological property of the intervention, but only the chronological age of the recipient? Or can a normative account of health and disease preserve the distinction even when statistical baselines are calibrated diachronically across age cohorts?
