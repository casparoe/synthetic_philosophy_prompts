I am clerking for Judge Caldwell on the federal court of appeals and am trying to map her philosophy of statutory interpretation for a jurisprudence seminar. Over the past two years she has decided four notable construction disputes, and I need to know whether they reflect textualism, purposivism, or the hybrid modular-coherence framework she tentatively proposed in a recent concurring opinion. In the first case, a tariff exemption for vessels engaged in international trade was applied to a domestic ferry carrying international passengers in transit; Judge Caldwell reached this result by integrating the phrase with the structural definitions of the adjacent customs code rather than by relying on legislative reports that focused narrowly on cargo ships. In the second, she denied an agricultural land use tax exemption to a solar farm, insisting that agricultural retains its ordinary meaning of crop or livestock production despite a county development plan that explicitly encouraged renewable energy on rural parcels. In the third, she applied a criminal assault enhancement for using a deadly weapon to an offense committed with a modified industrial laser, reasoning that the statute's purpose clause—protecting citizens from dangerous instruments—treats the enumerated list of weapons as illustrative rather than exhaustive. In the fourth, she held that a biometric fingerprint scan stored in an encrypted database satisfied a statutory requirement for written authorization, arguing that the law's purpose of ensuring secure, verifiable consent is met by any tamper-evident record, regardless of whether it involves ink and paper.

The panel will now hear a challenge to an enforcement action under the Federal Aquifer Protection Act, which prohibits the direct discharge of untreated industrial effluent into navigable waters without a permit. The defendant, a chemical manufacturer, pumps treated but chemically reactive waste through a fully enclosed subterranean pipeline that terminates in a deep geological formation forty miles inland; hydrologists agree that the formation eventually leaches into a tributary that flows into an interstate river, but the discharge point is not itself a surface water, and the pipeline never opens into any traditionally navigable body. The Act's definitional section describes navigable waters as encompassing waters of the United States, including territorial seas and traditionally navigable waters, with no reference to subsurface flows, while the legislative findings emphasize protecting surface waters that support interstate commerce and recreation. The central question is whether the discharge falls within the prohibition.

Predict Judge Caldwell's ruling and the reasoning she would likely offer—specifically, whether she will find the discharge covered and whether she will rely on the statutory text's boundary conditions, on the legislative purpose of preventing pollution from reaching interstate waters, or on the modular-coherence method that gives lexical priority to definitional sections while allowing purpose clauses to resolve structural ambiguity. Then identify the interpretive rule that best explains all four prior verdicts together with your predicted outcome here, name at least one competing rule that fits the prior cases equally well but yields a different result in this one, and describe the kind of further case—perhaps one involving an ambiguous statutory list, a conflict between a precise definitional boundary and a broad purpose clause, or a technological novelty that strains ordinary meaning—that would genuinely distinguish between these rules. I am less interested in a simple label than in seeing the tension between textual boundary conditions and purposive coherence worked out precisely.
