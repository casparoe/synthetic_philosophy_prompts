I retired five years ago from a career in comparative neurobiology and have since devoted myself to philosophy of mind and applied ethics, though I remain a layperson in both fields. I now serve on the research-ethics oversight committee for our university's marine biology station. By the end of next quarter we must circulate a formal recommendation to the station director: should our animal-welfare protocols be extended to cover cephalopods and decapod crustaceans used in behavioral studies?

The scientific context is that these animals possess distributed neural architectures—no centralized cortex—yet display flexible, goal-directed behavior that resembles conscious processing. In the philosophical debate this has given rise to two competing pictures. On one view, consciousness requires a unified global workspace; without such integration there may be sophisticated information processing but no phenomenal experience, hence no moral status beyond that of complex instruments. On the other view, awareness can be genuinely distributed or fragmented across semi-autonomous neural clusters, producing localized phenomenal fields without a single overarching subject; on this account, moral consideration should scale with the presence of any such fields, not with unity.

Our present regulations protect vertebrates but are silent on invertebrates. The research faculty warn that extending protection to every taxon with distributed neural complexity would make practical science impossible; the welfare advocate responds that if consciousness is not an all-or-nothing property tied to mammalian architecture, then our moral circle must expand according to where phenomenal fields actually occur, regardless of practical inconvenience.

Please draft the committee's written opinion—recommending approval with conditions, rejection, or a graded intermediate policy—with a concrete implementation timeline. The document should be addressed to the station director and suitable for circulation to faculty and external auditors. It must treat the strongest argument on the losing side with intellectual honesty rather than dismissing it, and it should avoid framing consciousness as a binary switch if the reasoning supports gradation. I do not need citations, bibliographies, or references to specific authors; I need conceptually rigorous reasoning that relies only on ideas a well-informed generalist could evaluate without external sources.
