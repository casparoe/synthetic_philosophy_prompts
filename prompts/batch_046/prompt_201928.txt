I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of science and economic history, and next month I will present a chapter of my dissertation in an interdisciplinary seminar on institutional evolution. My thesis is that canonical cases of so-called path dependence—standardized railway gauges, keyboard layouts, and similar coordination technologies—do not demonstrate that history is modally “locked in.” Instead, I defend a view I call functional convergence under perturbation: the observed institutional outcome is one of several causally distinct but structurally equivalent attractors that would have been reached regardless of the initial perturbation, so long as the underlying functional constraints (network externalities, coordination costs, physical compatibility) remain fixed. On my account, path dependence is a description of the actual causal trajectory, not a claim about counterfactual fragility; the same functional state would likely have arrived by a different historical route, which means the “lock-in” narrative systematically mistakes causal specificity for modal inevitability.

The audience will be ten to twelve scholars: senior economic historians, several of whom have published archival studies defending specific lock-in narratives, and philosophers of social science who treat path dependence as an ontological feature of institutional development rather than a descriptive heuristic. They are skeptical of armchair counterfactual reasoning, committed to the amplification of small initial differences into irreversible divergence, and quick to treat functionalist explanations as post-hoc rationalizations that ignore contingent political contestation. They will not be hostile to philosophy, but they will expect concrete historical mechanisms and will press hard on any claim that glosses over empirical detail.

Please prepare me for the objections I will actually face, ordered by likelihood and potential damage. For each objection, phrase it as one of these scholars would phrase it—using the vocabulary of institutional economics, archival historiography, or social ontology—indicate whether I should concede part of it, hold firm, or concede only tactically, and provide a concise reply suitable for a brief Q&A exchange. Identify the weakest point in my own position: the unstated assumption that counterfactual stability of functional constraints can be assessed independently of the very path that realizes them, and advise me on how to defend that assumption rather than hoping they overlook it. Finally, include the single question I am hoping nobody asks—the one that exposes the gap between my philosophical framework and the granular empirical evidence I am not engaging.
