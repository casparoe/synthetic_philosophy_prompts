In a seminar on institutional reform, a colleague invoked Chesterton's fence — the principle often rendered as don't take down a fence until you know why it was put up — to defend retaining an obsolete-looking bureaucratic review step that delays publication by months but rarely changes outcomes. She summarized the argument as follows: every institutional barrier was placed for a reason that may now be invisible to us; since we have not reconstructed that original reason, removing the barrier risks unintended harm; therefore preservation is the only rational default unless the founding rationale is fully recovered and shown obsolete. I suspect this packs several different philosophical claims into one slogan, and that her conclusion depends on picking the strongest version. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that Chesterton's fence might express? In particular, distinguish (i) a purely epistemic claim about our current ignorance, (ii) a functionalist claim that existing arrangements serve hidden benefits we have not identified, (iii) a conservative claim about the accumulated wisdom embedded in long-standing practices, and (iv) a risk-asymmetry claim about the irreversibility of institutional destruction. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and whether it actually supports her conclusion that the review step should stay in the absence of a reconstructed founding rationale. Finally, address a distinction I think her summary glosses over: does the principle require that we understand the fence's original purpose (as she assumes), or might it instead require that we understand its current functional role — a distinction that becomes critical when an institution has outlived its founding rationale but still serves an unanticipated purpose?
