I hold that the quantum-suicide argument for subjective immortality collapses once we combine an Everettian branching structure with a reductionist, no-self account of personal identity and a self-locating prior weighted by branch measure: there is no enduring subject to inherit the near-zero-measure survival paths, and the reasoning therefore predicts subjective extinction rather than eternal survival, undermining its own evidential premise. What would have to be true for this dissolution to fail? Map the defeaters precisely: which commitments about diachronic identity, self-location under branching, or the interpretation of quantum probabilities in first-person reasoning are load-bearing; whether a non-reductionist or psychological-continuity view of the self revives the argument in a form that survives the extinction prediction; and whether any formal result—say, about decision-theoretic rationality across branches or about the coherence of self-locating beliefs—would compel an agent to expect finding themselves in high-measure dead branches after all. Rank these from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which ones a defender of my view should concede as genuinely damaging rather than dismissible, and assess whether the position risks being insulated by qualifications—e.g., by defining away any possible first-person report that could contradict it—into unfalsifiability, and whether such insulation is a vice or an appropriate feature for a claim of this kind.
