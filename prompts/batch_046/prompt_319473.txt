I am a retired experimental physicist who, having set aside the laboratory, have lately turned to philosophy of science and metaphysics with some seriousness. In my reading I came across Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles and the familiar objection raised by Max Black's symmetrical spheres in an otherwise empty universe; I should like to see the issue explored not as a textbook rehearsal but through a freshly imagined scenario that forces one to decide whether numerical distinctness can survive the absence of every detectable difference.

Please write a short story, in British English, relating to this concept. Instead of spheres, consider two conscious computational substrates suspended in a featureless void, functionally identical in every respect—memory, architecture, and relational property—except that each harbours a private, in-principle undetectable indexical awareness, a bare 'thisness' inaccessible to measurement or to the other substrate. Present the narrative as exactly five scenes, numbered one to five, each rendered in one or two sentences, with neither introduction nor conclusion outside the scenes themselves; the story should examine whether that hidden residue preserves genuine duality or collapses the pair into one entity described twice.
