I have recently been working through the literature on rational commitment for a graduate seminar in decision theory and moral philosophy, and a particular tension in Derek Parfit’s discussion of the hitchhiker case has become personally relevant as I try to formalize cooperative bargaining under incomplete information. In the classic formulation, a hitchhiker promises to pay a driver after being dropped off in the desert, knowing that once the ride is complete there is no further instrumental reason to pay; yet if the driver anticipates this defection, the ride never happens. Parfit uses this to illustrate how it can be rational to cultivate dispositions or make commitments that are instrumentally irrational in particular instances—what he calls rational irrationality—because the expected benefits of being trusted outweigh the local costs of honoring the commitment.

What I am trying to understand more precisely is the conceptual status of this argument when we move from a transparent, publicly observable commitment device to one that is only partially observable and subject to the driver’s Bayesian updating. Suppose the hitchhiker installs a commitment mechanism—perhaps a locked container with payment that opens only after arrival—but the driver cannot verify whether the mechanism is genuinely binding or merely a cheap signal. Once the hitchhiker reaches the destination, he might disable or override the mechanism at negligible cost, but the driver must form beliefs about whether he will do so based on observable post-arrival conduct and on prior probabilities about the reliability of such devices. Does Parfit’s claim that rational agents should adopt policies of honoring commitments still hold if the rationality of the policy depends not on its direct causal efficacy but on its role in sustaining the driver’s posterior belief that the hitchhiker is the kind of agent who honors commitments? And more specifically, if the hitchhiker knows that his own future reasoning will be transparent to himself—he will see that disabling the mechanism is locally optimal—does this transparency undermine the very belief-state that made the initial commitment rational? I want an intuitive, conceptual explanation of whether rational irrationality requires a kind of self-opacity or strategic ignorance about one’s own reasoning process, and how this interacts with standard demands for dynamic consistency in sequential choice.
