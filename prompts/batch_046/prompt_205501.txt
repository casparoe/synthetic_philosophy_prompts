Consider a historian of nationalism who works within the conceptual frameworks developed by Eric Hobsbawm and Benedict Anderson—specifically, the idea that traditions presented as ancient are often recent constructions serving present political needs, and that modern nations are imagined communities sustained by mediated communication among people who will never meet face-to-face. She has rendered the following verdicts on whether a given collective exhibits an invented tradition and whether it qualifies as an imagined community.

First, on post-1861 Italy: she holds that both conditions are satisfied—the Risorgimento narrative projects modern statehood onto a fragmented past, and mass literacy together with standardized education created an anonymous horizontal fellowship.

Second, on the pre-nineteenth-century Scottish Highland clan system as reconstructed during the Victorian era: she holds that an invented tradition is present—the romanticized tartan, clan histories, and ceremonial loyalty were largely fabricated or systematized in modern times—but that an imagined community is absent, because membership remained kin-based, localized, and face-to-face, never achieving the scale of anonymous mutual recognition.

Third, on the twentieth-century Esperanto movement: she holds the reverse—an imagined community is present, since speakers dispersed globally share an internal mental image of collective belonging mediated by congresses and publications, yet no invented tradition is present, because the movement explicitly rejects claims to ancient ethnic continuity and celebrates its rationalist, modern origins.

Fourth, on the 1960s proposal for a Republic of New Afrika in the United States: she holds that an invented tradition is present—the claim of ancestral territorial and cultural continuity with pre-slavery societies projected onto the American South—but that an imagined community is absent, because the proposal never achieved sufficient institutional presence or internalized mass membership to sustain anonymous collective identity.

Now consider a new case: the Meridian Archive, a post-2030 polity of approximately forty thousand citizens distributed across floating habitats in international waters, governed entirely by algorithmic deliberation and cryptographic citizenship tokens, lacking any shared ethnicity or vernacular mother tongue, and maintaining collective identity solely through virtual rituals that trace constitutional principles to pre-industrial Polynesian navigation councils.

Predict this historian's verdict: does she classify the Meridian Archive as exhibiting an invented tradition, as an imagined community, both, or neither? In your answer, identify at least two distinct interpretive rules that could jointly generate her four earlier verdicts, show precisely how those rules diverge on the Meridian case, and specify what further hypothetical case—distinct from the four above—would best discriminate between the rules.
