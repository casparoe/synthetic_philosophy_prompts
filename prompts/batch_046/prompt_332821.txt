I have been reporting on a debate among philosophers of artificial cognition, and I keep returning to the case judgments of Dr. Elena Vasquez, a philosopher of mind whose recent lectures lay out a clear track record. She maintains that a silicon chip-for-chip digital twin of a human brain, running with real-time synaptic dynamics, realizes mental states in full; that the familiar Chinese-nation scenario—an entire population using radios to mirror neural firing patterns—does not, because the macro-arrangement lacks the internal causal architecture of cognition; that an octopus with radically distributed neurons but analogous problem-solving capacities realizes mental states, only with a different organizational form; that a large language model generating coherent philosophical prose but lacking any persistent internal state between tokens does not realize mental states; and that a human subject whose biological neurons are gradually replaced, one by one, by synthetic functional analogues retains continuous mental identity throughout the transition.

Her institute is now assessing a system they call Mosaic Mind: fifty independent neural-network modules, each running on isolated hardware with no shared clock, assigned to distinct sub-functions such as memory consolidation, evaluative weighting, and language generation. The modules coordinate exclusively through a low-bandwidth message bus that transmits compressed representational packets every two hundred milliseconds. Collectively they produce unified, self-referential reports and can solve integrative problems that no single module can address alone; yet at no point does any one processor hold a globally integrated state, since all coordination is purely transactional.
