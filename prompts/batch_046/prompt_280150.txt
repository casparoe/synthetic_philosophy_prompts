Two people in my study group got stuck on Braudels long-duration idea—geography and climate as slow structures versus actual events and individuals. One argued the framework collapses once you admit climate works through thresholds: centuries of gradual cooling don't matter until one winter crosses an agricultural yield boundary, then suddenly there's famine and revolt. So the 'event' is just the long structure finally becoming visible, and keeping them as separate layers is artificial. The other said that's wrong—around the same period a similar drought threshold hit Ottoman territories and they didn't collapse, which means the specific fiscal decisions made that year (the event/individual level) actually carried causal weight. The first replied that if the threshold is deterministic, then talking about Ottoman policy is just describing how the river takes a different path—it's still the river driving things. The second said that's determinism with extra steps, and if every event is just the structure expressing itself differently then Braudel's model can't explain why some societies adapt and others don't, which was kind of the point.
