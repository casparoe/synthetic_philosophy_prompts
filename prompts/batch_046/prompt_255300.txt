Write a dialogue between two people — call them M and J — about whether Hannah Arendt was right that political lying is specialy dangerous because it attacks the shared factual world we need to act together. M should defend Arendt's view, especially her idea from Truth and Politics that factual truth is fragile since it depends on witnesses and can be destroyed by organized deception. J should push back, but not by saying politicians lie all the time. Instead, J should argue that Arendt secretly relies on an old-fashioned, almost Cartesian picture of belief-formation — she assumes people hold factual beliefs like private mental files that can be corrupted one by one — and that this misses how modern publics stabilize knowledge through what J calls epistemic redundancy: overlapping institutions, records, distributed verification. M should respond that redundancy doesn't solve the problem Arendt cares about, which isn't individual false belief but the destruction of the common world: if enough people stop treating testimony as authoritative, the redundant systems themselves become hollow. J should then ask whether Arendt's fear is really about lying or about a deeper crisis of authority, and whether her distinction between factual and rational truth actually holds up under that pressure. Keep them talking past each other a little but eventually lock onto the real issue: is the danger epistemic (we don't know what's true) or ontological (we lose the shared reality that makes politics possible)? Make sure neither sounds stupid and that they actually answer each other's points rather than just monologuing. Thier disagreement should feel like it could go on for hours without either conceding.
