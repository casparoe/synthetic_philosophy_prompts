Frege's private political diary, discovered and published after his death, records explicit nationalist and anti-Semitic sentiments that appear incompatible with the universalist ethos of his philosophy: the doctrine that thoughts (Gedanken) are objective, non-psychological entities accessible in principle to any rational being regardless of social or ethnic particularity. The conceptual issue is whether a philosopher's private moral-political commitments can ever bear philosophically on the evaluation of their system without committing the genetic fallacy, and specifically whether Frege's logicist program implicitly presupposes a conception of the 'community of thinkers' that his diary either undermines or reveals as covertly restricted. Explain the distinction between structural objectivity—the thesis that logical and arithmetical relations hold independently of any particular thinker—and social objectivity—the thesis that grasping objective thoughts requires membership in a universal rational community. Is this distinction coherent within Frege's framework? Could one consistently defend the objective validity of arithmetic for all rational agents while denying, in private, that certain groups possess the rational capacity to grasp such thoughts? And if the exclusion is rejected, does that require revising Frege's conception of objectivity itself, or only his implicit sociology of reason? Analyze this in terms of the internal conceptual architecture of Frege's philosophy of logic and language, not in terms of biographical psychology.
