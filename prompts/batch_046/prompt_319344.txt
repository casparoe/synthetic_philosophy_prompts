Two readers in a discussion group disagreed about whether Rousseau's well-known attack on public theater is consistent with his praise for civic festivals. (1) The first said the position is plainly self-contradictory: both theater and festivals are spectacular gatherings that manipulate emotion and display virtue, so condemning one while endorsing the other is arbitrary unless one simply prefers the politics of the second. (2) The second replied that the corruption Rousseau identifies lies specifically in the spectator-performer hierarchy of theater, where citizens become passive observers of fictional characters, whereas civic festivals dissolve that boundary through collective participation in transparent social roles. (3) The first countered that festivals still involve assigned roles and onlookers, so the boundary does not actually dissolve; at best the second describes a difference in degree, not a principled distinction that could carry normative weight. (4) The second then argued that the decisive factor is not the presence of roles but the kind of identification they invite: in theater one emotionally identifies with imaginary persons whose virtues are unachievable, while in festivals one recognizes actual neighbors performing real social functions, so representational identification is absent and the corruption is avoided. They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating, then say which party is more right overall and what, if anything, both are missing.
