Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar on meta-epistemology and now find suspicious because it seems to dissolve every philosophical belief. (1) The outside view on a philosophical question reports only the distribution of expert opinion, whereas the inside view gives direct access to the specific reasons for and against each answer. (2) Epistemic modesty requires weighting one's view by the outside-view base rate whenever the inside view is unreliable. (3) In philosophy, the inside view is notoriously unreliable: for nearly any substantive thesis T there are recognized peers who deny T, so the expert distribution is often near even. (4) Consistent modesty therefore demands that we treat our own reasoning as no better than a random draw from that distribution, pushing credences in first-order philosophical beliefs toward 0.5. (5) But modesty itself is defended through inside-view reasoning about the value of deference, and there are recognized peers—steadfastness theorists—who reject it. (6) Applying (2)–(4) to modesty itself, we should reduce our confidence in modesty to near 0.5, then apply the same reasoning again to that reduced confidence. (7) Iteration collapses confidence in modesty toward zero, removing the pressure to defer to the outside view and thereby restoring full inside-view confidence in every philosophical belief, including the denial of modesty. (8) So consistent modesty produces incoherence: it annihilates itself and resurrects the very overconfidence it was meant to cure. (9) Therefore either modesty is false, or rational agents cannot consistently apply it. I want to preserve (1) and (4) if possible, and I resist abandoning (9)'s framing of the dilemma. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
