Construct a thought experiment set in a realistic institutional environment—such as a public-health research cooperative or a skilled manufacturing collective—presented in a single paragraph, in which an agent must choose between two institutionally available pathways that yield identical objective outcomes and identical subjective reward states, but differ radically in causal origin. In Pathway A, the agent performs genuinely demanding labor that develops a scarce skill and directly produces the organizational good. In Pathway B, the agent opts into a transparent, institutionally sanctioned protocol—combining calibrated behavioral feedback loops and, if appropriate, an existing pharmacological or gamified incentive system—with an automated backup mechanism that ensures the organizational good is produced regardless of the agent's direct labor; under this protocol the agent experiences the same subjective satisfaction and the same observable contribution, but acquires no skill and performs no difficult work, and no other party is harmed or deceived. Specify that the setting must contain no science-fictional brain implants, no demons, and no perfect predictors, and that the protocol must be genuinely available and not obviously defective. The case should be engineered so that a pure-state hedonist theory of well-being approves of Pathway B (or is indifferent), while a causal-achievement theory—according to which the value of an agent's contribution depends essentially on its causal origin in the agent's developed capacities and effort—must condemn Pathway B despite its hedonistic and organizational equivalence. After the scenario, walk through the verdict each theory delivers and the reasoning behind it; identify precisely which feature of the case (for instance, the decoupling of subjective state and world-state from the agent's causal history) performs the isolating work; explain how the verdicts shift if that feature is removed; and give an honest account of how a defender of the causal-achievement view would attack the case—perhaps by arguing that the protocol is not genuinely available in a stable form, that skill development is instrumentally necessary for long-run organizational reliability, or that the case illicitly separates causation from value. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
