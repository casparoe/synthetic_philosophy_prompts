Next Thursday I present a paper at an interdisciplinary seminar that advances a reading of Machiavelli I have not seen defended in the standard literature: that the pairing of fortuna and virtù across The Prince and the Discourses on Livy is best interpreted not as an individualist psychology of princely leadership, but as an early implicit theory of distributed institutional adaptability. On my account, fortuna is systemic contingency that outstrips any single actor's control, and virtù is therefore exercised collectively through organizational structures—republican rotation, mixed constitutional design, and adaptive civic-military coordination—that embed contingency-response mechanisms rather than concentrating them in one ruler. I developed this framing after noticing that modern institutional economists describe resilient governance in almost identical terms, and I want to test whether the analogy holds or collapses under historical and conceptual scrutiny.

The room is precisely split: three Renaissance historians who treat these texts as artifacts of Florentine civic humanism and will guard against anachronism; two institutional economists open to historical analogies but insistent on operationalizable mechanisms and predictive content; and one political philosopher who will press whether I have smuggled a normative defense of decentralized authority into what Machiavelli intended as descriptive realism. Prepare me for the objections in the order they will actually come, phrased in the vocabulary of each camp—historical-textual, mechanistic-institutional, and normative-philosophical—indicating for each whether I should concede ground, hold firm, or split the difference. For each objection, draft the reply I should deliver in about two minutes, flag the softest point in that reply, and identify the single objection I am hoping nobody asks. Finally, state explicitly what kind of textual counter-evidence or conceptual argument would actually change my mind about this institutional reading, and pinpoint the precise step in my reasoning that is most likely to fail.
