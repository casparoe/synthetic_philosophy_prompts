The group is sharp on computation but has only casual exposure to philosophy of mind, and several have already voiced the intuition that if a large language model can maintain a coherent first-person narrative across long contexts, it must possess something like a self—whereas they regard human selfhood as obviously real and therefore categorically different. I want to use Daniel Dennett's idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity—not as a substance or Cartesian theater but as an abstract organizational point around which actions, experiences, and commitments are gravitated, analogous to a physical center of gravity—to dissolve the false dichotomy they have constructed. Do not explain the concept to me; instead, plan the pedagogical sequence for this specific audience.

The plan should specify the exact order in which the components must be introduced, with explicit dependency relations: what must be in place before the center-of-gravity analogy can land without being read as mere poetry, and how to move from physical centers of gravity to narrative ones without letting the engineers conflate this with attention-weight distributions or embedding-space clustering in models. Choose one carrying example or scenario—explicitly not the standard Cartesian theater or split-brain patient, and justify why that alternative is more effective for listeners who think in terms of distributed systems—to anchor the entire explanation, describing precisely what features it must possess and what it must lack. Map the predictable misconceptions these participants will form, including but not limited to the belief that narrative gravity is just a metaphor for the default mode network, that a fictional center implies non-existence or illusion in a pejorative sense, and that coherence of generated text in an LLM is sufficient evidence of narrative gravity. The deliverable is the teaching plan: the arc, the example, the misconception map with intervention points, the check, and the boundary of omission.
