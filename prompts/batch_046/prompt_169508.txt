I'm currently taking an upper-level philosophy of physics course, and we've been working through Einstein's hole argument in general relativity. The familiar structure is this: specify the metric everywhere outside some open region, apply the field equations, and discover that more than one metric is compatible with those boundary conditions inside the region, with the alternatives related by a diffeomorphism that acts as the identity outside. The standard reply—whether traced to Einstein's point-coincidence reasoning or to modern relationalist readings—is that the two internal metrics represent exactly the same physical situation, so the failure of uniqueness is only a redundancy in our mathematical apparatus, not a genuine breakdown of determinism.

I've started noticing a recurring dialectical pattern that looks adjacent to this, but I'm not sure whether it is just the hole argument in different clothing or something with its own boundaries. The pattern runs like this: a theory assigns states to parts of the world such that, given every datum available from one region, there remain two or more distinct assignments inside a bounded region, and those assignments agree on every measurement that can be performed from outside. The usual defensive move is to treat the internal difference as purely representational—akin to a gauge freedom—because no external experiment can discriminate between them.

What I'm trying to isolate is a specific twist on that pattern. Imagine that inside the bounded region there are observers, or at least physical processes, that can in principle distinguish the two assignments. From the internal perspective, there is a fact of the matter about which assignment holds, and it makes a real difference to what occurs there. From the external perspective, however, the boundary conditions still do not determine which internal assignment obtains, and there remains no observational access from outside. So the theory fails to determine the internal state from the external data, yet the internal difference is not merely a redescription: it is physically consequential for those inside.

I've encountered informal versions of this when people ask whether an observer falling into the "hole" could ever detect which metric is present. But I do not know whether philosophers of physics have carved out a precise term for this hybrid configuration, in which the indeterminacy is epistemically sealed off from one region but ontologically substantive within another. Is this simply the hole argument with an internal observer appended? Does it belong to a broader distinction—perhaps between determinism relative to a region and determinism simpliciter? Or does it collapse into a familiar debate about observational equivalence, gauge symmetry, and physical reality?

If an established concept covers this, I would like to know its name, how exactly it differs from the standard gauge-redescription defense of general relativity, and whether the presence of internal observability undermines that defense or merely relocates the problem. If nothing in the literature maps onto it precisely, I would appreciate help in defining the phenomenon's edges: what conditions must hold regarding internal versus external access for the pattern to apply, and where does it blur into ordinary gauge freedom on one side or into straightforward metaphysical indeterminism on the other?
