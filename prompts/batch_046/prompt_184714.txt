Hey, I'm putting together a handout for a historiography seminar and I want a decision-tree taxonomy — not a point-scoring quiz, just a branching flowchart of questions — that sorts people by their actual commitments on historical objectivity rather than by which big name they like. The usual suspects are Ranke's idea that history should show things as they actually happened, Carr's scepticism that bare facts are mute without interpretation, and Hayden White's claim that every history is shaped by narrative tropes. But I don't want students to just check a box for Ranke vs White; I want each branch to be a concrete stance on a specific problem.

So please build teh tree like this: start with whether historical statements can be true in a way that doesn't depend on narrative form at all. Then branch through these splits. First, if an archive has a deliberate gap — say, destroyed ledgers for 1843-47 — do you treat the absence as noise, as meaningful negative evidence, or as proof that no objective sequence exists? Second, can you reconstruct a counterfactual path for an event without that reconstruction being just fiction? Third, does the social or ecological context of an event belong to the event's explanation, or is it external framing?

Here's the niche twist I want injected: use the idea of a "negative evidence protocol" — treating missing records as structured absence rather than random noise — as one of the branch nodes. I want to see whether adopting that protocol forces you into Carr-style constructivism, or if a Rankean can accept it as just another fact about the archive.

The leaves should include named positions where they actually exist — Rankean objectivism, Carr-style constructivism, Whitean tropological determinism, a critical realist middle ground — and "unnamed/unexplored" for empty combos. Please flag any pairs the tree can't really seperate, like if Carr and a weak version of White land on the same answers for some branches. Also note which empty spots look incoherent versus just unattractive or unexplored. Keep it in American English, write casually (a typo or two is fine), and don't ask for citations or a bibliography — the point is logical sorting, not source-hunting. The audience is grad students who know the basic debate but not this taxonomy. At the end, ask the response to say how confident it is in what it claims and single out the point it is least sure about. Maybe 5-6 branch nodes total.
