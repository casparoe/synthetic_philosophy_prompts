I am preparing an interview for an academic podcast series that revisits classical debates in epistemology and philosophy of religion through contemporary framing. Next week's guest is a philosopher who has recently advanced a reconstructed hybrid of Jamesian permissivism and Cliffordian evidentialism. On her view, Clifford's prohibition against believing without sufficient evidence applies rigorously to first-order empirical beliefs, but James's taxonomy — options that are live, forced, and momentous — governs only what she terms framework commitments: the methodological, logical, and interpretative presuppositions that structure inquiry itself rather than particular hypotheses within it. She contends that this division prevents James's position from licensing wishful thinking in empirical matters, while allowing that certain foundational commitments fall outside the scope of ordinary evidential appraisal because they are not responsive to observation in the same direct manner.

I need a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that move well beyond asking her to restate this architecture. The questions should press where existing literature on belief ethics, pragmatic encroachment, and collective epistemology already applies pressure. Please include at least one question that distinguishes her framework commitments from the practical postulates defended in Kantian-inspired pragmatism, together with a follow-up keyed to the reply that they remain genuinely doxastic rather than merely practical; include a further follow-up that asks whether doxastic status without evidential responsiveness is coherent. Another question should adapt Clifford's well-known shipowner analogy to her taxonomy, asking whether a research community that adopts a contested interpretative framework without sufficient evidential warrant is guilty of the same negligence Clifford describes, with a follow-up anticipating her likely claim that collective adoption differs structurally from individual belief formation. I also want a question addressing whether suspension of judgment is genuinely available for forced framework options — since one might simply refrain from adopting any framework — with a follow-up probing whether such suspension tacitly amounts to adopting scepticism. Finally, include a question about whether her view allows framework commitments to be revised in response to persistent anomalies without collapsing the distinction back into standard evidentialism, paired with the anticipated defence and its counter.

Please present the questions in formal, polished language appropriate for an informed but non-specialist philosophical audience, and write the response in British English.
