"The gradient view says consciousness comes in degrees based on neural integration — there's no hard cutoff. So an octopus, with about 500 million neurons and two-thirds in its arms, probably doesn't have one big mind. Instead each arm has its own low-grade phenomenal field, and maybe they add up like 0.2 + 0.2 + 0.2... to make something like a human but distributed. Actually I think they sum linearly, so the octopus has more total consciousness than a person, just spread out.

The threshold view says you need reflexive coherence — a system representing its own representations — and this is basically the same mechanism as global workspace broadcasting, just with a different name. Once that loop is present consciousness switches on fully. Because octopuses have no central cortex and only distributed ganglia, they can't form the loop, so they have zero consciousness, not partial.

Comparing them, the gradient view is more plausible for octopuses because it doesn't require a mammal brain. But there's a downside: if consciousness is continuous, moral status is continuous too, and we'd have to weigh each arm seperately in ethical calculations, which is impractical but consistent with the theory."
