In my graduate ethics seminar we have spent an entire term circling the familiar objection that treating individuals as interchangeable repositories of welfare permits one person’s catastrophic loss to be canceled out by modest gains spread across many others. Those who defend the objection insist that morality recognizes a structural boundary between persons: the fact that burdens and benefits are distributed across distinct lives is not a bookkeeping inconvenience but a deep constraint on justification, so that compelling one individual to bear severe costs for collective benefit requires a reason that addresses her specifically, not merely an arithmetic advantage. Those who reject it reply that the “boundary” is either a vivid metaphor without independent argumentative force or a restatement of the uncontroversial point that each agent has special reason to care about her own life; they maintain that once well-being is acknowledged as real and interpersonally comparable, refusing to aggregate it demands a separate principle—such as rights or agent-relative duties—rather than an appeal to “separateness” itself. We have worked through compensation schemes, rights-as-side-constraints, two-level moral theories, and every standard dialectical move, yet the discussion has stalled completely: both sides recognize each other’s arguments, neither detects a hidden empirical mistake, and no one revises their position.

I am trying to determine what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it verbal—hinging on whether “respect for persons” just means non-aggregation—or substantive, turning on whether interpersonal comparison of well-being is coherent or on whether agent-relative reasons can block otherwise valid trade-offs? I also wonder whether a hidden metaphysical question is driving the impasse: specifically, whether the conditions for personal persistence over time are loose enough that trade-offs within a single life (enduring present pain for future benefit) are genuinely analogous to trade-offs across different lives, which would undermine the intuition that inter-personal aggregation is uniquely objectionable. Are the two camps in fact answering different questions—one about the design of a social decision procedure, the other about the ontology of moral reasons—under the single heading of “the separateness objection”? Given that diagnosis, what should we treat as progress: a formal result about the impossibility of non-aggregative social welfare functions, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the boundary metaphor, an empirical investigation into whether people treat intra-life and inter-life trade-offs differently, or is this a bedrock clash of intuitions about which no further argument should be expected?
