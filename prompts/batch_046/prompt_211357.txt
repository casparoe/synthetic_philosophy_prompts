I am a doctoral student in philosophy of history, currently writing on epistemic dependence in ancient historiography. In a recent departmental seminar, a colleague defended the reliability of distant-past reconstruction by comparing the use of Herodotus and Thucydides to navigational triangulation: she said that treating these authors as two independent lighthouses, each emitting a somewhat distorted beam, allows us to fix the position of an event—say, the political structure of the Persian Wars—even when neither source had direct access and both wrote decades later with partisan motives. She used this to license the conclusion that convergence between the two narratives gives us justified belief about the distant past, despite the absence of contemporary eyewitness records.

I want to audit how far this analogy holds, because I suspect it smuggles in assumptions that do not survive scrutiny. Could you lay out the explicit correspondence: what in historiography plays the role of the lighthouse, the bearing, the navigator, the weather distortion, the geometric intersection, and the fixed coordinate being sought? Then identify the precise points of breakdown—especially the fact that our textual sources are not spatially independent instruments but may share a common origin of error through shared oral traditions or later editorial interpolation, and that narrative convergence can reflect shared rhetorical conventions rather than independent verification. Also address what the analogy imports about the nature of the past event itself: does it treat the event as a pre-existing spatial coordinate waiting to be fixed, and does that picture distort how interpretive frameworks partly constitute the reconstructed object?
