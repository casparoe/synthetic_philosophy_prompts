I've been trying to work out for myself what makes intertemporal choices genuinely irrational versus just messy, and I talked through several examples with a friend who has strong intuitive reactions to these scenarios even though she has no formal background in economics or philosophy. I want to see whether her judgments line up behind one consistent rule, or whether different rules could explain them equally well.

Here are the cases where she has already given clear verdicts. First, the classic cyclic-preference money pump: someone prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, and keeps paying a small fee to switch around the loop. She says that is flatly irrational—you're leaving money on the table to travel in a circle. Second, a smoker who genuinely wants to quit and tells herself every Sunday that the current pack is the final one, but buys another on Monday: she calls this irrational, but specifically because of the self-deception, not because smoking is unhealthy. Third, someone who adjusts their retirement savings rate every year—five percent one year, eight percent the next, three percent after that—based only on how stressful the year felt, with no new financial information: she considers this rational, just adapting to lived experience. Fourth, a person who keeps an unused gym membership for years, paying the monthly fee just in case they return: she thinks that is rational, like holding a low-cost option. Fifth, someone who takes out a high-interest payday loan to settle a family obligation rather than ask relatives for help, because pride makes the direct request feel worse: she judges this irrational, not because debt is bad in itself, but because pride is pushing them into a clearly worse deal.

Now I want your prediction about a new case I constructed, since I am not sure which rule she is actually running. Imagine a person who sets up an automatic budget-lock system: in any month they stay under their discretionary spending cap, the surplus rolls into a locked savings pool; in any month they exceed the cap, they pay a twenty-five-dollar penalty into that same pool. The system also allows them to pay a five-dollar reconsideration fee to retroactively erase one over-budget month—canceling both the penalty and the savings record for that month. They invoke this feature for every single month they go over, but never for months they stay under. Over the course of a year they pay sixty dollars in reconsideration fees, they accumulate no locked savings at all, and their net spending is exactly what it would have been with no system in place; yet they report feeling that they mostly stuck to the plan, since each overage was officially cancelled.

What do you think she would say—irrational or not? More importantly, lay out the competing rules that could accommodate all five of her earlier judgments just as well, show what each rule predicts about this rollback-budget scenario, and say which rule you would actually bet on, with your reasons for preferring it. Then name the further case—specifically designed to split those rules—that would best reveal which one she is really using. Please say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
