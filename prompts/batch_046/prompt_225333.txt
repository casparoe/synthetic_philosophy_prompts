I was talking with my roommate about logic and they mentioned dialetheism but I couldn't tell if they acutally meant it or just liked the word. Anyway, I want to see a dialogue between someone who genuinely thinks there are true contradictions - not just in some abstract liar-paradox way, but in actual institutional rules - and someone who thinks that's always a mistake of interpretation.

Specifically, imagine a city where the municipal code contains two ordinances passed in different decades: Ordinance 402 says every structure in the Riverfront District must maintain residential occupancy on at least 60% of floor space. Ordinance 517, added later after a development push, says every structure in that same district must maintain commercial occupancy on at least 60% of floor space. Both are still on the books, neither has a sunset clause, and the zoning board enforces both selectively depending on which developer applies - but they never officially repeal either one. The dialetheist thinks the legal system here literally contains a true contradiction: the same buildings are both required and forbidden from being mostly residential/commercial in the exact same respect. The classical opponent thinks there's always a hidden resolution - temporal priority, implicit exception, contextual restriction, or just bureaucratic inconsistency that shouldn't be reified into ontology.

Write a dialogue where they hash this out. The dialetheist should defend that institutional contradictions can be real and not just epistemic or pragmatic messes, maybe bringing in how paraconsistent reasoning lets officials navigate without exploding into arbitrary decisions. The classical logician should push back that accepting true contradictions here collapses the distinction between law and mere administrative noise, and should ask what empirical or practical difference it makes whether we call it a true contradiction versus a badly drafted code. They should actually respond to each other's moves - if the dialetheist mentions selective enforcement, the classical side shouldn't just ignore it and talk about explosion; they should address whether selective enforcement proves contradiction or just proves human error. Make it feel like a real argument, not two monologues, and keep both sides sounding smart.
