I am drafting a short essay under the working title "Privation and the Zero-Point: Tranquilism as Double Absence." The central claim is that tranquilism—the view that only the absence of craving and suffering carries axiological weight—should be interpreted not as a variant of negative hedonism but as an axiology grounded in privative ontology. On this reading, suffering is a positive perturbation of a baseline, while craving is not an occurrent desire but a second-order absence: the absence of the absence of suffering, or the anticipatory registration that the baseline may be disrupted. Tranquility is therefore a "double absence"—the absence of suffering together with the absence of craving—where the baseline itself is axiologically neutral rather than positively valued. The essay argues that without a clear distinction between first-order absence (no suffering) and second-order absence (no anticipation of suffering), tranquilism collapses into negative utilitarianism with only rhetorical differences in emphasis. It then considers an objection: that treating absence as normatively primary requires a metaphysics of negative properties that contemporary naturalists typically reject, and that without such commitments the "zero-point" is merely a heuristic for risk-aversion or adaptive preference. The essay concludes tentatively that tranquilism is defensible only if one accepts that absence can be causally and normatively primary—meaning the default state of a system is not "zero utility" but "no utility-bearing state at all"—and asks whether this constitutes a genuine revision of value theory or a category mistake.

Please comment on these notes. In particular: (1) Is the distinction between first-order and second-order absence coherent, or does it illegitimately conflate modal and occurrent mental states? (2) Does the collapse argument—that tranquilism becomes indistinguishable from negative utilitarianism without this distinction—actually hold, or is there a defensible intermediate position that preserves tranquilism's distinctiveness without invoking privative metaphysics? (3) Can the "zero-point" structure be reconstructed in standard welfare-economics or preference-satisfaction terms without metaphysical cost, and if so, does that show the framing is innovative or merely a redescription of baseline-sensitive utility? I am especially interested in whether the double-absence structure introduces a conceptually novel constraint on axiology or only renames familiar problems about adaptive preferences and reference-dependent welfare.
