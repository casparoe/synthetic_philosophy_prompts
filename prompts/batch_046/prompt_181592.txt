I serve on my university's Committee on Collaborative Research Norms. Over the past decade, an informal regularity has emerged in our interdisciplinary laboratories: when a senior theorist and a junior empirical researcher jointly construct a framework, they list the junior partner as "co-architect" rather than "co-author," with a shared understanding that this signals distinct but equal contribution without triggering the disciplinary disputes that explicit co-authorship would cause. The practice appears stable: participants expect it, prefer it to the available alternatives, and treat deviations as breaches of collegiality rather than mere mistakes. A proposal now before the committee would formalize this "co-architect" designation in the university's official authorship guidelines—mandating a standardized symbol, a disclosure form, and audit by the research integrity office.

Half the committee argues that formalization will protect junior researchers from exploitation and eliminate ambiguity; the other half argues that codifying the practice will destroy the very conditions that make it function as a convention in the relevant philosophical sense—namely, its arbitrariness, its basis in mutual expectation rather than authority, and its solution to a coordination problem that disappears once compliance is enforced. They invoke what they call a "reflexivity trap": the claim that institutional recognition transforms a Lewis-style convention into a bureaucratic rule, thereby dissolving the common-knowledge structure that sustained it and generating strategic behavior (symbolic compliance, form-checking, avoidance) that the original practice never produced.

Please draft the committee's written opinion for circulation before next Thursday's vote. Recommend a concrete course of action—adopt the proposal, reject it, or adopt it with specific conditions and a sunset clause—with a clear time frame for implementation or review. The opinion should treat the convention-analysis seriously: assess whether the current practice meets the conditions of a convention (a regularity in behavior, a system of mutual expectations, a coordination problem with arbitrary solutions, and common knowledge), analyze what formalization does to each condition, and address the "reflexivity trap" argument honestly rather than dismissing it. It should also engage the strongest consideration on the losing side—whether that concerns equity, transparency, or protection of vulnerable researchers—and explain why that consideration does not, in the end, determine the outcome. Write in formal policy-memo style, suitable for circulation to faculty and administrators who are not philosophers.
