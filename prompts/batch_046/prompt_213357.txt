I serve as the student representative on my school district’s Student Data Ethics Review Panel. At next Thursday’s meeting we must vote on a proposal called Project Compass: an algorithmic early-warning system that aggregates attendance logs, cafeteria transaction patterns, and library check-out records to flag students at risk of course failure. The vendor’s documentation repeatedly describes the system in intentional-language—saying it “believes” a student is disengaged, “wants” to redirect attention, and “recognizes” hidden motivational patterns that teachers miss. Some panel members argue this is harmless metaphor that helps educators take the alerts seriously; others say it obscures the physical-stance reality of statistical correlation and risks treating predictive regularities as if they were genuine mental states.

Please draft the short formal memo I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—together with a clear time frame for implementation or revision. The memo should apply Daniel Dennett’s distinction among the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance, and should specifically address whether the predictive patterns Project Compass detects count as “real patterns” in Dennett’s sense, in order to explain why adopting the intentional stance toward the algorithm is either institutionally justified or conceptually misleading in this context. It should also treat the strongest argument on the opposing side seriously, such as the claim that intentional-language improves staff engagement and that the design stance alone cannot capture the practical predictive utility educators actually need. The reasoning should be accessible to board members without philosophy backgrounds, yet precise enough that a philosopher of mind would recognize the conceptual work being done. Please write the memo in American English.
