For a graduate philosophy-of-biology workshop, design a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts respondents by their actual commitments on the species problem rather than by the labels they prefer. Start with a meta-branch: are species real entities in nature, pragmatic tools for communication, or fictions to be eliminated? From there, split by the decisive criterion—reproductive isolation, smallest monophyletic unit, ecological niche occupation, observable morphological similarity, or dynamic interaction-network stability—then by judgments on three concrete cases: a ring species where adjacent populations interbreed but the terminal ends do not, bacterial lineages blurred by extensive horizontal gene transfer, and a hybrid zone with partial reproductive barriers and merged phenotypes. The tree must have exactly eight to ten leaves, including these named positions: the Biological Species Concept, the Phylogenetic Species Concept, the Ecological Species Concept, Morphological Pragmatism, Species Nominalism, and an Interaction-Stabilization View that treats species as transient equilibrium states defined by interaction networks rather than by lineage or reproductive community. For every logical combination that remains unoccupied, provide a one-sentence diagnosis—whether incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Each node should be a concrete claim or case judgment answerable without knowing the position names. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves the tree fails to separate, particularly where reproductive-isolation and interaction-stabilization judgments coincide on hybrid zones. The deliverable should not request bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or named scholars, and should avoid verbatim quotations from any source.
