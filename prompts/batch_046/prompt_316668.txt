I recently joined the governance board of a community land trust, and we have become deadlocked over whether to adopt a hybrid ethical framework for allocating scarce housing units. Because my own family is now on the waiting list, the theoretical dispute has become personally urgent, and I need a rigorous adjudication of the arguments that have actually been exchanged.

The debate crystallized between two board members, Mara and Jonas. Mara argued that requiring every allocation decision to satisfy both deontological fairness rules—no one jumps the queue regardless of need severity—and consequentialist welfare maximization—prioritize those for whom housing yields the greatest well-being gain—is not merely politically necessary but morally rational. She said moral value systems, like economic preferences, can generate surplus through compromise: a pure deontologist and a pure consequentialist each get more of what they care about in a hybrid system than either gets in the other’s pure regime. She called this moral gains from trade.

Jonas countered that treating moral commitments as fungible preferences is a category error. He said deontological constraints are side-constraints, not preferences to be traded off; compromising them does not produce mutual benefit, it dissolves the very thing that made them valuable. He added that the hybrid framework Mara praises actually smuggles consequentialist weighing in through the back door: the fairness rules become mere thresholds that consequentialist reasoning can override once satisfied, so the deontologist has gained nothing—their values have simply been translated into consequentialist vocabulary.

Mara replied that Jonas assumes value incommensurability where none exists. She proposed that we distinguish between value-translation asymmetry—where one system’s metrics colonize the other—and genuine value co-realization, where each system retains its internal logic but operates within a bounded domain. In her view, our hybrid framework achieves co-realization: fairness rules govern the queue structure, while welfare considerations operate only within tied ranks, so neither vocabulary colonizes the other.

Jonas said this distinction collapses in practice because the boundary between tied ranks and untied ones is itself set by consequentialist reasoning about where welfare differences become significant. He argued that Mara’s co-realization is therefore unstable: the moment we need to decide how wide the tie-bands are, we have already conceded that deontological equality is merely a tie-breaker for consequentialist optimization, not an independent constraint.

Mara’s final move was that Jonas’s demand for pure, non-compromised values implies that any institutional design involving plural stakeholders is inherently corrupt. She asked whether he would reject democratic compromise on constitutional rights just because rights-language and utility-language differ.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of Mara’s and Jonas’s arguments—the initial trade analogy, the category-error and side-constraint objection, the value-translation versus co-realization distinction, the instability-of-boundaries critique, and the democratic-compromise reductio—rate whether it is clearly mistaken, partially right but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then state who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing, particularly whether there is a principled way to stabilize the boundary between co-realization and colonization that neither has proposed. Finally, reach a definite verdict on whether the board should adopt the hybrid framework, and explain whether moral compromise can genuinely yield gains analogous to economic trade.

Please state your final answer in a fixed, machine-readable form at the very end, beginning with Verdict: followed by your overall judgment and recommendation in one sentence.
