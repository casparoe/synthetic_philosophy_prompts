In discussions shaped by the Historikerstreit and by Zygmunt Bauman's claim that modernity carries a gardening logic—where the rational state cultivates order by weeding out elements defined as diseased or undesirable—the Holocaust is often interpreted either as a singular rupture or as an extreme but comparable product of bureaucratic rationality. Those who stress uniqueness argue that the racial-biological definition of the weed was historically specific and irreplaceable; those who stress comparability treat the genocide as one possible harvest of modern planning techniques. Audit Bauman's gardening analogy as it operates in this dispute. First, provide the correspondence table in detail: the gardener, the garden's boundary, the soil, the intended crop, the weed, the tools of cultivation, and the waste product. Second, identify the disanalogies that bear on uniqueness versus comparability: does gardening necessarily diffuse moral responsibility across a system in the way bureaucracy does, or does it retain a singular agent? Does the metaphor of disease falsely naturalize racial categories? Does the analogy suppress the specific intentionality of anti-Semitic ideology by framing it as functional maintenance? Third, assess whether the analogy itself carries the conclusion—whether treating the event as gardening makes comparability inevitable, or whether uniqueness can be preserved inside the metaphor by insisting on the uniqueness of the weed. Fourth, contrast the gardening image with an alternative you construct—say, modernity as an assembly line with a quality-control gate that discards defective units—and determine whether this substitute better respects both the modernity thesis and the singularity claim, or whether it introduces its own distortions. Separate cosmetic mismatches from those that actually undermine the argument each side wants to make.
