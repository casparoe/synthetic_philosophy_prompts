I am preparing the defense of a paper for an interdisciplinary seminar on philosophy of technology and embodied cognition. The thesis I am advancing is that contemporary practices of continuous biometric self-tracking—where individuals rely on algorithmic outputs from wearable devices to determine their emotional states, fatigue levels, and cognitive capacities—constitute a technologically mediated variant of Sartrean bad faith. Specifically, I argue that users flee the anguish of radical freedom by treating predictive data as revealing a fixed, essential self that they did not author, thereby evading responsibility for how they constitute themselves through action. The audience is composed primarily of philosophers of technology and cognitive scientists who are strongly committed to enactivist and extended-mind frameworks; they are likely to view such technologies as cognitive extensions rather than alienating instruments, and they generally reject Sartre's account of radical freedom as empirically implausible in light of pervasive cognitive biases and environmental scaffolding. I need a rigorous prep sheet for the question period: please identify the objections this audience will actually raise, phrased in their conceptual vocabulary, rank them by likelihood and potential damage to the thesis, indicate which objections carry real force and should be conceded in part, specify the reply that must land if the proposal is to survive, and flag the one objection I am hoping will not be raised. For each point, provide a concise reply together with an explicit note on where that reply is weakest. Throughout, please state how confident you are in each assessment, and clearly identify the single point about which you are least certain.
