Two members of my philosophy-of-technology seminar disagreed about whether modern predictive architectures solve the frame problem. Mara argued that large world-models implicitly learn invariant feature structures, so they update only the relevant latents and leave everything else untouched—precisely the selective updating the frame problem demands—without needing explicit frame axioms. Leo replied that stable weights are not the same as principled relevance: a model can preserve background features without knowing which ones ought to be preserved when a genuinely novel action opens an unforeseen causal path. Mara then proposed representing actions in negative space—encoding not what will change but which deviations should not occur—so that any unlisted effect becomes automatically salient. Leo countered that selecting which absences to list requires exactly the prior partition of relevant versus irrelevant effects that the frame problem denies us, and that her scheme either lists too much, making planning intractable, or lists too little, missing hidden side effects. They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate point by point: Mara's initial claim about implicit invariants, Leo's distinction between compression and relevance, Mara's negative-space proposal, Leo's relocation critique, and whether either position actually resolves the original frame problem. Separate settled issues from genuinely contested ones, and do not present a disputed claim as consensus. Respond with exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
