I'm the senior ethics advisor to a transitional justice commission that reviews proposed official state apologies for historical wrongs. We handle roughly three proposals annually, each under intense domestic and international scrutiny; we have a seventy-two-hour review window per case; our recommendations must be unanimous and published with explicit reasoning; and our staff are trained lawyers and historians, not philosophers. I need a reusable decision procedure — an ordered checklist with explicit pass/fail criteria, required evidence, and clearly marked judgment nodes — that translates the philosophical conditions for a legitimate collective apology into something my staff can apply consistently without philosophical training.

The procedure should operationalize the distinctions that actually determine legitimacy: whether the apologizing state is the correct moral successor to the perpetrator entity (and how to assess continuity across regime change); whether the apology is directed at the appropriate recipients, given that direct survivors may be dead, dispersed, or not the only affected party; whether the statement avoids being undermined by simultaneous justification, denial, or explicit continuation of the benefits derived from the wrong; and whether it satisfies whatever non-instrumentality and sincerity conditions apply, without requiring psychological mind-reading.

For each step, specify: the document or testimony required; the criterion for passing; the criterion for failing; and the precise point where judgment is unavoidable and must be flagged as a commission-level decision rather than a staff determination.

Then apply the procedure to three cases:

First, a democratic government apologizes for a colonial-era forced assimilation program that ended fifty years ago. The apology offers no material redress, but the government immediately dissolves the administrative bodies that executed the program.

Second, the same government apologizes for a more recent internment policy, accompanied by substantial financial compensation, but the apology is issued by a senior official who previously defended the policy, and the institutional structures that implemented it remain intact.

Third, a government issues an apology for historical chattel slavery, where no direct survivors exist, directed primarily at a diaspora that was not geographically present during the period of harm, paired with a commemorative monument but with no structural reform of the economic institutions that inherited wealth from the practice.

For each case, run the full procedure and state the recommendation. Include at least one case where the procedure yields a recommendation the commission will likely find unwelcome. Finally, specify clearly what categories of apology this procedure is not designed to evaluate, and identify the specific conditions under which it will systematically misfire or produce false positives.
