Consider the view that famines are not natural disasters but political crimes of entitlement failure: in the 1943 Bengal famine, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the Chinese Great Leap Forward, aggregate food availability did not fall below the level required for survival, and mass mortality arose instead because distribution mechanisms—markets, state provisioning, or relief—collapsed for specific populations, with the presence or absence of democratic accountability being the decisive explanatory factor rather than a mere correlation. Play this straight: what would have to be true for this thesis to be wrong? I want an honest map of its exposure. Would it be fatal if detailed demographic or supply-chain evidence showed that in one of these cases local food stocks were genuinely depleted below subsistence needs in ways that no feasible redistribution could have remedied? How damaging would it be to discover democratic regimes that experienced comparable entitlement collapses leading to mass starvation, or authoritarian regimes that successfully averted famine despite equivalent production shocks and absence of electoral feedback? Is there a risk that the concept of entitlement can be expanded so broadly—including failures of state command economies—as to render the claim unfalsifiable, and if so, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a defect? Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what a committed defender of the entitlement account would concede as genuinely counting against the view from what they would dismiss as missing the structural point, and assess whether the position has been qualified into a form that risks nothing empirically and whether that undermines its status as an explanation of these specific catastrophes.
