As a doctoral student in philosophy of religion working at the intersection of quantum foundations and comparative metaphysics, I am examining Schrödinger's later essays on Vedantic monism for a dissertation chapter on non-dual consciousness in twentieth-century physics. At a recent interdisciplinary workshop, a speaker kept invoking the formula "there is only one mind" — attributing the sentiment broadly to Schrödinger's engagement with the Upanishads — in order to dismiss debates about intersubjectivity and private mental states. The speaker treated it as a decisive objection: if consciousness is literally singular, then asking how one empirical subject relates to another is a category mistake rooted in Maya. I find this too quick. Schrödinger himself seemed to hold that personal identity and individual mortality are illusions, yet he also resisted reducing this unity to a merely physical fact, leaving an ambiguity I want to map precisely. Could you separate the distinct theses that "there is only one mind" might express in this specific Schrödinger-Vedanta context? Please lay them out as a menu: trivial or definitional readings; moderate readings that preserve some empirical distinction among subjects; and radical readings that collapse all subjectivity into one ontological substance. For each, say whether it is defensible on Schrödinger's own terms, whether it actually licenses dismissing intersubjectivity debates rather than reframing them, and whether it can accommodate the appearance of individual death and rebirth without contradiction — a tension central to Schrödinger's rejection of personal immortality alongside his rejection of reductive materialism. Finally, which reading best captures that tension, and which ones risk reducing his position to something a modest physicalist about consciousness could endorse without metaphysical cost?
