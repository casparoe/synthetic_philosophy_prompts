As a novelist drafting a scene in which mathematicians confront the possibility that their shared axiomatic foundation is silently compromised, I want readers to feel the institutional vertigo rather than merely understand it conceptually. Design a ninety-minute classroom activity for a seminar of about twelve advanced students, structured as a role-play with assigned disciplinary identities—analysts, algebraists, and logicians—each holding a major result whose proof depends on a different cluster of standard axioms. At minute thirty, introduce a hidden rule: a contradiction has been isolated not in an obvious tautology but in a rarely invoked consequence of the replacement schema, and its propagation through derived theorems is currently unmapped; the group must then negotiate, with small collective stakes—perhaps a shared provisional certification of which results remain trustworthy—which dependencies to sever, which to defend through insulation arguments, and whether to impose a moratorium on large-cardinal-adjacent machinery. The mechanics should force participants to discover dependency-tracking and epistemic risk-pooling on their own, before the facilitator names consistency concerns or Gödel’s second incompleteness result. Include a debrief that maps each negotiation phase onto a real feature of mathematical practice—disciplinary compartmentalization, the social distribution of foundational belief, and the asymmetry between local proof-checking and global consistency—while frankly noting which emotional feature of genuine foundational crisis, specifically the irreversibility of a discovered contradiction once made public, the simulation structurally cannot reproduce and must be supplied by narrative context. Keep the framing formal and avoid any request for citations, reading lists, or named texts beyond what participants already carry in the role cards.
