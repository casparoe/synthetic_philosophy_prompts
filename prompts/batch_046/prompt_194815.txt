Design a 90-minute workshop activity for 14 philosophy graduate students on the boundary between legitimate persuasion and manipulation, with particular attention to cases in which the persuader tells only the truth, applies no coercive pressure, and respects formal autonomy, yet covertly engineers the architecture of available reasons—reordering salience, hiding alternatives, or framing the choice set—so that the target’s decision feels self-authored but was structurally guided. The deliverable should be a runnable structured deliberation or simulation with assigned roles (e.g., architects who design the decision environment, choosers who decide under rules, and auditors who observe but may not intervene), small real stakes (points that determine a tangible group reward or privilege), and explicit rules forbidding falsehood and direct coercion. Arrange the rules and incentives so that participants first experience a “free” choice round, then a second round in which they discover—through direct comparison or auditor disclosure—that the space of reasons was invisibly reshaped, letting them feel the problem before it is named. Provide a minute-by-minute timeline, the debrief that maps specific moments onto philosophical distinctions (autonomy as procedural independence vs. transparency of influence, reason-responsiveness vs. engineered salience), an honest prediction of what will go wrong (e.g., choosers will personalize blame onto architects rather than recognizing structural opacity, or architects will rationalize framing as mere emphasis), and a frank admission of which feature of the philosophical issue the activity cannot reproduce—such as the long-term, iterative erosion of rational agency that distinguishes systemic manipulation from a single engineered choice—and state how the facilitator must supply that missing element verbally. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the design should stand on its own.
