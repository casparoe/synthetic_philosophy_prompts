Compose a satirical mock-policy memorandum—approximately 600 words, in the deadpan bureaucratic register of a university Office of Epistemic Affairs—declaring that the institution has formally adopted the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda (the many-sidedness of reality, including syādvāda qualified predication and nayavāda standpoint epistemology) as the binding framework for all administrative and academic decision-making. The satire must turn on taking the doctrine literally rather than metaphorically: every empirical or evaluative disagreement, from climate-data interpretation to tenure-review outcomes, is to be resolved by granting each competing standpoint its partial truth through formal syllogistic structures borrowed from Jain logic, specifically the saptabhaṅgī (sevenfold predication). Do not invent specific Jain scriptures, cite page numbers, or provide bibliographic references; instead, construct original, concrete scenarios—such as a promotion case in which a candidate’s publication record is simultaneously affirmed, denied, and declared inexpressible depending on the evaluative nayavāda, or an accreditation review in which the university’s own financial status is evaluated under all seven modal predicates—that demonstrate how practical decision-making collapses under consistent pluralism. The piece should remain fully legible to a reader who knows only that anekāntavāda rejects one-sided absolutism, but it must also include at least one structurally precise joke that depends on correctly applying syādvāda’s modal qualifier syāt (perhaps or conditionally). Maintain strict internal consistency: the administrator-author genuinely believes she is honoring intellectual diversity, never breaks character, and never slips into a hidden realist or compatibilist stance. The humor must arise from the view’s own logic pushed to its reductio, not from invented absurdities foreign to the doctrine. Conclude with an implicit reductio ad absurdum: the policy is adopted university-wide, and the final paragraph describes its first successful application—to the university’s own accreditation status—rendered in the same bureaucratic voice. Write as if drafting this assignment for a graduate seminar in comparative philosophy and political rhetoric.
