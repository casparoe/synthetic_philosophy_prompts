I need a taxonomy—structured as a branching decision tree—for sorting positions in the debate over illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, intended for use in an advanced undergraduate philosophy-of-mind seminar. The instrument should classify respondents by the answers they give to concrete cases and claims, not by the labels they prefer. Begin with a root case: a subject sincerely reports that seeing red involves a qualitative residue—“what it is like”—that survives even a complete neural and functional map of the state. From there, every branch node must be a specific diagnostic question that a student can answer without knowing which interpretation it selects, such as whether the residue is best explained as an indexical mode of presentation, as a transparency failure, as a misrepresentation of access-conscious states, or as a systematic conceptual error.

The tree must place at least the following seven leaves in the logical space defined by the branches: (1) a property-dualist or non-physicalist realist; (2) a phenomenal-concept-strategy physicalist; (3) an indexical-mode physicalist, on which phenomenal properties are real but essentially indexical, so their seeming non-physicality is an artifact of indexical presentation rather than ontological distinctness; (4) a transparency-based physicalist, on which phenomenal properties are real physical properties but necessarily transparent, generating the illusion of an intrinsic qualitative residue that cannot be introspectively isolated; (5) a strong representational illusionist, for whom phenomenal properties are entirely misrepresented access properties; (6) a conceptual-error illusionist, for whom the concept “phenomenal” is systematically empty yet functionally deployed; and (7) a metacognitive or global-workspace illusionist, for whom a higher-order workspace mechanism produces a false belief in phenomenal ownership.

Construct the tree from the logical combinations first: state the branching questions, then locate each named view in that space. For any combination that remains unoccupied—such as a dualist who denies phenomenal properties altogether, or a physicalist who both affirms and denies their reality—note it briefly and diagnose whether the vacancy is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Likewise, flag any pair of leaves that your chosen diagnostic questions cannot genuinely separate. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific texts; the taxonomy should be answerable entirely through reasoning about the cases. Keep every node phrased as a question a respondent could answer without prior knowledge of the philosophical position it leads to.
