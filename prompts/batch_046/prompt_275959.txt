Write the desiderata that any theory of material constitution—specifically, an account of how a material object like a statue relates to the matter that makes it up—must satisfy to count as adequate, with the motivating case behind each. I want the full specification first, not a survey of existing answers.

The theory must correctly adjudicate at least the following families of cases, and for each explain why getting it wrong is a failure of the theory rather than a mere oddity:
1. The classic coincidence case: a statue shaped from a lump of clay exists at the same time and place as the lump, yet they seem to differ in modal and historical properties (the statue could survive being reshaped; the lump could survive being squashed into a ball without being destroyed).
2. Gradual replacement: every particle of the original clay is replaced, one by one, with new matter of identical chemical kind, leaving macro-form and intended function intact.
3. The reverse-cast case: the original statue is destroyed and its clay pulverized and chemically altered; that very matter is then used to fill a mold reproducing the exact original shape, but with reversed causal history and no part-by-part continuity.
4. The synchronization case: two artists, working independently in different locations, simultaneously form statues with identical form from two distinct batches of clay drawn from the same homogeneous source.
5. The distributed-statue case: the constituent parts are spatially scattered—linked only by a sustained functional or informational network—yet continue to realize the same artistic form and intended function.

For each family, state whether the theory must deliver a single object, two coincident objects, no object, or an indeterminate answer, and justify that verdict.

Next, list the distinctions the theory must preserve without collapsing them:
- constitution versus identity,
- constitution versus mere composition,
- an artifact's kind-conditions (design, function, provenance) versus its material kind-conditions,
- temporal endurance of one object versus a sequence of temporal parts or stages,
- causal-historical continuity versus present structural similarity.

Then list the external constraints the theory cannot violate:
- consistency with the physical absence of sharp spatiotemporal boundaries at the macro-scale,
- no commitment to a metaphysically privileged frame that fixes object-boundaries independently of physical practice,
- logical consistency: the account must not entail both that the statue and the clay are identical and that they differ in properties, unless it supplies a well-motivated distinction that dissolves the contradiction.

Mark which of these are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps—for example, whether coincidence must be allowed, whether three-dimensional endurance must be preserved, whether any appeal to non-physical form or function is forbidden.

Identify any subset of desiderata that appears jointly unsatisfiable—e.g., if preserving all five case verdicts together with the no-collapsing distinctions forces a contradiction or an implausible ontology. If there is such a tension, say exactly where it lies and whether it is a fatal problem for the domain or merely forces a choice among camps.

Finally, briefly—no more than a short paragraph per view—indicate how the main contenders fare against the list: the strict identity view, the non-identity coincidence view (statue and clay as distinct but spatially coincident), the four-dimensionalist stage or perdurantist account, and a hylomorphic or functionalist account that treats form or intended function as partly constitutive. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; score them only against your criteria.

Conclude by flagging the single desideratum that, in your judgment, eliminates the largest number of popular theories, and explain why. Keep the weight on the criteria, their motivating cases, and their interrelations rather than on the scoring. The deliverable is the specification itself.
