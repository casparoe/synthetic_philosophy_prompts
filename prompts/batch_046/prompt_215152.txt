I spent three decades in experimental physics before retiring and turning, rather late, to philosophy of mind. I have been working through Lucas's argument and Penrose's elaboration of it, and I want to try out a framing I have not encountered in the literature—partly, perhaps, because it comes from my old discipline.

The familiar anti-mechanist claim runs as follows: for any consistent formal system F adequate for arithmetic, Gödel's method yields a sentence G_F that F cannot prove, yet a human reasoner can recognize that G_F is true; therefore human mathematical insight exceeds any formal system. The standard responses question whether humans are genuinely consistent, or whether we have privileged access to our own Gödel sentences.

My own thought is different. Rather than treating the recognition of G_F as evidence of non-computability, I want to ask whether it might be understood as a feature of evolved self-modeling systems. In molecular biology, DNA replication includes proofreading machinery—enzymes that detect and correct errors in the genome's own copying. This is a functional analog of self-reference: the system encodes not only its products but also a model of its own operation sufficient to catch specific failures.

If the brain is a physical system that evolved to model its own reasoning—perhaps for social prediction, planning, or error monitoring—then its capacity to recognize the boundary conditions of any particular formalization of its reasoning might be just such a self-correcting adaptation. On this "evolutionary Gödelianism," seeing that G_F is true is not a transcendence of mechanism but a mechanism's recognition of its own limits, comparable to a proofreader recognizing a mutation pattern it was selected to detect.

This seems to me to preserve the phenomenon without the metaphysical conclusion. But I am unsure whether the analogy holds. The syntactic self-reference in Gödel numbering may differ fundamentally from functional self-reference in biological error correction. I also worry that explaining our belief in G_F in evolutionary terms addresses its causation without touching its normative justification—why we are entitled to hold it true.

Could you comment on these notes? Specifically: does the DNA-proofreading analogy illuminate the Lucas-Penrose argument, or is it a category mistake? Does this framing collapse the distinction between human reasoning and formal computation, or does it leave room for a meaningful anti-mechanist residue? And is there an obvious objection—perhaps from evolutionary biology or the philosophy of mathematics—that undermines the proposal?

Please do not supply bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am working from the primary texts and my own scientific background, and I want to see whether the idea stands on its own.
