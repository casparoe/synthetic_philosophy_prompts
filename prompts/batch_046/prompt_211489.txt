At a recent rationalist meetup, two regulars—Jordan and Riley—spent an hour arguing about whether large language models should be said to hold beliefs in the intentional-stance sense, and I listened without taking sides. Jordan opened by observing that if treating an LLM as believing, say, that Paris is in France or that 7 times 6 equals 42 reliably predicts its outputs across ordinary prompts, then Dennett's criteria for the intentional stance are satisfied: predictive success justifies the attribution regardless of internal mechanics. Riley replied that predictive success inside a narrow training distribution is insufficient; human belief attribution works because beliefs exhibit counterfactual robustness—if I believe Paris is in France, that belief survives distractions, rephrasing, and mood shifts—whereas an LLM's corresponding output can be flipped by a single adversarial token or an out-of-distribution framing, so what we have is surface correlation rather than a stable intentional state.

Jordan then introduced a distinction he called functional credence, arguing that belief attribution should sometimes be assigned to the coupled human-AI system rather than to the model in isolation. On this view, the LLM functions as a credence aggregator within an epistemic network: the user integrates the model's outputs with their own reasoning to form beliefs, and attributing belief to the model describes its functional role in that network, not an inner mental state. Riley countered that this collapses the intentional stance into pure instrumentalism—if we can attribute belief to a calculator that returns correct sums, then belief has lost its explanatory work and become merely a label for a trusted tool in domain D. He added that what genuinely distinguishes belief is representational inertia, by which he meant the tendency of a representation to resist local perturbation and maintain coherence across contexts. Since LLM embeddings shift discontinuously with token-level changes, they lack representational inertia, and therefore lack beliefs even within a functional-credence framework.

Jordan's final move was to deny that representational inertia is binary. He argued that human neural representations are themselves locally fragile—transcranial magnetic stimulation can disrupt specific beliefs, and human reasoning collapses under fatigue or framing effects—so the relevant metric is not absolute inertia but graded predictive utility within a specified operational envelope. If an LLM maintains consistent outputs across a well-bounded set of prompts, then within that envelope it possesses the kind of stability that justifies intentional attribution, even if stability breaks at the boundary.
