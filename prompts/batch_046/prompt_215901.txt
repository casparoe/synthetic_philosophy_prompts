Here is an argument I built for a seminar on cultural explanation, and I find it suspicious because its conclusion seems to destroy the very possibility of explaining macrohistorical change through values.

(1) For a value-system V to causally explain an economic outcome O, there must be a specific mechanism—call it a value-translation pathway—through which the internalization of V becomes reproducible behaviors that aggregate into O.

(2) Weber's Protestant ethic thesis asserts precisely this: an elective affinity between ascetic Protestant values and capitalist accumulation, mediated by a pathway that rationalizes labor and capital reinvestment rather than consumption.

(3) But value-translation pathways are structurally underdetermined by the historical record. Given any value-system V and any observed outcome O, an interpreter can construct a pathway linking them; and given any case where V is present but O absent, the interpreter can invoke a competing value-system V' or an institutional blocker B that disrupted the pathway.

(4) Therefore, if such pathways count as legitimate causal mechanisms, they are available for every culture-outcome pairing. The explanatory form risks no contradiction from evidence and makes no distinctive prediction.

(5) An explanation that can accommodate any pairing without predictive constraint is not a causal account but a form of axiological overfitting—like a statistical model with enough parameters to fit any dataset.

(6) So Weber's thesis, if taken as a template for cultural causation, applies universally and explains nothing in particular; the only escape is to deny that values have any causal efficacy and reduce all explanation to material conditions.

(7) Hence either cultural values never cause economic outcomes, or Weber's specific claim about Protestantism and capitalism is trivial.

The reasoning feels like it proves too much: it collapses a specific historical thesis into either universal tautology or global falsehood. I want to keep the idea that values can genuinely cause outcomes, but I cannot see which step blocks the slide. Is the error in (3), which assumes that interpretive flexibility entails causal emptiness? Or in (4), which conflates the availability of post-hoc narratives with the absence of predictive constraints? Or is the argument valid, and the real cost lies in accepting that cultural explanation must be sharply circumscribed—perhaps to cases where a value-translation pathway can be independently specified before the outcome is observed? If I reject (3) to save Weber, do I commit myself to an implausibly rigid mapping between values and behaviors? And if the flaw is elsewhere, what does repairing it require me to pay?
