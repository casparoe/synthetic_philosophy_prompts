I'm preparing a position paper for an interdisciplinary genocide-studies workshop and want a rigorous consistency audit of my framework before I circulate it. Here is where I've landed:

(1) I treat the Holocaust as qualitatively unique in a strong, non-quantitative sense: the convergence of a biologically totalizing ideology—aiming at the annihilation of every Jew regardless of assimilation, utility, or geography—with state-wide organizational execution makes it incomparable in kind to other mass atrocities.

(2) At the same time, I accept Bauman's analysis that the genocide was made possible not mainly by exceptional ideological fanaticism but by specifically modern conditions—bureaucratic division of labor, instrumental rationality, technological distancing, and the separation of moral responsibility from technical action—which enabled ordinary people to participate in extraordinary violence.

(3) I endorse comparative genocide studies that proceed via structural homology—examining shared bureaucratic, dehumanizing, or state-organizational features—while insisting that comparison targets structures rather than moral weight and should illuminate institutional vulnerabilities without diminishing specific histories.

(4) I interpret the West German Historikerstreit of the 1980s not as a substantive historiographical dispute about method but as a proxy conflict over collective identity and normalization: the push for comparability served largely as rhetorical cover for relativizing German guilt, while the uniqueness defense functioned primarily as a political boundary maintaining irreducible responsibility.

(5) Given (2), I hold that the modern bureaucratic conditions Bauman identifies are globally distributed features of modern organization, not peculiar to 1940s Germany; consequently I treat the Holocaust as both historically specific and an exemplary limiting case that reveals structural risks inherent in modernity everywhere.

(6) Finally, I maintain that framing uniqueness as an ontological property of the event—rather than as a strategic, discursive boundary—risks sacralizing the Holocaust and actually obstructs the comparative institutional learning that Bauman's critique demands.

Please audit this package. I want you to go through the pairs systematically and tell me which commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and for each real tension how severe it is and what the least costly revision would be. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally problematic. Be specific about whether the friction lies between (1) and (2), (2) and (5), (3) and (4), (6) and (1), or elsewhere, and say exactly which commitment must give or be qualified. Then tell me which single revision—giving up or substantially weakening one commitment—would clean up the largest number of tensions at once. Give a committed answer: take definite positions, justify your ranking with reasons, and do not merely lay out options for me to choose between. I am especially interested in whether accepting Bauman's bureaucratic-rationality explanation undermines my uniqueness claim's reliance on exceptional ideology, and whether my dismissal of the Historikerstreit as identity politics leaves my own comparative practice without methodological defense.
