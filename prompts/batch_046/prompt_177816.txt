I serve on an audit board for autonomous and adaptive systems, and I must file a stance-and-pattern assessment for every incident report that reaches my desk—roughly two per week, with a 45-minute deadline, no philosophy consultant on call, and my signature required on the final form. I work with a modified version of Dennett’s framework of real patterns and the three explanatory stances (physical, design, intentional), but I impose two additional constraints that are not in the standard literature. First, the stance-purity rule: an explanation must not borrow predictive vocabulary from a higher stance unless it can specify explicit translation rules back down to the lower stance; if it borrows without such rules, that is stance leakage, and the audit fails at that gate. Second, the compression-boundary test: describe the predictive pattern in the lowest stance that can capture it; then translate that description upward by exactly one stance; if the translated description is not genuinely shorter—measured in predictive rules needed—than a direct description formulated natively in the higher stance, then the pattern is stance-local rather than cross-stance real, and it cannot support an upward explanation.

Turn this framework into a reusable audit procedure: a step-by-step decision flow in words, with explicit tests at each gate, that I or any other auditor can run in under 45 minutes without external reference. The deliverable must include: (a) a stance-selection protocol that determines the correct starting stance from observable evidence alone, not from prior intuition about the system’s purpose; (b) the compression-boundary test applied as a concrete comparison of rule-sets; (c) the stance-purity check, with specific indicators of leakage; (d) clearly marked judgment points—where the procedure cannot eliminate discretion and an auditor must sign off personally—together with a brief note on the risk of each; and (e) a catalog of failure modes, especially cases where the procedure forces an intentional-stance classification that triggers stricter regulatory review even though the auditor suspects a design-stance explanation is adequate.

Then apply the completed procedure to three hypothetical incidents, in this order:

1. A warehouse robot that consistently halts before crossing a line of blue tape on the floor, but only when the tape is wet; the sensors record no consistent physical anomaly, and the safety manual lists no tape-related protocol.
2. A hiring-filter language model that rejects applicants who use collective-first-person pronouns in cover letters; the engineers claim this is an emergent compression artifact of the training objective, yet the correlation is highly stable and predicts rejection more accurately than any documented feature.
3. A climate-adaptive building system that adjusts ventilation by computing what it calls “anticipated discomfort” for each occupant; it achieves lower energy use than any thermostat-based design-stance controller, but classifying its computation as intentional-stance (attributing predictive beliefs) would subject the building to human-subject oversight rules that the auditor wishes to avoid.

For each case, show the procedure’s step-by-step output, identify every judgment call, and highlight the result that I, as the auditor, will find unwelcome. Finally, state precisely what the procedure cannot do—name at least two categories of incident it is not designed to handle—and flag the single operational point where it is most likely to misfire in practice.
