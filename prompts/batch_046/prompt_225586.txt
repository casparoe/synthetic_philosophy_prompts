I have been following debates about Buddhist anattā — the denial of a permanent, underlying self — and its interaction with modern philosophy of personal identity, and I keep noticing a specific argumentative pattern that I do not have a precise name for.

Here are two concrete instances. First, a thinker argues that what we call "I" is only a temporary cluster of physical and mental events — bodily processes, feelings, perceptions, thoughts, and moments of awareness — with no hidden owner or core that persists through change. Yet when asked how we can justify caring about our own future suffering, or holding someone responsible for actions performed years earlier, the same thinker appeals to a chain of cause-and-effect connections among these events. This chain tracks exactly the features one would expect a permanent self to track: it preserves memories, carries forward habits and character traits, and links present choices to future consequences. The thinker insists that this chain is "only a practical convention", but in practice it is treated as if it picks out a real, bounded individual with special claims on present concern. The self is evacuated in theory, but its functional boundaries are rebuilt in the machinery of continuity.

Second, in discussions of continuation across lives without a transmigrating soul, some proponents treat the causal bridge between one life and the next as if it selects a specific future being for whom present moral and prudential concern is appropriate. The boundaries of that bridge appear to be drawn precisely where self-interest would draw them — favouring closer causal links, shared mental dispositions, and inherited consequences — even though there is said to be no single "person" that traverses the bridge. Once again, everything that matters about being the same individual is preserved, except the metaphysical label.

Does this exact configuration — the metaphysical evacuation of the self combined with the functional reconstruction of its boundaries through causal-continuity relations — have an established name in Buddhist philosophy or in contemporary philosophy of personal identity? I am not asking for the general distinction between conventional and ultimate truth, nor for standard reductionism about the self; I am asking whether there is a specific concept for the move in which continuity relations are made to do all the normative and explanatory work that the denied self performed, while being officially classified as merely conventional. If there is such a concept, how does it differ from ordinary reliance on conventional truth? If there is not, can you define the phenomenon precisely, specify whether its continuity must be causal rather than narrative or social, and distinguish it from nearby argumentative failures such as motte-and-bailey equivocation or plain ambiguity?
