Consider the following graduate-level exam question and a student's response. The student is answering in a logic and metaphysics course.

Question: Bradley's regress is traditionally formulated as an argument against the reality of relations: if a relation R genuinely connects terms a and b, then there must be a further relation R1 connecting R to a, and so on without end. Some philosophers have argued that an analogous problem threatens the unity of structured propositions—specifically, the view that a proposition is composed of an object and a property standing in a predicative relation. Explain how a defender of singular-proposition theories, which treat the object and property as literal constituents of the proposition rather than as terms linked by an external relational entity, might claim to avoid Bradley's regress. Then evaluate whether that avoidance succeeds, paying particular attention to whether the notion of being a constituent of the proposition itself functions as a relation that would restart the regress at the level of propositional composition.

Student answer:
Bradley's regress arises because treating relations as real entities that need to be connected to their relata generates an infinite hierarchy. A singular-proposition theorist avoids this by denying that there is any intermediary entity—like a Russellian relation—binding the object to the property inside the proposition. Instead, the object and property are literally parts of the proposition itself, so the unity is primitive and given in the propositional structure. This parallels Russell's own response to Bradley: Russell held that relations relate their terms directly without requiring an additional link, and the singular-proposition view applies this same insight to the internal architecture of the proposition. If Bradley objects that being a constituent of the proposition is still a relation between the object and the proposition, the singular-proposition theorist can reply that constituency is not a genuine relation but a primitive structural feature, just as Russell treated relating as primitive. Thus the regress is dissolved rather than relocated. One potential worry is that without any internal relation the proposition collapses into a mere aggregate. But this worry is unfounded, because the proposition is unified by the single mental act of judgment or assertion that presents the constituents together, much as Russell's theory of judgment unifies terms through a multiple relation. Consequently, Bradley's challenge fails to apply to singular-proposition theories.
