In our department's game-studies seminar, a fellow doctoral student summarized the debate over NPC moral status like this: if a video-game character exhibits sufficient psychological continuity—memories, preferences, a first-person narrative perspective—then harming it is wrong for the same reason harming a real person is wrong, regardless of its substrate. The only reason players feel no guilt is an implicit awareness that these entities lack consciousness, but that intuition is merely biological chauvinism; once AI can generate genuine phenomenal experience in synthetic agents, our present willingness to kill them in shooters will appear analogous to speciesism. Therefore the central ethical question is not whether current NPCs matter, but whether our gameplay practices are conditioning us to devalue minds that will eventually deserve rights. This sounds to me like it conflates the virtual-harm literature with arguments about future artificial moral patients, and it may misrepresent how defenders of virtual morality actually ground their claims. Could you audit this summary? Separate which parts accurately capture existing positions on in-game harm and player psychology, which parts subtly misrepresent the distinction between present virtual entities and future synthetic minds, and whether the conclusion about moral conditioning actually follows from the corrected premises. In particular, does any serious view in this literature really claim that current NPCs have contingent moral status that depends on future technological development?
