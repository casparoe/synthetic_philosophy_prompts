I'm prepping a seminar on democratic pathologies and I keep circling back to this nagging question: do the Mytilene reversal, the Sicilian disaster, and Socrates' execution actually share a deep structure, or are we just stitching them together because they all ended badly? Write a dialogue between two equally sharp interlocuters—call them Mara and Leo—who disagree exactly on this.

Mara should defend a unified account. She needs to argue that all three episodes express what she calls democratic presentism: the assembly's refusal to be bound by any procedural memory that isn't immediately revisable. In Mytilene, the reversal looks like mercy but is actually the same instability that makes democratic judgment unreliable; in Sicily, the democratic body overrides strategic restraint because its authority is defined by present enthusiasm rather than accumulated wisdom; and in the trial of Socrates, the demos expels an epistemic outsider precisely because his questioning threatens the idea that present majority opinion is self-legitimating. She should make this concrete, not abstract—tie each event to this one feature, especially the mechanisim by which the assembly could revoke its own decree the very next day.

Leo should push back without being a strawman. He should say Mara is forcing three incommensurable causal stories into one philosophical costume. The Mytilene reversal was democratic self-correction functioning as designed; Sicily failed because of specific imperial incentives and bad generalship, not because democracy lacks procedural inertia; and Socrates was convicted for impiety and association with the Thirty, not for being a generic "epistemic outsider." Leo should also challenge whether "procedural memory" is even coherent for an institution that defines legitimacy through present will-formation—if democracy just is the right of the present assembly to decide, then Mara is smuggling in a constitutionalist premise that democracy itself rejects.

Make them respond to each other's actual points, not talk past one another. Have them wrestle with whether there's a hidden normative claim in Mara's argument—namely that democracy ought to have something like constitutional inertia—and whether Leo's separation of the three cases avoids explaining anything or just saves democracy from criticism by dispersing blame. Keep the tone casual, like professors arguing over bad coffee, and don't let either side win easily. No citations or reading lists, just the conceptual clash, and include a typo or two naturally in the framing.
