I am writing a philosophy essay on abortion ethics, and I am trying to determine whether my intuitions about specific cases can be captured by a single principle. In Judith Thomson's violinist thought experiment, I believe you are permitted to unplug yourself from the violinist, since no one is obligated to serve as life support without consent; however, if you had explicitly agreed to stay connected, I think unplugging would be wrong, which suggests that consent modifies the bodily-autonomy claim. In a hypothetical case where medical technology allows a fetus at twenty weeks to be transferred to an artificial womb, I feel that terminating the pregnancy should be permissible only if the woman declines both carrying to term and the transfer—not if she demands that the fetus be destroyed—but I cannot explain why this feels different from a standard first-trimester abortion. I also think abortion is clearly justified in cases of rape or lethal fetal anomalies such as anencephaly, whereas abortion at eight months for purely elective reasons strikes me as seriously wrong, and using abortion repeatedly as contraception strikes me as wrong in a different, perhaps less grave, way. At the same time, I find Don Marquis's future-like-ours argument somewhat persuasive for late-term abortions but not for very early embryos that lack any neural structure. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps combining elements of consent, harm, and potential personhood—that accommodates most of these judgments? If not, which of my verdicts should be revised? Please also tell me what your principle predicts for the artificial-womb scenario, since I keep revising my view on it.
