In a seminar on early twentieth-century value theory, two of my peers—whom I will call Rowan and Sable—became locked in a dispute over how to read G. E. Moore’s treatment of intrinsic goods in Principia Ethica, specifically the relation between aesthetic appreciation and personal affection. Rowan maintained that Moore’s doctrine of organic unity implies that the contemplation of beauty possesses only derivative value unless it occurs within, or is directed toward, a relationship of friendship or love; on Rowan’s reading, a solitary individual experiencing a sublime landscape or an elegant proof enjoys at best a fragmentary good, because the greatest intrinsic value belongs to the whole composed of consciousness plus its object, and the highest such wholes for Moore are those involving affectionate relations between persons. Sable countered that this collapses two distinct goods into one: Moore lists both the appreciation of beautiful objects and the enjoyment of personal affection among the supreme goods, and while he certainly holds that a shared aesthetic experience can form an organic whole of higher value, he never suggests that isolated aesthetic consciousness is worthless—only that it is a different, and independently valuable, state of affairs.

Rowan pressed the point with a hypothetical: imagine a mathematician who spends decades in isolation deriving beautiful theorems, never forming close attachments, yet experiencing profound aesthetic satisfaction in the structures he discovers. If Moore truly regards friendship as among the supreme goods, Rowan argued, then such a life must be judged gravely deficient, and the mathematician’s aesthetic experiences must be seen as failing to realize the kind of value that would redeem them—suggesting that for Moore, beauty without friendship is like a melody without harmony, formally present but lacking the relational completeness that generates the highest worth. Sable admitted that Moore would indeed judge the hermit’s life less good than one enriched by love, but insisted that Rowan was conflating comparative judgment with constitutive dependence: the mathematician’s contemplative states still possess positive intrinsic value, and the deficiency of his life stems from the absence of friendship, not from the emptiness of his aesthetic pleasures. She added that if Rowan were correct, then any experience of beauty lacking an interpersonal frame—listening to music alone, for instance—would be nearly valueless, a consequence she took to be implausible both as an interpretation of Moore and as an account of human flourishing.

Rowan then introduced a further refinement: he granted that isolated aesthetic experiences might have some minimal value, but claimed that Moore’s emphasis on organic unity means their value is not merely augmented but transformed by being shared; therefore, to assess the worth of beauty in a life, one must ask not whether it is present in isolation, but whether it participates in a larger relational whole. Sable replied that this still fails to establish dependence, since transformation upon combination is compatible with the prior independence of the parts—salt retains its taste when dissolved in soup, yet its flavor is not constituted by the broth. She concluded that both parties might be missing Moore’s subtle distinction between the value of a whole and the value contributed by each part, and that the real disagreement concerns whether friendship functions as a necessary condition, an enhancing context, or a parallel good of comparable rank. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: evaluate Rowan’s initial interpretation, Sable’s response, Rowan’s hermit-mathematician counterexample, Sable’s reply about comparative versus constitutive judgment, Rowan’s refinement about transformation, and Sable’s salt-in-soup analogy. For each move, indicate whether it is clearly mistaken, largely correct but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then identify what, if anything, both disputants are overlooking about Moore’s broader framework—particularly whether there is a principled way to determine when an experience forms an organic whole with another, rather than merely co-occurring with it.
