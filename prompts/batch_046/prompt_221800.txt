Write a villanelle—nineteen lines arranged as five tercets followed by a quatrain, with two refrains (A1 and A2) and rhyme scheme ABA—intended to open a graduate seminar on Kant's transcendental deduction in the Critique of Pure Reason. The subject should be the specific architecture of transcendental arguments: the regressive path that moves from the fact of experience to its necessary conditions; the distinction between the subjective deduction (tracing the origin of the categories in the faculties of understanding) and the objective deduction (proving their necessary validity for objects); and the characteristic vulnerability that such arguments collapse into empirical psychology—merely mapping the mind's own grammar rather than establishing normative conditions for all possible experience.

Assign the refrains structural work. Let refrain A1 pose the regressive condition in the form of a question about what must already hold for experience to be given, and let refrain A2 voice the circularity objection that the speaker has only read their own syntax back to them. Through the first four tercets, build the naive argument: invoke the synthesis of apprehension, reproduction, and recognition; show the speaker finding conditions inside consciousness and assuming that finding them proves them universal. The turn should occur in the fifth tercet or the final quatrain, where the speaker recognizes that a condition discovered within experience cannot be shown necessary for experience without smuggling the conclusion into the premise. The rhyme scheme and repetition should formalize this trap: each return of the refrains should tighten the lock between claim and objection, and the final quatrain should take a definite stance—either treating the argument as tragically self-undermining but still normatively urgent, or rejecting it as a misdiagnosed psychological fact—rather than leaving both options suspended. The imagery and line logic should make the reasons for that choice visible, not merely named.

The poem should be accurate enough that a specialist in Kant recognizes the B-Deduction's two-step structure and the psychologism charge, but it should not rely on bibliographies, citations, or references to specific secondary texts that a model without web access could not verify. After the poem, provide a brief note (three to four sentences) explaining exactly where you have compressed Kant's argument or relied on a contested reading—for example, whether you conflate the subjective and objective deductions for poetic economy, whether you frame the problem through Strawson's reformulation of the bounds of sense rather than Kant's own framing, or whether you treat singular reference as the central case rather than judgment. Be explicit about any interpretive liberty taken, and state clearly which side of the circularity problem the poem ultimately endorses and why.
