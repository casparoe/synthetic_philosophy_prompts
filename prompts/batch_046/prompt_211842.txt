I'm on the ethics and policy board at Riverside Psychatric, and we've got this weird case that's turning into a charter fight. A long-term patient with cyclical addiction — call him J — signed a Ulysses contract last year while fully competent: if he's brought in during an acute episode and demands discharge or a change in withdrawal meds, staff should ignore that request and stick to the protocol he pre-specified. Now J's family and his current clinicial director want the board to do more than just honor it this once; they want us to amend our clinical governance charter so that future boards can't easily overturn these contracts — basically a constitutional precommitment clause baked into hospital policy. Our chair wants a memo by Friday recommending either adoption of the charter amendment with safeguards, or rejection with an alternative, and the recommendation needs a concrete timeline. I need to write the memo for circulation.

The philosophical mess is that this isn't just about one guy binding his future self — it's about the institution binding its future self to respect his binding. The strongest argument for the amendment is that without institutional precommitment, the Ulysses contract is just a suggestion; every new clinical director can waive it, which makes the patient's present autonomy kind of hollow. But the strongest argument against is that constitutional-style entrenchment in a clinical setting risks locking in today's values over tomorrow's better judgment — plus there's a democratic worry about one board tying the hands of future committees who might face different evidence or different patients. The memo should take both sides seriously, not just quote patient-autonomy platitudes, and it needs to land on a specific recommendation — I'm leaning toward a limited, time-bound adoption with a mandatory review trigger — but I want the reasoning laid out clearly. Also, there's this niche framing some of the drafters are using, 'nested sovereignty,' where the patient delegates binding authority to the institution, which then binds itself... it sounds fancy but I think it actually confuses the autonomy argument, and the memo should address whether that framing holds up or collapses into regular paternalism.

Can you draft the memo? It should be formal enough that the board can vote on it, but it doesn't need to be a law-review article. Include a clear bottom-line recommendation with a time frame — say, pilot the charter amendment for eighteen months with a mandatory ethics review at month twelve — and make sure the losing side gets an honest paragraph, not a strawman. No citations needed, just the reasoning.
