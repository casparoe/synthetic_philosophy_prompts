I am preparing a seminar on war and ethics, and I want to read Erich Maria Remarque’s All Quiet on the Western Front through Hegel’s philosophy of war in the Philosophy of Right — specifically his claim that war is not an absolute evil but an ethical moment in which the particular (individual life and private interest) is subordinated to the universal (the sovereign state), preventing the stagnation of civil society, and that the soldier’s willingness to risk death demonstrates the highest form of ethical virtue. Hegel also distinguishes true, conscious courage — a deliberate sacrifice for the universal — from mere mechanical obedience within a military apparatus. Read the novel against this framework: where does Remarque dramatise Hegel’s idea that war tests and preserves the ethical health of the state, and where does he show the modern industrial state reducing soldiers to mechanised parts that consume them without any genuine universal purpose? Focus on specific scenes — the schoolteacher’s exhortation sending boys to the front, Paul Baumer’s home leave and estrangement from civilian life, the shell-hole encounter with the French soldier Gérard Duval, and the death of Stanislaus Katczinsky — and say what each does to Hegel’s distinction between mechanical bravery and conscious sacrifice for the universal. Does the novel ultimately vindicate Hegel by suggesting that only in such extremity do the characters glimpse a solidarity beyond private interest, or does it expose that Hegel’s “ethical tonic” collapses when the state demands sacrifice for an empty cause? I want a reading that takes both Remarque and Hegel seriously, that does not reduce the novel to an illustration, and that ends by saying what the novel adds to the philosophical debate about war’s ethical significance that argument alone cannot.
