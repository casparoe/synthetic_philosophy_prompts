As a journalist who covers science and philosophy, I have been asked to draft the opening remarks for the dedication of the Vasquez Memorial Grove, an arboretum installation at a public research university honoring Dr. Elena Vasquez, an ecologist and independent scholar of classical Chinese thought who died earlier this year. The audience is deliberately mixed: her family and close friends who are not philosophers, biologists and philosophers who collaborated with her, graduate students, and local naturalists who knew her only through fieldwork. The speech must do more than decorate a eulogy with references; Zhuangzi’s view of death needs to shape what can and cannot be said. Specifically, the address should reject both the false comfort that Elena now watches over the grove from elsewhere, and the opposite Epicurean-style dismissal that death is simply nothing to us. Instead, it should draw on Zhuangzi’s less-cited framing—particularly the analogy in which a body returning to earth is likened to a sleeper returning to an immense, unawakened source—to present death as a transformation within a continuous natural process rather than as an endpoint or annihilation of an essential self. Because Elena insisted on placing a living decomposing-log nursery at the center of the memorial, where fungal networks visibly break fallen timber into soil for new saplings, the speaker should treat this installation not as scenery but as an active part of the argument: the rhetoric should address the decay directly and explain why she wanted living decomposition, not a polished monument, to mark her absence.

The speech should last about five to seven minutes, genuinely comfort people who are grieving without telling them anything Elena would have considered false, and avoid quoting Zhuangzi verbatim—paraphrase his ideas in plain, direct language. After the address, please include a brief, separate note stating how confident you are in this rendering of Zhuangzi’s position and explicitly identifying the point about which you are least sure, especially the connection between his anti-essentialist view of the self and his corresponding attitude toward mourning. Please do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
