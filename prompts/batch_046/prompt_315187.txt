I have been trying to understand how quantum suicide and quantum immortality relate, and I think there is a tension that undermines the usual first-person argument for the many-worlds interpretation. Quantum immortality, as I understand it, entails that for any lethal event there will always be at least one decoherent branch in which my consciousness continues, which is why from a first-person perspective I should never expect to experience death. Quantum suicide is then presented as an experimental amplification of this idea: by linking my survival to a quantum event, I ensure that in branches where the event goes one way I am dead, and in others I survive, so that continued conscious experience supposedly confirms branching. But here is my confusion: if quantum immortality is already true, then even without any apparatus, every lethal scenario already produces a survival branch. That means in a single-world interpretation, I could always retrospectively attribute my survival to a lucky quantum fluctuation in the one actual world. So doesn't quantum immortality erase the very distinction—between guaranteed branching and lucky coincidence—that quantum suicide is meant to establish? Additionally, if one applies the Everettian decision-theoretic framework that weights outcomes by branch amplitude, the subjective expectation of finding oneself alive seems to depend on how observer-moments are normalized across worlds. Given this, is the quantum-suicide argument actually self-defeating once quantum immortality is taken seriously, and is the whole reasoning vitiated by a false assumption that branch existence automatically translates into first-person inevitability regardless of the underlying measure?
