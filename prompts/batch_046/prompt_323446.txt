I'm a philosophy prof consulting with a regional land-use board, and they keep sending proposals framed as "this development actually helps wild animals by cutting suffering in nature." I want a reusable decision procedure — like a flowchart in words — that my staff (mostly ecologists with no phil background) can run in about 30 mins to decide whether altering or destroying a given habitat improves, harms, or leaves flat the net welfare of the wild animals there. Ground it in density-dependent population dynamics, the replacement problem applied to ecological cohorts rather than individuals, and temporal welfare aggragation across generations, but translate all of that into check-off steps. The checklist should move in order: identify the dominant life-history strategy, estimate pre- and post-change suffering densities, apply replacement logic carefully, then hit the exact point where biology runs out and a normative call takes over (e.g., how to weight vertebrate vs invertebrate suffering, or how many generations forward to count). Include stop conditions where missing data hands the decision to a person. Run it on three cases: draining a seasonal wetland with boom-bust frog larvae, fragmenting old-growth forest for a highway corridor (low-replacement, multigenerational stress effects), and an invasive plant that lowers herbivore density but raises per-capita forage quality. One of these should come out counter-intuitive — likely the wetland draining looking welfare-positive — and the procedure should flag why thats uncomfortable. Be explicit about where it breaks down: ecological non-identity across succession, climate lag effects, and invertebrate sentience gaps. No bibliographies or reading lists, please — they can't chase papers in the field and I don't want them guessing at sources.
