I am serving as a grader for an advanced seminar on G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica, and I need a careful second opinion before I finalize marks on one response. The assignment asked students to explain Moore's principle of organic unity—according to which the intrinsic value of a whole need not equal the sum of the values of its parts—using one of Moore's standard illustrations, and then to apply the principle both to aesthetic appreciation and to personal affection or friendship. The prompt further asked whether organic unity can help adjudicate between two lives that contain the same aggregate quantity of affection, one distributed across a single deep friendship and the other spread across ten superficial acquaintances, or whether the principle leaves that comparison indeterminate.

Here is the student's answer, which strikes me as competent on the surface but contains errors I want precisely located. The student writes that organic unity means value does not add up like arithmetic, correctly citing the case of retributive justice in which two negative parts can form a whole that is less negative or even positively valued. The student then applies this to beauty by noting that a beautiful landscape is not valuable merely as an aggregate of individually pleasing color-patches, since their arrangement produces a distinct aesthetic value. Turning to friendship, the student argues that the value of affection is similarly non-additive: a deep friendship involves mutual recognition and shared history, so its value exceeds ten times the value of a casual acquaintance, and therefore Moore should prefer the life with one deep bond. The student then claims, however, that Moore's method of isolation—imagining a world containing only the object in question—shows that friendship remains valuable even in the absence of felt pleasure, which the student takes to show that Moore is not really a hedonist about relationships after all. From this, the student concludes that organic unity applies mainly to negative wholes like punishment and offers no real guidance for comparing positive distributions of affection, so the objection about the two lives remains unanswered.

Please grade this answer—assign a specific letter or point-score and justify it with point-by-point comments that separate accurate exposition from subtle missteps. In particular, identify any mischaracterization of Moore's view on hedonism, any mistaken restriction of organic unity to negative cases, and any confusion in applying the isolation test to an essentially relational good like friendship. Also note what is missing: for instance, whether the student addresses the possibility that the value of a friendship might depend partly on the intensity and quality of the affection experienced, and whether Moore's ranking of personal affection among the greatest goods implies anything about quantitative comparison at all. Finally, please say where your own assessment is most likely to fail: which interpretation of Moore—say, about whether organic unity operates symmetrically for positive and negative wholes, or about whether pleasure is a constituent rather than merely a correlate of friendship's value—would cause you to revise your verdict on the student's errors, and what specific argument or textual consideration would change your mind.
