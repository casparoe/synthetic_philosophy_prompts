As a retired natural scientist now turning to philosophy, I propose a study of about 5,000 words—suitable for a workshop or journal submission—on whether self-deception can be coherently described without dividing the self. The standard paradox holds that one agent cannot simultaneously hold and reject the same belief; resolutions either posit a partitioned mind or deny the phenomenon exists altogether. I want to examine an alternative I call "epistemic shielding": a single agent structures her own inquiries so that disconfirming evidence about her motives or character is systematically rendered inaccessible, analogous to an experimenter designing a protocol that excludes anomalous readings without ever recording them. Does this mechanism explain first-person reports of self-deception, or does it collapse into mere error or ignorance? Existing answers leave this open because partition theories are ontologically costly, while eliminativist accounts struggle with the phenomenology of denial. I will analyze three concrete cases—motivated denial of professional failure, strategic avoidance of relational evidence, and health-risk reasoning—to determine whether shielding can operate within unified agency or necessarily requires sub-personal division. A successful result specifies the conditions under which shielding counts as deception rather than negligence, and predicts which first-person reports survive reinterpretation. The project fails if shielding proves indistinguishable from ordinary false belief or if it necessarily implies a divided self. The scope is realistic for one semester, since it relies on conceptual analysis rather than empirical work, though its risk lies in the possibility that the mechanism proves too vague to distinguish from standard cognitive bias. Please distinguish what is philosophically settled from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present either the partition model or the eliminativist stance as the established consensus.
