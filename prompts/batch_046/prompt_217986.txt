For an advanced undergraduate seminar in philosophy of action (16 students, 75 minutes, one session), design a structured in-class simulation built around Buridan's ass that never names the parable at the start. The aim is to make participants feel the conceptual pressure of acting when comparative reasons are perfectly balanced—specifically, whether rational agency requires determinacy or merely determinability, and whether a hidden tie-breaking mechanism restores the agent or exposes rationality as procedural all along.

Structure the simulation as a three-round collective decision game with small but real stakes mapped to a shared privilege (e.g., seminar points that determine the group’s choice of the next discussion topic or the ordering of a shared snack budget). In Round 1, present two options with clearly different yields. In Round 2, present options with identical yields but different framing, allowing participants to invent spurious distinctions. In Round 3, present perfect symmetry: two options identical in every described respect, with a visible 90-second timer and a starvation rule—if the assigned decider does not choose, the collective budget for that round is forfeited. Crucially, embed a hidden procedural tie-breaker revealed only after Round 2: a predetermined arbitrary rule (for instance, the decider with the earliest birth date in the semester must act, or the facilitator rolls a die out of sight) that resolves deadlock without appealing to content. Participants should not know this mechanism exists until they experience paralysis in Round 3.

Specify the exact rules, materials (role cards, envelopes, a visible countdown timer), and the payoff scheme tied to the collective privilege. Include a timeline dividing the 75 minutes between rounds, silent deliberation, forced choice, and debrief. Assign roles: active deciders, observer-recorders who must log every justification offered, and a silent mechanism-operator who applies the hidden rule without explanation. The rules should force real small-stakes action; avoid lecture or explanation until the debrief.

In the debrief, map Round 1 onto preference tracking, Round 2 onto rationalization of arbitrary differences, Round 3 onto the absence of comparative reason, and the revealed mechanism onto the question of whether rationality requires content-based determinacy or can tolerate procedural determinability. Be frank about which feature of the philosophical problem the simulation cannot reproduce—for example, the metaphysical claim about mental causation over indefinite time, or the phenomenology of private deliberation—and state what the facilitator must supply verbally. Predict what will go wrong on the first run: participants will likely try to negotiate split decisions, invent hidden distinctions in Round 3 despite explicit symmetry, or reject the legitimacy of the hidden mechanism; instruct the facilitator on how to handle this without breaking the symmetry or explaining the mechanism early.
