I am trying to work out my actual views on when money and organized pressure become corrupting in democratic politics, not by citing theories but by checking my intuitions against concrete situations. Here are my judgments, which I think are mostly consistent but contain at least one tension I cannot resolve. A defense contractor hosts a private fundraising dinner for a senator who chairs the relevant oversight committee, and within two months the senator advances a bill that extends the contractor's exclusive licensing agreement; to me this is straightforward corruption and should be criminal. A coalition of renters pools five-dollar contributions to fund television spots supporting a mayoral candidate who has pledged rent stabilization; I consider this healthy democratic expression, not corruption at all. A billionaire investor donates the legal maximum to both candidates in a primary election, explaining that he wants influence with whoever wins; I find this cynically self-interested but not corrupt in the same moral category as the first case, since there is no specific exchange being bought. A lobbying firm pays the senator's unemployed nephew three hundred thousand dollars a year for a consultancy with no deliverables, and though no explicit deal is discussed, the senator's voting record shifts on issues central to the firm's clients; I judge this corrupt, even without proof of agreement. By contrast, when a technology firm legitimately employs the senator's spouse as a senior engineer on projects unrelated to legislation, and she was hired before the senator took office, I do not think the subsequent vote on technology regulation is tainted, despite the appearance of conflict. A multinational corporation warns that it will relocate five hundred jobs out of a district unless the local representative opposes an environmental bill; she changes her vote. I think this is coercive and anti-democratic, but I do not classify it as corruption—it feels more like extortion. An anonymous donor-advised fund channels money through a nonprofit to air advertisements attacking a judicial candidate in the final week before an election; the ads never mention the donor's pending litigation before that court. I consider this corrupt and dangerous, especially because the anonymity prevents accountability. The tension that worries me is between the billionaire who buys access to both sides and the anonymous donor who buys hidden influence: both involve large sums distorting politics, yet I treat one as merely distasteful and the other as genuinely corrupt. Is there a principle that captures all seven verdicts without forcing me to abandon one, and if not, which verdict should be overturned?
