I'm drafting a novel in which the protagonist inherits nothing but a satchel of obsolete banknotes, and I'm trying to decide whether to treat that currency as pure signifier—paper that acquires value only through collective belief—or as a trace of real labor. This led me to George Berkeley's Querist writings, where he treats money as essentially a token representing labor and goods rather than an intrinsic store of value, and warns that speculative paper can detach from the real production it symbolizes.

Restate Berkeley's position—especially his distinction between the token and the thing tokenized, his account of how confidence sustains value, and his suspicion of paper credit—using the vocabulary of narrative craft I know best. In particular, I want to know whether Berkeley's worry about detachment from real economic production corresponds to anything in the novelist's risk of writing money that functions as a prop without narrative consequence—like a Chekhov's gun that never fires—or whether the analogy collapses at exactly the point where philosophy and storytelling diverge. I don't need citations or a reading list; I want an honest translation whose failures are as instructive as its successes.
