I am writing this request in my capacity as a novelist who has spent the past several years immersed in the conceptual architecture of the Warring States period for a historical fiction project, and I now find myself co-designing a graduate seminar on taxonomy and logical form in classical Chinese thought. For the final assessment, I want an essay question that refuses to reward mere paraphrase of the White Horse dialogue and instead forces students to work inside its logic. Specifically, the prompt should ask them to evaluate whether Gongsun Long's separation of form and color—expressed in the argument that a white horse is not a horse—can be reconstructed as a coherent intensional distinction rather than a simple sophism, and it should require them to apply that reconstruction to an invented modern case: an international heritage registry that protects pale limestone facades separately from limestone facades, with a lawyer claiming that protection of the former entails protection of the latter. Students must determine whether this analogy genuinely illuminates the School of Names concern with the relation between name and reality, or whether it collapses distinctions that the Mingjia tradition actually preserved.

Please provide the exam question itself, together with a model answer of approximately 650 to 800 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit analytical moves—each assigned a specific point value out of a total of 20—so that an A answer must contain all five with precision, while a B answer typically misses the subtle distinction between extensional and intensional class membership or misapplies the modern heritage analogy. The scheme should specify, for each point, the exact move required and the one-line failure mode that costs marks. The response should be written in American English, and I ask that it include an explicit statement of confidence regarding its claims, highlighting in particular the point about which it is least certain—likely the precise ontological commitment underlying the School of Names' treatment of similarity and difference.
