I am preparing notes for a graduate seminar in philosophy of biology, and a colleague offered the following summary of the etiological versus causal-role debate: etiological theories hold that a trait’s function is fixed by the specific selective history that originally produced it, so that ascribing a function requires reconstructing that actual evolutionary narrative; causal-role theories, by contrast, analyze function entirely through present-day contribution to a system’s capacities, making them synchronic and independent of evolutionary origin. She added that this makes causal-role accounts superior for handling exaptation—when a trait is co-opted for a new role—since they can simply re-analyze the system’s current organization, whereas etiological accounts must either deny the new function or treat the trait as malfunctioning relative to its original design. She concluded that etiological accounts are therefore empirically fragile, because they depend on counterfactual claims about what would have been selected for in ancestral environments that we can rarely verify.

This sounds largely right to me, but I worry she is running together metaphysical claims about what determines function with epistemic claims about how we discover it, and perhaps overstating the causal-role theorist’s freedom from historical considerations. Is her summary fair? Specifically, does the etiological view actually require direct access to ancestral selection narratives for every function ascription, or does it rely on a counterfactual structure that does not depend on our knowing that history? And when causal-role theorists accommodate exaptation by revising the functional analysis, do they genuinely avoid all normative appeals to design or origin, or do they implicitly rely on some notion of the system’s “normal” organization that reintroduces historical or teleological elements? Please distinguish what is accurate in her characterization, what is a standard but misleading simplification, and where she subtly misrepresents one or both positions—particularly regarding the handling of redundant or co-opted traits in modular systems.
