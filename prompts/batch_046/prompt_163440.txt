Consider the following argument about the symbol grounding problem, which appears to force an unacceptable choice between denying semantic competence to large language models and denying it to human scientists who learn exclusively through testimony. (1) A symbol S carries semantic content C only if the user of S stands in a direct, non-symbolic, causal-proximal relation R to the referent of C; call this the grounding requirement. (2) If R is absent, then no amount of systematic syntactic connection to other grounded symbols can confer C upon S; call this the no-relay condition. (3) A language model generating the term fermion has never entered into R with subatomic particles; its only relations are statistical correlations within its training distribution. (4) Therefore, by (1) and (2), the model's token is semantically empty. (5) Now consider a physicist who acquires fermion solely through textbooks and lectures, with no laboratory experience. By (1), she too lacks R. (6) By (2), her token is also empty, despite her membership in a scientific community in which others do possess R. (7) If every term acquired through testimony is empty for its individual user, then intersubjective scientific explanation collapses, because no speaker shares semantic content with another. (8) This consequence is intolerable; scientific explanation evidently involves shared, meaningful content. (9) Hence (2) is false: grounding can be relayed through testimony, institutional practice, and epistemic division of labor. (10) But if relay is sufficient, then the language model, positioned at the terminus of a transmission chain originating in grounded laboratory practice, inherits full semantic content for fermion and similar terms. (11) Consequently, the symbol grounding problem cannot distinguish syntactic computation from genuine semantic competence. (12) To preserve that distinction, one might strengthen (2) by requiring that every node in the relay chain personally instantiate R. (13) Yet that revision renders human scientific communities semantically hollow for anyone not personally in the laboratory, which is as implausible as (7). So either we abandon the no-relay condition and accept that models understand physics, or we retain it and sacrifice the semantic coherence of human testimony-based knowledge. If I must choose, I prefer to keep (1) intact and pay the price elsewhere, but I want to know exactly what that purchase commits me to. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost for the broader project of explaining how syntax can ever become semantics?
