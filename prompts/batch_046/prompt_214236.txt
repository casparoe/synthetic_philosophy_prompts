I am delivering the opening remarks tomorrow at a memorial symposium for my doctoral supervisor, a historian of physics who died last month. She spent forty years defending a rigorous form of Kuhnian incommensurability—the view that competing scientific paradigms do not merely use different vocabularies but operate with genuinely non-overlapping standards of evidence and explanation—yet I have just discovered in her private papers an unpublished manuscript in which she abandons that position in favor of what she terms "bridge anomalies": localized, persistent discrepancies that never resolve into full translation but allow partial, asymmetrical mapping between frameworks. The audience is sharply divided: half are philosophers of science, physicists, and former students who knew her work intimately and will recognize every allusion; half are family members, administrators, and colleagues from other disciplines for whom incommensurability is an unfamiliar abstraction. Because this discovery has made her legacy personally urgent for me, I need the speech to do structural work with the idea itself. I must honor her published defense of strong incommensurability without reducing it to a slogan, acknowledge her unpublished reversal without misrepresenting it as a simple contradiction, and recognize that certain sentences I speak will be heard differently—perhaps incommensurably—by the two halves of the room. Please write a formal address of about ten minutes that makes this split audience part of the form of the speech rather than a problem to be smoothed over, that comforts those who are grieving without offering false consolation, and that lets the philosophical concept shape what can and cannot be said from the podium.
