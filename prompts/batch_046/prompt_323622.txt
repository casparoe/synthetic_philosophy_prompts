I've been thinking about Scheffler's afterlife conjecture — the idea that extinction is bad not just for the bodies it kills, but because it wipes out the afterlife that gives present projects their point. But I'm not talking about generic future people; I'm talking about something more specific: the claim that a lot of our values are essentially open-ended in a modal sense — they only make sense if there's a non-zero chance of an unbounded continuation that can recieve and reinterpret them. Call it trans-generational modal dependency. So here's my position: human extinction is uniquely catastrophic because it collapses the modal space in which long-horizon commitments (science, moral repair, art) have any normative weight at all; without that open tail, they're just busywork.

What would actually count against this? I don't want the standard well-dead-people-dont-suffer rebuttal — that's beside the point. I want the real defeaters: which intuitions about meaning and time it conflicts with, what results in axiology or philosophy of action it risks collapsing into, and whether there's a hidden commitment to some kind of historical teleology I'm smuggling in. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward. And be honest: what would a defender of this view concede as genuinely damaging, versus what they'd dismiss as missing the point? Also, please seperate what's fairly settled in the literature from what's actually contested among people who think about this properly — don't present a minority take as the consensus view.
