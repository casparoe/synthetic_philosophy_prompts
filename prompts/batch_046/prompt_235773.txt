Two people there, Nina and Derek, got into a real disagreement about one particular object, and since I was just listening I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. I don't need references or a reading list — I only want someone to work through the actual arguments as they were made.

Nina had finished a chart that copies a 1743 coastal map exactly: same linen paper, same iron-gall ink, even the same foxing patterns copied over. But a section of coastline was torn away in the only surviving original, so Nina reconstructed that missing edge using modern bathymetric surveys, then rendered the new line in an 18th-century hachure style so it visually matched the rest. Derek said this crosses a real boundary: once you inject information the original maker could not have possessed, the object is no longer a facsimile but an "interpretive reconstruction," and those belong in different conceptual boxes with different standards of value and authenticity. Nina replied that every restoration already involves interpretation — you choose which tear to mend, which stain to leave, which faded color to revive — so the category "facsimile" was never a pure essence but a cluster of family resemblances, and her chart sits comfortably inside that cluster. Derek answered that there is a real difference between preserving what exists and disguising new content as old, and that if we erase that line, "facsimile" just becomes a prestige label for any derivative work. Nina then pushed back that Derek was treating a smooth gradient like a cliff: all replicas involve choices at the margins, so fixing the boundary precisely at "new geographic data" is arbitrary convention, not a discovery about the concept itself. Derek said conventions are exactly how we stabilize fluid concepts — without that rule, we get what he called "copycat drift," where every derivative slowly pulls the original category empty until the word "facsimile" describes nothing in particular.
