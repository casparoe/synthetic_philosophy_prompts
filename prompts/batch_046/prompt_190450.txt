In my seminar on comparative moral theory, two advanced students—one advancing a karmic-consequentialist reconstruction of Theravada and early Mahayana ethics, the other defending a sīla-constitutive, non-instrumental reading—have argued for more than a year without producing a shared framework. Rather than another round of rebuttals, I want them to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration: a single document both could sign. It should open with a numbered section of agreed claims about Buddhist moral psychology, including the centrality of cetanā as the driver of kamma, the structural role of the five precepts as conditions of practice, the soteriological framing that makes ethics inseparable from liberation, and the absence of a permanent ātman that might serve as a stable bearer of utility across rebirths.

Next, include a disagreements table in which each camp’s core thesis is formulated in wording that the opposing camp would accept as accurate to its own position, not as a strawman: for instance, whether the doctrine of vipāka (karmic ripening) functions as an agent-neutral evaluation of mental-state trajectories over multiple lives, or whether the precepts are constitutive conditions of the path whose value is not reducible to outcome-tracking. Identify at least three cruxes—sub-questions such that settling one would genuinely move at least one party. One crux should address a proposal recently raised in our discussions: that the Abhidharmic distinction between kamma-vipāka (causal ripening of action) and magga-phala (the fruits of path-attainment) maps analogously onto the modern distinction between instrumental value and constitutive value, and whether this mapping actually dissolves the consequentialist appearance of karma theory or merely relocates it into a two-level structure. For each crux, specify a concrete conceptual case or textual feature—something both sides accept as relevant—together with each side’s explicit prediction about what that case demonstrates.

Finally, the statement should isolate any verbal disagreements from substantive ones: in particular, whether part of the dispute turns on whether consequentialism necessarily requires an agent-centered calculus that anattā already undermines, and whether adopting a more capacious, agent-neutral reconstruction of consequentialist evaluation (permitting welfare-tracking across mental-state continua without a persisting subject) resolves the verbal conflict or leaves a genuine normative residue. The document should be formal, precise, and suitable for circulation among specialists in Buddhist philosophy; do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for further reading, since the purpose is conceptual mapping rather than source accumulation.
