As a retired geologist who has lately turned to the philosophy of history, I should like your help in adjudicating a disagreement that arose in our reading group last week. We had been discussing whether source criticism — from the comparative methods of Herodotus to the autopsy-based rigour of Thucydides — genuinely permits knowledge of the distant past, or whether it merely polishes biased fragments.

One member, Dr. Maya Reeves, argued that historical testimony can be treated analogously to stratigraphic correlation. Just as a geologist correlates rock layers across distant sites by identifying shared marker beds, she said, the historian correlates textual accounts through fixed chronological anchors — eclipses, regnal years, coin hoards — that function like isochrons. Herodotus’s practice of weighing competing oral reports, on this view, was an early form of cross-validation, while Thucydides’s insistence on eyewitness testimony amounts to demanding in-situ samples rather than transported or redeposited ones. Given sufficient interdisciplinary correlation with material remains — settlement stratigraphy, metallurgical debris, pollen profiles — she maintained that we can reconstruct distant events with a confidence not unlike that with which we reconstruct Palaeocene climates from ice-core isotopes.

Another member, Dr. David Al-Khalil, countered with what he called "textual taphonomy" and "epistemic diagenesis". He granted that texts can be internally corrected, but insisted that preservation is never random: just as fossil assemblages are systematically biased toward hard-bodied organisms in high-energy depositional environments, textual survival is filtered by institutional power, substrate durability, and the reproductive interests of copyists. Comparing Herodotus with Thucydides, he suggested, is therefore not like cross-checking independent instruments but like comparing two fossil beds shaped by the same selective pressures — both survive because later elites valued their literary form, not because they captured truth. Material evidence, he added, suffers its own taphonomic filters: metals survive where wood perishes, and urban centres are over-represented relative to rural ones. Without an unbiased "ground-truth" stratum, he concluded, we should adopt epistemic humility: we can construct probabilistic models of the past, but we cannot claim to know it.

Dr. Reeves replied that detectable bias is itself data. If every surviving narrative of a particular conflict derives from the victor’s administrative archive, the uniformity of that bias is observable, and its direction can be corrected against archaeological signatures — for instance, weapon distributions that contradict triumphalist claims. Thus, she argued, interdisciplinary triangulation does overcome textual taphonomy.

Dr. Al-Khalil responded that this merely compares two independently biased datasets — textual and material — without access to the "missing fossil record" of destroyed or never-recorded voices. He compared the situation to inferring an entire ecosystem from two selectively preserved bone beds: consistency between them demonstrates preservation conditions, not ecological reality.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the four principal moves — Reeves’s stratigraphic-correlation argument, Al-Khalil’s textual-taphonomy objection, Reeves’s bias-as-data reply, and Al-Khalil’s final comparison to dual biased fossil beds — rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify your rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both interlocutors are missing — particularly regarding the epistemic status of negative evidence (the absence of expected records) and the role of predictive modelling in historical reconstruction.
