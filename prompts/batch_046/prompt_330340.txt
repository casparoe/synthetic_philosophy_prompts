I'm working through some puzzles about what belongs in our ontology when information about an entity is permanently or structurally inaccessible, and I've noticed my judgments form a pattern I can't quite articulate. Here are my verdicts on several concrete cases, and I'd like to know whether any single principle captures them, or whether I have to abandon one.

First, consider the symmetric-universe scenario often discussed in metaphysics: an otherwise empty space containing exactly two perfect spheres with identical intrinsic properties and identical relational structures—no asymmetry in the environment, no hidden marks, no causal differences. I think there are genuinely two spheres, not one; the failure of individuation does not collapse them into a single object. Second, imagine two particles in an otherwise identical physical setup where a hidden-variable interpretation assigns them different deterministic trajectories that are in principle undetectable by any measurement, now or ever. Here I think those specific trajectories should be excluded from ontology: if nothing can ever reveal which path belongs to which particle, positing them is redundant. Third, suppose our best formulation of quantum field theory requires a particular non-separable Hilbert space to maintain consistency in the presence of certain boundary conditions, yet this structure has no spatiotemporal location and produces no empirically distinguishable predictions from a separable alternative. I think we should include the structure in our ontology, because theoretical indispensability in the formal framework matters even in the absence of direct observational access. Fourth, consider entities lying permanently beyond a cosmological event horizon formed in a pre-inflationary epoch, with no causal connection, no shared past light cone, and no possibility of interaction or signal exchange with our region—not merely hidden, but structurally isolated in a separate causal patch with no common origin accessible to us. Here I feel they should be excluded: unlike the hidden-variable case, there isn't even a shared explanatory framework that makes talk of those entities meaningful for our theories.

These judgments seem to pull in different directions, particularly between accepting indispensable but unobservable formal structures and rejecting permanently isolated entities. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving the kind of inaccessibility, the role within a shared explanatory or formal framework, or the presence of potential causal interaction—that saves all four verdicts? Or must I drop one? If there is a unifying account, please state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and say what it predicts about a case I haven't listed: for instance, a distant region currently beyond our particle horizon that will eventually enter our observable range, populated by entities whose present properties are now inaccessible but whose future interaction is assured.
