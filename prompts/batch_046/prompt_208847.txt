Consider the following argument, which extends the familiar diachronic money-pump against preference reversals into the dynamics of belief revision.

(1) Rationality requires diachronic consistency in betting dispositions: if an agent is willing to accept a unit bet on P at price p at time t, and no payoff-relevant event has occurred between t and t', then at t' she should not be willing to sell that same bet back at a price q < p.

(2) If (1) is violated, a bookie can construct a Dutch book: sell the agent the bet at q (when she values it low) and buy it back at p (when she values it high), extracting a sure net loss regardless of the outcome.

(3) Now treat degrees of belief as betting quotients. At t my fair price for a unit bet on P is 0.80; after I observe evidence E at t', I update and my fair price becomes 0.30.

(4) Applying the logic of (2) to this case, a bookie can sell me the bet at 0.80 before I observe E, then repurchase it from me at 0.30 after I update, guaranteeing an extraction of 0.50 per unit stake regardless of whether P is true.

(5) Therefore, if diachronic consistency is a universal rationality constraint on betting dispositions, then revising my credence in P downward upon observing E is irrational.

(6) But empirical learning consists precisely in such downward (and upward) revisions of credence in response to evidence. So the consistency constraint forbids all inductive updating.

(7) Hence the money-pump argument for diachronic consistency proves too much: applied uniformly to belief dynamics, it eliminates rational learning altogether.

The argument seems suspicious because belief revision feels different from capricious preference reversal, yet each step appears locally valid. Audit it precisely: identify the step or steps where the extension from preference reversal to Bayesian updating fails, explain why that step looked plausible, and price the alternatives. Is the error in (3)'s identification of credence with a static, state-independent betting price? In (1)'s failure to distinguish between changes driven by new information and changes driven by whim or framing? Or does the argument actually succeed in showing that diachronic consistency must be restricted rather than universal? If you reject (1), what weaker principle survives, and does it still block the classic money pump? If you keep (1) but block the inference to (5), what feature of belief revision—perhaps the epistemic versus practical status of the bet, or the agent's awareness of her own updating rule—makes it immune to the Dutch-book extraction? Be explicit about whether your diagnosis requires importing a distinction (e.g., between epistemic and practical rationality, or between state-dependent and state-independent utilities) that the argument suppresses, and state whether the conclusion in (7) is avoided or merely relocated.
