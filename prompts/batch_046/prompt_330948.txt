As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of language, I am struck by a tension in how large scientific collaborations issue claims. In instrument-based research—particle detectors, climate-model ensembles—no single researcher verifies every calibration step, yet the group publishes joint findings. I would like a dialogue between two philosophers of equal competence debating the norm of assertion in precisely this kind of distributed scientific context. One interlocutor should defend a strict knowledge norm: an individual speaker violates the norm by asserting a result they do not personally know, even if the collective is well-positioned and the claim is true. The other should defend a belief-based or collective-truth norm: a researcher may properly assert a finding they genuinely believe and that is in fact true, provided the division of epistemic labor is transparent and the apparatus is reliably calibrated, without requiring first-person knowledge of every intermediate link. The exchange must be genuinely back-and-forth—each character should respond directly to the other’s specific points about distributed cognition, testimonial dependence, and the pragmatics of scientific authority, rather than talking past one another. To keep the discussion from being a generic rehearsal, inject one specific conceptual twist: frame the debate around whether there is a sui generis category of instrumental assertion—assertions whose warrant derives not from a speaker’s mental state or direct observation, but from the calibrated reliability of an external apparatus together with the social protocols governing its use—and discuss whether standard norms of assertion apply to this category at all, or whether it requires its own separate taxonomy. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the dialogue should stand entirely on its internal conceptual arguments.
