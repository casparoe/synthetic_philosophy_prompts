I have been trying to work out my own view of dispositions, finks, and masks by testing it against a set of concrete cases, but I am struggling to find a single principle that respects all of my intuitions without becoming ad hoc. I want to know whether there is a unified account—perhaps one that distinguishes intrinsic structural features from environmental interference, or that draws the line between finking and masking in a particular way—that can deliver the verdicts below, or whether I have to abandon at least one of them.

Here are five cases and my judgments about them. First, consider a synthetic enzyme engineered to break down a specific toxin. The enzyme is chemically capable of catalyzing the reaction, but it contains a dead-man's switch: the moment it begins breaking down the toxin, it self-destructs. I think this enzyme lacks the catalytic disposition altogether—the self-destruction is not an external interruption but part of its functional architecture. Second, consider a ceramic sculpture made with stress-redistributing smart fibers woven directly into its clay matrix. If struck, the fibers instantly absorb and redistribute the impact. I think the sculpture is not fragile; the resistance is intrinsic to its material nature. Third, consider a person who is genuinely honest and disposed to tell the truth, but who has an implanted neural device—unknown to them—that suppresses truthful speech about money. I think they still possess the virtue of honesty; the device is an external fink, even though it is physically inside their skull. Fourth, consider a block of ice kept solid at room temperature by an internal refrigeration unit that is powered solely by the heat released when ice melts. The unit is built into the block, but I think the ice is still disposed to melt, and the refrigeration is best understood as a self-sustaining mask rather than a change in the ice's essential nature. Fifth, consider an ordinary sugar cube placed in salt water. I think it retains its disposition to dissolve, and the salt merely masks that disposition.

These verdicts seem to pull in different directions. The second and third cases both involve internal mechanisms that block the usual manifestation, yet I treat the sculpture's resistance as removing fragility and the implant as leaving honesty intact. Meanwhile, the first and fourth cases both involve internal mechanisms that sustain or prevent the disposition, but I deny the enzyme's capacity while affirming the ice's tendency to melt. Is there one precise principle—stated with its conditions, the case that motivates each clause, and its predictions for cases I have not listed—that can accommodate all five judgments? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and what does the simpler account predict about a case where a disposition is blocked by a mechanism that is both internal to the object and causally dependent on the very manifestation it prevents?
