I'm serving on a municipal advisory board that's designing a city-wide algorithmic system to recommend zoning adjustments, school boundary changes, and public-transit expansions. We've reached an impasse over whose values the system should encode. One neighborhood coalition, made up primarily of small-business owners and developers, argues that the algorithm should weight projected tax revenue and property-value appreciation heavily, on the grounds that economic growth funds the very services the other groups want. A second coalition—tenants, environmental organizers, and some religious leaders—insists the system must remain neutral among conceptions of the good: it should not treat market outcomes as privileged inputs, and should treat community stability, green-space preservation, and intergenerational continuity as goods that cannot be translated into economic terms. A third group, largely long-term residents in a historic district, wants the algorithm to actively privilege outcomes that preserve specific local traditions and social networks, even when those conflict with both growth metrics and formal equality of treatment across neighborhoods.

The city council wants one unified recommendation engine, not district-specific variants, and has asked whether we can aggregate these perspectives through a multi-objective Pareto framework that avoids collapsing them into a single utility scale. The engineers say this preserves neutrality because no value is hard-coded as dominant. But the tenant coalition objects that framing their concerns as one axis on a technical frontier still embeds a worldview—one that treats incommensurable commitments as fungible trade-offs. Meanwhile, the historic-district group rejects the Pareto approach altogether, arguing that any procedure that allows their cultural attachments to be "optimized away" in favor of other objectives is itself a form of value imposition, not neutrality. I feel there's a deeper philosophical problem here about whether a design can genuinely avoid privileging any particular conception of the good, or whether every method of combining perspectives—whether through weights, hard constraints, or procedural rules—inevitably embeds the values of those who can formalize their commitments. Can you help me articulate the exact structure of this conflict, particularly whether there's a principled difference between weighting values, refusing to weight them, and treating them as non-negotiable veto points, without just giving me generic advice about political compromise?
