After three decades in experimental physics, I have recently begun auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of mind, and I am now assisting with the final examination for an upper-level course on intentionality. I need an essay question that cannot be answered by reciting standard labels—"original intentionality," "derived intentionality," "the intentional stance"—but that forces students to navigate a precise conceptual tension. The question should center on Dennett's two-bitser scenario, in which one intentional system is engineered by another to track environmental states, and should pit this against Searle's insistence that syntax is never sufficient for semantics and that all derived intentionality remains observer-relative. Crucially, the prompt should inject a further pressure point drawn from evolutionary biology: if human intentionality arose through natural selection operating on purely physical mechanisms, does this evolutionary origin render biological intentionality derived in Dennett's design-based sense, or does it establish a distinct, non-observer-dependent category? Students should be asked to determine whether the two-bitser analogy survives this evolutionary extension, and to identify precisely where the analogy breaks if Searle's original/derived boundary is maintained.

Please draft: (1) the exam question itself, calibrated for a 75-minute response of approximately 1,200 words; (2) a model answer of roughly 800 words that demonstrates the required synthesis; and (3) a marking scheme totaling 25 marks, structured around five specific philosophical moves—each assigned its weight, accompanied by a one-line description of the common mistake that should cost marks, and distinguished by what separates an A-grade execution from a B-grade execution (for example, whether the student recognizes that treating evolutionary selection as a "designer" commits a category error, or whether they conflate derived intentionality with mere instrumental utility). The scheme should make clear that top answers must address the syntax/semantics boundary, the observer-relativity charge, and the evolutionary pressure point without collapsing them into one another. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests, as the assessment must be answerable through conceptual analysis alone.
