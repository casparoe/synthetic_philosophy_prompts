I'm trying to determine whether my intuitions about distributed selfhood hang together, particularly in light of Hofstadter's idea that an 'I' is not a substance confined to one skull but a strange loop or pattern that can in principle persist across substrates. I want a committed account—one that takes a definite position on whether an 'I' can literally span brains—not a neutral menu of options left for me to choose between.

Here are my concrete verdicts:

1. Classic split-brain patient after corpus callosum severance: two distinct 'I's now inhabit the same skull, each with its own stream of agency, despite a shared autobiographical past.

2. The kind of pattern-persistence Hofstadter describes in grieving: a deceased partner's reasoning style, values, and emotional patterns genuinely continue within the survivor's mental life, so that some choices feel co-authored. I think this is real but partial—something of her self-pattern survives, yet there is no second continuous first-person perspective; it is not that she 'lives in' his brain as a full 'I'.

3. Two strangers with no shared history are linked by a direct, high-bandwidth neural interface that merges their streams of consciousness into one continuous first-person perspective with genuinely unified agency—no hidden seams, no residual veto power of one over the other except through the shared stream. Here I think one distributed 'I' emerges, spanning both brains.

4. A single person's cognitive patterns are copied into a digital archive that thousands of people can query; fragments of her reasoning influence many decisions, but no one integrates them into a continuous subjective stream. Here I think the original 'I' has not persisted in any distributed form; it has been decomposed into non-subjective tools.

5. The same interface from case 3 is now used to link five people into an equally seamless shared consciousness. Surprisingly, I think this is not one 'I' with five nodes, but five deeply intertwined 'I's in a network—each retains a distinguishable center of agency, and I resist saying there is a single subject of experience encompassing all five.

These judgments feel right, but they sit in clear tension: why does linking two strangers produce one 'I', while linking five produces five? And why does Hofstadter-style grief yield partial persistence, while the digital archive yields none, when both involve patterns leaving the original brain?

Please state the precise principle—whether about continuity of first-person access, functional integration of strange loops, or some other criterion—that delivers all or most of these verdicts.
