After a long and somewhat heated discussion with a friend last night about whether phenomenal consciousness is a genuine feature of the world or a kind of conceptual confusion, I find myself leaning toward the stronger claim that qualia are not merely mysterious but actually incoherent as a posit. The argument I have in mind is in the spirit of Chase and Sanborn—roughly, that to posit an intrinsic, subjective, non-physical quality is to invoke something that fails minimal conditions for being a concept at all, since it is defined precisely by being incommunicable and yet is meant to do explanatory work. But I want to push the point in a slightly different direction. It seems to me that phenomenal terms operate as copycat concepts within a very local microdomain: they borrow the grammatical structure of objective descriptions—asserting that there is a redness here, or that this feels like that—yet they lack the intersubjective calibration, counterfactual stability, and compositional rules that make physical concepts projectable across contexts. The result is a fluid concept that looks as though it designates a property but actually slips its reference every time we move from present introspection to memory, imagination, or third-person ascription. If this is right, then qualia-talk is not false in the ordinary sense; it is structurally defective, like applying arithmetic to a domain with no additive closure.

I am not sure how exposed this position actually is. So I want to ask what would genuinely count against it—not just the familiar appeal to intuition that of course one knows what it is like to see red, but the kinds of considerations that a committed defender of the incoherence thesis would themselves have to take seriously. Would a successful neural correlates of consciousness program that maps every phenomenal report to a stable brain-state count as a defeater, or would the defender simply redescribe those correlations as tracking reports rather than qualities? Would the existence of rigorous intersubjective agreement in psychophysical matching experiments—where subjects consistently align on color-orderings or pain-scales—show that phenomenal concepts have enough calibration to avoid slippage, or can that agreement be explained away as behavioral convergence without shared reference? What about formal arguments: if someone demonstrated that phenomenal vocabulary can be embedded in a compositional semantic framework with well-defined projection rules across possible worlds, would that dissolve the incoherence charge, or is the problem prior to semantics? I also wonder whether the view risks being insulated by qualification—if every apparent counterexample is met by saying that it was not a genuine phenomenal concept but merely a copycat—and whether that insulation is a fatal methodological flaw or just the price of a radical conceptual critique. Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish those the view's defenders would concede from those they would dismiss, and tell me whether, on honest assessment, this position risks anything substantial or has been rendered unfalsifiable by its own defensive apparatus.
