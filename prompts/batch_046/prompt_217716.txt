I'm trying to pin down what actually makes consent valid, particularly where deception, coercion, and competence intersect, and I'd like to test whether my gut reactions hang together under one principle. Here are my verdicts on seven cases, which feel mostly coherent but leave me stuck on at least one. First, a surgeon who materially downplays a severe risk to secure consent for a life-saving operation: the patient survives, yet I think the consent is invalid because the deception touched the core risk calculus. Second, an employer buries an onerous non-compete clause in boilerplate and answers "this is standard" when asked; the employee signs without reading, and I judge the consent invalid because a material term was actively concealed. Third, a romantic partner lies about being single to secure intimacy; I think consent is invalid here, since the deception concerns a fact that centrally conditions the decision. Fourth, in a psychology study participants consent to "a memory study" but are actually being tested for stress responses; they suffer no lasting harm and are debriefed afterward—here I feel the deception is technically present but does not invalidate consent in the same moral register, as if deception about purpose differs categorically from deception about harm or terms. Fifth, competence seems domain-relative to me: a patient with moderate dementia can validly consent to a simple blood draw after a tailored explanation she can retain, but she cannot validly consent to a complex chemotherapy protocol she cannot grasp, even with the same good-faith explanation. Sixth, a person donates a kidney after family members imply they will be socially ostracized if they refuse; there is no explicit threat to bodily security or livelihood, only intense social pressure, and this is the case I keep reversing—sometimes I call it coerced and invalid, sometimes I treat it as hard but voluntary. Seventh, a fully competent seventeen-year-old consents to gender-affirming hormone therapy after being fed false statistics about regret rates; despite their maturity, I think deception about outcome probabilities vitiates the consent.

Is there a single principle—perhaps centered on whether the consenter retains epistemic sovereignty over the decision's materially relevant conditions, combined with a threshold for coercion that requires credible threats to basic interests rather than mere social costs—that delivers all seven verdicts? If not, which of my judgments should I abandon for the sake of simplicity, and what does the best surviving principle predict for two cases I haven't listed: a physician who uses truthful but differently framed survival statistics to nudge consent without lying, and a patient with high general intelligence but no medical literacy who consents to an experimental protocol after receiving accurate but technically impenetrable documentation? Please also be explicit about whether your principle forces me to overturn my ambivalence about the kidney-donation case, and whether it treats the research-deception case as genuinely valid or merely excusable despite being technically flawed.
