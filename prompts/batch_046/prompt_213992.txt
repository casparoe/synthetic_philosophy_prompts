The debate over whether psychiatric classifications describe real disorders or construct them through looping effects—where the act of labeling alters the behavior and experience of those labeled—has persisted since Hacking introduced the idea of interactive kinds, yet it has reached a kind of stalemate that neither empirical findings in psychiatric epidemiology nor successive revisions to diagnostic manuals have resolved. Realists maintain that conditions such as major depressive disorder or schizophrenia possess stable etiologies that nosological systems approximate, treating looping effects as superficial distortions of an underlying harmful dysfunction. Constructionists counter that the feedback loop between diagnosis and symptom expression is constitutive rather than distorting: the kind itself is transformed by being classified, so that observed stability is largely an artifact of institutional inertia rather than natural fact. My working hypothesis is that this disagreement is less about bare ontology than about explanatory standards—specifically, whether a successful psychiatric taxonomy must track mind-independent natural kinds, or whether predictive utility, clinical manageability, and responsiveness to intervention are sufficient criteria—while both sides present the dispute as if it concerns only the existence of the disorders. I also suspect that participants conflate first-order looping, in which patients modify their behavior and self-understanding in response to receiving a diagnosis, with second-order looping, in which clinicians revise diagnostic criteria in response to observed shifts in symptom presentation, and that this conflation conceals a deeper disagreement about whether psychiatric categories are genuinely interactive kinds in Hacking’s specific sense or merely inductive categories subject to noisy measurement revision.

Is this diagnosis correct, or is the stalemate driven instead by a hidden empirical or formal disagreement—for example, about the cross-cultural heritability of symptom clusters, the formal dynamics of feedback in nosological revision, or the evidentiary standards required to establish kindhood—that neither side has made explicit? Are the two camps actually answering different questions under the shared heading of psychiatric realism, with one camp concerned with causal etiology and the other with the pragmatics and politics of classification? Or is there a substantive ontological disagreement that has simply been obscured by ambiguous terminology?

Please deliver a structural diagnosis rather than a survey of interpretations: specify whether the dispute is verbal, substantive, or a mixture; identify any hidden crux—conceptual, empirical, or formal—that does the real work; and indicate what kind of progress, if any, could break the impasse. In particular, would a precise distinction between first-order and second-order looping, a formal model of feedback dynamics in diagnostic manuals, or an empirical design capable of isolating classification effects from baseline symptom variation actually resolve anything? Or does the persistence of the debate suggest that the framing question itself is ill-posed and ought to be replaced by a different inquiry?
