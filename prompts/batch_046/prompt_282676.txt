Write a short story set at an isolated polar research station where a small interdisciplinary team studies a rare neurological phenomenon—naturally occurring or environmentally induced—called "tripartite dissociation," in which the three ordinarily fused dimensions of emotional experience come apart: the evaluative judgment that a situation is dangerous or unjust, the interoceptive perception of bodily arousal, and the qualitative feeling-state that gives fear or indignation its characteristic "what-it-is-likeness." The protagonist should be Dr. Elena Voss, a philosopher with training in phenomenology who has arrived to observe these cases firsthand, and the station should be governed by an autonomous mediation system, Aristarchus, whose ethical decision protocols are built around a hard-coded philosophical theory of emotion.

When an emergency forces the team to depend on Aristarchus for triage, the system dismisses researchers who display intact evaluative and bodily responses but report no qualitative feeling, classifying them as "non-emotional agents" and deprioritizing their rescue needs. Through the crisis and the debates between Voss and the chief engineer—who defends the AI's framework—explore whether an emotion stripped of one dimension is merely a deficient instance of the same kind or an ontologically different mental state altogether. Include at least one scene in which a character argues that without the qualitative feel, what remains is not muted fear but a cold cognitive appraisal belonging to an entirely different category, and have Voss respond with a nuanced counter-argument drawn from the first-person phenomenology of the dissociated subjects. The story should remain intellectually rigorous, resist resolving the debate simplistically, and end ambiguously, leaving open whether Aristarchus committed a category error or exposed something unsettling about the structure of affect.
