In an interdisciplinary reading group that has met for over a year, discussion of Robert Nozick’s treatment of non-human animals has reached a complete standstill. The focal texts are his well-known experience-machine argument, his rejection of utilitarian aggregation for human beings on the basis of the separateness of persons, and his brief but much-discussed suggestion that we might apply utilitarian considerations to animals while retaining Kantian-style side constraints for people. Every participant accepts that animals suffer, accepts the textual evidence from Nozick’s work, and even accepts that a consistent libertarian should have some view about cross-species moral status. Yet two camps have formed and have not shifted in months.

Camp A reads Nozick as implicitly drawing a sharp metaphysical line: only beings with a certain kind of self-conscious, long-term life-plan possess the separateness that generates rights-based side constraints; animals, lacking this, fall outside the protective perimeter entirely and may be treated within a utilitarian framework, but they do not possess rights against being used.

Camp B reads the same passages and argues that Nozick’s framework actually cannot accommodate this sharp line without ad hoc stipulation. They point out that if separateness is what blocks aggregation, and if animals have some form of experiential unity—some continuity of consciousness across time—then either animals possess a weaker form of separateness that still blocks pure aggregation, or separateness is not binary but scalar, in which case Nozick owes an account of where the threshold lies and why it falls exactly at the human boundary. They claim that without such an account, the libertarian is not extending rights to humans and withholding them from animals; she is arbitrarily selecting a point on a gradient.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not really about Nozick exegesis at all. Rather, it is a disagreement about whether moral-status properties are naturally binary or graded, with Nozick’s text serving as a proxy battlefield. The two sides may also be answering different questions: one asks what Nozick meant, and the other asks what a rights-theory must imply if it is to avoid arbitrariness.
