In a seminar on the philosophy of normativity that I have run for several years, the question of why promises bind has reached an impasse that feels more structural than evidential. One faction holds that a promise binds because the promiser exercises a normative power: by uttering the commitment she legislates, as it were, for her future self, generating an obligation that is independent of any subsequent change in her desires or interests. The opposing faction insists that this picture mystifies the mechanism; on their view, the obligation is purely reliance-based and protective, arising from the promisee's reasonable expectation and the harm that disappointment would cause, so that if reliance is absent or withdrawn the obligation dissolves. Both sides accept the same canonical examples — promises made under duress, promises to do the impossible, and promises broken without anyone being harmed — and they agree on the moral weight of each. Yet neither has moved.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about the psychology of commitment or the sociology of human behaviour, but about what I will call the asymmetry of revocation: the fact that a promiser can sometimes unilaterally release herself from the obligation (by informing the promisee and repairing any reliance costs) but cannot always do so, even when no reliance remains. The authority theorists explain this asymmetry by saying that the normative power, once exercised, creates a reason that persists until properly rescinded; the reliance theorists must treat the residual obligation as a kind of phantom liability with no current victim. So the stall may conceal a deeper dispute about whether obligations can exist without present harm or present reliance — that is, whether normativity requires an affected party at every moment.

Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a hidden empirical claim about human expectations doing the work? Are the two sides actually answering different questions — one about the metaphysics of obligation-generation, the other about the justification for enforcing promises? And given your diagnosis, what would actually advance things: a conceptual distinction that separates legislative authority from protective reliance, a thought experiment that isolates the revocation asymmetry more cleanly, or an argument that the entire framework of 'bindingness' is ill-posed because it treats promises as transactions rather than as relations? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says about revocation and harm, not a survey of the literature, and an honest answer about whether any move could break the deadlock.
