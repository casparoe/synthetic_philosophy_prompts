I am a doctoral student in philosophy of economics preparing a single ninety-minute session for an interdisciplinary reading group of philosophy-of-science graduate students. The participants have working familiarity with subjective Bayesianism and basic decision theory, but no background in interwar economic thought or the history of statistics. I want them to understand John Maynard Keynes’s distinction between measurable risk and true uncertainty—not through the usual financial-investment illustrations, but through the often-neglected concept of “weight of argument” developed in his Treatise on Probability. Specifically, I want to show that for Keynes a probabilistic judgment can be evaluated along two independent dimensions: the numerical relation between evidence and conclusion, and the total evidential mass relative to ignorance. To keep the idea from collapsing into standard Bayesian confidence-updating, the session will turn on a constructed, deliberately niche carrying case: a research-ethics board in 2024 assessing whether to approve a novel synthetic-biology containment protocol for which no statistical frequency base exists, where analogies to past laboratory accidents are methodologically contested, and where the board must still form a rational judgment about catastrophic failure risk. I need a teaching plan—not the lecture script itself—that addresses the following. First, the dependency-ordered sequence in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced, explaining how to move from the Bayesian framework the audience already possesses, through Keynes’s logical-relation view of probability, to the separation of probability from weight, and finally to the normative implications for rational action when weight is low. Second, a precise specification of the carrying example: what structural properties the synthetic-biology case must possess (and what it must lack) in order to make the weight/probability distinction tangible rather than metaphorical, and the exact point in the sequence where it should be introduced so that it illuminates rather than confuses. Third, a map of the predictable misconceptions—such as conflating weight with subjective credence, interpreting Keynes as a blanket skeptic about quantification, or assuming that absence of frequency data entails arbitrariness of judgment—together with the specific moment in the sequence where each misconception should be anticipated and defused. Fourth, a concrete check for understanding that would reveal whether the group has genuinely separated weight from numerical confidence, rather than merely memorizing the terminology. Fifth, an explicit list of elements from Keynes’s broader epistemology—his engagement with G. E. Moore’s ethical intuitionism, his technical critique of the frequency theory, his later macroeconomic applications of uncertainty—that should be deliberately set aside in this session, with a brief justification for each omission. The deliverable should be purely pedagogical architecture; please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or suggested citations.
