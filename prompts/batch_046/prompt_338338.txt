I run a small literary review that publishes long-form narrative—novels, memoir fragments, and experimental prose—and I need a reusable editorial procedure grounded in Tolstoy's theory of art. By that I mean the view that genuine art is the successful transmission of a sincerely felt emotion from an artist to an audience, where the emotion is universal rather than confined to a class or clique, the transmission is clear rather than mannered or obscure, and counterfeit art either fakes the feeling, addresses only a narrow group, or hides its meaning behind technique. I want my editorial board—none of whom are philosophers—to apply this to any manuscript in roughly forty-five minutes and reach a preliminary verdict, knowing precisely at which steps they must halt and hand the decision to me because the issue is one of values rather than detectable textual features. Design the procedure as an ordered checklist of questions, specifying for each what counts as sufficient evidence in the text or its circumstances, where a negative answer is conclusive, and where ambiguity forces a judgment call that I must sign off on. The procedure must explicitly handle two modern complications Tolstoy did not address: first, narratives composed or substantially generated by artificial intelligence, in which emotional infection may occur without any human artist's sincere feeling; second, works written in deliberately flat, affectless prose that nevertheless leave readers devastated, which challenges the assumed link between expressive clarity and emotional transmission. Apply the procedure to three submissions: (a) a memoir of parental grief written in clinical, affectless language that readers consistently describe as overwhelming; (b) a commercially successful thriller engineered to satisfy market formulas that reliably moves its audience; and (c) an AI-generated novella that reproduces the emotional architecture of a well-known bereavement narrative with no human author behind it. Include at least one case in which the procedure yields a verdict I will dislike—most likely that the flat memoir is rejected for obscurity, or that the AI novella is admitted despite lacking sincerity. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: whether it can distinguish genuine art from a perfect counterfeit when the origin of the work is hidden, and whether Tolstoy's criterion of universality can be tested without already deciding what counts as the common human condition.
