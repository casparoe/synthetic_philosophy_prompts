You serve as the senior institutional-reform officer for a regional government that receives roughly ten to fifteen proposals each quarter to abolish, replace, or substantially redesign long-standing local regulations, customary administrative practices, or civic institutions—such as a ninety-year-old market-licensing board, a customary water-rights rotation, or a legacy procurement-signature rule. Each proposal must be evaluated within two working days; you possess no specialized historical training; an elected oversight board must sign off on your recommendation; and you must distinguish clearly between questions that can be settled with evidence and those that are irreducibly normative. Your task is to translate two conservative-philosophical commitments—the principle, often associated with G.K. Chesterton, that one ought not remove an institutional fence until one understands why it was originally erected, together with Burkean skepticism of rationalist redesign and the claim that long-standing practices embed distributed, tacit knowledge—into a reusable, step-by-step decision procedure that a non-specialist can run consistently.

The procedure must take the form of an ordered checklist or flowchart in prose: specify the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure issues a hard stop recommending against abolition or replacement, and the precise conditions under which it hands the decision back to the board because the remaining issue is one of values rather than facts. Be explicit about which steps are genuine empirical or historical checks and which are judgment calls that the procedure can only flag but not resolve. Include three worked applications: (i) a proposal to eliminate a seventy-year-old dual-signature procurement rule requiring both a technical officer and a community elder to approve contracts; (ii) a proposal to end a customary winter closure of a municipal park originally instituted to protect nesting grounds of a bird species now locally extinct; and (iii) one case of your own design in which the procedure returns an answer—retention or cautious reform only—that conflicts with the reformer's stated preference for efficiency or modernization, and explain exactly why the conflict arises. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: where it will systematically misfire, what kinds of hidden dependencies it is likely to miss, and why it should not be treated as a justification for never changing anything.
