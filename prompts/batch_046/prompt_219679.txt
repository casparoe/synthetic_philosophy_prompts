Having spent thirty years in experimental physics before turning to philosophy of technology, I have become convinced that what is commonly called "the AI alignment problem" is in fact at least three structurally unrelated problems that have been erroneously collapsed into one category, and I want you to help me make this case rigorously. Please argue that this conflation obstructs practical progress by treating distinct formal structures as if they shared a single solution space.

Specifically, defend the tripartite division I have sketched: first, "value calibration" — specifying a target function that captures human preferences prior to deployment; second, "value preservation" — maintaining that specification through ontological shifts in the system's world-model during operation; and third, "value negotiation" — resolving conflicts between the system's derived objectives and those of other agents or of the same system across different timescales. You should develop an explicit analogy to physical science: argue that treating these as one problem is like treating thermodynamic equilibrium, chemical kinetics, and quantum decoherence as a single problem of "energy management" merely because all involve energy. Defend the further claim that calibration is essentially a problem of measurement and representation (like defining a temperature scale), preservation is a problem of dynamical stability (like maintaining a metastable state), and negotiation is a problem of multi-agent game structure (like reaching equilibrium in an open chemical system).

Moreover, argue that once this division is accepted, solutions appropriate to one category become not merely inadequate but actively harmful when applied to another — for example, that rigid constraint methods suitable for preservation can suppress the adaptive flexibility required for negotiation, and that probabilistic preference aggregation suitable for calibration can destabilize preservation. Finally, address the objection that all three share a common core — ensuring the system does what we want — and show why this shared vocabulary masks genuinely different formal structures, different failure modes, and different success criteria. I am looking for a precise, structured argument that does not rely on bibliographic appeals but on conceptual analysis and analogical reasoning.
