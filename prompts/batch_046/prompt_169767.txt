For my philosophy of science seminar I have tried to articulate my commitments on rational updating, and I want an audit of whether they hang together. Here is the set: (1) I accept Jeffrey conditionalization as the correct rule for all updates driven by uncertain evidence, and I reject the idea that it is merely a special case of standard Bayes conditioning. (2) I take Jeffrey's rigidity condition — that probabilities within each cell of the evidential partition stay proportionally fixed — to be rationally mandatory, not just a conventional computational shortcut. (3) I apply this framework to old-evidence problems: when an agent already knows a fact but only later grasps its theoretical significance, the proper update is a Jeffrey shift over a partition of evidential-support hypotheses, not conditioning on the fact itself. (4) I hold that rational updating must be invariant under refinement of the evidential partition: if one cell is split into sub-cells, the posterior over the substantive hypotheses should not change. (5) I reject the principal principle and maintain that objective chances impose no normative constraint on rational credence. (6) I also think Jeffrey-style updates apply to conceptual reframing — for instance, recognizing that an interaction is best modeled as a prisoner's dilemma rather than a coordination game — where the shift is a restructuring of the decision space rather than a sensory observation.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to conflict. Order the real tensions by severity, say for each what the cheapest fix is and what it costs, and identify the single revision that would clean up the most inconsistencies. In particular, check whether (2) and (4) can jointly serve (1), and whether (6) is compatible with (5), but treat all pairs with equal care and flag any tension that dissolves once the distinction between evidential and conceptual partitions is drawn precisely.
