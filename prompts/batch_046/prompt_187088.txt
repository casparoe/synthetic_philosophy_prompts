I've been stuck on this debate in environmental history — people like Diamond, but also newer folks writing about the Little Ice Age and the Justinian plague, keep saying germs, climate shifts, and volcanic winters should count as real historical actors, not just background conditions. Meanwhile other historians call that enviromental determinism in a fancy coat and say if you treat a volcano like it has agency you've already given up on explaining human choices. The thing is, this fight has been going on since at least the 70s and nobody ever actually moves; the determinists cite demographic collapses after Tambora or the 536 event, the humanists cite how different societies reacted totally differently to the same drought, and both sides just talk louder.

My working theory is that it's not really about facts — everyone agrees on the temperature drop or the mortality rates — but about what counts as an explanation. One side thinks a good explanation traces causal salience regardless of intentionality, the other thinks explanation in history has to preserve the sense that people could have done otherwise. So maybe it's a metaphysical disagreement about causation dressed up as a methodological one? Or maybe they're answering totally different questions: one about why an event happened at all, one about why it happened in this particular social form. I want a real diagnosis, not just "they disagree about determinism." Is there a hidden empirical question about isolating climate effects from institutional responses, or is the whole "actor" framing just conceptually broken? And if you had to pick, what would actually break the stall — a formal distinction between causal contribution and agency, dropping the language of actors entirely, or admitting that history just has two incompatible explanatory goals?
