I serve on my university's Joint Committee on Research Integrity and Public Communication. Before our vote next Thursday, I need a formal recommendation memo I can circulate to members who are split over a concrete submission. The case involves a joint cognitive-science and computer-science group that has filed a peer-reviewed manuscript and an accompanying federal grant renewal describing their interactive tutoring system. Throughout both documents, they systematically employ belief-desire language about the underlying language model: they state that it harbors false beliefs about causal mechanisms, revises those beliefs under counter-evidence, and acts on a desire for internal consistency. They defend this framing as a rigorous application of Daniel Dennett's intentional stance—predictive shorthand without ontological commitment—while critics on the committee, including a philosopher of mind and a legal scholar, argue that in grant and press contexts this language inevitably functions as misleading anthropomorphism. The critics invoke the distinction between original and derived intentionality to press that even if users can assign representational content to outputs, attributing beliefs to the system itself risks treating a statistical artifact as an epistemic agent that can be held accountable for error. The proponents counter that banning such language forces researchers into mechanistic descriptions that obscure genuine, reproducible predictive patterns and that the intentional stance is standard in cognitive science.

We need a memo that recommends one of three concrete policies for official university outputs—publications, grant applications, and press releases: (A) approve unrestricted use of intentional-stance language; (B) permit it only under a strict predictive-use-only protocol that requires explicit disclaimers and prohibits normative attributions, such as saying the system should know better or is responsible for its error; or (C) prohibit belief-desire attributions entirely, requiring mechanistic or purely behavioral descriptions. Your memo should state a clear bottom line with specific conditions—if you choose B, specify exactly what the disclaimer must contain and which contexts it applies to. It must treat the strongest argument on the losing side with genuine intellectual weight: if you favor A, take seriously the institutional credibility risk and the slide from predictive to normative attribution in public-facing documents; if you favor C, take seriously the scientific distortion and artificial linguistic purity involved in suppressing a well-established explanatory framework. Additionally, please include an explicit statement of how confident you are in the recommendation overall, and identify the single point—whether about the empirical predictiveness of the intentional stance for large language models, the sociology of how disclaimers actually function in scientific communication, or the enforceability of distinguishing predictive from normative attributions—about which you are least certain. Write it as a formal document the committee could vote on, not as personal guidance.
