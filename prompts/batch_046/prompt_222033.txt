I am in the final year of my undergraduate degree in Philosophy and Logic, and I am currently taking a seminar on belief revision and uncertain evidence. During a recent discussion about Jeffrey conditionalization, I found myself stuck on a point that standard textbook presentations seem to gloss over: they always treat the evidential partition as already fixed within the agent’s conceptual scheme. Yet in actual scientific reasoning—say, a physicist interpreting a noisy detector reading—there is often ambiguity not merely about which cell of a given partition is actual, but about whether the measurement apparatus is carving up the possibility space correctly in the first place. I want to turn this into a term-paper proposal of roughly 6,000 words, to be completed over one semester.

My proposed question is therefore precise: can Jeffrey’s rule be coherently extended to updates where the prior is defined over one partition P, but the received evidence is expressed in terms of a different partition Q, with the mapping between P and Q itself governed by a second-order probability distribution rather than being deterministic? I want to argue that standard treatments implicitly assume a “partition-preservation” condition stronger than Jeffrey originally required, and that relaxing it either yields a novel form of dynamic incoherence or demands a supplementary “structural stability” constraint on priors.
