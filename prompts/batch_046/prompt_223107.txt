Analyze Stanley Milgram’s obedience experiments conducted at Yale in 1961–1962—specifically the baseline procedure in which subjects administered what they believed were electric shocks to a learner in an adjacent room, guided by standardized prods and an incremental 15-volt escalation—through the lens of Jean-Paul Sartre’s ontology of radical freedom and bad faith. First, map the experimental situation onto Sartre’s distinction between facticity and transcendence: in what respects does the subject’s physical presence, the authority of the white-coated experimenter, and the scripted prods constitute an objective situation (facticity) that the subject can nevertheless transcend through refusal? Second, examine bad faith: treat the subjects’ common justifications—being ordered to continue, the experiment requiring it, having no choice—not as causal explanations but as strategies of self-deception by which they deny their own freedom and adopt the being-in-itself of an instrument. Identify which specific design features—the separation of victim and agent, the incremental nature of the task, the experimenter’s refusal to accept refusal—function, on Sartre’s account, as social structures that facilitate flight from the anguish of choice, and whether the presence of the learner’s protests should have made denial impossible. Third, consider the Look (le regard): does the experimenter’s gaze objectify the subject into a compliant role, and if so, how does that interact with Sartre’s claim that we are always free to reject the roles others assign us? Fourth, assess what Milgram’s own debriefing—revealing the deception and emphasizing the subject’s autonomy—does to the analysis: is it a therapeutic restoration of freedom or an admission that the experimental frame had genuinely suspended it? Finally, evaluate the episode in both directions. Does the high rate of obedience (approximately 65 percent reaching the maximum voltage) confirm Sartre’s pessimistic view that humans systematically flee freedom when confronted with institutional authority, or does it expose a limitation in Sartre’s framework—namely, that structured social roles and incremental commitment can create conditions under which radical freedom is phenomenologically invisible or practically inert? Be disciplined about the mapping: specify who is the agent, what counts as evidence of refusal, and where Sartre’s theory is silent rather than forced to speak.
