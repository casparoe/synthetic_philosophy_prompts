I serve on the Charter Revision Subcommittee of the Rhine-Meuse Watershed Authority, a cross-border regulatory body with binding power over water allocation, flood-risk management, and pollution control across a basin that spans three national jurisdictions and several autonomous regions. The Authority's current charter restricts voting membership in its Assembly to delegates elected from municipalities lying inside an administrative boundary drawn in 1962. A coalition of downstream agricultural cooperatives, upstream indigenous watershed councils, and cross-border labor unions—some residing outside that 1962 line but within the hydrological catchment—has petitioned to replace the administrative criterion with what they term hydrological citizenship: full deliberative seats for all permanent residents within the catchment, regardless of legal nationality or jurisdictional residence, on the grounds that the 1962 line is an arbitrary political artifact that excludes precisely those whose lives are most shaped by the Authority's binding decisions.

Opposing this, representatives of two member states argue that extending membership beyond the administrative boundary dissolves democratic accountability: representatives must answer to a specific, bounded electorate that can remove them through recognized electoral mechanisms, and diluting that link transforms the Authority into an unaccountable stakeholder forum.

The Subcommittee must circulate a formal recommendation to the full Authority before its March session. The recommendation must choose among three concrete options: (1) retain the 1962 administrative-boundary criterion unchanged; (2) replace it with a hydrological-boundary criterion, granting full voting seats to all permanent residents within the catchment regardless of jurisdiction; or (3) adopt a hybrid stakeholder-assembly model in which affected outsiders receive weighted advisory votes and a limited veto over catchment-specific ordinances, but no binding legislative seats.

Please draft the Subcommittee's decision memo. It should state a clear recommendation—one of the three, or a modified variant—with specific reasoning the Assembly can act on, including a concrete implementation timeline if adopted. The memo must engage seriously with the strongest argument for the rejected option, particularly the tension between democratic accountability and the principle that all those substantially affected by binding collective decisions ought to have a say in them. It should treat hydrological citizenship as a substantive proposal rather than dismissing it as a category error. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations. Write it as a formal institutional document with an actionable bottom line.
