hey, quick question about something that's been bugging me in my grad seminar. we've been going in circles on whether hypocrites actually lose standing to blame, and i'm pretty sure the debate's stalled not because people are stupid but because we're running two different frameworks under one word. on one side you've got the relationists—think the people writing about second-personal address—who say standing is like a social license: you forfeit it when you violate the norm you're enforcing, so blaming becomes a kind of unfair demand. on the other side there's the consistency crowd, who say hypocrisy doesn't cancel standing, it just makes your blame hypocritical and therefore less credible, like a broken scale still measuring but nobody trusts the reading.

but here's the weird part i want you to think about: what if both sides are right about different speech acts? maybe 'blame' sometimes functions as an adressive act—you're calling someone out, expecting a response—and sometimes as an attributive assessment—you're just registering that they did wrong. if that's the split, then the relationists are talking about adressive blame and the consistency folks are talking about attributive blame, and we've been arguing past each other for a decade.

so my diagnosis is that it's partly verbal—around 'standing'—and partly a hidden disagreement about what blame is for. but is there an empirical crux? like, do people actually withdraw standing differently depending on whether the blame is face-to-face versus third-party? or is this purely conceptual? and honestly, is there anything—maybe a distinction between 'moral authority' and 'moral license'—that'd actually make one side concede, or are we just replaying a 19th-century german idealist argument about 'purity of will' without knowing it?

tell me what kind of disagreement this really is, and whether you think it's soluble.
