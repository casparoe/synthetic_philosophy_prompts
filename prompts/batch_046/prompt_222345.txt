Following a long and rather heated argument last evening with a close friend who also serves on our committee, I need your help drafting the formal opinion I must circulate before Thursday's meeting. I sit on the Editorial Oversight Committee for Posthumous Publications, and we must decide the editorial policy for the critical edition of the late philosopher of language Miriam Kessler's unfinished essay, tentatively titled On Compositionality and Context. The archive holds two complete typescripts. Version A offers a sustained defense of semantic holism; Version B advances a compositional account but is covered in marginal annotations in which Kessler rejects her own argument. More consequentially, a private 1973 letter to a former student—recently donated to the archive—states that Version B was composed during a prolonged illness, that she was not herself, and that she wished the manuscript destroyed, directing that Version A alone represents her mature view.

My friend will present the opposing position at Thursday's session. He insists that authorial intention must be treated as sovereign in philosophical editing. He argues that Kessler's explicit disavowal, made in reflective private correspondence rather than under publication pressure, settles the matter: to publish Version B, even in an appendix, misrepresents her conceptual commitments and violates a basic duty of respect for the author's autonomy. He further contends that philosophical texts are uniquely dependent on the author's intended meaning, since assessing an argument requires knowing whether its author endorsed the premises, and that any framing that treats the letter as merely one factor among others collapses into interpretive anarchy.

I remain unconvinced, for reasons I want the memo to articulate clearly without dismissing my friend's concern as mere sentimentality. In particular, I want to raise two considerations that emerged in our argument. First, the 1973 letter itself, when read closely, deploys precisely the compositional analytical techniques—breaking complex expressions into constituent semantic values—that Version B employs and Version A rejects. The letter's own argumentative structure therefore performs a kind of performative contradiction: it uses the conceptual apparatus of the disavowed view to deny that view's validity. Second, I want to defend a more modest claim about authorial sovereignty—namely, that in philosophy, the logical and evidential structure of an argument possesses a degree of interpretive autonomy from the author's subsequent psychological states, especially when the disavowal is embedded in a rhetorical context, here a face-saving explanation directed to a student who had championed Version B. The community's ability to assess the argument does not depend on Kessler's later self-assessment.

The memo should recommend a specific, actionable course of action. I am inclined toward the following: publish Version A as the principal text of the critical edition, but include Version B in a fully annotated critical appendix, accompanied by an editorial note that records the 1973 letter's disavowal in accurate paraphrase, draws attention to the letter's implicit reliance on compositional analysis, and explicitly states that the board treats authorial intention as a weighty but not absolute determinant of textual authority, particularly where the author's own reasoning undermines the disavowal. The recommendation should include a concrete timeline—specifically, that the appendix be prepared for peer review within six months and that no publication proceed without the board's final approval of the framing note.

Crucially, the opinion must engage the strongest version of my friend's objection rather than strawmanning it. It should acknowledge that there is a genuine moral and scholarly duty to respect an author's explicit wishes, especially in posthumous editing, and that treating private correspondence as rhetorically strategic risks a hermeneutical suspicion that could be applied to any disavowal. It should then explain why, in this specific case, the performative contradiction in the letter and the philosophical autonomy of argument structure provide sufficient reason to override the disavowal, without claiming that authorial intention is generally irrelevant.

Please draft this as a formal written opinion—clear recommendation, reasoning, acknowledgment of the opposing view, and the proposed timeline—suitable for circulation to the committee before Thursday's meeting. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or requests for citations; the board needs reasoning it can evaluate on its merits, not scholarly apparatus it cannot verify without external research.
