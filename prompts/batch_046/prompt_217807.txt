Consider the institutional practice in theoretical physics and applied mathematics of treating actual infinite sets—uncountably infinite manifolds, infinite-dimensional state spaces, and transfinite cardinalities—as unproblematic working tools in formal modeling, even while the same researchers acknowledge that physical measurement, computation, and observation are always finitely bounded. This practice is particularly visible in cosmological modeling, where one writes down metrics on spatially infinite or topologically non-compact manifolds to describe a universe that observational data may well be finite in extent, and in quantum mechanics, where the state space is an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space despite any physical system having only finitely many accessible degrees of freedom.

Within this practice, there is a puzzling asymmetry. When introducing students or lay audiences to infinity, physicists and mathematicians often invoke Hilbert's hotel to illustrate that infinite collections violate the part-whole principle and that intuitive counting fails, thereby emphasizing the strangeness of actual infinity; yet in technical work they move seamlessly between cardinal comparisons—treating ℵ₀ and 2^ℵ₀ as well-defined, hierarchically ordered sizes—without any sense that Cantor's results undermine the coherence of the structures they employ. Meanwhile, finitist critics—whether in constructivist, ultrafinitist, or physical-finitist traditions—are generally tolerated as philosophical curiosities but systematically excluded from the formal apparatus: a proof that rejects the actual existence of uncountable sets is not considered a viable contribution to quantum field theory or cosmological dynamics.

What philosophical commitments about actual infinity does this bifurcated practice presuppose? Does it presuppose a form of mathematical platonism about infinite sets combined with an instrumentalist attitude toward their physical interpretation? Or can it be sustained by a potentialist view that treats infinite structures as idealized limits of finite approximations, despite the fact that the formalism requires completed totalities—integrating over all points in a continuous manifold, summing over an infinite orthonormal basis? Reconstruct the implicit ontology: is the practice committed to the claim that actual infinities exist in the mathematical realm but not in the physical one, or does it instead depend on a distinction between "formal" and "ontological" infinity that collapses under scrutiny? Furthermore, examine whether the practice is consistent with itself. If Hilbert's hotel shows that part-whole reasoning fails for actual infinities, why does Cantor's hierarchy restore a form of size comparison that seems to rely on treating these totalities as completed objects? Would a consistently finitist or potentialist practice have to abandon not only Hilbert's hotel but also the cardinal arithmetic underlying modern physics, and if so, what exactly would have to change in the modeling practice—would discrete approximations have to replace continuous manifolds entirely, or is there a middle position that preserves the mathematics while denying the ontology?
