Construct a formal decision tree for classifying positions on historical inevitability versus contingency, intended as a polished seminar handout. The tree must be built from the logical space of commitments rather than from philosopher-names: begin with diagnostic questions that a respondent can answer without knowing any labels, then map the resulting answer-combinations onto six specified leaves. The leaves are: (1) strict macro-determinism — all large-scale historical paths are fixed by initial conditions; (2) modal fragility — outcomes are highly sensitive to minute perturbations, with no stable macro-path; (3) path-dependent convergence — local choices are open, but intermediate institutional or technological lock-ins drive outcomes toward a single global trajectory regardless of origin; (4) narrative-compression inevitability — the appearance of inevitability is solely a retrospective artifact of how we compress causal chains into coherent stories; (5) agent-centered contingency — only intentional human actions are genuinely open, while structural and ecological forces operate deterministically; (6) ontological pluralism — multiple fully-realized historical timelines exist, and our actual past is just one branch with no privileged modal status.

To prevent the branches from collapsing into vague intuition, ground the tree in the following analytical framework introduced for this exercise: the Counterfactual Stability Threshold (CST), defined as the smallest perturbation in an antecedent condition that would alter a specified macro-outcome; and the Path-Dependence Coefficient (PDC), defined as the degree to which intermediate steps constrain later ones independently of initial conditions. The root split should ask whether macro-outcomes possess a non-zero CST. The second-level splits should separate (a) views on whether PDC dominates initial conditions, and (b) views on whether retrospective narrative construction can generate false CST readings.

For each leaf, provide: (i) the exact path of answers leading to it; (ii) one concrete, non-generic historical scenario — for example, a technological adoption crisis, a diplomatic miscommunication, or an ecological shock — on which this position delivers a verdict that every other leaf rejects; (iii) the position's most awkward commitment, specifically regarding either a nearby possible world or the epistemic status of our actual past.

Additionally, survey the full logical space of answers to your root and second-level questions: identify any combination that remains unoccupied, label it unnamed / unexplored if it is coherent but unrepresented, and if it is incoherent provide a one-sentence diagnosis of the contradiction (for instance, denying both CST and PDC while maintaining deterministic macro-outcomes). Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, reading lists, or appeals to specific texts; reason entirely from the framework and cases as defined above. Present the result as a polished, formal taxonomy suitable for direct reproduction on a handout.
