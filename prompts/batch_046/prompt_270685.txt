I've been trying to formalize the Doctrine of Double Effect in decision-theoretic terms, and I think I've driven it into a corner where it either collapses into utilitarian allocation or forbids every triage protocol. Here's the chain:

(1) DDE permits causing harm H only if H is neither intended as an end nor intended as the means by which the good G is achieved, and the good is proportionate.
(2) "H is the means to G" should be analyzed counterfactually: if H had not occurred, would G still have occurred?
(3) In the standard trolley switch, diverting the trolley saves five; the one dies on the side track. If he had not been there, the five would still be saved. So H is not the means; DDE permits flipping the switch.
(4) In organ harvesting, killing one provides organs that save five. If the one had not died, the organs wouldn't be available and G wouldn't occur. So H is the means; DDE forbids it.
(5) Now apply the same counterfactual test to "The Allocation Protocol": a hospital has exactly one dose of an antidote and ten exposed patients. Five will die without it; five will survive with only mild symptoms. The protocol assigns the dose to five. For any specific patient X who is not selected and dies: if X had never existed, the protocol would still have assigned the dose to five others and saved them. X's death is not causally necessary for saving the five; it is a side-effect of the allocation mechanism.
(6) By (2)-(3), X is not used as a means. So DDE should permit the protocol, even if the selection criterion is not random but explicitly ranks patients by predicted social utility, as long as the harm to the low-ranked is not "used" to produce the benefit to the high-ranked.
(7) But now redescribe organ harvesting: we need one set of organs. If Patient A hadn't existed, we'd harvest Patient B. The specific identity of the victim is substitutable; for any particular victim, his death is not necessary—someone else could have filled the role. By the counterfactual logic of (5), no specific victim is "the means"; only the generic event "one death" is.
(8) Therefore, either DDE permits harvesting (by treating the victim as substitutable and therefore not individually a means), or it must forbid the Allocation Protocol (by treating exclusion as a means, despite the counterfactual independence). But the Allocation Protocol is just triage, and forbidding all triage seems absurd.
(9) I want to keep (1) and (3)—the core of DDE and its standard application to the switch case. If I have to give something up, I'd rather weaken (2) than abandon the doctrine entirely, but I'm not sure what a non-counterfactual account of "means" looks like without smuggling in physical directness or agent-causation in a way that makes the switch case suspicious too.

Where does this argument actually fail? Is there an equivocation in (2) between causal production and counterfactual dependence? Or does (7) commit a modal or scope error by shifting from token victims to types? If I reject (2), what do I owe—do I need a full causal-model account of "using as means," and does that reintroduce the very physical-contact distinction DDE was supposed to avoid?
