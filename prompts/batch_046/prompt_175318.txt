I'm putting together a seminar on collective memory and I'm getting a bit stuck on the conceptual groundwork. Everyone talks about the Lost Cause in the American South, Masada in Israeli identity, Kosovo Field for Serbian nationalism, and the Dunkirk spirit in Britain, but I want to know what a proper philosophical theory of defeat-based legitimation — my clunky term for the way a collective turns a historical loss into an ongoing moral and political resource — actually has to deliver. So write me the desiderata, please.

What cases must it handle without distortion? It needs to cover not just glorification of defeat but also the quieter version where the loss is treated as a kind of sacrificial foundation — think Masada versus Dunkirk, which feel morally quite different. It must preserve the distinction between commemorating suffering and rewriting the cause that led to it, because those keep getting conflatted in popular debate, and it can't contradict the basic historical record in ways that make it unfalsifiable, though it doesn't have to be a history textbook either.

I'm especially worried about one tension: any adequate theory probably needs to allow that some lost-cause myths are ethically toxic (the Confederate myth's racial revisionism, for instance) while others are relatively benign or even admirable (the Dunkirk narrative of collective resilience), yet it also needs some normative criterion for telling them apart that doesn't just smuggle in the conclusion — is that jointly satisfiable, or is it one of those lists where two desiderata are in direct conflict? Also there's this thing I keep noticing but don't have a proper name for — call it asymmetric commemorative grief: the same group can treat it's own defeat as a sacred wound demanding perpetual solidarity, while treating a rival's structurally similar defeat as just deserts. Does the theory need to explain that asymmetry, or can it bracket it? And which single requirement, if you had to pick one, rules out the standard sociological functionalist accounts that treat all these myths as interchangeable instruments of state cohesion? Give me the full spec — reasoning behind each line, flag negotiable versus non-negotiable, and if there's a hidden contradiction in the list itself, say so. A table's fine; I'm not after a bibliography, just the criteria.
