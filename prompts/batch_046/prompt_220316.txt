I serve on my university's General Education Reform Board, which must vote next month on whether to retain, revise, or eliminate the core humanities sequence currently titled "Western Civilization and Its Others." A working group has proposed replacing it with a two-course requirement called "Western Values: Origins, Tensions, and Critiques," organized around five explicit learning outcomes: commitment to rule of law, individual autonomy, secular rationalism, democratic deliberation, and humanitarian concern. Proponents argue that these form a coherent axiological cluster with shared genealogical roots in Greco-Roman and Enlightenment traditions, and that abandoning the framing improperly concedes that "Western" is only a geopolitical fiction deployed to legitimize exclusion. Opponents—citing developments in conceptual history and critical geography—counter that the cluster is genealogically fragmented (secular rationalism and democratic deliberation, for example, have antagonistic institutional histories rather than shared origins) and that the phrase "Western values" functions chiefly as a boundary concept in accreditation and funding rhetoric, not as a description of a stable normative set. They add that "humanitarian concern" was historically articulated through colonial administration, so including it as a defining value undermines the framework's own coherence. I have been asked to draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's board meeting. Please recommend a concrete course of action—retain and revise the proposed sequence, eliminate the "Western" framing in favor of something like "Comparative Axiological Traditions," or adopt a different middle path—and provide reasoning that seriously engages both the coherence objection and the rhetorical-function objection. In particular, address whether the five-value cluster can survive the genealogical fragmentation argument without becoming an arbitrary list, and whether a defensible middle position exists that treats the category as a useful but constructed organizational tool rather than either a natural kind or a pure fiction. The memo should be appropriate for faculty senators who are not specialists in conceptual history, and it should avoid presenting "Western values" as an unproblematic given.
