I am currently drafting a dissertation chapter on how Victorian literary secularists reconstructed moral authority after the decline of doctrinal belief, and a recent seminar discussion on secularization theory left me with a conceptual puzzle I would like to work through precisely. I want to compare George Eliot's conception of sympathy—which she treats not merely as an emotional response but as an almost physiological and cognitive mechanism for binding individuals across social divisions—with what I am tentatively calling normative scaffolding without transcendence: the view that religious practices can leave behind durable ethical structures that continue to regulate behavior and generate obligations even after the metaphysical beliefs that originally justified them have been abandoned. Specifically, I need to determine which of these two frameworks provides a more defensible account of how moral obligations persist and remain authoritative in a pluralistic, post-religious society, particularly with respect to obligations that extend beyond one's immediate community.

Can you compare them systematically on three points? First, the psychological mechanism by which each framework generates moral motivation—does Eliot's sympathy operate through affective identification, and does normative scaffolding operate through habit and institutional expectation? Second, vulnerability to what I call moral free-riding, where individuals benefit from shared norms without contributing to the emotional or institutional labor that sustains them; which framework is more resilient to this erosion? Third, whether either can generate obligations to strangers across deep cultural divides without collapsing either into sentimental overreach (on Eliot's side) or into authoritarian institutionalism (on the scaffolding side). I am not asking for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists—I am trying to map the conceptual architecture myself—but I do want the comparison to engage seriously with Eliot's own formulations, especially her essays on the religion of humanity and the social dynamics she depicts in Middlemarch, and to note whether her reliance on sympathy implicitly assumes conditions of emotional transparency and proximity that large-scale, heterogeneous societies rarely provide. If the comparison reveals that one framework is significantly more vulnerable to free-riding than the other, please identify which structural feature produces that asymmetry.
