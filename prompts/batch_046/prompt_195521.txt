As a novelist, I have long been struck by how a life can feel fully realized from within yet appear hollow or counterfeit from without, and I keep returning to a philosophical deadlock that mirrors exactly this tension: the debate over the experience machine and what it reveals about human well-being. For decades, hedonists have replied that we reject the machine not because pleasure is deficient but because contact with reality functions as an independent good; desire-satisfaction theorists invoke a second-order desire for authenticity; objective-list theorists point to achievement, knowledge, and genuine relationship as goods the machine necessarily withholds. Every reply is catalogued, every counter-reply anticipated, and yet the literature has not moved. I suspect the stall is not accidental. Perhaps the sides are not disputing a single concept of well-being but answering different questions—one about phenomenal quality experienced by a protagonist, one about narrative coherence evaluated by an implicit author—under one heading. Or perhaps there is a hidden formal disagreement about whether preference satisfaction should be calculated under conditions of opacity, masked by intuitive examples. Diagnose the structure concretely, using what each camp actually says about reality, desire, and achievement. Is this a verbal dispute about the word well-being, a substantive clash about which psychological states matter, an irreconcilable difference in bedrock intuitions about lived immediacy versus authored meaning, or a hidden empirical question about human motivation that neither side can settle from the armchair? And given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a distinction between protagonist-centered and author-centered valuation, a formal result about rational choice under manufactured experience, a reconceptualization of the question, or the admission that no progress should be expected and why?
