After a recent disagreement with a colleague about whether rational intention-formation can be evaluated independently of rational action, I want my students to feel why Kavka's toxin puzzle is not merely a curiosity about promises but a structural tension for any account of rationality that treats intentions as causally inert commitments. I teach an upper-level philosophy of action seminar—about 14 students, 80 minutes, meeting once. Design an in-class simulation that makes participants experience the gap between the ex-ante rationality of forming an intention to perform a costly act (thereby securing a reward) and the ex-post rationality of not performing it—specifically in a variant where the "observer" detects intentions with known but imperfect accuracy, so strategic misrepresentation is possible but risky. Use only index cards, a dice or card-draw mechanism for noisy revelation, and a point-based payoff scheme with no real money; assign roles (the agent, the observer, and a peer auditor who can check consistency after the fact) and include a compressed timeline with distinct "before-reward" and "after-reward" phases. Arrange the rules so that participants discover through their own choices that they are incentivized to form intentions they do not plan to execute, and that the observation noise makes credibility conditions unstable—before anyone names the toxin puzzle or refers to Kavka. Include a debrief that maps specific moments (writing the card, the noisy reveal, the execution decision, the audit) onto the formal distinction between rational commitment and rational execution, and be frank about which feature of the original puzzle—perfect self-transparency about one's own future mental states—the simulation cannot reproduce and you must supply verbally. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—likely confusion about whether points represent beliefs or actions, and how to correct it without breaking the experiential force—and separate what is settled in the literature about this puzzle from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, without presenting a contested interpretation (for instance, that the puzzle demonstrates a failure of expected-utility theory for intentions) as though it were the consensus view.
