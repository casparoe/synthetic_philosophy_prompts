I keep noticing this move in moral reasoning and I dont know if it has a proper name. Imagine someone refuses to rescue a stranger not out of cowardice, but because they catch themselves thinking "if I do this, it'll partly be to prove I'm brave"—and they feel that having that meta-thought in the background taints the act. So they hold back specifically to protect the purity of their motivation, almost like sabotaging a good deed to keep their own self-awareness out of it. Its in the neighborhood of Williams's integrity objection and that "one thought too many" thing, but its not the same: there the issue is weighing your spouse against a stranger, here the issue is your own anticipated self-image corrupting an altruistic act before it even happens. Does philosophy have an established term for this reflexive avoidance—maybe in virtue ethics or moral psychology—and if not, can you coin one and draw its exact boundaries, especially versus plain humility or self-defeating perfectionism? Give me your answer as a numbered list with exaclty five items, each a sentance or two, no intro or conclusion.
