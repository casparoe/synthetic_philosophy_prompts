Compare the democratic boundary principle that anyone significantly affected by a collective decision should have standing within the demos—what is often called the all-affected-interests criterion—with an alternative framework I will call the fiduciary-enclosure model. In the fiduciary-enclosure view, membership is not determined by the causal or spatial reach of a decision's effects, but by the presence of an explicit or implicit covenant of mutual accountability: you belong to the demos if and only if you are bound to answer to the other members, and they to you, regardless of whether any particular decision actually touches your interests. To test the contrast, imagine a regional water authority that votes to divert a river upstream. Under the all-affected-interests framework, downstream agricultural communities, recreational users in neighboring states, and possibly even ecosystems affected by salinity changes could all claim membership; under the fiduciary-enclosure model, only those who are party to the authority's governing covenant—perhaps the chartered municipalities—count as members, even if some live far away and experience no direct effect, while affected outsiders retain only advisory or protest rights, not voting membership. Which framework offers the more defensible answer to boundary inflation—the risk that expanding the demos to include every affected party dilutes meaningful collective self-rule into an unworkable global assembly? In answering, address whether the fiduciary-enclosure model can escape circularity in explaining who originally established the covenant and on what authority, and whether the all-affected principle can avoid collapsing into universal membership for every transnational policy decision without an arbitrary cutoff.
