I am preparing a graduate seminar on personal identity and substrate independence, and I have encountered a summary of the gradual-replacement argument for mind-uploading continuity that strikes me as too quick, though I cannot precisely locate the error. A colleague in cognitive science characterized the view in these terms: if a person's biological neurons are replaced one by one with functionally identical silicon chips, preserving every causal and informational relation, then because there is no discrete moment at which consciousness abruptly ceases—no sudden lights-out point—the original conscious subject necessarily persists through the transformation and eventually resides in a fully synthetic brain. From this, the colleague inferred that uploading is therefore not the creation of a destructive copy but a genuine continuation of the same person, and that any objection grounded in a fear of death is merely an intuitive recoil from a gradual change that we already tolerate in ordinary biological cell turnover.

I suspect that this summary conflates functional continuity with phenomenal continuity, treats the absence of an abrupt cutoff as sufficient for first-person survival, and uses the analogy to biological regeneration to smuggle in a conclusion about substrate independence that the argument does not earn. Could you provide a careful audit? Please distinguish which elements are faithful to the standard gradual-replacement thought experiment as it is developed in functionalist and personal-identity literature, which are subtle misrepresentations—such as presenting the absence of a sharp boundary as proof of identity preservation or equating informational equivalence with subjective sameness—and whether the conclusion that uploading preserves the original subject survives once these distinctions are drawn clearly.
