I'm writing on behalf of a small rationalist philosophy reading group that has spent the past year working through Spinoza's political writings. We have been tracking our collective interpretive verdicts on whether specific institutional designs are genuinely consistent with his account of democratic sovereignty and the conditions for philosophical freedom. So far we have reached the following rulings, which we think hang together but may rest on more than one underlying principle.

First, we held that a direct democracy with universal adult suffrage—including those with minimal formal education—is fully Spinozist, because sovereignty resides collectively in the whole multitude and any exclusion based on cognitive capacity divides common power unless it tracks dependency rather than ignorance.

Second, we held that an aristocratic council of citizens selected by lot, granted an absolute veto over popular assemblies, is not Spinozist, since it separates sovereign power from the multitude rather than expressing it.

Third, we held that the sovereign may legitimately suppress speech and religious practice that actively promotes sedition against democratic institutions, since Spinoza permits the suppression of doctrines that threaten the peace necessary for freedom of thought.

Fourth, we held that weighting votes by property or economic contribution is incompatible with democratic equality, as it introduces a hierarchy of natural right that Spinoza's democratic theory rejects.

Fifth, we held that excluding from the franchise those who remain under legal guardianship or direct dependency is permissible, because such individuals lack the independent rational will that democratic citizenship requires.

We now face a new proposal that none of us has seen treated in the standard commentary: a system of "epistocratic sortition" in which every adult citizen retains an equal vote in a primary selection of candidates, but the final legislative body is drawn by lot from a pool restricted to those who have passed a non-manipulable assessment of basic reasoning and civic knowledge. Within that body, deliberation is structured with adversarial advocacy, and no vote is weighted. The popular assembly retains formal sovereignty but may only choose between options pre-vetted by the deliberative body.

What should we rule if we wish to remain consistent with our track record? Which of our five precedents is the closest analog—the universal suffrage case, the lot-council veto case, the suppression-of-sedition case, the economic-weighting case, or the dependency-exclusion case? If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the verdict, what principle would we be adopting with each possible choice—pure equality of sovereign power versus rational-autonomy filtering, or something else? And which further hypothetical case—perhaps involving voluntary liquid delegation with proxy caps, algorithmic agenda-setting, or a conditional franchise based on demonstrated reasoning—would force us to commit to one interpretation of Spinoza over the other?
