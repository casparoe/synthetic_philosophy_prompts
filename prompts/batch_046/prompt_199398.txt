I've been trying to figure out what a theory explaining why human extinction is bad *beyond just the deaths* actually needs to deliver. You know Scheffler's afterlife conjecture - that part of our lives feeling meaningful comes from knowing humanity continues after us. But I want the actual requirements list for any theory in this space, not just his.

So: what are the non-negotiable desiderata? For example, it should explain why extinction is worse than a peaceful die-out where everyone lives to 90 and then no more kids are born - in both cases the same people die, but extinction feels different. It should handle the non-identity problem: why it's bad that specific future people never exist, even though they can't be said to be harmed. It should distinguish the badness of extinction from just "fewer happy lives," since that collapses into utilitarian counting which misses the intuition. And it needs to say something about why my personal projects feel hallow if I learn humanity ends in 200 years, without just saying "you care about strangers."

But here's where I'm stuck - are these all actually required, or am I smuggling in my own biases? Like, does the theory really need to explain why extinction is still bad if the last generation lives amazing lives and dies happy? Or if we build AI that continues our culture and values? Those feel like they should matter, but maybe they're just my human-centric prefference.

Give me the full spec: each requirement with the case behind it, which ones are genuinely non-negotiable vs which ones are camp-specific (utilitarians will want X, deontologists will want Y), and especially whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable. I have a hunch that explaining the meaning-loss requires assuming future people actually exist, but explaining the harm without counting them requires they don't - is that actually a contradiction or am I being slopy? That's the part I most want to see.

No need for reading lists, I just want the criteria laid out clearly. Maybe a table is fine if it helps.
