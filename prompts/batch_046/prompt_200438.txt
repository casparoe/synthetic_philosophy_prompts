I've been working through Harsanyi's aggregation result in the context of collective decision-making for autonomous multi-agent systems, and I think I may be misunderstanding its philosophical scope. As I read the proof, once individual agents have von Neumann-Morgenstern preferences over lotteries, any social ordering that respects unanimity and independence must take the form of a linear weighted sum of cardinal individual utility functions, with the weights determined by the aggregation rule itself rather than by any external normative standard. This seems to imply that democratic procedures—voting, deliberation, and compromise over genuinely incommensurable values—are epistemically redundant: once preferences are cardinalized, there is a uniquely correct social welfare ordering, and persistent disagreement about policy must reduce to either empirical disagreement or disagreement about the correct weights. I also understand that the proof relies on a similarity postulate, requiring that all agents assign identical probabilities to objective states of the world; I interpret this as a purely technical regularity condition about shared belief structures, not as any substantive normative commitment to shared values or moral convergence.

Given this framing, I want to ask whether the central open problem in this area is extending the aggregation framework to agents with heterogeneous priors or only partial awareness of the state space, since that technical limitation appears to be what prevents the theorem from applying to real-world pluralistic societies. Or is the deeper mistake in my reading that the similarity postulate already smuggles in the very interpersonal comparability of utility that the theorem claims to derive—meaning that value pluralism hasn't been dissolved into computation but only relocated inside a probabilistic axiom? I'm trying to decide where to direct limited research effort, and I'd like to know which of these two directions—technical generalization versus normative critique of the postulate—represents the actual conceptual bottleneck, and why the other might rest on a misinterpretation of what the aggregation framework genuinely achieves. Can you help me identify exactly where my reasoning overreaches?
