I sit on a federalism review board that adjudicates whether a policy should sit at the federal, state, or municipal level, drawing on principles familiar from U.S., German, Swiss, and EU subsidiarity debates. Our track record so far is as follows.

First, we upheld a federal carbon-emission trading scheme that overrode state-level carbon taxes, accepting the argument that atmospheric effects cross borders and that fragmented markets undermine efficacy. Second, we struck down a federal mandate prescribing the language of instruction in primary schools, leaving the matter to provinces despite a national interest in curriculum coherence. Third, we permitted municipalities to impose stricter historic-preservation zoning rules than their state baseline, rejecting state preemption. Fourth, we approved a federal framework for inter-state water allocation in a shared river basin, but required that day-to-day implementation sit with state-level boards, reserving federal arbitration for deadlock only. Fifth, we rejected a federal minimum-wage floor for state and municipal public employees, ruling that compensation for local-government workers falls within state fiscal autonomy even if labor mobility creates competitive pressure.

Now consider a new proposal: a uniform federal standard regulating artificial-intelligence analytics in municipal smart-city sensors—traffic and pedestrian monitoring systems. The federal government claims that data flows are interstate, cybersecurity vulnerabilities are national, and algorithmic-bias safeguards require uniformity. The municipalities reply that deployment is hyperlocal—street geometry and pedestrian patterns vary block by block—and that democratic oversight should remain with city councils, with state-level data-protection boards sufficient for coordination.

Predict or recommend our verdict for consistency with our precedents. Identify the two or three most plausible rules that could jointly explain all five past rulings—for example, a spillover-and-scale rule versus a democratic-proximity-and-autonomy rule, or a rights-based uniformity rule—and state what each predicts about the smart-sensor case. Give your best-guess verdict with the reasoning that favors it, and name the single earlier case that is the closest analog. Then say honestly whether our track record genuinely underdetermines the outcome; if it does, specify the principle we would be adopting by ruling federal and the principle we would be adopting by ruling local or state. Finally, describe one further hypothetical case—one that does not involve digital technology, climate, or water—that would best distinguish the competing rules you have identified.
