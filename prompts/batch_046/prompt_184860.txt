I am currently in the middle of a module on distributive justice and the administrative politics of social security, and a recent seminar on workfare left me frustrated with the usual pattern of debate: one student invokes the fraudulent claimant, another invokes the disabled single parent, and the argument stalls before any framework is actually tested. Before I commit to any particular normative account of welfare entitlement, I want the full conceptual specification up front. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of welfare conditionality—or of the normative boundary between the so-called deserving and undeserving poor—must actually meet if it is to be more than a rhetorical sorting device. I want the complete list of requirements: the cases it must handle correctly, including the sanctioned worker who refuses a zero-hours contract on dignity grounds, the claimant who misses a reporting deadline through bureaucratic confusion rather than deceit, and the invisible non-applicant who never enters the system at all because of stigma or eligibility complexity; the distinctions it must not collapse, such as that between procedural non-compliance and substantive moral failure, between temporary hardship and structural disadvantage, between a claim-right to subsistence and a revocable privilege contingent on behaviour, and between reversible and irreversible sanctions; and the empirical constraints it must respect, for instance findings from behavioural economics on present bias and administrative burden without collapsing into a blanket paternalism. For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or conceptual consideration behind it, and flag clearly which are genuinely non-negotiable and which are merely the prejudices of particular camps—libertarian, social-democratic, or paternalist. I am especially interested in whether any subset of these requirements proves jointly unsatisfiable—perhaps the demand for precise fraud detection combined with minimal administrative burden and full reversibility of sanction—and, if so, where exactly the tension lies. Then, very briefly, indicate how a strict workfare model, an unconditional basic-income framework, and a rights-based social-citizenship approach each fare against your list, but do not turn this into a comparative scorecard; my purpose is to fix the standard of evaluation first. Please write in British English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, as I am working without online access and want only the conceptual architecture.
