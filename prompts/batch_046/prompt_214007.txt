As a high school senior writing an independent research paper for my philosophy course, I recently read an essay that summarized Daniel Dennett's well-known critique of the Cartesian theater and his multiple drafts model of consciousness. The author describes the Cartesian theater as the widespread assumption—found in both dualist and materialist accounts—that there must be a single central location in the brain where distributed sensory inputs converge and become available to one unitary observer, much like a screen with an audience seated before it. She then presents the multiple drafts model as the claim that cognitive processing is thoroughly parallel and distributed, generating numerous temporary narrative fragments or drafts that are continuously edited and revised over time, with no privileged final version that gets officially published as the definitive conscious experience. From this, the essay draws two further conclusions that go beyond the basic model: first, that Dennett is committed to denying any genuine temporal continuity of the self, reducing personal identity to a loose statistical clustering of fragmentary drafts rather than an ongoing subject; and second, that if consciousness is genuinely fragmented in this way, then traditional moral agency—which seems to require one continuous decision-maker—must be replaced by a distributed responsibility model in which accountability is assigned proportionally to whichever draft-fragments happen to be causally active during an action. I feel fairly confident that the parallel-processing description and the rejection of a convergence point are accurate, but I am unsure whether the essay overstates Dennett's denial of narrative unity and whether it smuggles in an unstated premise linking cognitive fragmentation directly to distributed moral agency. Could you audit this summary for fairness? Please separate what is an accurate representation of Dennett's actual view, what is a subtle overextension or conflation—particularly regarding the precise target of the Cartesian theater critique and the status of selfhood over time—and whether the essay's conclusions about statistical identity and distributed responsibility actually follow from a properly corrected version of the multiple drafts model, or whether they depend on additional philosophical premises that need independent defense.
