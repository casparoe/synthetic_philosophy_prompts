I am drafting a short essay for an advanced graduate seminar on Renaissance political thought, and I want to pressure-test the central argument before I finalize it. The essay is titled Beyond the Dikes: Structural Virtù and the Residual Category of Fortune in Machiavelli. Its claim is that the standard reading of Chapter 25—fortune as a violent river, virtù as defensive dikes—captures only the surface of Machiavelli's model. If we read the Borgia narrative together with the chapters on founding new modes and orders, a different picture emerges: virtù operates not merely by resisting an independent flow of contingency but by redesigning the institutional, military, and emotional terrain that determines what will count as contingent in the first place. Borgia does not hold back fortune; he rebuilds the watershed so that certain outcomes become structurally probable and others are drained away. On this reading, fortuna is not an autonomous causal agent but a residual label for whatever remains outside the prince's engineered system—most starkly illustrated when Alexander VI dies at the precise moment Borgia needs continuity, an event Borgia's architecture could not absorb. The essay then asks whether this collapses Machiavelli's distinction into a tautology (fortuna is simply what virtù has not yet mastered) and whether the gendered rhetoric of fortune as a woman to be struck and subdued preserves an irreducible metaphysical commitment to brute, unassimilable contingency that the structural reading suppresses.

Please comment on the draft argument as it stands, without providing bibliographies, citation lists, or references to secondary literature. I am especially interested in three points.

First, does the move from defensive to structural virtù genuinely illuminate the Borgia chapters and the discussion of founding, or does it risk imposing a modern constructivist framework—something like institutional design theory—onto a text that treats fortune as a genuinely external, quasi-personal force?

Second, is it defensible to treat fortuna as a residual category, given that Machiavelli repeatedly personifies it and assigns it an active, almost hostile agency? If the answer depends on distinguishing rhetorical ornament from metaphysical commitment, where in the text does that distinction hold, and where does it break?

Third, the essay deliberately avoids engaging with the scholarly literature on Machiavelli's metaphysics and on Borgia's failure. For the purposes of this seminar, is that omission a strength that forces the argument to stand on close reading alone, or does it make the claim seem under-situated and therefore unpersuasive?

In addition, please flag any logical slips, over-readings, or moments where the distinction between what Borgia successfully engineered and what he failed to control is blurred. I want the argument to remain tightly tied to the internal logic of the text rather than to an imported theoretical vocabulary.
