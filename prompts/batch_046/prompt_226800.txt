I'm convening a small conceptual working group with about two years of funding and no empirical data-collection mandate, tasked with clarifying whether social generations — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, and analogous cohorts — are real kinds, nominal conventions, or something intermediate. Rather than a survey, I want a defended agenda: which three or four open questions in this ontology and its implications are most worth our collective philosophical and social-scientific effort, ordered explicitly?

To avoid rehearsing well-worn sociology-of-knowledge debates, please approach the problem through a framing I'm testing: that generational identity may function less like a biological species and more like a hinge commitment in Wittgenstein's sense — a background framing that makes particular beliefs, practices, and policies possible without itself being justified inside the system it grounds. If that framing holds even partly, it should reshape which problems are upstream.

Please prioritise among the following lines of work: (1) whether cohort membership is best analysed as a natural kind, a nominal kind, or a hinge-like framing device; (2) whether the classic age-period-cohort identification problem is fundamentally an empirical measurement failure or a deeper conceptual confusion about what a generation is meant to be; (3) whether generational labels carry genuine explanatory weight in sociology and public policy, or whether they are merely descriptive shortcuts that obscure finer-grained causal mechanisms; (4) what normative consequences actually follow if generations turn out not to be real kinds — for intergenerational justice, for the ethics of targeted marketing, for democratic representation; and (5) whether any coherent account of "generational consciousness" can be given that avoids collapsing into either essentialism or pure social construction.

For each item you include, explain why it is upstream or downstream of the others, what form of progress (conceptual clarification, formal modelling, normative argument) would settle it, and what development over the next two years would cause you to reorder the list. Make your ranking criteria explicit — tractability for philosophers and social scientists working together, neglect in current literature, and dependency relations among questions — and state clearly which lines you would actively deprioritise and why. I am writing this for a grant proposal, so I need reasoning about resource allocation, not a bibliography or reading list.

Please write your response in British English.
