Plato's Meno asks why knowledge is better than true belief if both guide action correctly. Socrates answers that true beliefs are like Daedalus's statues—beautiful but liable to flee—whereas knowledge is true belief anchored by an explanatory account of the reason why. Many readers take this anchoring as a claim about psychological stability: knowledge lasts in memory, true belief does not. But does that reading confuse a normative epistemological distinction with a claim about cognitive retention? If Plato only meant that knowledge is harder to forget, then his answer to the value problem collapses into psychology, and he has nothing to say to contemporary debates about whether justification adds intrinsic value. Or does the metaphor of anchoring point instead to a structural feature—having a reason—that makes knowledge resistant to a different kind of flight, namely being undermined by counterevidence? Clarify the confusion, and say whether correcting it makes Plato closer to modern reliabilists or leaves him addressing a problem they do not share.
