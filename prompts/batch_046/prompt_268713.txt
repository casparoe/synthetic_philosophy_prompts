Here is an argument I have been trying to evaluate, which seems to derive an implausible conclusion from Machiavelli's account of fortune and virtù. I would like to know which premise is the weakest and what giving it up costs.

(1) For Machiavelli, fortune (fortuna) is not random chaos but a structured flow of contingent events that offers occasioni—moments of openness—to be seized by a capable agent.
(2) Virtù is precisely the capacity to recognize and exploit these occasioni, which means virtù is relationally defined: an agent displays virtù only in relation to a specific occasion that matches his capacities.
(3) If virtù is relational in this way, then there is no stable, cross-situational property of being virtuous; one possesses virtù only when the occasion suits one's particular talents.
(4) Machiavelli praises rulers like Cesare Borgia for displaying extraordinary virtù, yet Borgia was ultimately ruined by an illness, a turn of fortune that he could not anticipate or control.
(5) If Borgia possessed virtù relationally, he should have possessed it in relation to the occasion of his father's death and the subsequent conspiracy, but not in relation to an unforeseeable illness.
(6) Therefore, Borgia's failure does not show a lack of virtù but only the absence of an occasion to which his specific capacities could apply.
(7) By this reasoning, no ruler can ever truly fail through misfortune, since any failure can be redescribed as the absence of a matching occasion rather than as a defeat by fortune.
(8) But this makes Machiavelli's frequent warnings about fortune's destructive power meaningless: if failure is always just an occasion-mismatch, then fortune never actually defeats anyone.
(9) Hence Machiavelli's view collapses into the claim that all political outcomes are fully determined by the fit between agent and occasion, leaving no independent causal role for fortune at all.

The conclusion strikes me as wrong because Machiavelli clearly treats fortune as an independent force that can overwhelm even well-prepared rulers. Yet premises (1) through (3) seem textually supported by his emphasis on the need to adapt one's methods to changing circumstances. Where is the error? Does (6) illicitly conflate the absence of an occasion with the presence of an overwhelming obstacle? Or does (2) misinterpret relational virtù as excluding stable adaptive dispositions? And if we reject (3) and allow that virtù includes general capacities for improvisation, how do we preserve the idea that fortune can genuinely defeat the ruler rather than merely present an unmatched occasion?
