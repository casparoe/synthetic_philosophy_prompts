For my current novel — a work of philosophical fiction set in postwar Oxford and concerned with how moral arguments outlive the institutions that provoke them — I am compiling an imaginary archive of adversarial collaborations, each one a document that opposing camps could genuinely co-sign. I need you to draft one entry: the joint statement that an Anscombe-style absolutist about intentional killing and a defender of institutional double-effect reasoning might have produced after sustained debate about G. E. M. Anscombe's 1956 protest against Oxford's awarding an honorary degree to Harry Truman.

The document should open with a numbered section of agreed claims: about the historical events of August 1945 and the 1956 proposal; about the formal structure of Anscombe's objection — that honoring a figure can constitute sharing in the guilt of his actions through praise and flattery — presented without either camp's endorsement of its soundness; about the logical distinction between foreseeing harm and intending it as a means; and about the epistemic limits of what can be established from historical records of executive decision-making. This should be followed by a disagreements table stating each side's position in wording its own members would accept: specifically, whether the civilian deaths at Hiroshima and Nagasaki were intended as part of the means to end the war or merely foreseen consequences; whether moral predicates such as murder apply without category error to collective state agents; and whether the technological and bureaucratic mediation of nuclear command — the distance between presidential authorization and physical execution — alters the ontology of the agent's intention in ways that undermine the applicability of individual-level moral psychology. Identify the cruxes: I take one to be whether collective intentionality can bear the same moral properties as individual intentionality, and another to be whether the doctrine of double effect presupposes a phenomenological immediacy between agent and harm that technologically mediated warfare structurally precludes. For each crux, propose a concrete case or conceptual test both sides agree is germane — for example, how they would morally classify a strategic bombing ordered through a mediated chain of scientific and military advisors — and record in advance each camp's predicted verdict. Conclude with a brief meta-section in which both parties state whether any portion of their dispute is purely verbal, such as disagreement over the term 'murder' masking shared judgment about the wrongness of honoring Truman, and specify exactly which portion, if any.

Please write this as a polished, formal archival document. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; a reader without access to external sources should be able to follow every claim from the text alone. Where you refer to Anscombe's essay or protest, paraphrase without quotation marks; do not quote verbatim unless you have fetched the exact passage from a source. The result should be rigorous enough for philosophers yet self-contained enough to serve as a fictional artifact in the narrative.
