I am a doctoral candidate in political history examining how sub-state municipal identities in post-Yugoslav regions are reconstructed through digital archival practices. A senior colleague recently characterised, in a seminar paper, the combined thesis of Hobsbawm and Anderson as follows: that Hobsbawm’s ‘invented traditions’ prove national rituals—coronations, folk dress, public monuments—are deliberate nineteenth-century forgeries designed to fabricate historical continuity, while Anderson’s ‘imagined communities’ prove that nations are ontologically fictitious because no member can know all co-nationals, with both scholars therefore concurring that nations are purely modern ideological artefacts lacking any pre-industrial antecedents. The colleague further inferred that, since Anderson ties national consciousness specifically to print-capitalism, any collective belonging prior to mass literacy—such as the Roman civitas or the medieval regnum—must be categorically excluded from being ‘national’, and that Anderson’s account therefore amounts to a technological determinism in which the printing press alone generates collective identity.

I am inclined to think this summary misrepresents both authors, particularly in conflating Hobsbawm’s targeted analysis of ritual invention with a blanket denial of historical continuity, and in treating Anderson’s structural claim about the scale of solidarity as an ontological claim about falsity. Nevertheless, I am unsure whether the inference about pre-modern polities or the charge of determinism can be dismissed so quickly. Could you provide a careful audit? Specifically: which parts of the summary are accurate renderings of Hobsbawm’s The Invention of Tradition and Anderson’s Imagined Communities, which parts involve subtle conflations or overstatements, and does the conclusion about technological determinism and the exclusion of pre-modern polities survive once the misrepresentations are corrected?
