I've been thinking about secession in places like Scotland, Catalonia, and Quebec, and I keep landing on this view: in a functioning liberal democracy you can't justify breaking away based on some romantic national destiny thing. Instead it has to be remedial - you've only got a real moral claim if your region is structurally stuck being outvoted on issues that shape your collective life, and there's no credible constitutional path to fix it. But I want to add this counterfactual stability test I made up: the grievance only counts if people would still want independence even if the central state suddenly offered perfect accomodation - full fiscal autonomy, locked-in language rights, veto powers, whatever. If the independence movement collapses the moment the state stops being a jerk, then it wasn't a secessionist claim, it was just aggressive bargaining. So my position is that remedial secession is valid, but only when exit is desired regardless of reform.

But I'm worried I've just insulated my view from defeat. What would actually count against it? I want the arguments and evidence that should move me, sorted by how decisive they are - including stuff thats arguably already happened in these three cases, plus whether my 'stability test' makes the view unfalsifiable. Would defenders of remedial-right theories themselves concede some of these objections? Is there a formal result showing that democratic exclusion can't be isolated this way? And be honest: if I find that independence demands always fade after reform, is that decisive proof I'm right, or just awkward? Also, has the view been qualified into predicting nothing that rival theories don't? I'm particularly interested in whether theres a case where massive accomodation was offered and people still pushed for exit, or the reverse, and whether that actually distinguishes my view from just saying 'people secede when they're angry.'
