I've been reading about panpsychism, the idea that consciousness is kind of everywhere, not just in brains. The part that confuses me is the combination problem—how do all these tiny bits of awareness in atoms or whatever add up to my one big experience of seeing red? People talk about it like there's just one problem, but it feels like their are a bunch of different puzzles hiding inside. There's the question of how micro-subjects combine into one macro-subject (the subject-summing thing), and then there's whether the qualities of tiny experiences can blend into new qualities like red or pain, and also whether there's some structural relation that explains why my consciousness feels unified instead of like a crowd of voices.

If you had to advise someone like me—no PhD, just really curious—who wants to spend a couple years thinking hard about this, which of these open angles actually matters most? I'm not asking for a reading list or who wrote what. I mean, should someone focus on cracking the subject-summing issue first, since if you cant explain why there's one 'me' then the rest is moot? Or is the quality-blending problem more fundamental, because even if you solve the unity part you still havent explained why macro-experience has the specific feel it does? There's also this weird idea I came up with—maybe the combination isnt spatial at all but temporal, like micro-experiences sync up in really precise rhythms to create a single stream, kind of like how musicians keep time. I dont know if that's been done to death or if it's a dead end.

Can you rank these lines of work—subject-summing, phenominal blending, temporal synchronization models, and whatever else is acutally important—by which ones are worth a non-academic but serious thinker's limited time? For each, why is it upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable is it with just reasoning and thought experiments, and what kind of insight (not empirical data, since I dont have a lab) would actually move the needle? I want to know where my energy should go and which problems are just distractions that philosophers keep arguing about because they're stuck.
