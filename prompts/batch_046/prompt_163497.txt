I cover democratic theory and institutional design for a publication that tracks how philosophical concepts migrate into actual governance structures, and last week, after discussing the boundary problem with colleagues over a long editorial meeting, I was asked to advise a municipal deliberative council in a mid-sized port city facing an unusual franchise question. The council is designing a participatory climate-adaptation referendum—specifically a binding vote on flood-mitigation infrastructure funded through a progressive property tax—and it must decide who belongs to the demos for this decision. The options under consideration are not the standard ones; rather than choosing between citizen-only and all-affected, the council wants to evaluate a graduated membership model in which registered citizens hold full deliberative votes, long-term undocumented residents hold weighted consultative votes, future generations are represented through randomly selected youth proxies with advisory standing, and ecological stakeholders—specifically the estuarine system affected by the seawall—are represented by appointed ecological advocates who carry a conditional veto rather than a direct vote. This framework treats democratic membership not as a binary boundary but as a portfolio of deliberative weights calibrated to different modes of being affected or subjected, and the council has asked me to recommend a concrete design with an implementation timeline for their meeting next Tuesday.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate to the council members, written entirely in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if it were a formal letter. The opinion should recommend a specific course of action—whether to adopt the graduated portfolio, to reject it in favor of a narrower franchise, or to adopt it with conditions—together with a clear time frame for implementation or further review. It must engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side, which in this case is the argument that diluting citizenship through proxy representation and ecological veto undermines the reciprocity and mutual accountability that make democratic authority legitimate rather than merely managerial. I need this to be something the council can actually vote on, not an abstract essay, and I would prefer that it not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for further citations, since I intend to distribute it immediately.
