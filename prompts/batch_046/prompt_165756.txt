I'm a novelist (currently between drafts) and I'm giving the opening remarks tomorow at a small workshop called 'The Fence and the Foundation.' Half the room are philosophers who work on tradition and conservative politics, half are actual stonemasons and restorers who spend their days deciding whether an old wall is load-bearing or just sentimental. I want to hang the remarks on Burke's idea of prescription and Chesterton's fence, but I don't want the usual conservative boilerplate. Here's the twist I need: treat the fence not as a 'do not enter' sign but as a kind of stored negotiation — Burke's 'prejudice' not as blind habit but as distributed disagreement that nobody remembers having. That should do real structural work: it means I can't praise tearing things down, and I can't praise keeping everything either; I have to talk about listening to the mortar. The philosophers should hear the conceptual move (prescription as memory, not obedience), the craftspeople should feel like I'm describing their actualy work with lime and chisel. About five minutes. Start with the remarks themselves, no preface, and please don't append a reading list or citation block — I'm speaking, not submitting an article.
