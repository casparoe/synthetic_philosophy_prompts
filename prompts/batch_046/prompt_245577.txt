I'm drafting a novel in which the central mystery is not solved but multiplied: an unopened letter, discovered among a deceased father's papers, is interpreted differently by each surviving child—one reads absolution, one reads final cruelty, one insists the text is simultaneously both and neither, depending on the angle of light falling across the page. Rather than resolving their dispute in prose, I want to embed a short poem—seven tercets, free verse, with no required rhyme scheme—that performs the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda through the specific logical structure of saptabhaṅgī, the seven modes of conditional predication. Each tercet should adopt one mode regarding the letter's emotional and moral significance: for example, the first might speak from the standpoint that in one respect the letter is merciful; the second that in another respect it is not merciful; the third that it is both; the fourth that it is inexpressible in these terms; and so through the full sevenfold sequence. I want the poem to turn on a recurring physical image—the wax seal, intact, catching light differently in each stanza—so that the formal repetition enacts the doctrine's refusal of absolute, unqualified assertion, while the shifting modal framing does the philosophical work. The tone should be contemplative rather than didactic, accurate enough that a reader familiar with Jain logic recognizes the saptabhaṅgī progression rather than a vague pluralism, and the poem should leave the seal unbroken at the end, since in this view no single predicate exhausts the object. Because the character who might have composed this is not a scholar but a grieving daughter who recently encountered an old book on Jain epistemology, the verse should sound like marginalia—personal, slightly unsettled, rigorously structured without being academic—and it must stand on its own in the narrative without asking the reader to consult any outside source.
