Following a long and inconclusive argument with a friend last night about whether Kant’s universalizability tests are anything more than an ornate way of saying “be consistent,” I have decided I actually need them operationalized for work. I serve on the ethics-review panel of a regional clinical-research consortium. We meet twice monthly, evaluate about eight trial protocols per session, have roughly forty-five minutes per proposal, employ no staff philosophers, and I must personally sign every approval or conditional-rejection form. The stakes are high—an erroneous approval exposes vulnerable participants to unjustified risk and exposes the consortium to liability—yet the pace is relentless. I need a reusable, sequential decision procedure—essentially an ordered checklist with explicit tests—that applies Kant’s universalizability formulations (the Formula of Universal Law, distinguishing contradiction in conception from contradiction in will, and the Formula of Humanity) not to private intentions but to the governing institutional maxims actually embedded in trial protocols. The procedure must be executable by a non-philosopher under time pressure; it must mark every step where judgment is irreducible rather than mechanical; it must handle collective maxims—recruitment rules, consent architectures, data-use policies, allocation algorithms—rather than individual actions alone; and it must flag cases where universalization succeeds formally but the humanity audit still fails. Provide worked applications to three hypothetical protocols: (a) a conventional double-blind placebo-controlled study of a common nutritional supplement with standard informed consent and an independent data-monitoring board; (b) an oncology trial that withholds a marginally effective standard-of-care from the control group because the sponsor requires an unconfounded efficacy endpoint for regulatory filing, with full risk disclosure in consent forms; and (c) a mental-health intervention delivered via smartphone that excludes applicants with limited digital literacy, defending the exclusion as technically necessary for app-based delivery even though it disproportionately screens out elderly and low-income candidates. Ensure that one of these yields a verdict I will not like—specifically, that it condemns a protocol that standard research practice treats as ethically routine. Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot do: for example, whether it can resolve conflicts between universalizability and consequentialist risk-benefit balancing, whether it can substitute for legal regulatory compliance, whether it can settle ambiguity when sponsors frame institutional maxims in strategically vague language, and whether the two Kantian formulations can ever pull in opposite directions that the checklist itself cannot adjudicate without an arbitrary weighting. Identify the hard cases the procedure must handle and the points where I, as the signing officer, must still exercise irreducible judgment.
