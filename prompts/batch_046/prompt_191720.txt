I am preparing a conceptual framework for a seminar on the ethics of dramatizing real lives, and I want an adversarial collaboration document between two camps on what obligations creators owe to deceased persons they portray. The first camp—call them the Archival Substitutionists—holds that when a biopic or historical drama becomes the dominant public trace of a life, especially for figures whose primary sources are sparse or institutionally suppressed, the dramatist acquires a duty of epistemic fidelity that is not merely aesthetic but archival: misrepresentation does not just take artistic license but actively replaces the dead in the historical record with an artifact shaped by present ideology. The second camp—call them the Memorial Sovereigntists—holds that dead persons lack the moral standing to be wronged by representation, that dramatization answers to present audiences and present moral needs rather than to documentary completeness, and that treating dramatic license as an ethical failure mistakes remembrance for historiography and thereby obscures rather than repairs the silences already present in official archives.

Please draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign in good faith. I want a numbered section of agreed claims—including shared descriptions of archival substitution (the phenomenon by which a widely circulated dramatization supersedes documentary traces in collective memory) and of the irreducible gap between aesthetic and historiographical aims—followed by a disagreements table. For each crux, specify a concrete test case or comparative argument both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's predicted outcome in advance. The document should not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; it should rest entirely on conceptual analysis that a reader without web access could evaluate. Make the formulations exact enough that neither side can later claim the wording smuggled in the opponent's conclusion.
