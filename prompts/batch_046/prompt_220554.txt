Since I recently began drafting welfare and oversight guidelines for an institution whose work spans vertebrates, invertebrates, and automated environmental-monitoring systems, I have become personally concerned with whether moral considerability genuinely comes in continuous degrees or collapses into sharp categories. I am asking for the joint statement that a continuous-gradationist camp and a categorical-threshold camp could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on sentience and moral status.

Name the two sides precisely. The gradationists hold that phenomenal consciousness and its corresponding moral weight vary smoothly across organisms and devices, with no non-arbitrary cutoff—so insects, mammals, and even simple feedback systems differ only in magnitude, not in kind, of consideration owed. The threshold pluralists hold that moral status is categorical—perhaps binary—at specific capacities such as integrated affective suffering or self-representational awareness, even if the underlying neural and functional properties are themselves graded; on this view, a being either falls within the protected category or does not, regardless of where it sits on a continuous physiological scale.

Structure the document as follows.

First, a numbered section of claims both sides can accept. These should cover: what is known about nervous-system architecture across phyla; the behavioral and physiological markers associated with nociception, learning, and integration in insects versus mammals; the logical structure of aggregation problems that arise when assigning probabilities to sentience across large populations (the analog of the preface-paradox-style tension between high confidence in each individual case and low confidence in the universal claim); the conditions under which an artificial system like a thermostat possesses feedback loops without phenomenal consciousness; and what kind of finding—neural, behavioral, or conceptual—would genuinely count as evidence that a being lacks phenomenal consciousness entirely.

Second, a disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, provide two formulations: one phrased so that the gradationist camp would accept it as an accurate statement of its own view, and one phrased so that the threshold pluralist camp would accept it as an accurate statement of its own view. Do not use straw-man wording. I want the disagreement made exact—down to whether the dispute is about the ontology of consciousness, about the correct aggregation rule for moral weights, or about the epistemic standards required to assert a category boundary.

Third, identify the cruxes—the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I want two cruxes treated explicitly, and I want you to inject a framing that is not standard textbook material: treat sentience not as a single axis but as a multidimensional matrix comprising at least (i) affective valence intensity, (ii) temporal integration of experience, (iii) cognitive access or self-representation, and (iv) social-relational embedding. Crux A: is sentience genuinely multidimensional in this way, and if so, does moral status aggregate across dimensions linearly, multiplicatively, via a dominance rule, or through some other function? Crux B: does evolutionary continuity or naturalness have independent moral weight—specifically, does the fact that nociception in insects and pain in mammals share evolutionary origins matter for whether they share moral status, and does the absence of evolutionary history in a climate-control device fully explain why it lacks status?

Fourth, for each crux, name a concrete case or comparison that both sides agree is relevant, and record in advance what each camp predicts about it. For Crux A, use the comparison between a honeybee’s apparent integrative learning and a thermostat’s temperature-feedback loop—both sides should agree this comparison bears on whether dimensions like cognitive access and temporal integration are present. State exactly what the gradationists predict about the bee’s non-zero moral weight relative to the thermostat’s zero weight, specifying on which dimensions the difference lies. For Crux B, use the paired case of progressive neurodegeneration in a mammal and progressive sensory-degradation in an aging insect colony—both sides should agree this bears on whether moral obligations diminish continuously or drop discontinuously. State exactly what each camp predicts about whether obligations fade smoothly or vanish at a functional threshold.

Fifth, conclude by indicating whether any portion of the dispute is purely verbal—for example, a disagreement about the extension of the term suffering versus a disagreement about the underlying phenomena—and specify which consistent reformulations of language would dissolve part of the conflict without requiring either side to change its substantive commitments.

Write formally and precisely. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke obscure theories, named experiments with contested details, or specific scholarly attributions that a model without web access could not reliably verify. The deliverable should be a constructive text that both parties could put their names to, with every residual disagreement rendered in wording each side recognizes as its own.
