I sit on the certification panel for an international registry of digital typographic variants. Our board must repeatedly decide whether a submitted glyph counts as a legitimate stylistic instance of the lowercase Latin letter 'a' — eligible for registration — or whether it has become a different symbol, an illegible mark, or a form belonging to another script. Decisions must be made quickly, without consulting philologists or designers, and must be defensible to both conservative registrars and accessibility advocates.

Please construct a reusable decision procedure, in ordered stages with explicit pass/fail criteria, that operationalises the philosophical distinction between essential letter-identity conditions and merely stylistic variation (the problem of what makes every 'a' an 'a'). The procedure should be runnable by non-philosopher reviewers; it must identify the point of mandatory rejection, the point of mandatory acceptance, and the precise stage at which the question becomes one of values rather than classification and must be escalated to the full board.

Apply the procedure to three submissions: (1) a single-storey 'a' whose tail loops dramatically over the bowl, proposed for an ornate calligraphic face; (2) a double-storey form with an open top bowl fused to the stem so that it risks being read as a rotated 'q', submitted within a dyslexia-friendly type family; and (3) a hybrid that retains the bowl of an 'a' but adds an ascender like that of 'l', created for a constructed script adapting Latin shapes to novel phonemes.

For each case, show the procedure's path. Then state explicitly what the procedure cannot settle — for example, whether cross-script adaptation or accessibility redesign should override formal classification — and identify any case where the procedure's output conflicts with inclusive-design priorities. Write in British English, in a formal institutional register.
