I've just come out of a long argument with a close friend about democratic boundaries, and I want to check whether my own package of commitments actually hangs together or whether I'm smuggling in inconsistencies I wouldn't tolerate in other contexts. Here is roughly where I've landed.

(1) I accept a sharpened version of the all-affected principle: membership in the demos should track durable subjection to the coercive authority of a decision-making body—call it the stake principle—not mere causal impact. If you are durably under the law's coercive reach, you belong in the body that authorizes it.

(2) At the same time, I think democratic legitimacy requires more than being affected; it requires that members stand in relations of mutual justification. The demos is not just an aggregation of stakeholders but a body of joint authors of law, and its members must be capable of giving and accepting reasons that others in the group can recognize.

(3) From these premises I defend compulsory voting and lowering the voting age to 16. If you are durably subject to state coercion, you have a claim not merely to be heard but to be required to participate in authorizing that coercion; political equality demands that the weight of one's stake not depend on one's enthusiasm for politics.

(4) I also hold that long-term resident non-citizens should vote in local and municipal elections but not in national ones, on the grounds that their stake is territorially bounded: local coercion is immediate and continuous, whereas national coercion is mediated and can be exited through migration or naturalization.

(5) Finally, I think the boundary of the demos cannot itself be settled by democratic vote without vicious circularity—there must be a constitutional or pre-political settlement that fixes who counts as a member for the purpose of deciding membership.

My friend claims that (1) and (4) collapse in the wrong direction, that (2) undermines (3) rather than supporting it, and that (5) is incompatible with the democratic ethos implied by (1) and (3) taken together. I am not convinced, but I also do not trust my own instincts on which commitment I would actually abandon if forced. So I want an audit, not a defense: trace the genuine tensions in this set, ordered by severity, noting which pairs only appear to conflict and dissolve on inspection. Specifically, is the territorial bounding in (4) defensible under the stake principle in (1), given that national law also exercises durable coercion over non-citizen residents? Does the mutual-justification requirement in (2) conflict with compulsory voting for 16-year-olds in (3), or with including non-citizens in any vote? And does (5) genuinely contradict (1) or (3), or is that tension merely apparent? For each tension you identify, say what the cheapest repair is and which commitment it costs. Do not lecture me on democratic theory in general—just tell me whether this particular package hangs together, and if not, what the minimal damage to restore consistency would be.
