Consider the opening of the East German Stasi archives after reunification in 1990: the Ministry for State Security had compiled extensive files on millions of citizens — dissidents, neighbours, colleagues, family members — through official officers and unofficial informants. After the fall of the Wall, activists occupied offices to prevent destruction; eventually the Federal Commissioner for the Stasi Records (BStU) was established to manage access. Individual citizens could apply to read their own files, often discovering intimate betrayals, while vast portions remained destroyed, redacted, or unevenly catalogued, so the archive became simultaneously an instrument of personal reckoning and a continuing site of state-controlled disclosure.
