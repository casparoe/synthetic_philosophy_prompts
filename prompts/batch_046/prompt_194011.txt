I was listening to a podcast episode about belief and physics during my commute, and the host summarized a principle attributed to David Lewis called the Principal Principle. According to the summary, if you know that the objective physical chance of an event is exactly thirty percent and you possess no other information that bears on the outcome, then rationality requires your subjective credence to be exactly thirty percent as well, because your beliefs ought to mirror the statistical structure of the world rather than adding your own spin. The host went on to say that Lewis acknowledged one obvious problem—if you have direct observational access to whether the event occurred, you should not still believe it is thirty percent likely—so he added an exception for what critics later named the inadmissible-evidence restriction. The host presented this restriction as an ad hoc patch invented after the fact to save the principle from counterexamples, and concluded that the whole view is circular anyway: objective chances are just long-run frequencies defined by physics, but the principle tells us to define rational belief by deferring to those frequencies, so it never explains where either concept actually comes from.

I have been trying to apply this to ordinary situations—like whether I should trust a weather forecast that says a thirty percent chance of rain—so the idea seemed practical, but the summary left me unsure about a few things. Is this a fair account of what the Principal Principle actually claims? I want to distinguish the parts that are accurate representations, the parts that are standard simplifications a popularizer might use, and the parts that misrepresent the view—particularly whether the exception for direct evidence was part of Lewis's original formulation or indeed a later repair, whether the principle really demands exact numerical equality in every case, and whether the circularity charge is as damaging as the host suggests. I am not looking for a bibliography or a list of further readings; I want to understand the conceptual structure well enough to think clearly about how belief should relate to statistical information in everyday reasoning.
