Please explain the conceptual tension between Good's theorem on the value of information and cases of informational aversion in which an agent's preferences are epistemically state-dependent. Specifically, consider a physician who may receive a costless diagnostic result before selecting a treatment, but where learning a grave prognosis systematically shifts her risk-weighting over outcomes—so her utility ordering over actions is not invariant across information states. Does Good's proof, which applies the law of total expectation to an action policy conditional on each possible signal, still guarantee that observing the result is ex ante beneficial? Or does the non-negativity result depend essentially on the assumption that the agent's decision criterion updates only beliefs, leaving the underlying preference ordering untouched? Clarify whether extending the theorem to agents with context-sensitive rationality requires modifying coherence conditions on belief alone, or whether it also demands cross-context stability in the utility weights themselves.
