As a novelist drafting a near-future political allegory, I have been turning over a conceptual problem I have come to call the recursive abstention lemma. The premise is straightforward enough: if each rational agent calculates that her vote is vanishingly unlikely to be decisive, the instrumental cost of participation exceeds its expected benefit, and she should therefore abstain. But if rationality is distributed more or less uniformly across the electorate, this reasoning applies symmetrically to everyone, suggesting that a fully rational polity should converge toward near-total abstention. The paradox, as I understand it, is usually treated as a localized puzzle about individual motivation; yet it strikes me that it carries a far more corrosive collective implication. If those who remain are precisely the ones who do not perform this cost-benefit calculus—whether out of habit, identity, or cognitive error—then democratic selection mechanisms are not merely inefficient but structurally divorced from rational preference aggregation. In my narrative, this leads to a proposed remedy I term epistocratic selection: a random draw from the citizenry combined with a reasoning-competence screen, designed to bypass the abstention spiral by making participation obligatory for the selected few while filtering out non-instrumental motivation.

What I find puzzling, and what I have been debating with friends who study political philosophy, is why democratic theorists do not treat this entailment as a fatal reductio of universal suffrage. Is the failure to pursue it merely a normative commitment to participatory equality regardless of rationality, or is there a technical error in my linking of individual instrumental reasoning to collective epistemic legitimacy? Specifically, does this logic of rational abstention actually predict an abstention cascade, or does empirical voting behavior already falsify the uniform-rationality assumption in ways that dissolve the collective collapse? For the architecture of my novel, I need to know whether this line of argument genuinely supports replacing elections with a cognitive lottery, or whether the confusion lies in treating a paradox of individual action as if it were a proof about the epistemic quality of collective outcomes. Could you identify the precise point where this reasoning fractures, and whether any serious philosophical work has pursued this specific entailment—not as an aside, but as a foundational challenge to democratic legitimacy?
