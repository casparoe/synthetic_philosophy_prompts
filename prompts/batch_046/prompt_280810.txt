Is there a name for this? In philosophy of science debates about models with wild unrealistic assumtions -- perfect rationality, infinite agents, that kind of thing -- I keep seeing defenders pull what feels like a two-step. First, when someone attacks the premise as unrealistic, they cite Friedman's F-twist: predictions are what matter, unrealistic premises are totally fine instrumentally. But then, if the model actually fails a prediction, they pivot and say the unrealistic premise is still conceptually essential -- it's the core mechanism, you can't drop it without missing the point entirely. So the same premise gets defended instrumentally when predictions work, and essentially when they don't. It's not quite the F-twist itself, and not exactly special pleading, since the two justifications are genuinely different in kind. A couple concrete cases: rational-choice models in econ that keep homo economicus even after empirical failures, or ideal-gas law defenses that switch from "it predicts well" to "it reveals the underlying law". Is there an established concept for this specific flip-flop? If not, can you coin a term with a sharp definition and boundary cases -- especially how it differs from plain equivocation or motte-and-bailey?
