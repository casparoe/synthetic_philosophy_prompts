I cover science and ideas for a general audience and think naturally in epidemiological network terms—R₀ values, superspreader dynamics, asymptomatic carriers, and herd-immunity thresholds—though I have no training in nineteenth-century philosophy. Restate Kierkegaard’s critique of the press and the phantom “public” (the leveling of qualitative difference, the substitution of abstraction for inward commitment, and the destruction of the single individual’s responsibility before God) using only that vocabulary, treating the circulation of opinions as analogous to pathogen transmission through a population. Be precise: which parts of the critique map cleanly onto superspreading events or asymptomatic carriage, which survive only with distortion, and which—especially anything tied to non-quantifiable inwardness or the “single individual”—have no epidemiological counterpart at all. Then report whether that residue indicates a genuine descriptive limit of network epidemiology or reveals that Kierkegaard’s argument was partly rhetorical rather than structural. In doing so, separate what is well settled in Kierkegaard scholarship from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present any disputed interpretation as the consensus.
