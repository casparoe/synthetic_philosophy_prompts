I've been thinking about Keynes's essay on our grandchildren — the one where he says once we solve the economic problem we'll face the real problem of living well. I think he believed the good life is mostly about non-instrumental stuff like friendship, art, and leisure, and that he actually drew a sharp line between absolute needs (the ones that matter) and realtive needs (status competition). My view is that he was basically right, except he underestimated how capitalism doesn't just meet needs but actively manufactures relative ones even after absolute scarcity is gone. So what would count against this? Not the usual 'he was wrong about growth' stuff. I want to know: did Keynes himself ever concede that the economic system could keep generating artificial wants? Is there anything in his later writting — maybe the General Theory or his letters — that shows he saw this coming? And if capitalism really does keep inventing new relative needs, does that destroy his vision or just push the good life further away? Also, what would a committed Keynesian admit is the weakest point here — not what Marxists say, but what defenders of his view themselves have to conceede?
