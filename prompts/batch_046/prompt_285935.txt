I've been thinking on my own about the film The Prestige, in which one of the magicians uses a duplication machine for his teleportation illusion. Every night he steps into the device, and one copy falls into a hidden water tank and drowns while the other is transported to a balcony to take the bow. Over time, the dead bodies accumulate in storage. The survivor remembers every previous performance—entering the machine, feeling the fear, watching the other version die—yet each copy also seems to experience its own terror and its own will to survive. I find the horror hard to place. Is it the murder of a stranger who happens to share my memories, or is it something weirder about what kind of thing the magician is?

I've recently tried to understand a philosophical debate I believe is called endurantism versus perdurantism. As I grasp it, endurantism holds that a person is wholly present at each moment of their life, like a single object that simply persists through time. Perdurantism holds that a person is more like a long four-dimensional worm made of temporal slices or stages, and that "the same person" is really a chain of those slices linked together. If endurantism is right, then every use of the machine creates a fully separate person, and the magician is repeatedly murdering innocent strangers who happen to inherit his memories. If perdurantism is right, then the dying slice and the surviving slice are both parts of one extended worm, so the death is closer to losing a limb—awful, but not the annihilation of a distinct self.

Can you read the film carefully through this lens? I want an honest two-way reading: where the metaphysics illuminates a scene or a character's choice, and where the film resists being reduced to an illustration. Specifically, the survivor's continuous memory suggests the worm view, while the fact that each copy screams and fights for its own life suggests the fully-present view. Does the film need one view to generate its moral horror and the other view to make the illusion technically coherent? Is there a moment—perhaps when the survivor decides to continue the trick despite knowing he might be the one who dies—where the metaphysics breaks down and the plot requires a confusion between them?

I also want to inject an idea I haven't seen named in any book. Call it the relay-race view: identity might be neither being the same object through time nor being part of a four-dimensional worm, but rather a kind of psychological baton-pass, where one stage runs, then hands off its memories and intentions to the next stage. Under this view, Angier is not killing a stranger, and he is not cutting off part of himself—he is killing the very runner who just handed him the baton. Does The Prestige accidentally dramatize this relay idea? For example, do the copies inherit not only memories but obligations—to maintain the illusion, to hide the bodies, to protect the survivor's reputation—suggesting that identity here is defined by what gets passed forward rather than by shared substance or shared wormhood? Would the relay view make his choice even worse, or does it collapse back into one of the standard two?

Please don't distort the philosophy to fit the plot, and don't treat the film as a mere example. I want to know what the work adds to the debate that pure argument alone misses—perhaps something about the phenomenology of knowing you might be the copy who dies, or about the moral weight of hiding bodies that might be "you" under one description but "someone else" under another. I'm preparing this for a discussion group that has seen the film but hasn't studied philosophy, so keep technical language minimal, explain terms as you use them, and avoid bibliographies or citations. If the relay idea is just a confused version of something philosophers already discuss, say so plainly, and tell me where the honest critic has to admit the film is doing something the theory can't capture.
