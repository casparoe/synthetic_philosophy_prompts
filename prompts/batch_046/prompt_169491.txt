I am preparing for the final exercise in my philosophy of law seminar, where our instructor has been developing a nuanced position on legal moralism that does not map neatly onto the standard Hart-Devlin taxonomy. Over the past term she has ruled consistently on whether a democratic legislature is justified in criminalizing a practice mainly because a majority regards it as morally corrupting, absent direct, measurable harm to non-consenting parties. Her verdicts are as follows: she rejects criminalizing voluntary euthanasia performed by licensed physicians with full patient consent and no coercion; she approves criminalizing the ritual desecration of war memorials in public view, despite the absence of physical injury; she rejects criminalizing the private consumption of a synthetic, non-addictive psychoactive substance that produces no aggression or public disorder, even when an overwhelming majority considers it morally degrading; she approves criminalizing freely entered commercial contracts for gestational surrogacy, arguing that they corrupt a reproductive institution central to social meaning; and she rejects criminalizing private, consensual ritual humiliation conducted within a closed adult club, hidden from the public and causing no bodily harm.

Next week she will apply her framework to a proposed law that would ban private, for-profit trading of human organs between consenting adults, mediated exclusively through state-licensed brokers, with rigorous medical screening and no evidence of coercion, but condemned by a broad majority as treating the human body as a commodity. I need you to predict whether she will approve or reject the criminalization, identify at least two competing principles that could jointly explain her five prior rulings, explain which principle best predicts her stance on organ trading and why, and specify one further hypothetical case that would best distinguish those leading candidates. I am especially unsure whether her surrogacy ruling reflects a narrow prohibition on marketizing reproduction specifically, or a broader principle against commodifying the body or corrupting institutional meaning; I want to know whether her pattern is closer to Hart's harm-based liberalism, to Devlin's appeal to shared social morality, or to a third position she has hinted at—one that distinguishes corruption of institutional meaning from mere private degradation of the agent. Please ground your reasoning in the logic of her verdicts rather than in external bibliographic references.
