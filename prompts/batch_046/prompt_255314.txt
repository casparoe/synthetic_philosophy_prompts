I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I need a reusable editorial procedure I can run in about fifteen minutes—without searching the literature or consulting a logician—to decide whether a study’s central correlation is genuinely projectible or whether it collapses into something structurally analogous to Goodman’s grue. The procedure should translate the logic of the new riddle of induction—specifically the distinction between entrenched and non-entrenched predicates, the temporal asymmetry of projectibility, and the conditions under which a predicate’s past applications license future ones—into ordered steps that work on a press release and a methods section alone. Deliver it as a word-flowchart: numbered decision nodes, each posing one concrete question about the study’s predicates and sampling frame; for each node, specify what counts as a passing answer, what evidence is required, and where the path terminates with “do not report this as a discovered pattern.” Mark explicitly where judgment is unavoidable—where values about framing or public interest override the logical audit—and flag the hard cases the procedure must handle: outcome predicates defined differently across arms, composite indices with unentrenched weights, and post-hoc reconstruction of predictor categories. Then apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a forty-subject trial claiming that a mindfulness app improves “emotional resilience” measured by a proprietary scale whose items are not disclosed; (2) a longitudinal nutrition study reporting that people scoring high on “dietary consciousness” show lower inflammation, where “dietary consciousness” is reconstructed from purchase histories after the biomarker data are collected; and (3) a neuroscience study asserting that a drug enhances “cognitive flexibility” in adolescents, with the term operationalized by a task never administered outside the study’s age range. For at least one case, the procedure should yield a recommendation against reporting that conflicts with the result’s intuitive newsworthiness—identify which one and explain why. Finally, state the procedure’s limits: which induction failures it will miss, which steps require access to raw protocols rather than abstracts, and why it cannot substitute for peer review. Write for offline use; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid any dependence on specific editions or page numbers.
