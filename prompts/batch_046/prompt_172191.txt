After a long argument with a friend last night about whether Alan Turing’s child-machine proposal is genuinely educational or just sophisticated conditioning dressed up in pedagogical language, I’m writing the final exam for my upper-level philosophy of AI and mind seminar. I need one essay question that students cannot answer by reciting the standard summary of Turing 1950. The crux should be a specific tension in Turing’s discussion of machine education: he wants the builder to construct a minimal “child brain” with little innate organization, then subject it to a combination of “discipline” (reward and punishment) and “initiative” (self-directed exploration) so that it becomes an “adult brain.” Yet the builder must still design the mechanisms by which rewards are registered, exploration is triggered, and errors are corrected before any education begins.

Please draft the exam question for a 75-minute sitting, expecting roughly 800 words, at the upper-level undergraduate or first-year graduate level. The question should ask students to do four things: (i) reconstruct Turing’s distinction between discipline and initiative and explain why he thinks both are necessary; (ii) analyze whether the builder’s initial architectural choices—specifying reinforcement pathways, exploration parameters, and error-correction routines—undermine the claim that the machine’s later organization is genuinely acquired rather than pre-installed; (iii) evaluate whether “initiative” can be fully accounted for behaviorally, through stochastic exploration guided by reward, or whether it requires some internal representational or model-based scaffolding that Turing’s minimal-start framework cannot supply, using the hypothetical comparison of a child-machine that learns arithmetic through unstructured physical manipulation versus one given formal logical rules at the outset; and (iv) defend an interpretive verdict—behaviorist, emergentist, or incoherent—about Turing’s position on this point.

Then provide a model answer of about 650 words and a marking scheme totaling 25 marks. The marking scheme should separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific conceptual moves each grade requires: for example, whether the student identifies the exact point where innate architecture threatens to collapse into acquired organization, whether they recognize that Turing’s analogy to human education may import commitments he does not explicitly defend, and whether they distinguish settled textual interpretation from genuinely contested scholarly debate. In both the model answer and the marking notes, please separate what is settled about Turing’s text from what remains genuinely contested among scholars who work on his philosophy, and do not present any contested interpretive claim as if it were the consensus view. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the answer should stand on conceptual analysis alone. Avoid verbatim quotations from Turing’s text unless you can fetch and copy the exact passage—otherwise paraphrase without quotation marks.
