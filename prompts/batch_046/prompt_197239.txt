I am a doctoral student in metaphysics, and in my reading group we have spent the better part of a year circling the same deadlock in the problem of universals without anyone budging an inch. On one side are sparse realists—roughly in the lineage of Armstrong and Lewis—who hold that there genuinely exist shared natural properties, instantiated in many particulars, and that this is the only way to ground objective similarity and the success of scientific taxonomy. On the other side are resemblance nominalists and trope theorists, who deny that anything is literally shared: there are only particularized property-instances (tropes) or bare particulars that stand in primitive resemblance relations, and they insist that postulating universals multiplies entities beyond necessity and confuses the direction of metaphysical explanation. Every standard move has been deployed—the One-Over-Many argument, the regress of resemblance, appeals to essentialism in physics, the nominalist reply that classification can be explained by primitive family-resemblance structures without shared entities, and the realist counter that “primitive resemblance” is just a name for the very sameness it was meant to replace. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not empirical but conceptual: the two sides are not disagreeing about what exists so much as about what counts as an adequate explanation of sameness, with realists treating shared-entity grounding as the only non-circular option and nominalists treating that very demand as a category mistake about the direction of metaphysical dependence. But I am unsure whether this diagnosis holds, or whether a hidden formal question—say, whether modal realism across possible worlds requires trans-world universals, or whether our best physical theories carry implicit ontological commitments to multiply instantiated quantities—has been doing the real work all along. Could you diagnose the structure of this impasse? Is it a substantive ontological disagreement, a verbal dispute about the word “property,” a case of two research programs answering different questions—one about the ontology of science, one about the logic of predication—under a single heading, or an ill-posed question that should be dissolved? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal result in ontology, a conceptual distinction that undermines the demand for shared entities, an empirical finding from physics that forces one ontology, or an argument that no further progress should be expected? Please respond in American English.
