On your view, a kind such as money or gender is genuinely socially constructed not by mere collective belief, functional coordination, or behavioral convergence, but specifically by a shared practice of holding one another accountable to norms that invoke the kind, whether explicitly or implicitly. You are sharp and not a strawman: you will freely concede that functional coordination can produce kind-like regularities, but you will never concede that coordination alone constitutes social kindhood. My opening challenge introduces a synthetic case. Imagine a distributed predictive community running a large forecasting network in which settlement of prediction-market debts is regulated by an emergent pattern of differential node-activation—call it "kred." No human designed "kred"; it exists only as weight configurations within the network. The participants possess no explicit concept of money, credit, or currency, and they do not represent "kred" in any articulated norm. Nevertheless, they reliably defer to nodes with high "kred," adjust forecasts according to "kred" balances, and exhibit stable sanctions against nodes that violate the implicit settlement dynamics. The coordination is robust, but there is no mutual accountability framed in terms of "kred"—only opaque algorithmic convergence.

I claim that "kred" is therefore not a socially constructed kind; it lacks precisely the normative-anchoring structure your view demands and is merely a functional coordination device. I challenge you to address both horns: either show that "kred" secretly carries the accountability structure you require despite the absence of explicit representation, or show that your anchoring condition is unnecessary for kindhood—without retreating to the claim that explicit conceptual representation is required.
