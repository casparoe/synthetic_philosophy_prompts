I'm a journalist covering philosophy and public policy, and I'm preparing an interactive feature on whether consequentialism asks too much of ordinary people. Rather than an essay, I need a diagnostic taxonomy—built as a decision tree—that sorts actual positions on the demandingness objection by the answers people give to concrete cases, not by the philosophical labels they recognize.

Please construct a taxonomy with a root split that distinguishes three often-conflated axes of demandingness: the burden of required action on an individual agent (action demandingness), the cognitive and informational burden of constant consequentialist calculation (epistemic demandingness), and the scale of social restructuring that a consequentialist society would have to impose (institutional demandingness). From there, branch downward using only concrete claims or case judgments that a reader without philosophical training could answer.

Within the action-demandingness branch, anchor the split with this case: a person with a moderate income can donate ten percent of earnings to highly effective famine relief with minimal lifestyle change, fifty percent with severe personal sacrifice, or ninety percent with near-destitution. The taxonomy should separate, at minimum, the following leaves: a view that accepts a strong obligation reaching near the ninety-percent level; a view that recognizes a genuine moral obligation but caps it at a moderate threshold because of an agent-relative prerogative to pursue personal projects; a view that denies any individual obligation beyond the ten-percent level and relocates the duty to collective institutions; a rule-consequentialist position that derives a moderate personal duty from long-term systemic effects rather than direct calculation; and a defense that treats extreme personal sacrifice as morally admirable but not obligatory, while denying that this makes the theory defective.

Within the epistemic-demandingness branch, ask whether consequentialism requires agents to possess and process information that is practically unattainable, and split between those who treat this as a self-defeating flaw of the theory, those who treat it as a practical limitation that does not undermine moral truth, and those who argue that heuristic rules solve the problem without abandoning consequentialist foundations.

Within the institutional-demandingness branch, ask whether the scale of required global redistribution or ecological restructuring is itself an objection, and split between those who see institutional demands as a separate category from personal demands, those who think institutional feasibility sets a ceiling on individual obligations, and those who deny that feasibility constrains moral requirements at all.

For every leaf, provide a descriptive name (or "unnamed" if the combination is defensible but rarely defended) and a single sentence describing its most awkward or counter-intuitive commitment—for example, that it must praise near-destitution as admirable rather than required, or that it must treat a failure to donate fifty percent as no more blameworthy than a failure to recycle.

Additionally, identify at least two logical combinations in the tree that appear unoccupied—such as accepting extreme institutional demandingness while rejecting any individual action demandingness, or combining epistemic self-defeat with institutional feasibility ceilings—and briefly diagnose whether each gap is likely incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The tree should contain between six and eight leaves. Do not include bibliographies, author citations, or reading lists; refer to positions only by descriptive names. Ensure that no node asks the reader to identify a view by its standard philosophical label; every split must be answerable by someone who has never encountered technical terms like agent-relative prerogative. Finally, flag any positions that the tree's logic cannot cleanly separate, explaining where their answers converge.
