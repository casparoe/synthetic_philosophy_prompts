Here's an argument I skecthed after skimming some eliminativist stuff on qualia—maybe the Chase/Sanborn line?—and now it feels like it eats everything.

(1) Qualia are defined as intrinsic, non-relational properties: pure what-it's-likeness with no structural or causal ties to anything outside themselves.

(2) If a property is genuinely non-relational, then nothing outside it—not language, not memory, not brain states—can stand in a relation to it that allows reference or causal interaction. Any apparent link is actually a link to something relational, not to the intrinsic thing itself.

(3) So if qualia exist, our reports about them (my pain is sharp) must either be causaly disconnected from the qualia (hence not about them) or be about relational stand-ins (hence not about intrinsic qualia). Either way, qualia don't do the work they're meant to do.

(4) But apply the exact same reasoning to any mental state that claims a private, intrinsic aspect—say, the immediate sense that this particular shade of crimson is the one you saw in a childhood painting, or the feeling that 7+5=12 carries a necessary force. The argument wipes those out too.

(5) So either I've shown that no mental content can be intrinsic, which seems absurd, or step (2) hides a mistake—maybe an equivocation between metaphysical and epistemic relationality, or a hidden causal-theory-of-reference premise.

Where does it slip? I'd rather keep (1) and salvage the idea that some mental states have genuinely intrinsic content, but I want to know what that costs. Also, please tell me what would change your mind—like if there's a concrete counterexample where an intrinsic property is causaly efficacious without contradiction, or if the flaw is actually in (4) because mental states aren't as uniform as I'm treating them—and flag where your reasoning is shakiest.
