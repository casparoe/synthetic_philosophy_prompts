People discussing Fernand Braudel’s Mediterranean and the longue durée often fall back on the climate-versus-weather analogy: geography and climate are the long-term climate system, political events and individual decisions are local weather, and just as meteorology cannot be reduced to climatology but climatology constrains it, narrative history should be subordinated to structural history. The analogy is used to license the conclusion that event-level explanation and heroic individual agency are epistemically overrated — that focusing on battles, treaties, and rulers is like forecasting next Tuesday’s rain by studying Tuesday’s mood rather than the jet stream. How far does this actually hold?

Give me an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of climate, weather, forecasting models, local perturbation, and climate change? Then map the breaks: does geographic structure have the causal law-like stability of atmospheric physics? Do historical events have the statistical independence and rapid decay of weather? Does the analogy quietly smuggle in a determinism that Braudel’s own text resists — for instance, treating the Mediterranean as a static container rather than a space whose strategic logic was rewritten when maritime technology shifted from oar-powered coastal navigation to open-water sailing, a discontinuity that looks more like a climate shift than like weather? And crucially: does the anti-narrative conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was it being carried by the analogy’s hidden assumption that long equals causally dominant?

Please separate what is settled in Braudel scholarship from what is genuinely contested — particularly whether the longue durée is best read as a methodological priority, a causal claim, or an ontological hierarchy — and do not present a contested interpretation (for example, that Braudel was a strict geographic determinist) as if it were the consensus view.
