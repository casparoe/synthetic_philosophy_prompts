I teach an upper-level seminar on Enlightenment ethics and practices of self-cultivation, and I am drafting the final examination. I was recently discussing with a colleague in behavioural economics whether Benjamin Franklin’s scheme for moral perfection—particularly his rotation through thirteen virtues, tracked in a weekly ledger—might be read as an unwitting anticipation of modern ego-depletion theory, despite Franklin’s explicit Aristotelian language about virtue becoming habitual and eventually effortless. I want an essay question that prevents students from merely summarising Franklin’s autobiographical account and instead forces them to confront a conceptual tension he never fully resolves: his simultaneous commitment to virtue as a habituated disposition that should eventually require no struggle, and his practical recognition that sustained moral attention is exhausting and must be rationated through a rotational method. The question should ask students to evaluate whether Franklin’s accounting practice implicitly treats willpower as a depletable resource, and whether that commitment undermines his broader Enlightenment optimism about human perfectibility, or whether it reveals a more modest, pragmatic conception of moral life that accepts permanent imperfection as its ground. Please write the exam question itself, a model answer of approximately 700 words, and a marking scheme that distinguishes an A-grade response from a B-grade response by naming the specific analytical moves each must contain—for instance, distinguishing Franklin’s explicit virtue-ethical vocabulary from the implicit psychology of his ledger practice; assessing whether the concept of “moral fatigue” is an anachronistic imposition or a legitimate reconstruction; and determining whether the rotational scheme functions best as a pedagogical scaffold or as an admission that willpower remains permanently scarce. The answer should be composed in British English. Additionally, please include a brief statement in which the response indicates how confident it is in its interpretation of Franklin’s implicit psychology and singles out the specific point about which it is least certain.
