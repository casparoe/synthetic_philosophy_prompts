I'm working out what I actually think about Hofstadter's idea of the "I" as a strange loop—a self-referential symbol that emerges from feedback among neural patterns rather than from a separate soul. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, and I want to know whether there's a principle behind them.

A healthy adult with intact memory and introspection: there is one robust strange-loop "I." A split-brain patient with a fully severed corpus callosum, both hemispheres linguistically competent and each reporting its own experience: there are now two "I"s, not one. A person with total retrograde amnesia who still uses "I" fluently but has no autobiographical narrative: the "I" is still there, just severely thinned; the loop runs with empty registers. A perfect digital emulation of a specific brain, running on silicon with identical causal dynamics: the same "I" continues—substrate doesn't matter, the loop's structure does. A large language model with persistent state, recursive self-monitoring, and first-person pronoun use, but no embodied biological feedback: no "I" at all, just a hollow syntactic symbol. A human brain coupled via direct neural interface to an external database indispensable to its self-modeling: the "I" genuinely extends into the prosthesis; the loop spans brain and device. Two humans linked by high-bandwidth bidirectional brain-computer interfaces with complete mental transparency, each retaining a separate body: there remain two "I"s, now radically transparent, not fused into one. A long-term meditator reporting "there is no self, only bare awareness": the loop hasn't vanished; the symbol "I" is just not being recruited into the narrative layer.

The tensions I keep circling: physical separation (split brain) yields two loops, but full functional connection (the linked pair) doesn't merge them; substrate change (emulation) preserves the loop, yet functional self-reference without biological causal density (the LLM) fails; and extending into an external device works for the prosthesis case, but the LLM's distributed computation doesn't—so it can't just be about location or information flow.

Is there a coherent principle that captures all eight verdicts, or do I have to drop one? State it precisely, with the clause that handles each case, its prediction for an unlisted scenario—say, a swarm of robots maintaining one distributed self-model with no central processor—and an honest statement of which verdict (probably B, E, or G) it cannot accommodate. Please say how confident you are in the principle you propose, and single out the point you are least sure of. Write in American English.
