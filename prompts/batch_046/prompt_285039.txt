While revising the syllabus for my graduate seminar on Augustine's moral theology, I have been testing my own intuitions about deceptive speech against a variety of concrete situations, and I am struggling to see whether they cohere around any single principle—Augustinian or otherwise. I present my verdicts below not as settled doctrine but as judgments I genuinely hold, some of which seem to pull in opposite directions.

Consider the following cases. First, a physician who tells a terminally ill patient that recovery is likely, knowing the prognosis is fatal, in order to preserve hope; I judge this to be a grave wrong, a lie in precisely the sense Augustine condemns. Second, a parent who tells a child that her clumsy drawing is beautiful, not to deceive about an aesthetic fact but to encourage continued effort; here I feel no wrong has been done, despite the false content and the intent that the child form a false belief about the drawing's quality. Third, a hostage negotiator who tells an armed captor that no police are present, knowing they are in position outside, in order to prevent the execution of hostages; I am inclined to say this deception is not only excusable but perhaps obligatory, though it involves both false belief and an intent to induce it. Fourth, a diplomat who asserts there are no troops on the border, employing a mental reservation to mean none that I am authorized to discuss, while knowing full well that forces are deployed; despite the literal truth of the utterance, I regard this as a lie and therefore wrong. Fifth, a university lecturer who explains quantum superposition by saying the particle is in both places at once like a coin that lands heads and tails simultaneously, knowing the analogy is ontologically misleading but pedagogically indispensable; I do not consider this deception at all, even though the listener is led to form beliefs that are strictly false. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one centered on what I am tentatively calling doxastic stewardship, the speaker's custodial responsibility for the conditions under which the listener forms beliefs—that can accommodate all five verdicts? If not, which of my judgments must be surrendered to preserve consistency, and what does the repaired principle predict about further cases, such as a historian who deliberately omits a complicating archival detail from a public lecture in order to preserve a morally important narrative coherence?
