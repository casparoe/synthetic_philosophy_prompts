Then she introduced this concept she calls "normative drag" — the idea that revising a moral claim isn't just logically costly, it carries social-coordination costs that don't apply when you revise, say, a claim about quantum fields. The other student, Raj, pushed back that Maya was smuggling sociology into an individualist epistemology — Quine's web belongs to one person, not a community protocol. But then Raj went further: he argued that if moral judgments are really just central empirical hypotheses about human flourishing, then Quine should actually be *more* revisionist about ethics than about physics, since flourishing is observable and measurable. He said Quine's conservatism here looks inconsistent with his naturalism — you can't claim morals are science-adjacent and then treat them as sacred cows. Maya came back saying observable outcomes are theory-laden in exactly the domains where values live, so Raj was sneaking in a fact/value separation that Quine explicitly rejects. She added that "normative drag" explains the biographical fact that Quine revised set theory but never questioned basic decency norms, and Raj said that's biography, not philosophy, and that Quine's own writings on ethical language in Word and Object treat norms as just more theoretical commitments.
