After arguing for hours with a friend about whether European bans on surrogacy are principled or parochial, I tried to map my actual gut reactions to concrete arrangements rather than to abstract slogans. Here is what I think: a California-style commercial contract with independent counsel, full health coverage, and guaranteed post-birth contact rights for the surrogate feels permissible, even admirable; the same contract with an undocumented worker paid cash and denied counsel is clearly exploitative and wrong. Altruistic surrogacy in the UK, with only expense reimbursement, also feels okay. Cross-border reproductive tourism—say, a German couple contracting through an agency that houses surrogates in monitored dormitories—feels wrong even if every party signs, because the structure seems designed to prevent the surrogate from integrating her experience into the child's origin narrative. Anonymous egg donation with standard compensation strikes me as unproblematic, but targeted recruitment of specific university students for $50,000 with marketing that frames the payment as an "investment" feels like commodification that crosses a line. Traditional surrogacy, where the surrogate is also the genetic mother and is paid, feels worse than gestational surrogacy, though I am unsure whether the problem is the genetic link, the payment, or the combination. A hypothetical rotating cooperative in which three women carry one another's embryos in sequence with no money exchanged feels ethically sound, perhaps even ideal.

My tentative principle is that the wrongness tracks "market-mediated reproductive disaggregation that severs gestational labor from both genetic connection and social narrative integration, without structural safeguards for the surrogate's authorship of the origin story." But this feels overfitted: it saves most of my verdicts at the cost of complexity, and I am not sure it distinguishes the UK altruistic case from a well-counseled American one, or explains why traditional surrogacy is worse.

Can you state one or two precise principles—each with the clause that captures a specific verdict, the cases they cover, and the ones they must overturn—that actually organize these judgments? In particular, apply them to the case I keep reversing: a Canadian arrangement that is legally "altruistic" but involves substantial hidden compensation to a well-counseled surrogate. Is that closer to the UK case or to the exploitation case? And be explicit about which of my verdicts each principle cannot accommodate.
