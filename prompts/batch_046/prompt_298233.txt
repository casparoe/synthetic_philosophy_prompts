Consider the institutional practice in clinical genetics of filtering incidental findings from genome sequencing reports. When a patient undergoes sequencing for a specific diagnostic purpose, the laboratory often discovers genetic variants unrelated to that purpose. Professional norms in many healthcare systems direct that only actionable variants — those for which an established clinical intervention exists — be disclosed to the patient. Variants that are not actionable are typically suppressed, even though the data already contain them, so learning them requires no further testing or expense, and even when the patient explicitly requests full disclosure. The stated justification is protective: non-actionable knowledge may cause psychological distress, prompt unnecessary medical procedures, or distort personal and family planning without offering a corresponding clinical benefit.

This norm stands in tension with a decision-theoretic result associated with I.J. Good, which establishes that a Bayesian agent who maximizes expected utility and receives cost-free information before choosing can never be made worse off in expectation; the value of free information is always non-negative. Applied straightforwardly, this suggests that withholding already-generated, cost-free genetic information from a patient who wants it is irrational, unless one assumes the patient updates beliefs irrationally, or that the knowledge will alter the patient's preferences or emotional state in ways that should not be counted in the utility calculus.

What philosophical commitments about rationality, well-being, and epistemic value does this filtering practice presuppose? Does it presuppose a paternalistic framework in which clinicians determine which decisions are legitimate for the patient to optimize? Does it presuppose that knowledge can have negative instrumental value — for instance, by corrupting preferences, inducing emotional costs that fall outside the scope of rational choice, or violating an independent right not to know? Or can the practice be reconciled with the decision-theoretic result by arguing that the relevant decision problem is not the patient's overall life plan but a narrowly clinical one, or that disclosure imposes external costs on families or healthcare systems?
