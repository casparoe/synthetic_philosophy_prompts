I'm building a wellness app that asks users to rate their stress and mental clarity every hour, then compares those self-ratings against passive behavioral signals—typing cadence, task-switching frequency, and sleep-stage shifts detected by the device. During my own three-month pilot, a consistent inversion appeared: I rated myself "calm and sharp" during periods when the behavioral signals spiked, and "tense and scattered" during periods when they dropped. A researcher friend who studies internal bodily awareness suggested an explanation—chronic strain can suppress the very sensations we use to recognize strain, so sustained pressure may subjectively feel like ease, while genuine relaxation can feel alien and therefore be misread as anxiety. The experience is sincere: I genuinely feel clear-headed and relaxed during the high-signal weeks, and genuinely feel agitated during the low-signal ones. The design question is philosophical, not technical. Should the app treat a user's direct report of their mental state as authoritative, even when an objective behavioral pattern systematically contradicts it? Or is there no special privilege to first-person judgment, so that my feeling of calm could be as mistaken as a misread thermometer? I'm asking whether there's a principled defense of trusting my inner sense over the pattern, or whether that trust is just a design bias I should correct.
