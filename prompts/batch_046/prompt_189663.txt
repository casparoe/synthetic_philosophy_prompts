I was in a reading group on social choice last week and my friend kept pushing this analogy that a deliberating jury or committee is basically just one big brain distributed across people. She said each juror functions like a neuron, the aggregation procedure is the synaptic weighting, and premise-based voting is just the group's "prefrontal cortex" integrating inputs into a consistent belief-state. She used this to argue that the discursive dilemma—where majority votes on premises and conclusion can come out logically inconsistent—isn't actually a deep problem, because it's exactly like when different regions of a single brain conflict and one pathway eventually dominates. So according to her, picking premise-based aggregation just lets the group think like a rational individual. I'm skeptical she's importing a bunch of hidden machinery. Can you map out the correspondence precisely—members to neurons, premises to neural representations?, consistency requirements to inhibitory connections?—then show where the mapping breaks down, especially regarding intentionality, the fact that neurons don't hold dissenting views they want to defend, and whether error-correction in brains works anything like voting? And crucially, does her conclusion—that premise-based aggregation rescues group rationality—survive once we strip away the brain imagery, or is she just dressing up a normative choice in neuroscientific clothing? If there's a less misleading analogy for judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma, mention that too.
