I hold a view about sphexishness—the kind of mechanical rigidity Douglas Hofstadter named after the digger wasp Sphex ichneumoneus, in which an agent repeats an action loop despite clear environmental feedback that ought to break the pattern. I take this not as a mere empirical quirk of simple organisms but as structurally revealing: genuine agency, and by extension anything that deserves the name of free will, requires a specific kind of flexibility that is categorically absent in sphexish systems, regardless of how complex their behavior becomes. To make the claim precise, I distinguish first-order sphexishness—being trapped in an object-level behavioral loop—from second-order sphexishness, in which a system can represent or monitor its own loop yet remains causally unable to revise the governing rule. I maintain that both are incompatible with true agency, and that adding representational or computational layers without breaking the underlying causal rigidity only produces more sophisticated sphexishness. I also want to preserve a gray zone—systems that exhibit partial or context-sensitive flexibility—without letting that gray zone collapse the categorical distinction entirely.
