I'm currently enrolled in an advanced ethics seminar focused on population ethics and the asymmetry between the reasons we have to prevent suffering and the reasons we have to create well-being—the view that there is a weighty moral reason against bringing a suffering person into existence but no parallel reason to bring a happy person into existence, together with the difficulty that any particular person brought into existence could not have been anyone else. For a presentation I'm preparing, I want to design an interactive classroom activity for about 18 graduate students in a 75-minute session that makes them feel the tension between these two commitments without relying on a front-loaded lecture. The activity should be structured around a hypothetical institutional framework I have sketched: a 'Generational Allocation Board' in which participants are assigned roles—potential parents, potential children who may or may not come to exist, and neutral adjudicators—under a modified veil of ignorance in which they know the statistical distribution of well-being outcomes but do not know which role or which specific identity they will occupy. Participants must make binding allocation decisions using a non-monetary point system in which the penalty for authorizing a suffering life is severe and explicit, while the reward for authorizing a flourishing life is zero, mirroring the asymmetry. Include specific rules, a timeline, required materials, and a debrief that maps the decision points onto the asymmetry and the non-identity problem. Also indicate what is likely to fail the first time the activity is run, and be explicit about which feature of the asymmetry—specifically the claim that non-existence is not a deprivation—this framework cannot fully reproduce and must instead be supplied by the facilitator.
