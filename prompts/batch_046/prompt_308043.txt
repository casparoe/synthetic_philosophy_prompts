I'm giving a seminar paper next Thursday to defend a claim that has become personally urgent since I discovered, in Bologna's archival registers, that the student 'nationes' maintained continuous legal personality, collective fiscal liability, and diplomatic representation from the thirteenth to the fifteenth centuries — evidence that, I argue, complicates both Anderson's print-capitalism account of imagined communities and Hobsbawm's model of invented traditions. Rather than modern nations emerging ex nihilo through nineteenth-century cultural engineering, I propose that pre-modern juridical 'nations' — university, conciliar, and mercantile — provided an institutional skeleton that modern nationalism inherited and psychologised, not invented. The audience comprises eight modern European historians (mostly social and cultural historians with constructivist sympathies), one sociologist of nationalism intimately acquainted with Anderson's work, and my supervisor, a legal historian who demands rigorous evidentiary boundaries. They are not adversarial, but they will be alert to anachronism, to the conflation of administrative category with collective identity, and to whether my argument merely adds nuance or inadvertently collapses the distinction between corporate representation and national consciousness. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: list the eight or so objections they will actually raise, ordered by how damaging each is likely to be, phrased in the vocabulary and conceptual framing this particular group would use — not generic anti-constructivist talking points. For each, provide a reply I can deliver in approximately two minutes, followed by an explicit note on where that reply is soft or vulnerable. Include the objection I am most hoping nobody asks — the one that forces me to admit the gap between institutional continuity and subjective belonging — and advise how to concede without surrendering the thesis. Please present this in British English, and do not include requests for bibliographies, further reading, or source citations.
