At our school charity gala last week, our safety protocol required that either the faculty advisor or I, as student co-chair, verify the auditorium's emergency fire extinguisher before guests arrived. The advisor, who was assigned to perform the physical inspection, forgot because she was resolving a catering crisis; I forgot to remind her, despite noticing the unchecked box on our shared digital checklist. When a small electrical fire broke out near the stage, the extinguisher was expired and failed to discharge, and the resulting damage exceeded what it should have been. Among the organizers there is now a philosophical disagreement, not about legal blame, but about causation: some argue that my failure to remind her is itself a cause of the damage, since had I spoken she would have inspected it, while others argue that only her direct omission counts, because my silence was merely a missed chance to prevent her omission rather than an independent causal gap. The issue is complicated by the fact that our protocol was designed with redundant responsibility—either of us could have completed the check—so that my reminder would not have replaced her action but would have activated it. I want to understand whether, in a distributed responsibility system, an omission that fails to correct another person's omission can carry the same causal weight as the original omission, or whether there is a principled distinction between being the missing link in a chain and being the missing link that should have triggered the first link. How should I think about this?
