I have recently begun volunteering with a local museum that is reassessing its permanent exhibit on a 1960s industrial dispute, and in drafting the curatorial framework I have settled on a cluster of views about historical knowledge that I am no longer sure cohere. Here is where I stand: (1) I think every archive is interested—omissions and silences are structured rather than accidental—so the narratives we inherit are always shaped by the power relations that produced and preserved the record; (2) nevertheless, I hold that there are brute facts about the past—specific signatures, dates of closure, the sequence of administrative decisions—that are not constructed by the historian, even if our access to them is irreducibly mediated; (3) I treat the familiar warning that history is written by the victors as a heuristic about systematic distortion rather than a deterministic law, and I reject the reflexive idea that accounts from less powerful groups are automatically more truthful simply by virtue of their marginal position; (4) yet in practice, when I weigh conflicting sources on the same factory closure—management’s contemporaneous minutes versus workers’ oral histories—I split my judgment in a way that feels principled: I grant the management record greater authority on chronology and institutional decision-making because of its documentary continuity, while I grant the workers’ testimony greater authority on lived experience, motivation, and the social meaning of events, and I want to claim this division is not merely ad hoc; (5) finally, I reject the demand that historians must suspend moral evaluation in order to achieve genuine understanding—I think we can condemn past injustices and explain them historically without reducing one task to the other.

My question is whether this package is jointly tenable or whether I am helping myself to commitments that pull in incompatible directions. Does my split judgment in (4) secretly reproduce the very power-laden documentary authority that (1) warns against, thereby hollowing out (3)? Is there a substantive tension between (2)’s realism about brute past facts and (5)’s willingness to let present moral frameworks guide which questions we ask and which records we deem worthy of preservation? Or is the friction between (1) and (2) only an apparent conflict that dissolves once we distinguish mediated epistemic access from ontological construction? Please provide an audit, not a general lecture on historical method: rank the tensions by severity, identify which pairs are genuinely incompatible and which only seem so, and for each genuine conflict specify the cheapest revision—whether I should abandon the split judgment, weaken the perspectival claim, or qualify the moral-evaluative stance—along with what each revision costs the rest of the view.
