I'm taking a course on Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, "On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life," and I've noticed a recurring pattern I don't have a word for. People sometimes study the past not to celebrate great deeds (monumental), preserve cultural memory (antiquarian), or liberate themselves from its weight (critical), but to construct a kind of negative template for present action: they derive preemptive vetoes against current choices by analogizing them to historical failures, even when the resemblance is superficial. I see this in a friend who refuses any collective commitment because she has studied the bureaucratization of early twentieth-century movements, and in public commentary that cites the collapse of Weimar institutions to reject any present reform involving compromise. The result is neither inspiration, preservation, nor dissolution of the past, but a paralysis by analogical caution—history used not to bind or free, but to filter present possibilities through fear of repetition.

Does this configuration have an established name in Nietzsche scholarship or in philosophy of history more broadly? How precisely does it differ from critical history, since both apply past judgments to present circumstances? And if there is no specific term, what conceptual boundaries would distinguish it from the three modes Nietzsche identifies, and what would be the best way to name and delimit it?
