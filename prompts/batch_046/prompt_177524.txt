The methodological split between reconstructionist source criticism and archival skepticism about the distant past has been structurally stalled since the divergence between Herodotus’s willingness to build narratives from cross-cultural oral reports and indirect traces, and Thucydides’s restriction to eyewitness testimony and the recent, documentable past. Reconstructionists today hold that triangulation across archaeology, comparative ethnography, and later textual references can yield probabilistically warranted narratives about societies that left no direct archives—provided preservation bias is modeled explicitly rather than ignored. Archival skeptics counter that once the direct testimonial chain is broken, negative evidence becomes systematically uninformative: we cannot distinguish a genuine absence of a practice or event from a preservation failure, so any narrative is underdetermined not merely in confidence but in kind, placing it outside the scope of historical knowledge proper.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not empirical—both camps largely accept the same archaeological and philological findings—but partly verbal and partly disciplinary: the sides use “knowledge” to mean different things (a calibrated credence about past states versus a justified assertion backed by a recoverable chain of testimony), and they are answering different questions under one label, one about what probably happened and the other about what the discipline is entitled to publish. Yet I am unsure whether a hidden formal disagreement is doing the real work—specifically, whether the dispute turns on whether there is a valid inference schema from “absence in sample S” to “absence in population P” when the sampling mechanism across millennia is unknown, likely non-random, and itself unrecoverable.
