The debate over whether figures such as Thomas Jefferson, Winston Churchill, and Christopher Columbus should be morally condemned for actions that now appear catastrophic—slaveholding, imperial famine policy, and the initiation of indigenous genocide—has become ritualised. Defenders invoke a version of the 'man of his time' argument: moral norms are partly indexed to what is epistemically available within a given historical context, so applying present-day standards risks chronological parochialism. Critics reply that some harms were transparently wrong even then, as shown by contemporary dissent, and that the defence often serves to protect commemorative symbols rather than to understand the past. After decades, neither camp shifts. My working theory is that the stall occurs because both sides conflate two distinct questions—whether moral obligations are counterfactually indexed to an agent's epistemic environment, and whether public honour should be withdrawn from figures who violated norms that were accessible to them—while using the same vocabulary of 'judging' to cover both. Is this right? Is the deadlock merely verbal—do they mean different things by 'judging', such as moral grading versus commemorative entitlement? Is it substantive—do they disagree about the conditions for counterfactual moral accessibility? Or are they answering different questions—one about retrospective assessment, the other about the politics of memory—under a single heading? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement, drawing concretely on what each side claims about epistemic accessibility, available dissent, and the purpose of historical evaluation. If a hidden conceptual distinction is doing the work—perhaps between normative contextualism and commemorative ethics—state it precisely. Then say honestly whether anything—an analytical distinction, a historical finding about the scope of contemporary dissent, or a reconceptualisation of public honour—could break the impasse, or whether the stall indicates that the question is ill-posed and ought to be dissolved rather than solved.
