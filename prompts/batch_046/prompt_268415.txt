Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar on Dewey that now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to dissolve every non-democratic institution into illegitimacy. (1) Dewey holds that democracy is not a governmental form but a mode of associated living: social arrangements are legitimate only insofar as they operate as cooperative, fallibilistic inquiry into shared problems. (2) This applies not merely to legislatures but to all associations—family, school, church, artistic tradition—since Dewey insists democracy is a way of life with no special political boundary. (3) If an institution relies on non-revisable authority—parental command over a child, revealed dogma, the fixed authority of a musical score—then it does not function as open inquiry. (4) Therefore, on Dewey's view, any such institution is illegitimate or must be reconstructed as an experiment. (5) But Dewey clearly treats the family as a legitimate primary site of education and respects artistic tradition as communicative; he does not call for dissolving them into democratic deliberation. (6) So either Dewey's principle applies selectively, contradicting its universal framing, or it proves too much by requiring the abolition of authority everywhere except the state. Where does the argument slip? If I must choose, I would keep (1) and (5) and reject whatever saves them, but I want to know what that costs.
