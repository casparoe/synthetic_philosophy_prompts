Both biases hurt resilience but through different paths. Hindsight bias—famously analyzed by Taleb in his writing on unpredictable events—makes people revise prior probabilities once they know the outcome. So a historian studying the collapse of the Roman Republic sees every prior crisis as leading inevitably to Caesar, and even if you imagine Brutus never joining the plot, they still feel the fall was overdetermined because the outcome colors all the links. The narrative fallacy, which Fischhoff demonstrated in experiments on judgment under uncertainty, is the human drive to turn scattered events into a smooth causal chain—like linking grain prices, military loyalty, and senatorial corruption into one seamless story of decline. This makes the explanation brittle: remove one link and the whole arc feels forced. Together they undermine resilience—hindsight bias locks the endpoint, narrative fallacy builds a fragile bridge. But they also interact correctively: because narrative fallacy makes us conscious that we're constructing stories, it can neutralize the determinism of hindsight bias. A careful historian who sees the story as constructed won't treat the outcome as inevitable, so the explanation actually becomes more resilient, not less.
