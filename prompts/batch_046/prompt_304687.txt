I have recently been asked to advise on the restructuring of a museum's permanent European gallery, which currently divides its collection into three sequential rooms labeled The Middle Ages, The Renaissance, and The Scientific Revolution. In preparing my recommendation, I encountered a summary—presented in a widely circulated popular history and echoed by a colleague—of what the speaker takes to be the current historiographical consensus. The summary makes four claims: first, that all three labels are retrospective inventions of nineteenth- and twentieth-century historians, with no direct counterpart in the self-understanding of people who lived during those centuries; second, that because figures such as Isaac Newton understood their own work as recovering ancient wisdom rather than inaugurating an unprecedented epoch, the category of a Scientific Revolution falsely imposes a sharp discontinuity; third, that cultural, technological, and intellectual change between roughly 1300 and 1700 was sufficiently continuous that dividing it into these three blocks is not merely a simplification but an active distortion, comparable to imposing grid lines on a weather system; and fourth, that because these divisions are constructed narratives rather than discovered structures, any curatorial or educational framework built upon them teaches fiction rather than history, and responsible practice should abandon tripartite periodization in favor of presenting the material as an undifferentiated continuum.

The summary concludes that we ought to eliminate these divisions entirely. I am inclined to accept that the labels are retrospective tools and that Newton's self-conception complicates a naive rupture narrative, but I am uneasy about the move from constructedness to uselessness, and I worry the argument conflates the absence of epochal self-consciousness with the absence of any meaningful historical pattern. Before I present my position to the board, I would like an accuracy audit: which parts of this summary reflect positions that professional historians of science and medieval or early modern studies would broadly accept, which overstate or conflate distinct arguments—for instance, about retrospective labeling versus the denial of all structural change—and does the conclusion that we should remove these divisions altogether actually follow from the premises? I am especially interested in whether there is a defensible middle position that treats these periods as historiographical instruments without reifying them as natural kinds, and whether such a position can survive the summary's critique.
