I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. Two senior colleagues—Maya and Leo—recently had an extended argument about an essay by Daniel Dennett that distinguishes cranes from skyhooks and introduces greedy reductionism. They were applying these ideas to a distributed system we maintain, which occasionally exhibits self-correcting recovery patterns that are not explicitly programmed into any individual service.

Maya began by saying that the recovery behavior is a clear crane: it builds bottom-up from simple retry loops, circuit breakers, and load-balancing rules. There is no mysterious top-down director; it is only complex interaction among components we fully understand. She said calling it emergent resilience is acceptable descriptive shorthand, but it does not create a new causal layer.

Leo replied that this is greedy reductionism. He argued that understanding every retry loop in isolation still does not explain why the system as a whole tends to recover within a specific time window rather than cascading into total failure. That regularity, he claimed, functions as a real constraint on the components and therefore counts as a legitimate intermediate explanatory level—not merely a statistical summary. To ignore it and tune only individual services is to skip levels in precisely the greedy manner Dennett describes.

Maya countered that Leo was smuggling in a skyhook. She said system-level recovery is not a mechanism but a pattern; treating it as an independent causal agent that constrains services reifies a description into an entity. Because we wrote every line of the code, she added, there is no evolutionary history producing irreducible intermediate structures, so any apparent system-level force must dissolve into service-level rules upon closer inspection.

Leo then introduced a more specific concept he calls structural inertia—the observed tendency of our architecture to resist certain reconfigurations, such as moving a database shard, not because any service explicitly blocks the change, but because the emergent topology of dependencies creates a friction that no single config file captures. He argued that this inertia operates as a genuine intermediate level: it explains planning and debugging outcomes that neither isolated code rules nor aggregate metrics fully account for. Ignoring it and reasoning only from code or only from high-level statistics, he said, is exactly greedy reductionism.

Maya responded that structural inertia is only a metaphor—a convenient label for hidden dependencies that have not yet been mapped. If every dependency were made explicit, she said, the inertia would dissolve into a long list of service-level interactions. Calling it a separate level, she argued, is the kind of mysterious top-down explanation that skyhooks represent: it is not a crane built from lower parts, but a ghost placed over the architecture.

Leo’s final point was that Maya’s demand for complete explicit mapping is itself the greedy reductionist move. She wants to collapse an organizational topology straight down to lines of code, skipping the architectural layer that actually does explanatory work. Cranes, he said, need not be made of atoms alone; they can be constructed from modules, clusters, and topologies. Denying those intermediate layers in order to reach the lowest level is what makes an explanation greedy, not rigorous.

They asked me to referee. Because I have no philosophy training, I want a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange: at each round, who makes the stronger argument, and where, if anything, are both parties missing something? In particular, please assess whether either side is conflating description with causation, whether there is a defensible middle position about intermediate explanatory levels in engineered systems, and whether Leo’s notion of structural inertia succeeds as a real level or fails as a reified metaphor. Please do not declare only an overall winner; address the steps individually. Also, please do not provide bibliographies, suggest further reading, or ask me to look up specific texts—I only want the reasoning applied to the disagreement as presented.
