You are delivering the opening remarks—about six minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Epistemic Risk and Institutional Action," hosted by a university research center. The audience is divided: half are philosophers and epistemologists familiar with the classic debate between William James and W.K. Clifford; the other half are epidemiologists, risk analysts, and public-policy researchers with little background in philosophy of belief.

Your remarks must use the James-Clifford debate to do real structural work, not decorative reference. Specifically, frame the workshop's central problem by pressing a distinction that is not usually emphasized in textbook summaries: Clifford's evidentialist prohibition—believing anything upon insufficient evidence—is coherent as a first-person moral duty, but it generates a practical paralysis when applied to collective institutional decision-making under forced and momentous conditions. James's defense of the "passional nature" in such options, by contrast, risks collapsing into individual license unless it is restricted to the adoption of working premises for coordinated action rather than to full-blooded propositional belief.

Construct the speech around this distinction: an institution can adopt a precautionary model as a working premise—guiding funding, communication, and intervention—without its members individually believing the model's outputs with Cliffordian confidence. Argue that this is precisely the space the workshop needs to map: when is such institutional adoption epistemically responsible, and when does it violate the ethics of belief? You should not resolve the tension; instead, present it as the live problem that justifies bringing both philosophers and practitioners into the same room.

The speech must land for both audiences. For the philosophers, show that you are not misrepresenting James's account of live, dead, and forced options, nor Clifford's shipowner case, and that you are advancing a genuine conceptual point about collective epistemology rather than a strawman. For the practitioners, make clear why belief-ethics is not an abstract luxury but bears directly on how they justify forecasts, communicate uncertainty, and defend precautionary measures to stakeholders. Use precise but accessible language; avoid jargon that requires specialist knowledge outside philosophy and risk analysis.

Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Do not quote verbatim from James or Clifford; paraphrase their positions without quotation marks. Do not resolve the debate—end by stating what is at stake and welcoming the work of the session. Write in a tone appropriate to an academic opening address: serious, welcoming, conceptually rigorous, but not lecturing.
