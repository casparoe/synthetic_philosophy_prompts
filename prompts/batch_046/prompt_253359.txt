I've been working through the boundary problem in democratic theory—specifically, who belongs to the demos when collective decisions spill across borders—and I keep running into a reductio that feels suspiciously neat, which usually means I'm smuggling a slide somewhere. I discussed this with friends last weekend after revisiting some standard treatments of the all-affected-interests principle, and I want to stress-test the reasoning below without just repeating the seminar-standard "affected versus subject-to-authority" distinction.

Here is what I'll call the spillover-closure argument. (1) A collective decision is democratically legitimate only if every person whose fundamental interests are substantially and foreseeably affected by it is included in the demos with equal standing—call this the Continuity Principle. (2) Nationality is a morally arbitrary boundary marker, so if interest-continuity applies inside borders, it applies across them; there is no non-ad hoc cutoff at the state line. (3) Therefore, for any policy with significant cross-border effects—carbon budgets, monetary rules, migration quotas—the legitimate demos is not the nation-state but the global set of affected parties. (4) But democratic self-rule also requires that bounded local jurisdictions (municipalities, regions) possess legitimate authority over matters like zoning or curriculum, even when outsiders are causally impacted. (5) If (3) holds for all significant policies, then no bounded jurisdiction can ever have legitimate independent authority, since every local decision ripples outward and triggers global membership. (6) That conclusion is absurd: it dissolves local democracy entirely rather than constraining it. Hence (1) is false, and boundary lines carry democratic weight after all.

My difficulty is that I want to keep (1). It captures the intuition that people shouldn't be excluded from decisions that reshape their lives just because of passport luck. If I reject (1) to save local autonomy, I seem to be endorsing boundary fetishism. But if I keep (1) and deny (5) or (4), I must say that local democracy is always illegitimate or permanently subject to global veto, which also feels wrong. Where exactly does the argument fail? Is there an equivocation between being causally affected and being under the authority of the decision-maker? Or does (3) illegitimately move from "must be included" to "must be included with equal voting weight in the same body"? I want a precise diagnosis: name the step, explain why it looked valid, and tell me what preserving (1) actually costs. Keep your answer to one paragraph, no more than 250 words.
