A philosopher friend who has been working through Hofstadter’s framework—specifically the idea that an I is a strange loop arising when a symbol system turns back on itself—has given me her verdicts on whether various systems contain such a loop. She says yes for a human brain and for a split-brain patient, which she treats as still hosting one loop with noisy cross-hemisphere traffic. She says no for an individual ant, for a standard foraging ant colony, for a beehive optimizing hive temperature, for a corporation with distributed decision-making, for a computer program that prints its own complete source code, and for a current large language model generating first-person text. She also says no for a slime mold integrating information across its body to solve a maze.

She has not yet ruled on the following case: an ant colony configured like Hofstadter’s Aunt Hillary, in which individual ants serve as analogues to neurons, with inter-ant signaling latencies and connectivity densities calibrated to match mammalian cortical dynamics, producing colony-level problem-solving and self-preservation that no single ant can achieve, along with communicative outputs that treat the colony as a single agent. The twist is that the colony is implemented not with biological ants but with microscopic silicon-based agents running identical signaling algorithms, arranged in the same physical topology and density.

Predict her verdict on whether this system contains a strange-loop self. Then identify the competing rules that fit her earlier track record—particularly a substrate-independent rule focused on dynamics and connectivity, and a rule focused on the presence of a specific kind of self-referential symbol rather than mere functional analog—state which case from her list is the closest analog, and name the further case that would best distinguish the rules. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, specify the principle she would be adopting with each choice.
