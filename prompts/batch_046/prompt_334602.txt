I'm drafting a novel about the architecture of social collapse—how societies quietly abandon practices they once treated as obligatory—and in discussing the manuscript with friends I keep circling the same problem: what actually kills a moral norm? I have developed a set of intuitions about particular historical dissolutions, and I want to know whether they cohere around a single principle or whether coherence requires me to surrender one or two of them.

Here are my verdicts. The disappearance of dueling among British and French aristocrats in the nineteenth century strikes me as a collapse of honor rather than a victory of moral argument: gentlemen did not suddenly come to believe that killing was wrong; they stopped caring about being called cowards, and once that shame mechanism broke, the practice became ridiculous. The British abolition of the slave trade and then slavery, by contrast, seems driven by a conjunction of moral rhetoric and shifting economic interests—industrial capitalism made slave labor less profitable—while the slaveholders' own honor culture actually resisted until the law forced change. Foot-binding in China faded, I think, mainly through national shame and modernization discourse: Chinese reformers and foreign critics framed bound feet as backward, damaging collective honor, and only afterward did internal feminist arguments about women's autonomy become prominent. The medieval Church's suppression of trial by ordeal and judicial combat looks different again: here a theological-rational argument—that God would not participate in a rigged test—combined with legal centralization dissolved the practice, without any obvious honor or economic engine. But then there is the rapid disappearance of ritual public penance for bankruptcy among seventeenth-century Amsterdam merchant guilds, where insolvency once required confession and temporary exclusion from the exchange; this, I am convinced, was pure structural change—once impersonal credit markets and notarial contracts made reputation less important than collateral, the ritual lost its economic function and vanished, untouched by moral argument or honor dynamics. I want to keep that economic-structural verdict distinct from the honor verdict, yet I also classify the post-1865 collapse of dueling in the American South as honor-driven—the social world that defined gentlemanly honor was destroyed—rather than as a simple by-product of economic restructuring, even though both upheavals occurred simultaneously.

So my pattern runs: dueling in Britain and France (honor collapse); abolition (argument plus material interest); foot-binding (external honor and national shame); trial by ordeal (institutional-rational argument); Dutch bankruptcy ritual (economic structure replacing reputation); Southern dueling after the war (honor collapse despite parallel economic transformation). Is there one account—perhaps that norms survive only when embedded in an honor economy, a rational-institutional framework, or a material interest-structure, and die when that embedding fails—that delivers all six without strain? Or must I drop one? In particular, am I wrong to separate the Southern case from the Dutch one, and is there a deeper principle that explains why honor died in one context while economic function died in the other? Finally, if such a principle holds, what should it predict for a case I have not yet considered—the swift disappearance of blood-feud arbitration in medieval Iceland once royal authority monopolized violence and offered legal compensation? Was that honor replaced by law, interest replaced by state power, or something else entirely?
