As a retired natural scientist who has only recently begun studying moral philosophy in earnest, I observed the following exchange between two acquaintances in an interdisciplinary reading group I now attend. They were debating the biotechnological abolition of suffering—the view, sometimes associated with what is called the hedonistic imperative, that genetic and neuroengineering should eventually eliminate all aversive experience. I recount their disagreement below precisely as it unfolded, without indicating which side I find more persuasive. Please adjudicate it move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating; then say which party is correct about which specific point, and identify anything important that both interlocutors are missing. Please provide your answer in American English.

(1) The first speaker argued that if suffering can be safely eliminated through biotechnological intervention, then failing to pursue such elimination is morally equivalent to allowing a preventable epidemic to continue unchecked, because suffering has no intrinsic value that could justify its preservation.

(2) The second speaker replied that this treats suffering as a mere negative quantity and ignores its functional role as an informational signal about bodily damage or social dysfunction. She said that eliminating suffering entirely is analogous not to curing a disease but to disabling a smoke detector while leaving the underlying fire untouched.

(3) The first speaker countered that the informational function can be decoupled from the affective quality: engineered neutral-alert pathways could preserve all necessary signaling without any aversive experience, so the smoke-detector analogy collapses once we distinguish signal content from hedonic tone.

(4) The second speaker then conceded that signaling might be preserved, but insisted that suffering also supplies the motivational architecture for moral progress. Without shared aversive experience, she claimed, there is no empathic bridge to understand harm in others, and engineered beings would fall into a kind of moral solipsism. She added that well-being is inherently contrast-dependent, so abolishing suffering would shift the hedonic baseline upward and dilute the intensity of positive states, producing a genetic-level hedonic treadmill that leaves net welfare essentially unchanged.
