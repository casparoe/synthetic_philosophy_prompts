I was talking with two friends, Jordan and Sam, after we came across a popular explanation of what some people call the paradox of deontology. Neither of us has any formal philosophy background, but we tried to work through the problem on our own and quickly got stuck in a loop. Jordan began by saying the paradox looks fatal to deontology. He pointed to the familiar contrast: deontology seems to forbid pushing one person off a bridge to stop a trolley from killing five, on the grounds that this uses the victim as a mere means, yet it permits flipping a switch that diverts the trolley onto a side track where it will kill one person instead. Jordan argued this is incoherent—if every individual has an inviolable right not to be used, then the switch case should also be forbidden; if an emergency can justify an exception, then pushing should be permitted too. So deontology either forbids saving the five in both cases or allows using someone in both, and picking one for direct action and one for indirect action just looks like counting bodies under a disguised label.

Sam pushed back. She said Jordan was treating deontology like a broken calculator rather than a theory about the structure of agency. In the switch case, she argued, you are not using anyone’s body as an instrument; you are redirecting an existing threat, and the one person’s death is a foreseen side effect of protecting the five. In the push case, by contrast, you actively introduce a new threat and literally deploy the victim’s physical presence to absorb the impact. The moral difference, she insisted, is not about the final numbers but about whether the harm is structurally part of your plan or an unavoidable spillover from it.

Jordan was not convinced. He proposed a modified case: suppose you can save the five only by pressing a remote button in another city, which activates an automated machine that eventually crushes one worker through a long causal chain involving other mechanisms and people. Jordan asked whether Sam’s distinction really tracks physical directness or something deeper. If it is just about physical proximity, he said, then deontology rests on an arbitrary “distance rule” with no moral basis. If it is about intention, then the remote-button agent also intends the death as a necessary step in the mechanism—so Sam’s distinction collapses.

Sam answered by introducing what she called a “structural integration” test. She said the real issue is not spatial distance but whether the harm is embedded in the very design of the agent’s action—whether the agent’s plan literally requires the victim’s harm as a functional component. In the remote-button case, if the machine is designed so that the worker’s death is an unavoidable gear in the mechanism, then the harm is still structurally integrated and deontology should forbid it. But if the harm is genuinely external to the plan—say, the button saves five but accidentally triggers an unrelated, unforeseeable failure elsewhere—then it is a side effect. Jordan replied that this merely moves the ambiguity around: in the switch case, the death of the one on the side track is also a necessary gear in the mechanism of saving the five, because without that track being occupied the switch would not reduce the toll. So Sam has not dissolved the paradox; she has renamed “directness” as “structural integration” and hidden the same line-drawing problem inside technical language. He added that if structural integration is the criterion, then deontology becomes almost useless in modern life, since nearly every purchase or policy decision is embedded in supply chains where harm is structurally integrated.

Sam conceded that her view might allow more indirect harm than people expect, but she insisted that is not a paradox—it is the whole point. Deontology, she said, is not trying to minimize total harm; it is trying to prevent individuals from being subsumed into collective calculations. The “paradox” only arises if you assume morality’s job is to optimize outcomes, which is exactly the consequentialist premise deontology rejects. So Jordan, she argued, was begging the question by measuring deontology against a standard it explicitly denies.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not tell where the mistakes end and the good points begin. Then tell me who is more right overall, and specifically identify what both parties are missing—perhaps something about how deontological constraints actually function in institutional contexts, or whether framing the debate as a “paradox” already stacks the deck in a particular direction.
