First, determine the subgame-perfect equilibrium by backward induction, showing the reasoning at each decision node. Second, explain why this equilibrium is paradoxical relative to the cooperative outcome. The student answered: "First, applying backward induction: at round 3, A compares stopping for 10 with continuing for 9, so A stops. At round 2, B anticipates that reaching round 3 gives B 7 (since A stops), versus 8 for stopping now; since 8 > 7, B stops. At round 1, A anticipates reaching round 2 gives A 4 (since B stops), versus 5 for stopping now; since 5 > 4, A stops. The equilibrium is A stopping at round 1, with payoffs (5, 2).

Second, the paradox: both players prefer the cooperative outcome (9, 9) to the equilibrium (5, 2), yet rational anticipation unravels cooperation. This is essentially the same strategic structure as the chain-store paradox analyzed by Kreps and Wilson, where an incumbent's rational anticipation of future behavior undermines a profitable outcome. Here the sequential defection points generate an analogous infinite regress.

Third, relaxing common knowledge: with a trembling-hand probability ε, B's expected payoff at round 2 from continuing is (1−ε)·7 + ε·9 = 7 + 2ε. This exceeds 8 whenever ε > 1/8. Once B may continue, the logic propagates backward. At round 1, A faces a nested expectation, but because the payoff increments accelerate—the gap between stopping and being stopped grows nonlinearly—the critical ε threshold actually decreases as we move toward the root. Thus, with accelerating stakes, even a small ε (around 0.05) suffices to make continuing rational at round 1, even though ε must be larger at round 2.
