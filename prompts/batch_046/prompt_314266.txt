A colleague who recently worked through G.E. Moore's Principia Ethica summarized Moore's treatment of beauty and friendship to me as follows. First, Moore holds that only pleasure, the appreciation of beautiful objects, and personal affection are intrinsic goods. Second, he analyzes friendship as essentially a form of aesthetic appreciation directed at mental qualities: we value a friend in the same way we value a beautiful painting, namely through the intrinsic value of the conscious state that perceives excellent mental properties. Third, because Moore insists that all intrinsic value must belong to states of consciousness, the friend herself is only an external cause or condition for the valuable state; she is not part of the intrinsic good. Fourth, applying Moore's principle of organic unity, the colleague claims that adding the reality of the other person to the conscious appreciation does not increase value, since the appreciation already contains the full value and the reality is merely an additional, separable component. From this the colleague concludes that Moore's ethics cannot distinguish genuine friendship from a perfect illusion of friendship, that it reduces reciprocity and commitment to instrumental means for sustaining pleasant consciousness, and that it therefore fails to capture what makes friendship morally significant.

How fair is this summary? Go through it claim by claim. Which parts accurately reflect Moore's actual arguments about intrinsic goods, consciousness-dependence, and organic unity? Where does it conflate Moore's rejection of ethical naturalism with a reduction of friendship to aesthetic hedonism? Does the principle of organic unity actually license the claim that the reality of the friend is value-neutral, or might it instead suggest that a real friendship is an organic whole whose value exceeds the sum of its conscious parts? And does the conclusion about illusion and reciprocity survive once the summary's misrepresentations are corrected?
