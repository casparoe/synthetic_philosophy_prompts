I am preparing a consistency check for a metaethics seminar and want an audit of the following five commitments. (A) Moral sentences express non-cognitive planning commitments—attitudes that bind an agent to a course of action—rather than beliefs or mere approvals. (B) The Frege-Geach problem is solved for this view because embedding a moral sentence in a conditional or negation merely propagates the same planning commitment to the embedded position, with no need for descriptive content to preserve logical form. (C) Despite (B), moral sentences in embedded contexts possess full truth-conditions and can be literally false whenever the corresponding planning commitment is violated or absent. (D) Unembedded moral judgment is necessarily motivating: sincerely judging that an act is wrong supplies a direct motivational disposition against it, not through a separate desire but as an intrinsic feature of the commitment itself. (E) I reject both Blackburn-style quasi-realism and any hybrid cognitivist-expressivist account that assigns moral language both expressive and descriptive functions. Which of these commitments pull against one another, which conflicts are only apparent, and what is the least costly revision? Rank the genuine tensions by severity, identify the single change that cleans up the most problems, and say what it costs. Respond in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introductory or concluding paragraph.
