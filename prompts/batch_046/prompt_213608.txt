As a retired physicist who has lately turned to philosophy of science and ethics, I have been struck by an institutional asymmetry in how two fields treat direct manipulation of reward mechanisms. In artificial intelligence safety, the "wireheading" problem—where an agent modifies its own reward signal—is treated as an existential failure mode requiring strict architectural isolation: researchers typically forbid agents direct write-access to their reward-function weights, though they routinely allow the agent to learn predictive representations of those weights through ordinary sensory and internal-state channels. By contrast, in clinical neuroscience, patients with implanted deep brain stimulators are often permitted, and sometimes encouraged, to manually adjust their stimulation parameters to optimize subjective well-being, provided the adjustment is routed through an external handheld controller rather than through direct neural rewiring of the reward circuitry itself. In both domains the neurochemical endpoint can be identical, yet one practice treats direct, unmediated alteration as catastrophically corrupting and the other treats mediated, instrumentally routed alteration as an exercise of autonomy.

What philosophical commitments about agency, authenticity, or the structure of well-being does this cross-disciplinary divergence presuppose? Does it presuppose that a self must stand in a specific causal relation to its own values—perhaps that authentic welfare requires values to be "endorsed" by a separate evaluative subsystem rather than rewritten by the very mechanism they govern? Or does it instead presuppose a theory of personal identity in which direct rewiring collapses the distinction between the subject who chooses and the mechanism that registers the choice? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, say whether it is compatible with rival accounts such as a purely hedonistic view or a preference-satisfaction view of welfare, and tell me what would have to change in either AI architecture or clinical protocol if that implicit theory were rejected in favor of a view that treats all reward-pathway modifications, direct or mediated, as ethically equivalent.
