In a seminar on strategic rationality, a discussant dismissed my defense of a particular mixed-strategy profile by invoking the maxim “never play a dominated strategy.” The context was an argument about iterated elimination in a continuous game with no pure-strategy equilibrium: I had pointed out that a player might rationally randomize over actions that are each weakly dominated in pure form, provided the mixture itself is not dominated by any other feasible mixture, and that strict-dominance rationalizability can leave exactly such mixtures intact. The slogan was being used to treat any presence of a weakly dominated pure action in the support of a strategy as automatically irrational, without distinguishing pure-strategy dominance from mixed-strategy dominance, or strict from weak dominance, or individual actions from complete strategies. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses this maxim might express—for example, as a tautology about best-response consistency, as a normative rule applying only to pure strategies, as a claim about strict dominance being order-independent under iteration, as a prediction about what rational agents will actually do, and as a stronger thesis that any mixture containing a weakly dominated action is itself irrational? For each reading, say whether it is trivial, whether it is substantive and defensible, and whether it is false. Then identify which reading the discussant was most likely relying on, explain whether that reading actually undermines the mixed-strategy profile I described, and flag the ambiguity that lets the slogan slide between a harmless consistency condition and a false generalization about randomization.
