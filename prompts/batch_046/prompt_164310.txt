In revisiting Lewis Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise, I was struck by how precisely it maps onto a phenomenon I encountered in recent cognitive-science reporting, where researchers describe "rule-stacking fatigue"—a state in which reasoners, under pressure, keep demanding ever-higher-order premises to license basic inference steps. This led me to wonder whether Carroll's regress is not, as philosophers usually present it, a logical paradox about the justification of deduction, but rather an early phenomenological record of cognitive overload: the tortoise is not advancing a logical objection but exhibiting a fatigue-induced refusal to apply modus ponens. If that reinterpretation holds, then the standard formalist reply—embedding inference rules in syntax so that no explicit application-premise is needed—would seem to miss the target entirely, since it eliminates the structural stack without addressing the psychological state that generates the demand for it. Given this, why do philosophers of logic continue to treat Carroll's dialogue as a live challenge to the rational grounding of deduction rather than relegating it to the psychology of reasoning?
