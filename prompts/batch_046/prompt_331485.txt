I've been trying to work out a stronger version of Burkean conservatism for a paper I'm maybe writing — mostly I'm just argueing with my roommate about it over coffee. The core idea is Chesterton's fence taken seriously as an epistemic principle rather than a cute metaphor: if you can't articulate what specific cordination problem or hidden dependency an institution was solving, you shouldn't tear it down. But I'm pushing it further — I think the fence applies even when you can see the institution causing real harm right now, as long as you can't reverse-engineer its original function. So preservation is the default, and the burden is entirely on the reformer to show not just that the status quo has costs, but that they understand the machinery well enough to replace it without blowing up unseen dependencies.

What I'm actually stuck on is figuring out what would genuinely count against this position, not just make it look awkward. I don't want the usual "this is just status quo bias" dismissal — I want the hard defeaters. For example, what about institutions whose original purpose was explicitly malicious, like segregation-era zoning codes that were designed for exclusion? Does knowing the original function was morally indefensable dissolve the fence obligation, or does it still apply to the secondary social functions that accreted later? Or take a purely ceremonial practice that solves no coordination problem and persists only through inertia — if tearing it down still causes unpredictable social friction, does that save the fence principle, or does it show the principle is unfalsifiable?

More generally, I'd like you to map out which kinds of evidence or arguments are fatal versus merely annoying. Would a formal result showing that incremental reform is systematically worse than revolutionary redesign in complex adaptive systems be decisive? Would a philosophical argument that epistemic humility doesn't generate any moral presumption at all? Or maybe a detailed case study where reverse-engineering failed but the institution was still net harmful? Tell me what defenders of this strong fence view would themselves concede as counting against them — not just what critics say — and where they would just dig in and claim the burden hasn't been met. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether this position is actually well-insulated or whether it risks collapsing into a rival view once pressed.
