ok so my study group got into it about supererogation and I think I've accidentally built an argument that collapses the whole category, but I can't find the leak. here's the chain:

(1) Supererogatory acts are good but not required -- if you do them you recieve praise, if you skip them you're not blameworthy.
(2) Picture two people, A and B, both able to save a drowning kid at the cost of wet shoes, and neither has any special duty.
(3) If A jumps in, that's classic supererogation: nice work, but B wasn't wrong to keep walking.
(4) Now suppose neither jumps in. Intuitively at least one of them failed.
(5) But if it's genuinely supererogatory for A, then A wasn't required; same for B.
(6) From (5) we can't derive "someone was required" without a quantifier shift I'm probably committing right here.
(7) If we avoid that shift and say only that each wasn't required, then we get a moral vacuum: preventable harm with zero responsible parties.
(8) Moral vacuums can't be right -- if harm is avoidable and agents are present, blame has to land somewhere.
(9) So the framing in (1) must be wrong: in any case like this, at least one specific agent is genuinely required.
(10) Generalize and there's no room left for "good but not required" -- every heroic act is just an undiscovered duty.

I really want to keep (1) and (3) because they match my intution, but then I'm stuck accepting (7) or denying (8). Is the exit in (6) -- recognizing a disjunctive obligation that binds "A or B" without binding either individually? Or should I bite the bullet and reject (8), allowing that sometimes harm just happens with no one at fault? What's the cost of each? And please -- if this rests on a stupid confusion about collective vs distributive obligations, just say so plainly. don't give me a polite dodge.
