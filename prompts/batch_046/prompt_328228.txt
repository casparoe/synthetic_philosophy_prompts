Below is a short argumentative essay I am drafting on self-amending constitutional rules and the logical structure of legal self-reference. I have constructed a hypothetical constitutional order—Lex Aeterna—to isolate a particular recursive tension. I would appreciate targeted comments on the essay's conceptual coherence, the accuracy of its analogies, and whether its proposed resolution is stable or collapses into a different problem.

Essay notes and argument:

Consider Lex Aeterna, a constitutional system whose Article VII contains the following clause: "This Article may be amended only by a procedure requiring the unanimous consent of all bodies whose authority to consent derives solely from this Article." Formally, this creates an institutional loop. The bodies empowered to amend the article are constituted by the article itself; consequently, their authority to initiate amendment depends on the article remaining in its original, unamended form. Before any amendment can occur, the authorizing bodies must already exist in their authorized form, yet that form is precisely what the amendment might alter. The structure resembles the Liar paradox—"this statement is false"—translated from semantics into institutional architecture: the rule's application conditions depend on the rule's current state, which depends on its application conditions.

A standard jurisprudential response distinguishes the rule of recognition from the rule of change, allowing the framework of authority to remain fixed while amendment procedures operate within it. But in Lex Aeterna, the rule of recognition is not external; it is embedded in Article VII itself. There is no archimedean point outside the loop. The system must therefore either accept an arbitrary founding act—constituent power that breaks the circle by fiat—or enter an infinite regress in which every authorization awaits a prior authorization.

What interests me is whether this regress is genuinely vicious, or whether legal practice possesses implicit resources for truncating it without an explicit meta-rule. Customary interpretation might gradually crystallize a convention that treats the initial bodies as provisionally legitimate, even though their legitimacy formally depends on the very rule they are meant to amend. If so, the paradox dissolves not through logical resolution but through sociological performativity: authority is established by practice rather than derived from prior authorization. Yet this raises a further difficulty. If performative crystallization does the work, then the self-amending rule does not actually govern its own change; it is governed by the interpretive practices that stabilize it. The normative claim that the rule binds future amendment then appears to be a retrospective rationalization of social convention rather than a genuine constraint.

Comments I am seeking:

First, is the analogy between Article VII and the Liar paradox structurally sound, or does the institutional context—particularly the temporal extension of legal acts—break the analogy in ways I have not accounted for? Second, does the distinction between constituent and constituted power genuinely resolve the loop, or does it merely relocate the paradox to the level of the founding act? Third, are there actual constitutional provisions with comparable recursive entrenchment—perhaps clauses that protect amendment rules themselves through mechanisms that depend on bodies defined by those rules—and if so, how have jurists addressed them conceptually rather than doctrinally? (Please do not supply a bibliography or reading list; I want to assess the conceptual structure, not track literature.) Fourth, is the turn to performative crystallization coherent, or does it undermine the normative force of the self-amending rule in a way that makes the original clause redundant?

Please respond with your comments and suggestions, treating the essay as a draft in need of philosophical sharpening rather than empirical correction.
