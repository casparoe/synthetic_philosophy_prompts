I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind and I'm trying to get straight what I actually think about whether pain is bad because of its affective quality or its sensory quality, using some wierd clinical and hypothetical cases.

Here are my gut verdicts:

1. Normal stubbed toe: obviously bad. The hurting is the bad part.

2. Classic pain asymbolia — patient reports sharp needle pain but doesn't withdraw, doesn't seem distressed, says 'it hurts but I don't mind it': I think this isn't actually bad for them. There's nociception going on, but the suffering is missing, so the badness is missing.

3. Phantom limb burning in someone on high-dose SSRIs who says it feels like 'a loud radio in another room' — intense sensory presence, zero emotional pull. Verdict: not bad, just wierd sensory noise.

4. Someone with selective ACC damage who can rank thermal stimuli perfectly but describes them as 'loud' or 'bright' rather than unpleasant, and will keep their hand on the plate longer to 'test the volume'. They cause real tissue damage. Here I'm torn: part of me says it's still bad because they're destroying tissue and the nociceptive signal is still an evolutionary alarrm, even if they don't feel alarmed. But another part says if there's genuinely no negative affect, then the harm is just the tissue damage, not the pain being bad.

5. A congenital insensitivity to pain patient: they don't experience anything bad, but they're missing something important. I don't think they suffer bad pain; they just lack a protective system.

6. A meditator during extreme heat exposure who reports intense sensation but frames it as 'pure presence', no suffering, and doesn't avoid it. Verdict: not bad, maybe even valuable.

So my pattern seems to be: badness tracks affective suffering, not sensory intensity. But case 4 breaks that, because I want to say there's something bad happening even though they report no suffering. Is there a principle that saves my verdict on 4 without making 2 and 3 come out wrong? Or do I have to drop one?
