I am writing for my senior-year philosophy elective, where we have been studying what our instructor calls the paradox of deontology: the difficulty that a system of absolute moral constraints can, in extreme cases, seem to require or permit outcomes that undermine the very dignity or agency those constraints were meant to protect. I want to read Christopher Nolan’s *The Dark Knight* through this specific lens—not as a simple illustration of “deontology versus consequentialism,” but as a narrative that tests whether the paradox is structural. Focus on two sequences: Batman’s refusal to kill the Joker despite the escalating death toll, and the ferry scene in which civilians and prisoners must each decide whether to detonate the other boat. I am especially interested in whether the film shows that a deontological prohibition against killing depends on a hidden assumption about how moral risk is distributed among agents, and whether the Joker’s manipulation of collective expectation breaks that assumption. Does Batman’s individual refusal remain coherent as an agent-relative constraint, or does it collapse into a kind of moral vanity once we see that the Joker exploits the collective anticipation that Batman will not break the rule? And in the ferry scene, do the passengers’ choices reveal that universalizing a prohibition on killing fails precisely when agents do not share the same moral commitments? I want a reading that takes both the film’s internal logic and the philosophical problem seriously, without reducing either to an example, and I would like it to address whether distinguishing agent-relative from agent-neutral reasons can actually resolve the tension the film dramatizes, or whether the narrative ultimately suggests that any such distinction breaks down under extreme pressure.
