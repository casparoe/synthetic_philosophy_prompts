I am trying to decide whether to use a pre-implantation screening service that predicts not only medical risks but also broad behavioural and motivational profiles in embryos. The clinic presents two viable embryos: one with a profile strongly associated with high conscientiousness, sustained achievement, and a lower lifetime risk of psychological distress, yet also with a tendency to experience success as flowing from fixed innate traits; the other with a more moderate predicted trajectory but a robust correlation between effort, deliberation, and a felt sense that outcomes are genuinely authored by the individual. My partner argues that selecting the first embryo is plainly the responsible choice — it maximises expected well-being, confers only advantages, and there is no harm in giving a child a head start they did not choose. I feel uneasy: if our child grows up attributing their successes to something predetermined before birth, they may lack a form of ownership over their life that matters independently of happiness. Is my partner's reasoning sound, or does it overlook a distinct value in the conditions that make a life genuinely one's own? I want to understand whether there is a philosophical conflict between maximising welfare for a future person and preserving the kind of agency or self-authorship that some regard as essential to human dignity, and if so, which considerations ought to prevail in a reproductive decision of this kind.
