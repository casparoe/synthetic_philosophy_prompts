In my philosophy of science and epistemology seminar we have been discussing whether using AI tools changes the kind of knowledge a researcher actually possesses, and I noticed an apparent inconsistency in our own department’s institutional rules. For the doctoral comprehensive exam, candidates are permitted to use an LLM to synthesize recent literature during the written portion, but they are graded specifically on whether they can flag which premises in that synthesis they understand firsthand and which they accept solely on the model’s authority; yet in regular coursework essays, using an LLM to construct arguments is treated as plagiarism, on the explicit grounds that philosophical understanding requires that one construct the reasoning chain oneself. What philosophical commitments about the nature of understanding, epistemic dependence, and testimonial knowledge does this two-tiered policy presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it assume there is a meaningful distinction between first-person cognitive achievement and second-hand epistemic reliance, and that only the former counts as genuine understanding? Is the practice actually coherent given that both settings involve the same agent relying on the same external cognitive tool? And if we replaced the underlying view with a straightforwardly reliabilist or virtue-epistemological account of scientific knowledge, what specific changes would have to be made to the exam and coursework policies to remain consistent with that replacement?
