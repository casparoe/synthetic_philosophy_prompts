I'm taking an upper-level metaphysics seminar on fundamentality and grounding, and after our recent discussions of ontological dependence versus explanatory priority I tried to test whether my own intuitions about concrete cases line up with a single, coherent principle. Here is where I stand. I think the fact that this wooden table exists is grounded in the fact that these planks are arranged table-wise, with no leftover ontological commitment. I think the fact that a particular utterance expresses the proposition that snow is white is grounded jointly in the speaker's mental intentions and in the linguistic conventions of her community; neither factor alone seems sufficient, and I resist reducing it purely to physical brain states. I think the fact that there is a hole in this donut is grounded in the donut's material shape together with the absence of dough in that region, though I deny that any separate hole-substance is being added to the ontology. I think the fact that seven is prime belongs, if anywhere, at the fundamental level of abstract reality and is not grounded in physical or mental facts. But I also think the fact that this particular painting counts as a masterpiece is neither fully reducible to its perceptual properties nor grounded solely in the mental responses of viewers, yet I do not want to place it at the fundamental base alongside physical laws or mathematical truths. Finally, I think that a legal statute's being in force is grounded in both the mental acts of legislators and in broader social practices, though I feel a persistent pull to say that its normative force is not entirely captured by either factor alone.

The tension, as I see it, is that my judgments about the utterance, the masterpiece, and the statute seem to require some kind of intermediate or plural grounding relation—perhaps jointly sufficient but individually insufficient grounders, or a distinction between determination and constitution—that my judgments about the table and the prime number do not obviously accommodate. Is there a coherent account that delivers all six verdicts, and if so, what does it predict for a case I have not listed: suppose a fully virtual object inside a high-fidelity computer simulation behaves exactly like a physical chair—does its existence depend for its ground on the physical states of the computer, on the programmer's design intentions, on both jointly, or is it merely a useful fiction with no genuine grounding relation at all? If no single principle can save every verdict, then please say which ones I should overturn: should I treat aesthetic and normative facts as fully reducible after all, or should I instead accept that they are fundamental despite seeming less basic than physics or mathematics? I want the principle, or the small family of candidates, stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, its verdict on the virtual-object case, and an honest statement of which of my six judgments it cannot accommodate.
