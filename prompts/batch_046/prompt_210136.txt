I'm preparing a graduate seminar on Kierkegaard's social philosophy, centered on A Literary Review and the adjacent journal reflections on the press, and I want to test whether his conceptual apparatus can be extended without distortion. Rather than asking for a standard summary, I ask you to rank four distinct configurations of abstract publicness—each structurally analogous to Kierkegaard's diagnosis of the press and the public, but operating through a different mechanism of abstraction—according to the severity with which they realize what he calls leveling and the dissolution of the single individual into a numerical phantom. Please assign each a rank from 1 (the configuration that most thoroughly evacuates inwardness, eliminates personal responsibility, and substitutes quantitative aggregation for qualitative relation) to 4 (the configuration in which these effects are weakest), and justify each placement in one or two sentences that appeal directly to Kierkegaard's criteria: the impossibility of decisive commitment, the replacement of action by talk, and the loss of inward appropriation.

Here are the four configurations. First, the classical newspaper public of Kierkegaard's own century: a uniform, anonymous daily mechanism that binds no one to any position, transforms every occurrence into material for detached discussion, and generates a public opinion that is everywhere present and nowhere accountable. Second, the algorithmic feed-public: a decentralized architecture that does not produce one shared phantom crowd but rather millions of parallel, personalized phantom publics, so that abstraction is not uniform but fragmented, and the very awareness of belonging to a leveling collective is dissolved. Third, the institutional committee-public: an agency structure in which decision is distributed across procedural roles, minutes replace conviction, and no individual member can be said to have acted, so that leveling targets not opinion but the capacity for individual agency itself. Fourth, the expert-spectator public: a relation in which specialized knowledge is produced and then consumed by an audience that judges, praises, and condemns without ever existentially appropriating the content, turning truth into a currency of reputation and leaving the spectator unchanged. I am especially interested in whether you regard the fragmentation of the second case as an intensification of leveling—because it prevents even the recognition of abstraction—or as a dilution of it.
