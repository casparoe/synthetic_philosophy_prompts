I'm moderating a colloquium Q&A with a philosopher who has been developing what she calls "calibrated demandingness consequentialism." On her view, the classic demandingness objection succeeds against naive act-utilitarianism—because that framework measures the moral cost of a sacrifice solely in terms of welfare foregone—but fails once we substitute a structured "reason-weight index" calibrated to objectively central human capacities. The index is meant to preserve consequentialism's aggregative structure, yet it explains why an agent need not surrender every discretionary resource to distant strangers: the disruption to a life-plan is weighted not by how intensely the agent happens to care about her projects, but by how severely the sacrifice undermines capacities that are constitutive of flourishing across agents. She insists this is neither a retreat to Bernard Williams-style integrity objections nor a covert appeal to supererogation, and she denies that fixing the calibration rules turns her view into rule-utilitarianism. I need eight to ten interview questions that do not allow her to restate these commitments in broad strokes. At least one should press on how the reason-weight index is constructed without circular reliance on our pre-theoretical intuitions about permissible self-concern; at least one should ask whether the view collapses into a form of rule-utilitarianism once the calibration rules are fixed and publicly known; and at least one should probe what happens when an agent's central life-plan is itself morally objectionable—for example, a career sustained by exploitation—since her framework grants protective weight to projects based on their structural centrality rather than their moral content. For each of these three, include a follow-up keyed to her most likely reply: if she claims the index is validated by a contractualist thought-experiment, press on whether that smuggles in non-consequentialist premises about what agents can reasonably reject; if she says the rules are merely epistemic heuristics rather than normative constraints, press on whether she can still avoid Williams's "alienation" problem without reintroducing agent-centered prerogatives; and if she grants that immoral projects forfeit protection, press on whether her view then licenses intrusive third-party judgments about the worth of others' life-plans in ways that reproduce the very demandingness she set out to limit. The remaining questions should turn on concrete comparative cases—such as whether her index permits greater retention of resources for artistic or scientific pursuits than for comfort-based consumption, and how she handles cumulative small sacrifices that individually fall below the threshold but collectively devastate a life-plan—so that the exchange stays anchored in the mechanics of the view rather than in generic defenses of consequentialism.
