The debate has stalled in a pattern familiar from LessWrong-style discussions: philosophers and behavioral scientists agree that agents systematically take smaller-sooner rewards over larger-later ones they sincerely claim to prefer, and they agree this pattern is costly and correctable through mechanisms like precommitment. Yet the literature has not converged in decades. Philosophers typically diagnose akrasia as a failure of practical reasoning or executive integration—either ignorance of the good or a gap between judgment and action—while behavioral economists diagnose the same behavior as rational given non-exponential discount curves, correctable not by “strengthening the will” but by restructuring incentives, cooling-off periods, or binding contracts. Both sides cite smoking, procrastination, and diet failure; both propose remedies; neither treats the other’s remedy as addressing the real problem.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is partly verbal—the word “will” functions as a normative capacity for one side and as a descriptive bargaining mechanism for the other—and partly metaphysical, turning on whether the agent at time t1 and the agent at t2 are parts of one diachronic self with authority over the other, or competing interest-groups with no privileged arbitrator.
