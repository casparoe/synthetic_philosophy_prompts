Write a dialogue between two equally capable philosophers—one a defender of Russell’s quantificational analysis of definite descriptions, the other an advocate of what she calls “modally anchored singular reference”—as they examine a hypothetical archive of descriptions evaluated across worlds in which existence conditions vary (for instance, “the reigning monarch of France” in worlds where the monarchy was never abolished versus our own). The Russellian should insist that descriptions are not singular terms but concealed existential quantifiers with uniqueness conditions, so that apparent reference in counterfactual contexts is really just the satisfaction of those conditions in the evaluated world, and that this analysis cleanly separates false existential claims from genuine reference failure. The opponent should argue that this dissolves an important intuitive distinction: some descriptions seem to be genuinely empty in the actual world yet still “point” to specific individuals in accessible worlds, functioning more like singular terms whose descriptive content is fixed by contextual constraints rather than by explicit quantification, and that Russell’s analysis cannot capture this cross-world stability without secretly smuggling in world-relative parameters. Each speaker must respond directly to the other’s last point—no talking past one another—pressing specifically on whether a description that fails uniqueness here but succeeds elsewhere should be treated as false, as meaningless, or as referring under a modal shift. Include at least one round in which the Russellian accuses the opponent of reintroducing direct reference through the back door of “contextual anchoring,” and one round in which the opponent charges that the quantificational view makes counterfactual reasoning about specific non-actual objects impossible without reconstructing them as sets of properties. End with both positions intact and neither convinced. Keep the exchange conceptually rigorous but do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or named texts beyond the general Russellian framework.
