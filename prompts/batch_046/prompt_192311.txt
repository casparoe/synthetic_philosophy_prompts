A classmate sent me the following summary, which they say captures the position defended in the recent literature on deliberative insulation and reflective equilibrium:

"The indirect-deliberative model holds that normative authority is located in the structural rules governing practical reasoning rather than in the evaluation of particular acts. What an agent ought to do is fixed by the rules that her fully informed, rationally coherent, idealized counterpart—operating in a state of reflective equilibrium—would endorse as the permanent framework for her reasoning. Because these rules are justified by their long-run stability and coherence rather than by case-specific outcomes, an agent should adhere to them even when they yield locally worse results. From this it follows that asking 'what ought I to do in this situation?' is always a mistake; morality is exclusively concerned with 'what deliberative framework ought I to inhabit?' Moreover, since idealization requires total transparency of motives, any preference that survives the process is by definition rational and universalizable. Consequently, the distinction between indirect and direct normativity collapses: the rules themselves are subjected to a Kantian universalizability test, and once endorsed, they generate obligations without any further appeal to circumstances. Finally, because the idealized self retains no non-rational residues—no projects, attachments, or preferences that fail to survive reflective scrutiny—the model eliminates all genuine conflict between personal concerns and moral demands; moral motivation is therefore unproblematic, since there is never a remainder of desire that resists the endorsed framework."

My classmate draws two conclusions: first, that the view eliminates direct evaluation of actions altogether, reducing practical reason to a single meta-level question about framework selection; and second, that it solves the problem of moral motivation by showing that ideal agents have no recalcitrant preferences that could conflict with moral rules.
