I am designing a module for a graduate seminar on institutional epistemology and democratic design, and I want a dialogue that moves well beyond the standard sortition-versus-election framing. Please write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—call them Mara and Lin—debating a specific hybrid legislative proposal: half the seats are filled by single-use sortition with strict three-year terms and permanent ineligibility for reselection, while the other half are filled by conventional elections with no term limits whatsoever.

The conceptual crux of their exchange should be what I will call intertemporal epistemic custody: the idea that career politicians accumulate not merely technical expertise but a form of institutional memory that allows them to bind future assemblies to credible long-range commitments, whereas rotated citizens preserve unfiltered local knowledge but exit before the outcomes of their decisions fully materialize. Mara should defend the hybrid system but argue that without term limits on the elected half, careerists will eventually colonize the reasoning protocols of the citizen chamber through procedural capture—shaping agendas, defining what counts as relevant evidence, and thereby eroding the epistemic diversity that the sortition half is meant to inject. Lin should resist term limits, but not by appealing to generic experience; instead, Lin should contend that the very fragility of rotated citizens—their insulation from electoral accountability for post-departure outcomes—creates a perverse incentive to maximize short-term symbolic victories at the expense of intertemporal welfare, and that careerists serve as necessary commitment devices precisely because they remain present to suffer the reputational costs of broken promises across electoral cycles.

The dialogue must contain real back-and-forth: each speaker should respond directly to the mechanism the other identifies—procedural capture on one side, commitment-device fragility on the other—rather than talking past each other or restating fixed positions. Neither should be a strawman. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to named authors or specific texts. Keep the exchange conceptually dense but entirely self-contained, so that it requires no external lookup to follow.
