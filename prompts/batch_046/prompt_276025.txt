In my philosophy course we have been examining Nyāya epistemology and the pramāṇas—particularly how direct perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), and testimony (śabda) are meant to distinguish genuine knowledge from mere true belief—yet I find the classical categories blur once I apply them to cases outside the textbooks. Rather than memorizing definitions, I have been testing my own intuitions against concrete situations to see whether there is an underlying principle that captures my judgments, and I keep hitting a tension I cannot resolve. When I observe smoke rising over a distant ridge and infer an unseen fire behind it, I judge this knowledge through inference: my belief tracks reality through a reliable causal connection. When a biology instructor I trust explains mitosis and I grasp the reasoning for myself, I accept this as knowledge through testimony, but only because I understand the explanation rather than parroting it. Yet technologically mediated beliefs unsettle me. If I wear a high-fidelity virtual-reality headset that renders roses with perfect fidelity, and I form the belief “there are roses present,” I want to deny that this is knowledge—even though my perceptual mechanisms are functioning correctly and the belief is true within the simulated environment. By contrast, when I see a distant object through atmospheric distortion and misidentify it, I also deny knowledge, but for the opposite reason: the belief is false. The virtual-reality case troubles me precisely because the belief is true relative to its environment, yet it feels detached from the physical world in a way that undermines justification.

The hardest pair involves algorithmic and social mediation. Suppose I rely on a weather-prediction model—generated from data I have never personally inspected—to form the true belief that it will rain tomorrow. Is this like testimony, since I depend on an authoritative source, or closer to the virtual-reality case, because my own cognitive engagement is minimal? Even more difficult: a close friend tells me she is grieving, and I come to know this through her expression, tone, and the history of our relationship; but a large language model, analyzing her written messages, outputs the classification “grief state detected,” and I form the identical belief based solely on that algorithmic report. Both beliefs turn out correct, and the algorithm may be more statistically reliable than my intuition, yet only the first feels like knowledge; the second feels like a lucky, detached guess. Another tension involves memory: I recall that Paris is the capital of France, originally learned years ago from a map. If that map had happened to be erroneous but was correct by chance, do I now genuinely know it, or is my justification too remote and accidental? I suspect there is a principle here—perhaps involving not only reliability but also some form of direct cognitive ownership, appropriate causal contact, or what I might call “epistemic transparency”—that separates these pairs, but I cannot formulate it precisely. Could you help me articulate one or more precise principles that accommodate my verdicts, indicate which of my judgments must be revised if the principle is to remain simple, and suggest how the Nyāya framework might need to be refined—perhaps with a niche condition addressing synthetic or algorithmically mediated cognition—to cover these modern variations without collapsing testimony and inference into one another?
