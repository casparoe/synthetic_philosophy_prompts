Exam question:

In his framework for bounded rationality, Herbert Simon distinguished procedural rationality—assessing the quality of a decision process given cognitive and environmental constraints—from substantive rationality, which evaluates outcomes against an external optimum. Simon also described rational agents as adjusting their aspiration levels in response to past success and failure, a dynamic he called aspiration adaptation. He further characterized rational behavior with a scissors metaphor, in which cognitive capacities form one blade and the structure of the task environment forms the other.

Consider a public agency that hires technical specialists through a sequential rule: it interviews candidates one at a time and extends an immediate offer to the first applicant whose standardized assessment score exceeds a fixed aspiration level, never comparing that applicant with those remaining in the pool.

Analyze this hiring rule through Simon’s framework. Specifically:
1. Is the rule best interpreted as (a) an implicit form of expected-utility maximization that internalizes search and evaluation costs into the objective function, or (b) a genuinely non-optimizing procedure that substitutes an aspiration-level check for inter-candidate comparison?
2. How does the distinction between procedural and substantive rationality bear on evaluating this rule?
3. If the agency systematically raises or lowers its aspiration level based on past hiring outcomes—aspiration adaptation—does this dynamic strengthen the case for treating the rule as rational in Simon’s procedural sense, or does it instead reveal a hidden optimizing mechanism operating at a meta-level?

In your answer, address the role of environmental structure in Simon’s scissors account, and clarify whether defenders of ecological rationality—who argue that simple heuristics can outperform complex strategies in specific task environments—must reject the normative authority of expected-utility theory altogether, or only restrict its domain of application.

Student answer:

The agency’s rule is a textbook satisficing procedure: it fixes an aspiration level and stops at the first success, which avoids the costs of exhaustive search. This clearly falls under option (b): it is genuinely non-optimizing because comparison is abandoned altogether—there is no weighing of one applicant against another, only a binary check against the threshold. Simon’s scissors metaphor highlights exactly why this is necessary: our mental machinery is too limited to process large applicant pools, so bounded rationality forces us to rely on such shortcuts. Turning to procedural versus substantive rationality, the rule looks poor substantively, since a stronger candidate might appear later in the sequence; but it is rational procedurally, because it respects real-world costs of information and computation. Aspiration adaptation strengthens the procedural defense: if offers are rarely accepted and the agency lowers the threshold, this shows the system is learning from feedback rather than engaging in hidden optimization. Finally, ecological rationality—associated with researchers like Gigerenzer—shows that heuristics are matched to environmental structure and can achieve greater accuracy than complex optimization in data-scarce settings. These defenders do not reject expected-utility theory entirely; they merely confine its normative authority to idealized conditions of full information and costless computation. Therefore, the hiring rule should be judged by procedural standards, not by whether it secures the best possible hire.
