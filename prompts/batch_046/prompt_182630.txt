Write a proposal for a one-semester philosophy term paper (approximately 6,000 words) on the following problem. In metaphysics and philosophy of science, entities are sometimes posited whose intrinsic properties are permanently inaccessible—whether because of principled epistemic barriers, hidden-variable structures, or modal essences that lie beyond observational reach. The familiar responses are either structural realism, which grounds ontology in relational structure alone, or essentialism, which insists that identity requires access to intrinsic nature. Both leave a gap: they do not explain how we might identify an entity when we can specify what it is not and how it functions within the accessible domain, yet never observe what it positively is in itself.

State the precise question: can an ontology of permanently inaccessible entities be secured through an exclusion-profile—a network of negative constraints combined with functional roles—without collapsing into pure structuralism or into mysterious direct reference? Explain why existing positions leave this open. Structural realists risk ontological underdetermination, since distinct intrinsic ontologies can share the same relational profile; essentialists must justify identity conditions when intrinsic access is blocked in principle rather than merely in practice. Your proposal should identify the specific failure mode in each camp that makes room for a third option.

Lay out the approach. Select two or three cases where inaccessibility is principled, not temporary. For each, construct the exclusion-profile: specify which properties are precluded, which observational interfaces are fixed, and what the entity cannot be without violating the dynamics of the accessible domain. Then test whether this profile uniquely determines identity conditions or still permits empirically equivalent but ontologically distinct rivals. Introduce a supplementary condition—functional anchoring—that specifies exactly how the inaccessible entity must interface with observable processes, and examine whether this resolves underdetermination without smuggling in direct access. Compare the results across your cases and assess whether the framework applies generally or only locally.

Say what a successful result would look like: a defensible account of identity conditions for inaccessible entities based solely on exclusion and functional anchoring, together with a clear boundary marking where the framework stops working. Say what failure would look like: if every exclusion-profile still allows rival ontologies with identical empirical signatures, or if the appeal to functional anchoring secretly presupposes the kind of intrinsic access it was meant to avoid. Finally, give an honest scope assessment: is constructing and testing exclusion-profiles across multiple cases too ambitious for one semester, and if so, which case should be retained and which dropped?
