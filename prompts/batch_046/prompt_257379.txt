I'm preparing a seminar paper on non-human agency in environmental history, and my study group has constructed an argument that we fear proves far more than we intend. We want to treat the 1815 Tambora eruption, the 1816 climate anomaly it triggered, and the 1817–1824 cholera pandemic as genuine historical actors — forces that reshaped European politics, art, and imperial administration without being guided by human intentions, as when the confined Geneva summer produced Mary Shelley's Frankenstein and when monsoon disruptions accelerated British sanitary centralization. Yet we also want to avoid collapsing into full environmental determinism. Here is our reasoning, laid out step by step.

(1) If an entity's contribution to a historical outcome is counterfactually robust — removing it changes not merely the probability but the qualitative character of the result — then that entity counts as a historical actor. (2) Removing Tambora eliminates the specific 1816 conditions that confined writers at Lake Geneva and alters the specific agricultural and sanitary failures that shaped cholera's spread; European cultural and imperial trajectories change in kind, not only in degree. (3) Therefore Tambora, the climate anomaly, and the pathogenic agent are historical actors. (4) Now, if E is a historical actor whose actions causally determine the conditions for human action H, then H is not an autonomous agent but a conduit expressing E's agency. (5) Shelley's authorship and the British sanitary reforms were causally determined by these non-human forces. (6) So these achievements are not genuinely autonomous human actions. (7) By extension, since every human act is embedded in climatic, pathogenic, or geological causal chains, all human agency is actually distributed non-human agency. (8) That is environmental determinism in its strongest form. (9) But determinism is false: we distinguish Shelley's writing a novel from a volcano erupting. (10) Therefore the counterfactual-robustness criterion in (1) must be rejected, and non-human forces can never be historical actors.

We think the argument is valid, yet its conclusion erases exactly what we hoped to preserve. Which step should we abandon? Identify the specific premise or inference, explain why it initially seemed defensible — especially how step (4) trades on an ambiguity between causal determination and agential determination — and then map out what three standard positions must pay to reject it: the strict intentionalist who reserves agency solely for conscious beings; the compatibilist who accepts deep causal embedding without sacrificing autonomy; and the distributed-agency view in the style of actor-network theory that treats humans and non-humans as symmetric actants. Please structure your response as follows: "The weak link" (about 3–4 sentences), "Why it looked plausible" (about 4–5 sentences), and "The price of denial" (about 5–6 sentences, addressing each of the three positions in turn).
