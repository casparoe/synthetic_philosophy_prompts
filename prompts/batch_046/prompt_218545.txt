Next Thursday I present in our graduate seminar on early modern metaphysics a revisionist reading of Leibniz's claim that this is the best of all possible worlds. My thesis is that "best" should not be read as a comparative predicate applied to a genuinely open menu of worlds among which God selects, but rather as an internal optimality condition—maximal variety governed by minimal laws—operating within the single maximally consistent set of compossible essences that the principle of sufficient reason renders metaphysically necessary. On this view, divine "choice" is not selection among alternatives but the recognition and actualization of the only totality that satisfies both logical consistency and the demand for a sufficient reason; consequently, the familiar theodicy problem dissolves not because evil is justified by comparison with worse worlds, but because there never was a modal space of genuinely available worse worlds against which this one competes. The audience will include two specialists in Leibniz's theodicy who treat the comparative language of the Theodicy as literally descriptive of divine deliberation, a modal metaphysician who will resist any collapse of metaphysical possibility into a single actualizable set, and several sharp graduate students eager to press the objection that this renders God's freedom illusory and makes the existence of suffering inexplicable in different terms. I need the objections ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased in the vocabulary each camp actually uses: the textualists' charge that I am imposing a Spinozistic necessitarianism onto a thinker who explicitly rejects it; the modalist's attack on whether my construal of compossibility secretly begs the question against a robust account of unactualized possibles; the graduate students' theodicy objection reframed for my position; and the hidden theological worry I am hoping no one voices. For each, tell me what I should concede up front—perhaps about Leibniz's rhetorical commitment to comparison—what I must hold—especially regarding the role of the principle of sufficient reason in constraining divine freedom without eliminating it—and where each reply is weakest. Include a concise reply calibrated for a twenty-minute workshop exchange, and identify the single point in my argument—likely where I move from logical consistency to metaphysical uniqueness—that I should defend openly rather than hope they miss. Finally, please say explicitly what consideration—whether a specific textual passage I have misinterpreted, a formal result about modal space I have ignored, or a theological cost I have underestimated—would lead you to change your assessment of how I should prepare and advise me to concede the central point before I enter the room.
