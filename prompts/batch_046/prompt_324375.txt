I was arguing with my roommate about Zhuangzi and death last night and I realized I don't actually understand whether he's advocating some kind of personal continuity or just saying that the self that worries about death is already an illusion. So I'm drafting a short essay for my eastern philosphy course and I want to run my core claim by someone. Basically I think Zhuangzi's idea of transformation (hua) gets misread as you become something else after dying, but really he's dissolving the boundary between life and death altogether. The self isn't a thing that persists or transforms; it's a temporary convergence of qi that has no reason to fear dispersal. I call this ontological unbinding — the point isn't comfort about the afterlife, it's that there's no me there to be comforted.

Then I look at the tub-drumming story after his wife's death, and I argue people misread it as calousness when it's actually consistency: if there's no substantial self that owned her presence, there's no substantial self that can be robbed by her absence. Does that sound like I'm forcing modern process philosophy onto an ancient text? I'd especially like comments on whether ontological unbinding captures Zhuangzi without distorting him, and whether my take on the drumming episode ignores its emotional texture. Any suggestions on where I'm being sloppy?
