Consider the following exchange that occurred during a graduate seminar on practical rationality and institutional ethics. Two colleagues—call them Dr. Voss and Dr. Lin—were debating whether the board of a municipal archive should authorize the final phase of a restoration project.

(1) Dr. Voss argued that the archive had already expended roughly eighty percent of its conservation budget on a water-damaged medieval ledger that, even if fully restored, would remain too brittle for public display or digital scanning. She said that to approve the remaining expenditure would be a textbook sunk-cost fallacy: the funds already spent are irrecoverable, and rational decision-making requires comparing only the marginal cost of completion against the marginal benefit of the finished project. Since the benefit is negligible, the board should halt the work and redirect the remaining funds.

(2) Dr. Lin replied that this framing treats the archive's commitment as a purely instrumental investment, like a stock portfolio. He claimed that when an institution accepts an artifact into its permanent collection, it assumes a fiduciary duty of stewardship that is not reducible to expected utility. Abandoning the restoration midway, he said, would breach that duty—not because of the money already spent, but because the institution made a public promise of care. The sunk-cost error, he insisted, applies only to goods valued solely for future returns, not to goods whose value is partly constituted by the continuity of the commitment itself.

(3) Dr. Voss countered that fiduciary duty does not mandate the most expensive fulfillment. The archive could satisfy its duty by stabilizing the ledger at its current state and storing it in climate-controlled preservation, which would cost a fraction of the final restoration phase. She said Dr. Lin was conflating the institutional goal—preserving the artifact—with a particular, expensive method of achieving it. By her lights, using the language of duty to justify further spending was precisely the emotional rationalization that the sunk-cost fallacy describes.

(4) Dr. Lin then observed that choosing the cheaper stabilization also requires abandoning the expert conservation plan that was designed specifically for this manuscript's chemical profile. More fundamentally, he argued, if institutions treat every long-term obligation as fungible—revisable whenever marginal costs exceed marginal benefits—then the very trust that enables donors and communities to entrust artifacts to them collapses. He concluded that the irrationality of honoring sunk costs holds for purely transactional choices, but fails for commitments that partly constitute the institution's identity and relational standing.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the four moves, rate its strength from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and briefly justify your rating. Then say who, if anyone, wins overall, and identify what—if anything—both parties are missing about the structure of institutional commitment and practical rationality.
