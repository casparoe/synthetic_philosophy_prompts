As a journalist covering the philosophy of technology and digital culture, I have been following debates around what developers now call "provenance-locked artifacts"—persistent virtual objects in immersive environments whose interaction histories, collaborative construction logs, and accumulated social rituals are cryptographically bound to a singular instance, so that any copy breaks the continuity chain and is treated by the community as an inauthentic replica. During a recent panel discussion, I observed a disagreement between a digital anthropologist and a philosopher of technology about whether the deliberate destruction of one such artifact—a communal memorial garden that had been tended for several years by dispersed users—constitutes a harm comparable to the desecration of a physical grave site. The anthropologist argued that because the garden's value lies not in its visual appearance, which could be duplicated, but in its irreplaceable relational history—the specific grief rituals performed there, the exact sequence of collaborative modifications, and the place-bound memories indexed to that instance—its annihilation is an ontological loss rather than a mere disappointment. The wrong done to the creators is structurally analogous to damaging a physical memorial: something with real, non-derivative relational properties has been destroyed, and treating the harm as purely psychological misses that the object itself was a node in a living social practice.

The philosopher conceded that the users' suffering is genuine but insisted that framing it as harm to the object is a category mistake. The artifact, he said, is ultimately a pattern of information instantiated on replaceable hardware; its "singularity" is only a tracking convention, not a metaphysically weighty fact. If we grant virtual objects standing comparable to physical ones solely because they carry social meaning, we commit ourselves to an implausible ontology in which information patterns have intrinsic value independent of substrate—one that would imply that accidentally deleting a server backup of a beloved virtual space is a kind of second destruction of the same object. The anthropologist replied that physical originals are themselves valued for causal and relational histories rather than for bare materiality—if every atom in a painting is gradually replaced, we still value its continuity—so the substrate distinction is a red herring, and the real question is whether relational persistence conditions can be satisfied in virtual space as robustly as in physical space. The philosopher countered that virtual environments uniquely permit perfect restoration: if the relational history can be migrated onto a new instance without loss, then the specific destroyed instance never had non-derivative value, and the harm, however painful, is technically reversible in a way that physical destruction is not. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: evaluate the initial analogy to grave desecration, the category-mistake rebuttal with its information-ontology objection, the relational-continuity reply, and the reversibility argument—rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justifying each rating, identifying what both parties overlook about whether the harm attaches to the object, to the community's ongoing practices, or to the creators' autonomy, and saying which interlocutor is more right overall.
