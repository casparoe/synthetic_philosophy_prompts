I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who, after leaving the laboratory, have spent the last three years studying philosophy with the same rigor I once applied to cryogenic measurements. I now serve as an independent ethics auditor for a mid-sized university’s Office of Research Integrity, where I review roughly a dozen proposed studies each month—mostly in cognitive neuroscience and synthetic-data modeling. The stakes are concrete: funding release and publication clearance depend on my sign-off. I have about forty-five minutes per protocol, no legal counsel present, and I must document my reasoning in two paragraphs or less. The committee expects a clear recommendation, not a philosophical treatise.

You are an expert in Kantian moral philosophy with substantial experience in applied research ethics. Please think step by step, and begin with brief brainstorming on which conceptual distinctions from Kant’s universalizability framework must survive translation into a practitioner’s checklist and which can be safely collapsed for a non-specialist auditor working alone.

I need you to turn Kant’s universalizability tests—specifically the Formula of Universal Law, with its contradiction-in-conception and contradiction-in-will distinctions, and the Formula of Humanity—into a reusable audit procedure I can apply repeatedly. The deliverable should be a procedural flowchart in words: ordered tests, decision nodes, explicit stop-points, and a small set of reconstructed test sentences an auditor can apply without consulting texts. Ground the procedure in what Kant’s framework actually requires, but do not ask me to verify editions, page numbers, or bibliographic references; paraphrase any Kantian claim without quotation marks, and if you need a precise formulation, present it as a reconstructed audit sentence rather than a verbatim citation.

Fix the practitioner context precisely: I apply this alone, sign off personally, work under severe time constraints, and must flag cases where judgment is irreducibly contested rather than pretending certainty. The procedure must handle hard cases that recur in my docket—protocols relying on anonymized secondary data, studies that require unavoidable deception, and experiments whose benefit is purely epistemic with no therapeutic value. Include two to three worked applications: at least one where the procedure yields a negative verdict on a protocol that initially looks benign, and one where it yields a positive verdict despite an intuitive ethical discomfort. For each, show how the test sentences operate on the maxim.

Explicitly mark the points where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment and must escalate to the full committee, and state frankly what the procedure cannot do—for instance, resolving direct conflicts between the Formula of Universal Law and the Formula of Humanity, or substituting for institutional legal authority. Keep the tone formal and polished, write in American English, and avoid generic prompts like “what is your view on ethics?” Build this as an operational tool for a practitioner who understands physics well but is still learning philosophy.
