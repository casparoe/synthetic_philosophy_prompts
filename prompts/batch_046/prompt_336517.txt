For my senior philosophy independent study, I am examining whether moral excellence can survive being measured and rewarded. Write a short story of roughly 800 words set in a near-future city where every resident is assigned a transparent algorithmic "civic duty quota." Meeting obligations earns standard merit; actions that exceed the quota are formally classified as supererogatory and generate "surplus tokens" that the actor may donate anonymously, keep in a public registry, or forfeit. The protagonist, a transit maintenance worker named K, witnesses a sudden structural failure in an elevated rail hub. Saving all trapped passengers requires physical risk and resource expenditure well beyond her calculated quota. If she succeeds, the system will credit her with surplus tokens she can donate; if she fails, she will be registered in "moral debt" for overreaching her assigned capacity. Through K's decision, her internal reasoning during the crisis, and a brief exchange with a stranger who argues that institutionalizing supererogation destroys its moral value, show whether going beyond duty remains meaningful when society tracks, quantifies, and rewards it. The narrative should not lecture; let the conceptual tension emerge from the rules of the world and K's choice within them. Please avoid bibliographies, citations, or references to specific philosophers by name, and do not include an explicit moral at the end—end on the moment of consequence.
