Following a long argument with a friend about distributive ethics, I am trying to assess an analogy she kept returning to. She compared leveling-down equality to cutting the legs off tall people so that everyone is the same height, using it to claim that any view giving intrinsic weight to equality must permit reducing the welfare of the best-off even when no one benefits. She then argued that prioritarianism escapes the absurdity because it only applies greater weight to benefits for the worse-off, never requiring equalization for its own sake. I want a precise audit of this comparison. First, lay out the explicit correspondence: who or what corresponds to the tall and short people, to height itself, to the surgical intervention, and to the social decision-maker? Second, identify the disanalogies that matter for her argument — for instance, that physical height is not a transferable resource, whereas welfare can sometimes be redistributed without direct harm; that the analogy treats welfare reduction as a pure loss with no compensating gain, which may misrepresent tax-and-transfer mechanisms; and that it assumes equality is pursued as an end in itself rather than as a by-product of a concave social welfare function. Third, evaluate whether her conclusion survives: can a sufficiently concave prioritarian function still generate recommendations that look like leveling down (e.g., taxing high welfare to fund modest gains that, due to diminishing returns, never actually reach the bottom)? Distinguish carefully between ex post equality of outcomes and ex ante weighting of marginal benefits. Finally, if the analogy collapses at a point essential to her defense of prioritarianism, propose a more apt comparison that captures the structural difference between egalitarian and prioritarian reasoning without importing misleading physical imagery.
