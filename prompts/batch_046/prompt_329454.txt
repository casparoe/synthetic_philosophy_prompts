Design a decision-tree taxonomy that classifies philosophical positions on supererogation—acts that appear morally excellent yet not strictly required—by mapping them onto the logical space of answers to three concrete diagnostic questions, rather than by asking anyone to identify with a named school. The tree should be built bottom-up: first the branching nodes, then the answer-combinations they generate, then the actual positions assigned to the occupied leaves, with at least one unoccupied combination explicitly noted and briefly diagnosed.

Use exactly these three branch nodes, each phrased as a question answerable without knowing philosophical labels:

1. The moderate-risk rescue case. A trained bystander can save a drowning child at the cost of a broken arm and temporary hypothermia, with no legal or contractual obligation to enter the water. Is failing to rescue (a) blameworthy, (b) neither blameworthy nor especially praiseworthy, or (c) not blameworthy but genuinely worthy of praise?

2. The structural possibility of optional obligation. Can a moral system consistently recognize obligations that bind if voluntarily undertaken but do not bind one to undertake them in the first place, or must every morally relevant act fall into either strict requirement or full permission?

3. The explanatory source of moral excess. Assuming supererogation is real, is it best accounted for by (a) a quantitative cost-threshold separating ordinary duty from heroic action, (b) a qualitative virtue-based account in which moral excellence exceeds minimal compliance, or (c) a pluralist view that treats supererogatory choices as responses to values incommensurable with duty?

The taxonomy must contain at least six leaves, including: (i) strict rigorism that denies supererogation altogether; (ii) standard deontological allowance using a cost-threshold; (iii) virtue-theoretic heroism that denies optional obligations; (iv) pluralist supererogation; (v) a consequentialist threshold view; and (vi) one additional position of your own design that occupies a different branch. For each leaf, state which combination of answers to nodes 1–3 leads there, and briefly describe the view’s core commitment in one sentence.

Additionally, identify at least one logical combination that appears unoccupied—for example, denying optional obligations (node 2 answered negatively) while still holding that the rescuer genuinely exceeds duty (node 1 answered with c and node 3 with b)—and diagnose whether its absence stems from internal incoherence, hidden attractiveness, or mere neglect in the literature.

Keep the instrument focused on sorting commitments to cases and structural principles. Do not ask users to self-identify with named theories, and do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or reading lists.
