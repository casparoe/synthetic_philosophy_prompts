I am delivering the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop on the ontology of life, hosted jointly by my Department of Philosophy and the Centre for Synthetic Biology. The audience is divided evenly between philosophers of biology and practising molecular biologists, with several astrobiologists present; none are metaphysicians by training, but all are conversant with the standard definitional debates. I want a formal address of roughly eight minutes that treats the problem of defining life not as a taxonomic exercise but as a structural constraint on how we frame both synthetic creation and extraterrestrial discovery.

The philosophical idea must do real work in shaping the speech. Please organise the remarks around what I shall call the genealogical bias: the tendency of evolutionary and functional definitions alike to include metabolically inert but genealogically embedded entities—viruses, dormant endospores—while excluding metabolically active but genealogically orphaned entities, such as de novo synthetic protocells or artificial chemotrophic networks assembled without evolutionary history. The speech should show how this asymmetry determines what counts as “finding life” versus “making life”, without resolving whether the bias is scientifically justified or metaphysically defensible.

Constraints: do not cite specific authors, papers, or texts by name; do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or suggestions for further study; avoid presenting well-known positions (for instance, the NASA working definition or the concept of autopoiesis) as settled authority, though you may treat them as common reference points. The tone should be polished and accessible—formal enough for philosophers, but without condescending simplification for the scientists, and without unnecessary metaphysical jargon. Use British English throughout. The address should close by posing one genuinely open question that sets the agenda for the workshop: whether excising the genealogical condition from our concept of life would expand our ontology in a scientifically fruitful direction, or whether it would simply dissolve the concept into a continuum of chemical complexity. Please write the remarks.
