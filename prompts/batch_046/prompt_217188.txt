Please explain the tension between the inside view and the outside view, particularly as it arises when an agent must evaluate the reliability of her own reasoning. I am not asking for generic advice about self-confidence; rather, I want to understand the conceptual structure of a specific difficulty. Often, the outside view is presented as an objective statistical correction: one compares one's own case to a reference class and adjusts expectations downward to avoid overconfidence. But consider situations in which membership in the relevant reference class is itself determined by an inside judgement—for example, asking for the success rate of researchers who have genuinely identified a deep structural problem in an established framework, where "genuine identification" is precisely what is in dispute. In such cases, applying the outside view seems to presuppose the inside view, or else to classify one with a group that may be composed largely of the overconfident. How should epistemic modesty operate here? Is the conflict fundamentally epistemic—about which beliefs are rationally warranted—or practical—about how to allocate effort given that one's first-personal access cannot be fully verified from the third-personal stance? Please develop this with conceptual precision, and feel free to illustrate it with a concrete scenario, such as a scholar deciding whether to pursue a highly unorthodox theoretical line that she judges internally coherent despite sustained peer scepticism, though I do not want career guidance.
