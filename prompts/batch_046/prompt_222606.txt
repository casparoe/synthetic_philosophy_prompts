I'm closing a semester-long philosophy-of-technology seminar with a session on whether harms sustained inside fully immersive, persistent virtual worlds—environments in which a user's proprioceptive map is indexed one-to-one to an avatar and social relations are governed by the same relational norms as offline interaction—count as genuinely real harms, or remain merely representational artifacts mediated by the render pipeline and server architecture. The group has split not over whether people suffer, but over whether that suffering has non-derivative moral weight: the realist camp argues that if phenomenal quality and relational structure (exclusion, humiliation, fear) are preserved, the substrate—code, database, headset—is ontologically irrelevant; the anti-realist camp insists that causal closure within the stack means every virtual harm is still constituted by the very medium that produces it, so the harm can never act back on the user except through its own representational mechanism, undermining its status as an independent moral fact. We've reached an honest impasse, and rather than resolve it with another round of arguments, I'd like to close with a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a concluding quatrain, with two refrains and two rhymes—written from the realist's voice but structurally compelled to house the anti-realist objection inside the form itself.

Please make the two refrains do the philosophical labor: one should voice something like the realist's claim that the harm is real because it is felt in embodied, indexical space, and the other should voice the anti-realist counter that the harm is only code rendering a phantom. The exact wording is yours, but the form must track the argument: let the refrains alternate dominance across the tercets so that the reader experiences the two positions circling and passing each other without convergence, and in the final quatrain—where both refrains are forced into the same stanza—stage the precise conceptual crux: whether the distinction between constitutive and representational harm collapses, or whether it refuses to collapse even when pressed together. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with debates over virtual ontology—specifically the question of whether virtual objects have existence conditions independent of their database entries, and whether virtual harms can be non-derivative—recognizes the actual argument rather than seeing vocabulary merely name-dropped. Don't sermonize about intervention or shrug with a circle-of-life resignation; let the repetition of the villanelle show why the tension remains open, and let the envoi hold both refrains side by side without forcing a false synthesis. No citations, bibliography, or reading lists—just the poem.
