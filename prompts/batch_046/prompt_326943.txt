In philosophical discussions of deception, a long-running dispute asks whether there is a distinctive moral wrong in lying that is not present in merely misleading someone—for instance, by selectively withholding information, using technically true but deliberately ambiguous phrasing, or arranging contexts so that false beliefs form without any false utterance being made. Proponents of a special wrongness, often drawing on Kantian or trust-based considerations, argue that lying violates a direct norm of sincerity and corrupts the interlocutor’s rational autonomy in a way that strategic omission or implicature does not. Critics, often working from consequentialist or speech-act-theoretic premises, reply that moral evaluation should target the overall communicative strategy and its foreseeable effects rather than the syntactic form of the utterance; on their view, lying and misleading are morally on a par, and treating them differently reflects an unjustified fetishization of literal falsehood.

Despite extensive exchange—spanning philosophy of language, moral psychology, and the ethics of testimony—the debate feels stalled. Each side can accommodate the other’s examples by adjusting auxiliary claims about speaker intention, audience expectation, or the strength of implicature, and no empirical finding about deception detection seems to settle the normative question.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is partly verbal—hinging on whether “lying” is defined narrowly as uttering a falsehood with intent to deceive, or broadly as engaging in deceptive communicative conduct—but partly substantive, hiding an unacknowledged disagreement about the proper unit of moral evaluation. One side seems to evaluate isolated speech acts against Gricean maxims of quality and quantity; the other evaluates conversational trajectories as strategic interactions. Moreover, neither side typically defends the Gricean framework they implicitly invoke. Importing Austin’s distinction between performative and constative utterances might reveal that “lying” is being condemned not as a constative failure but as a performative breach of a social contract of sincerity, which would relocate rather than resolve the disagreement. A further hidden division may concern whether the relevant moral relation is speaker-centered—focusing on assertoric commitment and evidential responsibility—or audience-centered, focusing on the listener’s entitlement to form beliefs.
