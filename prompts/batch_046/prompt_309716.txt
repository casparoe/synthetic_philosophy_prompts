I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I was chatting with some friends over coffee wether moral worth is really just about doing the right thing or if the hidden reason behind it changes everything. I think there's a real gap in how people teach this stuff — it's always "act for the right reason" but no one makes you actually feel the weirdness when you don't even know your own motive until after the act is done. So I'm running a workshop in a few weeks, about 14 people (some philosophy students, some curious laypeople), one hour, and I want a classroom game that gets them to feel the force of the idea that moral worth depends on acting for the right reasons, not just acting right.

To make it not just another Kant lecture, I want you to inject a specific mechanic: a "retroactive reason registry". Participants first make a quick observable moral choice — say, whether to return a lost wallet. Only after they've locked in the choice do they draw a hidden "motive card" — genuine duty, calculated self-interest, or an ambiguous mix — and they must then decide whether to register that as their true reason, pay a "revision cost" to rewrite it, or reject moral credit for the act entirely. The rules should be arranged so that the same behaviour gets rated differently based on an opaque internal state, and they should feel that tension before anyone names it.

Don't lay out a menu of options — take a clear stand on whether the registry mechanic should show that motives are auditable without corruption, or that auditing them destroys the very value we're measuring. Present your design as exactly five numbered items — setup and roles, rules with small stakes, the revelation moment, the debrief mapping each step to the moral-worth problem, and an honest failure note — each just a sentence or two, with no intro or outro. Keep it in British English, casual is good, and a typo or two won't hurt. Make sure they come away seeing that the reason isn't just decoration but structurally changes the moral status of the act, and be frank about which part of that idea the game can't reproduce.
