Student answer: Scheffler's afterlife conjecture is essentially the claim that people seek a form of immortality through the survival of humanity and culture, analogous to religious belief in an eternal soul. Because we implicitly want our lives to continue through descendants, the prospect of extinction robs present people of their personal afterlife. This is different from preference-frustration accounts, which hold that extinction is bad because the people who never get born have their preferences thwarted. Scheffler instead focuses on present individuals losing their legacy. He further argues that if there are no future generations, then nothing we do now has any point, since value requires an audience that outlasts the present. So extinction harms us now by making our projects meaningless. The objection that this is just a psychological need for legacy misses the point, because Scheffler thinks all value is audience-relative in this way.
