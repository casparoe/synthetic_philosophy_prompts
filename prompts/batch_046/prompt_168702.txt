Consider the paradox of tragic pleasure: audiences seek out art that evokes sorrow, fear, or grief, and often describe the experience as valuable despite—or because of—the negative affect involved. In this course unit we examined three responses: (1) the Aristotelian catharsis model, in which tragedy purges or transforms pity and fear through a structured emotional release; (2) the Humean conversion model, in which the skill of the artistic representation transforms the quality of sympathetic pain into a kind of reflective pleasure; and (3) the controlled-exposure model, in which the value lies in experiencing intense negative affect within a context stripped of practical urgency and action demands, allowing for emotional calibration without harm.

A student was given the following exam question:

"Can the controlled-exposure model account for cases where the audience reports that the negative emotion persists long after the artwork ends—for example, a lingering melancholy that the viewer describes as part of the value—without appealing to any positive emotional counterpart? And does this persistence pose a special problem for the Humean conversion model? In your answer, distinguish carefully between the object of the emotional response (the fictional events depicted) and the cause of the pleasure (the emotional experience itself), and explain whether either view requires that the negative emotion be partially cancelled out by a positive counterpart."

The controlled-exposure model handles lingering melancholy well. The object of the emotion is the fictional protagonist’s suffering, but the cause of the value is the viewer’s own calibrated emotional processing. Because this processing is decoupled from action, it can continue after the object is no longer present—just as one might keep working on a puzzle after putting it away. So the persistence of sadness is not a problem; it is the calibration system operating in the background, and since there is no practical urgency, the affect remains valuable.

By contrast, the Humean model holds that the aesthetic frame contains the emotion without altering its quality, allowing us to observe our sympathy safely. But in this case the viewer reports feeling genuinely distressed, not merely observing sympathy. If the frame were only containing the emotion, we should expect the after-effect to fade quickly once the frame is removed. Instead, the melancholy persists, which suggests that something more active—namely, the calibration process—is sustaining it. This shows that conversion is not occurring, and that the value must come from exposure rather than containment.

It is important not to confuse this with the Aristotelian view. Aristotle believed that catharsis requires a release or purging that restores emotional balance, and that this release depends on a positive emotional counterpart cancelling the negative one. The controlled-exposure model does not require release or cancellation; the negative emotion can remain fully present as long as it is decoupled from action demands. Therefore, the persistence of melancholy supports controlled exposure and undermines Hume.
