I am running a philosophy workshop for approximately fifteen advanced undergraduates in a single sixty-minute session. I want a structured role-play or deliberation that makes the participants feel the conceptual tension of supererogation—acts that appear genuinely good yet not strictly required—before anyone names the phenomenon. Rather than relying on familiar heroic rescue scenarios, the exercise should inject a somewhat niche framing: collective contribution to a shared resource governed by sharply diminishing marginal returns, so that each individual must decide whether to keep contributing once the collective need is technically satisfied, and whether continuing to do so is admirable, foolish, or obligatory. Design the full protocol: the division of participants into roles (active contributors, observers who evaluate, and a neutral recorder), the rules governing the pool and a scoring or payoff structure using non-monetary stakes (reputation tokens or access to a shared bonus) that makes over-contribution personally costly, the staged progression through initial cooperation, saturation of collective need, and then the over-contribution phase where pressure to continue conflicts with rational self-interest, and a debrief that maps each stage onto the distinctions between duty, permissible self-interest, and supererogation. Be explicit about which feature of the idea—particularly the asymmetry between first-person cost assessment and third-person praise—the exercise can reproduce through its incentives, and which feature the facilitator must supply because the simulation cannot generate it. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong on the first run—such as participants collapsing the distinction between praise and obligation too early, or the observers applying uniform standards regardless of role—and how to correct for it. The prompt should be self-contained, with no bibliography, citation requests, or references to highly obscure literature.
