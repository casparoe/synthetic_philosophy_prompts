After a long argument last night with a friend about whether Leibniz's moral philosophy can be reconciled with ordinary obligations to the vulnerable, I tried to formalize the tension and produced a chain that seems to collapse into quietism and cognitive elitism. Here is the structure: (1) Leibniz distinguishes three grades of natural right—strict justice, equity, and universal charity or piety—where piety is the highest and requires loving every substance in direct proportion to its objective degree of perfection. (2) For Leibniz, perfection is not measured by sensory pleasure but by the clarity and distinctness of perception, which means rational, morally cultivated beings rank higher than infants, non-human animals, or severely cognitively limited adults. (3) Because every substance already mirrors the entire universe, harming a being of lower rank does not reduce the total representational richness of the world, whereas harming a being of higher rank diminishes the clarity with which the cosmos is expressed. (4) Given finite resources and attention, piety therefore demands that we prioritize the preservation and flourishing of the most perfect beings, even when that requires allowing suffering among those of lower rank. (5) At the same time, since God has already arranged the best of all possible worlds, any particular suffering is metaphysically compensated by a greater good that could not exist without it; so human intervention risks substituting a locally preferred outcome for the globally optimal harmony. (6) Taken together, these premises imply that Leibnizian morality licenses, and perhaps requires, a hierarchical neglect of the vulnerable combined with a general prohibition on interfering with suffering. (7) This conclusion is intolerable, so one of the links must break.

I suspect the error lies between (3) and (4), or in the global-optimization move of (5), but I cannot isolate it precisely. I want to preserve (1) if possible—Leibniz's taxonomy of right seems too central to his practical philosophy to abandon lightly—rather than save ordinary moral obligations by discarding it. Which premise or inference should I reject, and what does that rejection cost? Does it require denying the mirror thesis, or conceding that finite agents are never permitted to reason globally about harmony?

Please identify where this reasoning is most likely to fail, and be explicit about what would change your mind: whether it is a correct reading of how piety actually operates in Leibniz's jurisprudence, a mistake about the relationship between local moral agency and pre-established harmony, or an equivocation between metaphysical perfection and moral desert. I also want to know the weakest point in the chain so that I do not build my seminar paper on a hidden confusion.
