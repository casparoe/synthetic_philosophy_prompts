I was chatting with two people in my philosphy study group about Sartre's stuff on radical freedom and bad faith, and they got totally stuck arguing over a specific case. One of them, Alex, talked about a coworker in corporate compliance who constantly jokes that he's just a corporate drone playing a part—he never misses a chance to say he's only there for the paycheck and the job doesn't define him. Alex said this is classic bad faith: the guy treats himself like an object with a fixed essence, the ironic drone, and uses that persona to hide from the fact that he genuinely chooses to stay every day. Alex compared it to Sartre's waiter who performs the role with exaggerated precision—except here the over-identification is with being detached. The other friend, Jordan, pushed back and said bad faith specifically involves denying either your transcendence or your facticity, and this coworker actually admits both: he knows he could quit and he knows he needs the salary. Jordan argued the irony isn't denial but an authentic recognition that the role doesn't exhaust him, and that by Alex's logic anyone who reflects critically on their job is in bad faith, which makes the concept useless. Then Alex came back: Sartre says you can't hold transcendence and facticity in a comfortable theatrical distance, you have to own your choice without a shield. The irony is just a meta-role, a way of fleeing the anguish of being the one who picked this life. Jordan replied that there's no principled line between healthy self-awareness and bad faith here, and that Alex is just labeling anything emotionally distant as inauthentic. They asked me to refere and I honestly couldn't. Can you go through their exchange move by move—rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating?
