I'm writing to seek your analysis of a practical dilemma I'm facing with a close friend, though I suspect the tension runs deeper than ordinary career counseling. After years of exhausting himself in a high-pressure creative field—chasing commissions, enduring harsh reviews, and feeling a constant, gnawing sense that he was never quite enough—he has deliberately stepped away from that world. He now works a quiet, unremarkable job that demands little and excites less. When I asked whether he regrets giving up the possibility of real achievement and the intense satisfactions that come with mastery, he said that for him the only metric that matters is the absence of craving and the absence of suffering. He insists that a life in which nothing is lacking and nothing hurts is not merely acceptable but optimal, and that adding "positive" goods—exhilaration, acclaim, passionate commitment—only reintroduces the conditions for future pain.

I feel a strong pull to argue that he is mistaking tranquility for a good life, yet I cannot articulate precisely where his reasoning fails without simply assuming that well-being must include some flourishing or excellence beyond mere non-disturbance. I find myself wondering whether there is a coherent philosophical position that makes the absence of craving and suffering not just a component but the complete and sufficient standard of a life well lived, and whether defending that position requires treating every positive emotional state as essentially a masked form of dependency or risk. If such a view is defensible, does it collapse into the claim that a sedated or minimally conscious existence is best, or can it distinguish between different ways of being free from disturbance?

Can you help me understand the structure of this disagreement? I want to know whether my intuition that he is "missing something" depends on smuggling in an unexamined theory about what makes life good, and whether his own view can be held consistently without reducing all value to the privation of pain.
