I'm drafting a one-year workshop proposal on presentism in historiography and need to sharpen a half-formed idea into a precise, tractable plan. The conventional literature distinguishes normative presentism—judging past actors by current moral standards—from conceptual presentism—applying anachronistic categories like "economy" or "identity" to societies that lacked them—and treats reflexive contextualism as the remedy for both. I suspect this framework misses a structural dimension I want to call institutional presentism: the way modern funding structures, archival acquisition policies, and peer-review expectations silently determine which questions about the past are askable at all, regardless of the historian's personal reflexivity.

The proposal should state precisely whether institutional presentism is genuinely distinct from its normative and conceptual counterparts, and whether it can be mitigated within current academic structures. It should explain why the standard two-part distinction leaves this open—specifically, that both normative and conceptual presentism are treated as cognitive failures correctable by better method, whereas institutional presentism may be baked into the material conditions of research itself. For the approach, I propose a comparative study across two subfields with different relationships to present-day institutional incentives—pre-modern legal history, which is largely insulated from contemporary policy demands, and environmental history, which is tightly coupled to current governance frameworks—analyzing whether research-question framing tracks source availability or tracks present institutional salience. Please justify why comparative field analysis is preferable to single-field reflexive ethnography, specify what success looks like (a working taxonomy of institutional framing mechanisms with at least one demonstrable divergence between the fields), and state honestly what failure looks like: perhaps discovering that archival gaps fully explain framing differences, or that institutional presentism collapses conceptually into normative presentism. Keep the scope realistic for one year of conceptual and comparative work rather than a statistical survey, avoid any bibliography or citation list since I will supply those myself, and frame the deliverable as clarifying the structure of the problem rather than resolving the entire historiographical debate.
