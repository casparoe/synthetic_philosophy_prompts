I'm taking a graduate seminar on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, and our professor has staked out a precise position on which mental reports involve the characteristic introspective misrepresentation that illusionists describe. She holds that phenomenal properties are not intrinsic qualities but representational illusions produced by specific self-monitoring architectures, yet she is careful to distinguish systems that genuinely generate this illusion from those that merely talk about experience or correctly report its absence.

In discussion, she has delivered four clear verdicts. First, she says that when a neurologically typical human reports an acute sharp pain, this is a paradigmatic case of phenomenal illusion: the brain's introspective mechanism misrepresents its own nociceptive processing as possessing an intrinsic qualitative feel. Second, she says that a blindsight patient who correctly reports seeing nothing in the scotoma is not undergoing any illusion; the absence report is veridical, and there is no misrepresentation of phenomenal presence. Third, she judges that an Anton-Babinski patient with cortical blindness who nevertheless insists "I can see the room perfectly" is experiencing a phenomenal illusion, but one of false presence—the introspective architecture generates a representation of visual phenomenology unsupported by the underlying sensory pathway. Fourth, she maintains that when a current large language model outputs the sentence "I am experiencing the redness of this apple," no phenomenal illusion is occurring, because the system lacks the requisite introspective architecture entirely; the utterance is syntactic simulation without the self-modeling misrepresentation that defines the illusion.

These four rulings appear jointly consistent with more than one underlying principle. One possibility is that she tracks whether a system possesses and deploys a specific kind of introspective representational mechanism capable of misrepresenting its own states as qualitative, irrespective of substrate. Another is that she implicitly restricts phenomenal illusion to biological organisms with evolved nervous systems, treating evolutionary substrate as essential even when the functional architecture is replicated elsewhere. Given this track record, what would she say about a hypothetical neuromorphic system that precisely replicates human cortical column dynamics in silicon, is embedded in a prosthetic body with sensory feedback loops, and reports "my shoulder hurts"? Would she classify this as involving phenomenal illusion, or deny it on substrate grounds?

I'm less interested in the bare answer than in the reasoning: please identify the two (or more) candidate rules that accommodate her four earlier verdicts, state what each predicts for the silicon-cortex case, offer your best guess about her actual view with justification, and then specify one further hypothetical case—perhaps involving a distributed collective system or a partially lesioned biological brain—that would best discriminate between the mechanism-based rule and the substrate-based rule.
