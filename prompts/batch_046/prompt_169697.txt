As a novelist accustomed to thinking in terms of plot integrity, I have become convinced that the Meno problem is best answered not by appealing to reliability or practical utility, but by recognizing that knowledge possesses a kind of revision-resistant narrative architecture: the knower holds a true belief within an interdependent web of explanations such that the belief survives the removal of any single supporting premise, much as a well-constructed novel retains its coherence when one scene is excised because the remaining chapters redistribute its dramatic weight. Mere true belief, on this account, is structurally brittle—accurate but isolated, like a sentence detached from its narrative context—so that justification is not primarily a truth-tracking mechanism but a form of internal plot coherence. What would count decisively against this position? I am particularly interested in whether there are clear cases where a belief exhibits this narrative architecture yet fails to be knowledge—for instance, because the explanatory web is systematically false despite its internal consistency—or, conversely, cases of bare knowledge that lack any such architecture, acquired through authority or intuition without any embedding explanatory story. Which of these objections are genuinely damaging, which are merely awkward, and what vulnerabilities would a defender of this view be compelled to concede?
