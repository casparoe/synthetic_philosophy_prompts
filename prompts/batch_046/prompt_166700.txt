At a philosophy of language reading group, two colleagues—Dr. Elena Vasquez and Marcus Chen—fell into a sustained disagreement over Douglas Hofstadter's satire A Person Paper on Purity in Language. Vasquez began by arguing that Hofstadter's reversal of gendered pronouns exposes a genuine cognitive bias: when English defaults to masculine forms like he or man as generics, speakers do not process them as truly neutral but instead activate male-specific mental imagery, which means the so-called default is never innocent but always carries a hidden framing effect. Chen replied that Vasquez misreads the rhetorical strategy: Hofstadter invents deliberately absurd replacements—womyn, herstory, personhole cover—to show that any programmatic purge of gender from language quickly becomes ridiculous, demonstrating that masculine generics are not oppressive framing devices but efficient grammatical conventions that carry no necessary cognitive load. Vasquez countered that Chen mistakes the target of the satire: Hofstadter is not mocking language reform itself, but the hysterical resistance to flipping the default, as shown by how violently readers react when she becomes the universal pronoun; she added that the modern success of singular they proves reform can be functional rather than absurd, since it avoids invented morphology altogether and simply extends an existing form. Chen then argued that singular they actually vindicates his reading: it works precisely because it avoids the kind of engineered replacements Hofstadter parodies, which shows that linguistic change succeeds when it follows natural efficiency rather than activist redesign; therefore, he said, the masculine form was never a biased cognitive frame but merely the historically efficient option for a society that happened to organize itself around male norms. Vasquez pressed further: if they now serves the same universal function without absurdity, then the earlier insistence on he was not a neutral efficiency but a convention that required male normativity to seem natural, and Hofstadter's paper reveals exactly that hidden dependency. Chen concluded that this confuses sociology with semantics—norms change, and language tracks those changes efficiently, but no single pronoun ever carried an intrinsic cognitive bias; the bias, if any, was in the society, not in the grammar.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each of Vasquez's and Chen's main moves—her claim about cognitive framing, his claim about absurd replacements undermining reform, her defense of the satire's target, his interpretation of singular they as vindicating efficiency, and her final point about hidden normativity—rate whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, what important consideration both are missing about how satire functions as an argumentative form rather than as a direct proof, and whether there is any coherent middle position that preserves Hofstadter's insight without committing to the strongest version of either speaker's claim.
