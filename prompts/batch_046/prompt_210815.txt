I spent my career in condensed-matter physics, and since retiring I have been working through moral philosophy with the same expectation of precise distinctions. I want to compare two analyses of hypocrisy in relation to standing to blame. First, the familiar view that hypocrisy undermines standing because the blamer violates a condition of moral equality—one cannot legitimately blame another for X while oneself doing X. Second, a view I have sketched for myself—call it the epistemic-privilege view—according to which the hypocrite, precisely because they share the fault, possess privileged first-person knowledge of its rationalization structures and therefore have enhanced, not diminished, normative standing to blame, provided they explicitly acknowledge their own complicity. Compare these two analyses specifically in cases of institutional blame, such as a regulator condemning an industry practice on which the regulator's own budget depends. Does the epistemic-privilege view collapse under institutional scale, or does the moral-equality condition need reformulation rather than abandonment? I invite plain pushback: if my framing illegitimately imports norms from scientific self-correction into moral philosophy, or if the comparison rests on a confused premise, say so directly rather than forcing an answer. Please respond in no more than 300 words, without citations or reading lists, and put your bottom line in the first sentence.
