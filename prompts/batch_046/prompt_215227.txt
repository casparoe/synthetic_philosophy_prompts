Write a villanelle that stages Gilbert Ryle's distinction between knowing how and knowing that, letting the form itself perform the philosophical argument. Use two refrains in alternating tercets: one spoken by a practitioner—"I ride without the theorem in my head"—and one spoken by the intellectualist challenger—"But every skill conceals a rule unsaid." Structure the poem so that its cyclical returns enact the regress Ryle describes: each attempt to ground knowing-how in a prior knowing-that only demands another knowing-how to apply that prior knowledge. In the central tercets, contrast multi-track dispositions—balancing on ice versus dry pavement, each adjustment different without an explicit mental calculus—against the challenger's demand for a unified propositional core. The volta should occur when the challenger claims that even error-correction requires a tacit representation of correct form; the response should show correction operating through direct normative sensitivity rather than comparison to an internal model. In the closing quatrain, bring the two refrains together side by side without synthesis, leaving the tension structurally unresolved, since Ryle's claim is not that knowing-how is mysterious but that it is not reducible to knowing-that. The poem should be precise enough that a reader familiar with The Concept of Mind recognizes the specific arguments—especially the regress and the multi-track nature of dispositions—rather than treating the distinction as a vague opposition between practice and theory.
