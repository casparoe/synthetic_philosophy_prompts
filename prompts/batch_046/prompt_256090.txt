I hold that when forecasting the long-term durability or decline of large-scale governance institutions—democratic federations, international regimes, and comparable structures—the reference-class approach should dominate inside-view causal reasoning. Specifically, one ought to rely on base rates drawn from the lifespan, dissolution patterns, and recovery dynamics of past empires, republics, and confederations, rather than on analysis of current policies, technological capacities, or leadership quality. I also hold that the standard objection—that modern institutions are sui generis because of information technology, strategic deterrence, or global economic integration—fails, since analogous features existed in earlier reference classes without preventing institutional failure. I want an honest map of what would count against this position. What structural differences, if any, would genuinely break the reference-class comparison? Which empirical patterns in institutional failure modes, half-lives, or post-collapse recovery trajectories would make base-rate forecasting misleading rather than merely noisy? Is there a live risk that defenders of this view have qualified it—by allowing ad hoc modernity adjustments—into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that insulation a problem for a forecasting methodology? I ask you to separate settled findings in comparative history and forecasting methodology from interpretations that remain genuinely contested among specialists, and not to present a disputed account of imperial decline—say, whether fiscal-military overextension or elite fragmentation is the primary driver—as if it were consensus. In particular, address whether the integration of rapid algorithmic feedback loops into bureaucratic and military decision-making represents a true discontinuity that invalidates historical analogues, or whether it is absorbable as another communication-speed and coordination-cost variable within existing reference classes. Rank potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which ones committed defenders of the outside view would themselves concede as damaging, and assess whether the position risks being well insulated in a way that is either a virtue or a vice.
