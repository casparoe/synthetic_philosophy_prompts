I defend a unified reading of Bernard Williams's integrity objection and his "one thought too many" case: both reveal a structural failure in normative theories that require an agent to treat her ground projects and intimate attachments as fungible inputs within a decision procedure. On my view, the failure is not merely moral but constitutive—if an agent's practical identity is formed by commitments that are by nature non-substitutable, then any theory demanding their suspension for aggregate benefit, consistency across agents, or procedural neutrality does not ask for self-sacrifice but for a kind of self-annihilation. I extend this beyond act-utilitarianism to structural analogues: certain contractualist frameworks that permit ranking commitments by mutual justifiability, ranking forms of virtue ethics that treat character traits as revisable instruments of flourishing, and rational-choice models that reduce loyalty to expected utility. What would genuinely count against this position? I want the arguments, cases, and phenomenological descriptions that should move me, sorted by how decisive they are. In particular: are there coherent accounts of agents who revise or abandon ground projects without losing practical identity; does the phenomenology of deep moral conflict actually support non-fungibility rather than painful substitution; do contractualism or virtue ethics genuinely replicate the fungibility problem, or have I overextended the analogy; and does my framing risk unfalsifiability by redefining "ground project" whenever a counterexample of successful revision appears? Please distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that is merely awkward, include what defenders of fungibility-tolerant theories would themselves concede as damaging, and offer an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into insulation—and if so, whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a claim about the conditions of agency.
