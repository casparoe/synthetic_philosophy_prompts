Construct a realistic clinical-triage thought experiment, confined to a single paragraph and set in an emergency ward rather than in science fiction, in which a physician must allocate one remaining dose of an analgesic that suppresses first-order phenomenal experience of pain but leaves all behavioral, neural, and cognitive-access functions entirely intact. The ward contains exactly two patients who are observationally identical—same vital signs, same verbal reports, same neural activation patterns—but they differ in a way invisible to any scan or interview: Patient A possesses full first-order phenomenal pain without any higher-order cognitive access to it (no reportability, no self-monitoring), while Patient B possesses perfect higher-order access and reports severe pain but entirely lacks first-order phenomenal experience. After presenting the case, demonstrate what phenomenal realism and access-functionalism respectively prescribe about who should receive the drug and why; identify the specific structural feature that makes the phenomenal realist’s choice seem counterintuitive; explain how a phenomenal realist could defend that choice by attacking the coherence of the dissociation the scenario assumes; and state what collapses if that dissociation is denied.
