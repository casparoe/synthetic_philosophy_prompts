As a retired physicist who has recently begun auditing graduate seminars in moral and political philosophy, I have been tracking my instructor's rulings on cases of what she terms political dirt—actions by officials that breach ordinary moral norms for the sake of a public good. Her verdicts to date are these: a president who forges diplomatic cables in secret to avert a nuclear exchange is justified, on condition that she later confesses and accepts legal penalty; a health minister who suppresses preliminary vaccine-safety data to preserve vaccination rates during an epidemic is not justified, because transparency is structurally required by the office regardless of lives saved; a mayor who directs police to use excessive force against a violent attacker in order to protect bystanders is justified, but only if the mayor personally accepts criminal liability rather than claiming institutional immunity; and a legislator who accepts a private payment to vote for climate legislation she already supported is not justified, since corrupting the deliberative process is irreparable even when the legislative outcome is beneficial.

The case now before us concerns a prime minister who must decide whether to divert public funds without parliamentary approval to a covert biomedical program operating in an allied jurisdiction with weak regulatory oversight. The research employs what its own protocol calls "non-volitional consent proxies"—using biological material derived from unconscious patients to accelerate development of a cure for a pandemic projected to kill millions—and it functions under a self-amending oversight clause that permits the researchers to revise ethical constraints once initial funding is secured. The prime minister knows that disclosure will trigger immediate international shutdown, whereas secrecy offers an 85 percent predictive confidence of distributing a cure within eighteen months. Should she proceed, she intends to publish a full accounting only after distribution, accepting personal prosecution.

Assuming she wishes to remain consistent with her four prior rulings, what should her verdict be? Please identify at least two distinct principles capable of generating all four past verdicts, state what each predicts for this case, identify the closest analog among her precedents, and offer your best guess with reasons. If her record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, be explicit about the principle she would be adopting with each option, and specify one further hypothetical case that would distinguish the competing rules.
