I am a doctoral candidate in political economy, and in a recent graduate seminar on collective-action theory I found the standard pedagogical opposition between Garrett Hardin’s tragedy-of-the-commons narrative and the economic taxonomy of public goods somewhat unsatisfying when applied to institutions that reward abstention rather than consumption. Over coffee with a colleague, I sketched a hypothetical municipal arrangement—let us term it a “recessive commons”—governing a shared acoustic buffer zone in a densely populated district. In this scheme, residents hold non-transferable, non-marketised rights to register abstention from generating noise within the zone; the ecological and social value of the buffer increases not with aggregate use, but with the aggregate number of registered abstentions, while any individual resident can unilaterally diminish the collective benefit by withdrawing their abstention and emitting sound. The resource is rivalrous in degradation but non-rivalrous in protection, and exclusion of non-residents is technically feasible though administratively costly.

Could you compare Hardin’s tragedy-of-the-commons framework with the public-goods framework—specifically the interaction of non-rivalry and non-excludability—as tools for analysing the collective-action dynamics of this recessive commons? In particular, I would like you to identify which analytical features of each framework illuminate the incentive structure here, which features mislead or require significant modification, and whether the scheme is best understood as a common-pool resource, a pure public good, a club good, or a conceptually distinct category. Please present your answer in British English, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or specific page citations; I am interested in the conceptual architecture and its internal tensions rather than textual exegesis.
