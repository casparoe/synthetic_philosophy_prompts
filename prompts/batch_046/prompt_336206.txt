As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am preparing a feature on self-referential structures in constitutional law and want to use the 2019 United Kingdom prorogation crisis as a case study. The episode is this: in September 2019, Prime Minister Boris Johnson advised the monarch to suspend Parliament for five weeks ahead of the Brexit withdrawal date; Parliament was sitting and actively debating the terms of withdrawal; the Supreme Court, in a unanimous ruling, held that the prorogation was unlawful and void because it prevented Parliament from carrying out its constitutional functions without reasonable justification. The government had defended the suspension in part by invoking the 2016 advisory referendum, which Parliament itself had legislated to hold but never made legally binding, treating its result as a democratic mandate that should override ordinary legislative procedure. This seems to generate a loop: the people authorize Parliament; Parliament enacted the referendum; the referendum result was treated as a meta-level command that the executive could deploy against Parliament; and the judiciary restored parliamentary authority by appealing to the very sovereignty that had been circumvented. Analyze the crisis through the lens of tangled hierarchies and the notion of an inviolate level, drawing on the recursive structure illustrated in Escher's Drawing Hands. Identify the levels precisely—popular mandate, parliamentary sovereignty, executive prerogative, judicial review—specify who occupies each level and what the meta-level move consists in, and determine whether any level functions as an inviolate base that breaks the loop. Does the framework illuminate why British constitutional discourse fractured, with some actors treating the popular will as unchallengeable and others treating parliamentary supremacy as absolute, or does it force a mapping that the institutional facts resist?
