I've been arguing with my roommate about causation by omission — you know, the idea that not doing something can actually cause stuff to happen. We were talking about that classic plant-watering case where you forget and it dies, and she said that's only a cause because there's an implicit expectation you'd care for it. That made sense, but then I started thinking about these "structural ommissions" my prof mentioned in distributed systems — like when a power grid fails not because someone flipped a switch wrong, but because no agent replaced a specific component that the design assumed would be monitored collectively. There's no single person with a duty there, just a gap in the system's self-correction loop.

So here's where I'm stuck. If omissions always need some baseline or default state to count as causal, and in structural cases that baseline is just the system's design parameters, then isn't picking that baseline always kind of arbitary or at least context-dependent? Like, we call the missing component an ommission because we designed the grid for redundancy, but if we'd designed it differently the same absence wouldn't be an omission at all. Given all that, doesn't it follow that omission-causation is really just us projecting our framing choices onto the world? Shouldn't we just drop omission-talk entirely and say all real causation is positive interaction, with omissions being nothing more than explanatory shortcuts we use when we want to assign blame without pointing to a specific action?
