I have no academic background, but I have spent the last year arguing with friends about why large historical events actually happen—not in a vague “everything matters” way, but in the specific situation where it looks like several different things could each have caused the same outcome on their own. For my concrete example, I keep returning to the collapse of an empire: the economic crisis seems sufficient, the military defeat seems sufficient, and the failure of leadership seems sufficient, yet they all happened together. I want to know what the real logical options are for thinking about this, not just a list of famous historians’ names. Please build me a taxonomy—specifically a decision tree with four branching nodes—that sorts someone from concrete answers about cases to a named position, or to an explicitly unnamed one. Each node must be a question a person could answer without knowing philosophical labels, using only my kind of example.

The four nodes should be, in order: (1) Can one historical event have two different causes, each of which—if the other had not occurred—would still have produced that event? (2) If yes to (1), should a good explanation list every such sufficient cause, or should it apply a selection rule to list only one? If no to (1), do the necessary causes combine like weights that sum up, or like parts of a recipe that only work in specific combinations? (This is node 3, on the “no” branch only; present it as the alternative split.) (3—cross-cutting, applied to all paths) Does the logical treatment change depending on whether the cause is structural (economic, geographic), agential (a decision by a person), or accidental (a storm, a plague)? Then split the “yes” path by (2) list-all versus select-one, split the “no” path by (3) additive versus configurational, and then apply (4) cause-type-sensitive versus cause-type-uniform to every resulting branch, yielding eight leaves total.

For each leaf, give the position name if scholars actually defend it, or mark it “unnamed.” Then, in one sentence, state its most awkward commitment—the thing someone holding it must accept that sounds strange in ordinary conversation. Next, list the combinations of answers that fall outside your tree (for instance, “yes to overdetermination but neither list nor select”) and for each say whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves that your tree genuinely cannot separate, and explain exactly which distinction collapses and why.

I do not want bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I want to discover whether my own intuitive view—probably somewhere in the middle—lands in a real position or in a logical gap. Structure your response in this order and approximate length: (1) “The case I keep returning to” (~120 words), framing my non-specialist puzzle with the empire example; (2) “The four branch nodes” (~150 words), presenting each question in tree order with the path conditions clearly shown; (3) “The eight leaves” (~250 words), as a structured list with name/unnamed tags and the one-sentence awkward commitment for each; (4) “The empty branches” (~150 words), diagnosing unoccupied logical spaces; (5) “What the tree cannot separate” (~100 words), with explicit flags and reasons.
