I am drafting an essay for a literary quarterly that grows out of a formal problem I encountered while writing a novel in which readers collectively determine narrative outcomes through an open-source architecture. The problem concerns Rousseau's well-known critique of theatrical representation: that it corrupts civic virtue by substituting passive spectacle for active participation and by allowing performers to display artificial virtue without genuine moral commitment. I want to ask whether this critique inverts, rather than extends, when applied to participatory fiction in which the audience becomes co-author. In such forms, I suggest, the hazard is not the vanity of the performer or the emotional manipulation of the spectator, but a diffusion of moral accountability across an anonymous collective of creators, so that no individual bears the burden of the represented action. I further propose an analogy between Rousseau's preference for the civic festival—where participants are simultaneously actors and observers—and these modern collaborative narrative structures, though I worry that I am projecting a digital-age concept onto an eighteenth-century framework in a way that obscures more than it reveals. Without asking for bibliographic references or source verification, I would welcome your critical assessment of two points: whether my reading of Rousseau's distinction between authentic collective action and mediated spectacle is philosophically sound, and whether the comparison to collaborative narrative design genuinely illuminates a tension in his thought or merely imposes an anachronistic category upon it. I am especially interested in whether there is a deeper ontological question about the status of represented action—perhaps one that concerns the relation between authorial intention and collective execution—that my comparison has overlooked.
