I am drafting a short essay for a philosophy-of-mind workshop on the frame problem in artificial and natural cognition. Below is the draft. I want critical comments on whether the central distinction I introduce is genuinely clarifying or merely renames existing difficulties, and I ask that you not supply a bibliography or citations since I am testing the argument against conceptual resources alone.

The frame problem is usually introduced as a problem of representational economy: after an agent performs an action, how does it revise its beliefs about the world without checking, for every belief it holds, whether that belief remains true? Classic responses invoke frame axioms, non-monotonic inference rules, or relevance-logic filters that block the derivation of irrelevant consequences. These approaches treat the difficulty as essentially logical: the agent needs a formal mechanism that restricts belief revision to the affected sector of its knowledge base.

I think this standard treatment addresses only half of the problem, because it assumes that the agent already knows which sector is affected. Before any logical filter can be applied, the agent must solve what I call the salience-selection problem: it must determine which features of the current situation are relevant to the action just performed. This is not a matter of deriving logical consequences but of directing cognitive resources. To make this explicit, I distinguish two kinds of frames. A structural frame is a representational schema that encodes the constraints and relations within a particular domain—for example, the physical rules governing object persistence. An attentional frame is a dynamic mechanism—whether implemented as a neural gating process, a heuristic priority queue, or a learned policy—that determines which structural frames are activated and which are suppressed in a given context.

On this view, the traditional frame problem concerns structural frames: given an activated schema, how do we represent change without exhaustive update? But there is a deeper, prior difficulty concerning attentional frames: when an agent encounters a situation that does not match any familiar schema, how does it construct or select the appropriate structural frame in the first place? Solving the logical problem—designing efficient belief-revision rules—therefore does not solve the cognitive problem of attentional allocation. If this distinction holds, then much of the philosophical debate about whether formal tools like non-monotonic logic solve the frame problem may be addressing a symptom rather than the underlying condition.

Please comment on the accuracy of my characterization of the standard frame problem, the coherence of the structural-frame/attentional-frame distinction, and whether the claim that attentional framing is prior to logical framing is defensible or collapses into a familiar point about context-sensitivity in belief revision. I am especially interested in whether I have misrepresented the role of relevance logic or frame axioms, and whether there is a well-known objection to the priority claim—perhaps that selecting the relevant frame is itself just another instance of the logical frame problem applied at a higher level—that I should address.
