I've been working on a novel about a bureaucrat who slowly realizes the government he serves is illegitimate, and to get the ethics right I was talking with a friend who studies philosphy. She summed up this one view — I think it's related to fair-play theory but she called it the 'nested acquiescence' model — and said you don't consent to the state by voting or signing anything. Instead, every day you stay and don't emigrate you passively accept the rules, and that creates a real moral duty to obey, kind of like not leaving a potluck means you're on the hook for washing dishes. But she said civil disobedience is only okay when the state breaks what she called the 'recognition condition,' meaning it stops listening to formal protests altogether, and even then you can only disobey in ways that don't damage the shared goods you depend on. So violence is automatically out, and if you just think a law is unfair but the courts still hear your case, you have to obey. She concluded that this makes the view basically conservative, because almost any functioning democracy meets the recognition condition, so rebels are almost always wrong.

Something feels off about how she tied the duty to stay rather than to recieve benefits, and I'm not sure she got the part about violence right either. Is this a fair summary? Which parts are accurate to the underlying idea of associative obligation, which are her own additions or common simplifications, and does the conservative conclusion actually follow from the accurate parts? I don't need citations or a reading list — I just want to know if I'm writing my character's crisis of conscience on solid ground or if I'm accidentally making her sound dumber than she should be.
