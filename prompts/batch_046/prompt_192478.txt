So I retired from the lab about three years ago and started reading philosophy for the first time, which probably explains why I'm writing this like a grant proposal that went wrong. Anyway I'm working on a little essay about intuition pumps and whether they actually do the work philosophers think they do. My hunch is that they behave less like experiments and more like calibration errors in old instruments—some amplify a real signal, others just show you where the lens is scratched.

I'm proposing we split them into "convergent pumps" and "refracting pumps." A convergent one is like Galileo's falling bodies thought experiment: people with different backgrounds tend to agree on the verdict once the setup is clear, and the disagreement that remains tracks real theoretical differences. A refracting pump is more like the Chinese Room or Mary's room—people don't converge even after clarifying every term, and the split seems to come from which background framework they already bought into. The pump isn't measuring intuition; it's measuring how much your prior theory colors the instrument.

The twist I'm adding is that refracting pumps might still be useful, but not for the reason philosophers usually claim. They don't reveal conceptual truths; they act like interferance patterns that map the boundaries between competing frameworks. So a philosopher running Mary's Room isn't testing whether physicalism is true—she's just seeing where her own conceptual grid diffracts.

I'm wondering if this framing collapses too quickly. Does it unfairly dismiss the normative force of cases like the trolley problem? Is there a third category I'm missing—maybe "degenerate pumps" where the setup is so underdetermined that every answer is equally compatible? And am I right that scientists deal with this by controlling for instrument bias, whereas philosophers often treat the bias itself as data?

I'd appreciate comments especially on whether the analogy to instrumental artifacts holds up, whether the convergent/refracting distinction is sharp enough to be useful, and if there's an obvious counterexample I'm ignoring because I haven't read enough yet. No need for a full bibliography—just tell me where the reasoning slips or sounds like I'm smuggling in my old lab habits where they don't belong, and where I recieve the wrong signal.
