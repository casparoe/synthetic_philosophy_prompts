Design a 75-minute workshop for 18 graduate students in a philosophy-of-culture seminar. The aim is to make participants feel the structural pressure of Confucian role ethics—not as a catalog of virtues but as a relational field in which obligations accumulate rather than cancel when roles overlap. To achieve this, build the activity around an invented mechanic you should call “cross-role residue”: when a participant resolves an obligation in one relational axis (for example, filial duty), the resolution does not discharge the debt but transforms it into a residual obligation that carries over into a different axis (for example, civic or professional duty). The residue is not identical to the original obligation and may directly conflict with the direct obligations of the new context.

Structure the session as a staged deliberation with hidden scoring. Assign each student a nested set of roles—parent/child, magistrate/citizen, elder/junior colleague—across three escalating cases in which the same proposed action receives incompatible evaluations depending on which relational axis is activated. The rules must prohibit participants from stepping outside their assigned roles, from appealing to universal moral principles, and from negotiating away obligations; they may only deliberate within the vocabulary of the roles they occupy. After the cases, reveal that the hidden payoff system has not been tracking satisfaction of individual roles in isolation, but coherence across the residue chain—something participants could not have calculated in advance because the scoring weights were not disclosed.

In your design, specify the timeline minute-by-minute, the exact rules governing speech and decision, the three cases with their nested conflicts, and how the hidden scoring works. Then provide the debrief: map each moment of the activity onto a corresponding feature of role ethics (the non-transferability of obligations, the absence of an Archimedean standpoint outside relationships, the idea that ren is a property of the relational field rather than of the agent). State clearly what will predictably go wrong in running it—participants attempting to resolve tension through individual autonomy, or treating roles as masks rather than constitutive identities—and how the facilitator should handle those moves without breaking the frame. Finally, be explicit about what the mechanics cannot reproduce: the temporal depth of lifelong role cultivation and the gradual, affective extension of care from family to wider circles, which the facilitator must supply through brief narration rather than rules. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the prompt should be runnable by a facilitator with no external texts.
