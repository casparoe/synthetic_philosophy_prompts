I am preparing a long-form feature on the conceptual architecture of consciousness studies, and after a recent workshop discussion with philosophers and neuroscientists I find myself returning to a tension that is rarely formalized in public writing: the standoff between those who hold that phenomenal properties can be causally inert yet remain epistemically accessible, and those who hold that such inertness renders introspective knowledge impossible. Rather than commissioning another polemic, I would like you to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps—designated here as the Correlationist Epiphenomenalists and the Causal-Access Interactionists—centered on the paradox of phenomenal judgment, but approached through a framing my editor and I have been developing, which we call the introspective exclusion problem: the specific worry that if phenomenal states possess no causal efficacy whatsoever, then the physical mechanisms producing our verbal reports about those states are causally decoupled from the states themselves, leaving it unclear why those reports should constitute knowledge rather than merely well-calibrated but causally blind dispositions.

Please organize the deliverable as a formal collaboration document with four parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: precise statements about the formal structure of the paradox, about the general reliability of first-person phenomenal reports, about the involvement of neural correlates, and about what the relevant no-go results in the field actually rule out—worded so that representatives of both camps could sign without reservation. Second, a disagreements table in which each remaining dispute is rendered twice: once in the exact wording the Correlationists would accept for their own view, and once in the exact wording the Interactionists would accept for theirs, with no strawman language on either side. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them in one direction would genuinely move at least one camp. I want particular attention here to whether lawlike correlation without causal transmission can ground epistemic justification, and to a hypothetical scenario we are calling the bifurcated-report architecture—an arrangement in which the neural pathways generating verbal reports are structurally isolated from those sustaining phenomenal experience, tracking them only via an intermediate computational layer. For this scenario, record in advance what each camp predicts about the content and accuracy of the reports, what each counts as decisive evidence, and whether both agree that an empirical realization would resolve the debate or merely displace it. Fourth, an assessment of whether any portion of the disagreement—particularly around terms such as access or knowledge—is partly verbal rather than substantive; if so, specify exactly which portion and why, using wording both sides could endorse. Write in a polished, formal register appropriate for a science-ideas publication; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation lists, and avoid verbatim quotations or specific historical attributions that depend on precise textual verification.
