I have been working through Augustine's treatise on lying and the reply he wrote to Consentius, and I find myself caught on what seems like an internal tension in his account. On the one hand, Augustine insists that lying is always impermissible because it violates the nature of language as instituted for the communication of truth, and because it corrupts the speaker's soul by introducing a division between heart and tongue. On the other hand, he repeatedly defines a lie specifically as a false statement uttered with the will to deceive, which sounds like an intentionalist criterion that applies only to beings capable of volition. Given this, I wonder whether Augustine's framework actually allows for a category of non-lying falsehood—statements that are factually wrong and harmful to hearers, but produced without deceptive intent—such that he might judge a modern language model that hallucinates a legal citation differently from a human lawyer who knowingly fabricates one?

Or is the intentionalist definition merely a diagnostic feature, while the deeper wrong lies in the distortion of the communal order of truth regardless of the speaker's psychology? I ask because recent discussions of artificial cognition have made me wonder whether Augustine's absolute prohibition is best read as a claim about the moral psychology of the speaker, in which case it might not extend to non-volitional systems, or rather as a claim about the teleological purpose of speech itself, in which case any deployment of false language—whether by a human or by an algorithm trained on human data—would fall under the same condemnation. I would like to understand whether this distinction collapses under scrutiny, or whether there is a coherent Augustinian position on false speech generated without belief or intent.
