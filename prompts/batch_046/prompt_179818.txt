I'm drafting grading materials for a workshop I run for fiction writers who use metaphysical thought experiments to sharpen structural attention to causal and explanatory chains. I am evaluating a student essay anonymously and do not wish to signal my own view of its quality. Here is the exam question and the student's response.

Question: In discussions of metaphysical grounding, some distinguish between "big-G" Grounding—the relation connecting derivative reality to a single, unified fundamental base—and "small-g" grounding, which comprises localized explanatory relations between specific entities, such as a statue and its clay, or a shadow and the object that casts it. Consider the invented case I call the Mirror-Ground problem: a polished marble statue stands before a mirrored wall, and the visual image in the mirror is plausibly grounded jointly in the statue and in the reflective properties of the glass. A philosopher argues that because the image branches—having two distinct intermediate grounds rather than one—this demonstrates that grounding is not transitive, that no unified fundamental base exists, and therefore that the big-G/small-g distinction collapses. Evaluate this argument.

Student answer: The Mirror-Ground case is interesting because it shows an entity can have more than one ground. The image is grounded in the statue but also in the glass, so there is no single chain running all the way down to atoms. This means transitivity fails: if A grounds B and B grounds C, it does not follow that A grounds C, because B might branch into separate paths. So big-G grounding, which requires one base, is impossible, and only small-g relations remain. The statue itself is also only partly fundamental—its left half is fundamental, but its right half depends on the mirror's reflection—so fundamentality comes in degrees. Therefore the distinction collapses entirely.

Quote the specific phrases where these occur. 3. Grade and justification (about 100 words): assign a letter grade and justify it with precise language for anonymous feedback, treating me as a fellow instructor who needs point-by-point justification rather than a general impression.
