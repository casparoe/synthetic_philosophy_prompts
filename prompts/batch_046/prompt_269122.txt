In the lounge of a writers' residency, two guests—a playwright and a novelist—spent an evening in disagreement that I observed without intervening. The playwright defended the view, associated with the tradition of reactive attitudes, that resentment and gratitude are not judgments about metaphysical freedom but expressions of an interpersonal expectation of goodwill. If determinism is true, she argued, it does not dissolve moral responsibility, because these attitudes are withdrawn not upon learning that an action was causally determined, but only when we judge that the agent is incapable of entering into the reciprocal relations that make the attitude meaningful—what is sometimes called taking the objective stance. The novelist countered with three successive objections. First, he said that reactive attitudes carry an implicit control condition: we suspend resentment upon discovering hypnosis, coercion, or severe mental disorder precisely because we think the causal story undermines the agent's authorship, which suggests that universal determinism should trigger the same suspension globally. The playwright replied that the objective stance is not triggered by causation as such but by the absence of the capacity for rational responsiveness; a deterministically acting agent who can still understand and answer to resentment remains within the participant framework. Second, the novelist introduced what he called "shadow reactive attitudes"—the genuine indignation he feels toward Iago for betraying Othello, directed at the character rather than at Shakespeare, despite Iago having no causal independence whatsoever. If reactive attitudes can be fully directed at entities that lack any real-world freedom, he argued, then the claim that they are anchored in actual interpersonal vulnerability collapses; they are representational responses to intentional structures, and moral responsibility, if grounded in them, is a feature of narrative coherence rather than of metaphysical agency. The playwright responded that such fictional responses are parasitic analogues, lacking the practical demand for acknowledgment and repair that defines genuine resentment, so they pose no threat to the real-world framework. Third, the novelist proposed the phenomenon of "structural resentment"—indignation directed not at any individual but at an institutional arrangement that produces harm without any particular wrongdoer's intent, functioning normatively like a reactive attitude yet lacking a specific second-personal target. He contended that this shows reactive attitudes are not essentially interpersonal but are broader normative evaluations of justification failures, and that the focus on personal emotion therefore misses the actual locus of moral responsibility.

Neither yielded. I ask for a point-by-point adjudication: on the determinism objection, on the shadow-attitude objection, and on the structural-resentment objection, who is right about which specific claim, and what are both missing? In particular, do the novelist's injected concepts reveal a genuine instability in the participant-objective distinction, or are they category mistakes that the playwright correctly identifies? And does either side address whether reactive attitudes require not merely rational responsiveness but a particular kind of vulnerability—being susceptible to the attitude in a way that can actually alter one's standing?
