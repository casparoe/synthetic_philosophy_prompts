Here are notes for a short essay I am drafting. The core claim is that Confucian role ethics faces an internal tension when extended to modern bureaucratic and technologically mediated roles—say, being a content-moderation auditor or a call-center supervisor—because these positions lack the affective density and reciprocal particularity that the classical five relationships (parent-child, ruler-subject, husband-wife, elder-younger sibling, friend-friend) presuppose. I argue that the standard role-ethical defense—roles are defined by correct performance of reciprocal obligations, not by emotional warmth—misses something: in the Confucian framework, ren (humaneness) is not merely behavioral compliance but a cultivated perceptual attunement to the specific other within the relational position. When a role is mediated by algorithmic protocols, scripted interactions, and high turnover, that attunement becomes structurally impossible. The essay then considers two escapes. First, one might say modern roles are simply deficient and should be reformed to recover relational thickness; but I worry this concedes that role ethics offers no normative guidance for the institutions people actually inhabit. Second, one might drop the perceptual-attunement requirement and reduce role performance to correct action guided by li (ritual propriety), turning the view into a thin deontology of office. I tentatively suggest this collapse is unavoidable, but I want to check whether there is a third option—perhaps a distinction between primary roles (which demand attunement) and secondary institutional roles (which demand only fidelity to procedural norms)—that saves role ethics without making it merely deontological. Please comment on three things: (1) whether my characterization of ren as requiring perceptual attunement to the particular other is defensible within Confucian texts, or whether I am importing a modern relational-phenomenology reading; (2) whether the argument unfairly assumes modern institutions cannot be reformed rather than rejected, and if so how the essay should address that objection; and (3) whether the proposed collapse into procedural deontology is genuinely the only alternative, or whether there is a Confucian resource—perhaps in the concept of shu (reciprocity) applied analogically rather than interpersonally—that I have overlooked. I am especially interested in whether the essay’s framing is conceptually interesting or whether it restates a familiar modernity-versus-tradition complaint in Confucian vocabulary.
