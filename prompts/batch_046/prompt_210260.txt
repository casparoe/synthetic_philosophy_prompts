Consider a stringent reading of Buddhist anatta (no-self) that incorporates what might be called functional discontinuity: the claim that mental states are not only impermanent (anicca) but also lack any diachronic integrating substrate, so that successive cognitive moments are causally linked through dependent origination (paticca-samuppada) yet share no common owner, persisting mental stream, or binding mechanism beyond immediate causal succession. Please describe the most important implications of this combined view for three specific issues. First, how does it affect the coherence of karmic moral accountability—whether within one severely disrupted life (for instance, after extensive dissociative episodes or radical memory reconstruction) or across rebirth—if there is no enduring subject to bear the fruits of action? Second, what becomes of the justification for long-term ethical cultivation and the development of stable virtues (sila) if the agent being cultivated is never the same entity from one moment to the next? Third, can this position avoid collapsing into ethical nihilism or fatalism, or can it be reconstructed into a viable relational ethics grounded purely in moment-to-moment causal interdependence without smuggling in a hidden substrate of personal identity? Address each issue with conceptual precision, and explain whether the view ultimately strengthens or undermines the practical goals of the Buddhist path.
