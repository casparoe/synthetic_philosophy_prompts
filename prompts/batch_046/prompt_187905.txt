You are a philosopher of science with experience delivering workshop-opening addresses to interdisciplinary audiences. Before drafting, briefly brainstorm: (a) how the inside-view/outside-view distinction can be made to threaten each audience's self-image rather than decorate the occasion; and (b) how the niche idea of "recursive modesty"—treating one's own judgment about when the inside view applies as itself belonging to a reference class—prevents the speech from collapsing into generic "listen to both sides" advice.

Write the opening remarks (approximately 800–1000 words, about six minutes spoken) for this year's "Judgment and Uncertainty" workshop. The room is divided: half are philosophers and decision theorists who work on epistemic rationality and reference-class reasoning; half are practicing clinicians, forecasters, and policy analysts who use statistical models but are not trained in formal epistemology, along with a few graduate students and administrators. The philosophical idea—the tension between reasoning from the specific features of one's case (inside view) and reasoning from the statistical behavior of comparable cases (outside view), together with the demand for epistemic modesty—must do real structural work. It should determine what you can and cannot claim: do not praise "balance" or urge both sides to "learn from each other," since that framing already presupposes an unwarranted inside view about the value of dialogue. Instead, show that each group's characteristic strength is exactly what requires the outside-view treatment. For the philosophers, conceptual precision is not an exemption from base-rate neglect; for the practitioners, domain experience is not an automatic license to override the reference class. Center the argument on recursive modesty: the deepest difficulty is not selecting between inside and outside, but recognizing that one's decision about which mode is warranted is itself a judgment that belongs to a reference class and must be subjected to the same scrutiny. This should leave both expert halves of the room uneasy—each should feel the argument applies to them, not merely to the other side—while allowing the non-specialists to grasp the stakes without technical apparatus. Keep the register formal and polished, avoid citations, named works, or bibliographic references, and close by framing the workshop's task as investigating whether, and under what conditions, an inside-view override can ever be justified without begging the question.
