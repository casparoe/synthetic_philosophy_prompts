I retired from microbiology about three years ago and since then I've been trying to get my head round philosophy — it's all a bit overwhelming, but I'm particularly drawn to questions about causation and who or what gets to count as an agent in history.

Last Tuesday I went to a museum in Manchester that had an exhibtion on the so-called Year Without a Summer. The display boards kept describing the Tambora eruption and the cholera bacillus as if they were proper characters in the narrative: one panel claimed Tambora redirected the course of European politics, and another claimed Vibrio cholerae forced the hand of reformers in building London's sewers. The curators weren't just listing causes; they seemed to be giving these non-human things a kind of agancy.

So I'm trying to work out what philosophical commitments that kind of curatorial practice presupposes. Does treating a volcano or a germ as a historical actor commit you to the idea that they have intentions, or can it be made consistent with a purely mechanistic view of nature? And there's something that puzzles me: the same museum's medical wing describes bacteria in strictly biochemical terms, with no narrative role at all, yet here in the history gallery they are given motives and influence. Is the practice quietly endorsing environmental determinism — the idea that climate and disease drive human affairs — or is it compatible with thinking that people still make genuine choices? I'd like someone to reconstruct the implicit theory of agency and causal responsibility that makes this framing coherent, and to say whether it's philosophically consistent or just a muddled metaphor that falls apart when you press it.
