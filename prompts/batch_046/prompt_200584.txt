In my retirement from experimental physics I have taken up philosophy of knowledge, and in my seminar we examine case judgments about belief, evidence, and closure. One of my fellow students, a mathematician turned epistemologist, has rendered consistent verdicts across several scenarios involving historical testimony, skeptical alternatives, and derivation from false premises, but I have not determined whether she applies a principle of epistemic closure, Nozick's tracking conditions, a safety condition, or a prohibition on false intermediate lemmas. Her judgments are these. First, she holds that a historian who examines authentic archival documents knows that a particular treaty was signed in 1848. Second, she denies that the historian knows those same documents have not been retrocausally altered by a future institution that left no present trace. Third, she affirms that the historian knows the logical entailment that if the treaty was signed in 1848 then it was signed before 1900. Fourth, she affirms that the historian knows the treaty was signed before 1900, having derived this belief solely from her knowledge of the signing date together with her grasp of the entailment. Fifth, she holds that a researcher who correctly identifies the treaty's authenticity, despite relying on an expert's false claim about the paper's isotopic profile—where that false claim is inessential because direct paleographic comparison independently confirms the date—nonetheless possesses knowledge of the authenticity. Sixth, she denies knowledge to a researcher who reaches the same true conclusion about authenticity solely through that false isotopic claim, with no independent confirming path.

We will next discuss a case in which a researcher knows, from direct paleographic examination, that manuscript M contains clause C; she recognizes that C entails M was not composed after 1950; she forms the explicit belief that M predates 1950 purely through this deduction, without ever independently investigating the date; and in nearby worlds in which M was composed in 1960 by a forger who inserted C, she would still examine the manuscript, observe C, and derive the same belief. Predict my colleague's verdict on whether the researcher knows M was not composed after 1950. Identify the principle—closure, tracking, safety, or no false lemmas—that best explains her entire pattern, cite the closest analog among her earlier judgments, and specify the further case that would best discriminate between the two leading interpretations. I am particularly interested in whether her tolerance for inessential false premises can be reconciled with a rejection of closure, or whether the two commitments together point uniquely to tracking.
