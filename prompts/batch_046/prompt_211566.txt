Please explain the conceptual issue at the centre of money-pump arguments against diachronic rationality, but do so through a specific variant that standard expositions usually omit. Consider an agent whose bare preference ordering over outcomes is stable, complete, and transitive, yet who is nevertheless vulnerable to being pumped for money across a cyclical sequence of pairwise choices. The twist is that each reversal is driven not by a shift in her preferences, but by a change in her belief about her own future epistemic reliability: she trusts the judgement she will exercise in state A more than that in state B, and this differential self-trust alters the value she assigns to being in one state versus the other at the point of decision. Is this vulnerability genuinely a failure of diachronic preference consistency, or is it a rational form of belief-dependent evaluation being exploited? In your answer, distinguish sharply between (i) dynamic inconsistency of preference, (ii) utility that is indexed to an agent's evidential state, and (iii) the normative claim that diachronic rationality should constrain belief revision as well as preference. I want a conceptual analysis that shows precisely where the pump exerts pressure and whether that pressure demonstrates irrationality rather than rational self-correction.
