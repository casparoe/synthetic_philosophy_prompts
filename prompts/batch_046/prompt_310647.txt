For a graduate seminar in collective rationality (10 students, 90 minutes), design a structured in-class simulation that makes participants feel the force of a judgment-aggregation paradox—specifically, a comparative variant of the discursive dilemma in which the premises are graded pairwise judgments (e.g., “policy A is more equitable than B,” “B more equitable than C,” and “C more equitable than A”). The group must aggregate individual transitive rankings into a collective output using an explicit rule they choose themselves. Include: (1) rules and materials that let each student submit private pairwise comparisons, then reveal them collectively, with a payoff scheme that rewards consistent group outputs (e.g., shared reputation points or a collective prize that is forfeited if the ranking is cyclic); (2) a timeline that forces the group to act—vote, discover the cycle, and then choose whether to revise an input, change the aggregation rule, or accept inconsistency—before any facilitator explanation; (3) a debrief that maps each decision point onto the formal tension between premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation, names the conditions (independence, unanimity, non-dictatorship) that any deterministic rule must violate, and states frankly which formal feature (e.g., logical independence of binary premises) this comparative setup cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator; and (4) an honest note on what will predictably fail the first time (e.g., students treating pairwise judgments as preferences rather than premises, or resolving cycles by deference to the loudest voice) and how to correct it without breaking the experiential logic. The participants should leave having constructed the cycle themselves and having tried, and failed, to escape it by procedural tinkering. Do not name the standard result until the debrief, and do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
