hey so i'm running a workshop for my phil of math reading group — about 8 grad students, 90 mins, mostly logic people but a couple metaphysicians who think Cantor was just misinterpreting formalism. i want them to actually feel the clash between actual infinity and finitist intuition, not just debate it over coffee. could you design a structured in-class game?

split them into "hotel management" (3 people) and "guest registry" (5 people, since tracking cards is work). the hotel is infinite — rooms 1, 2, 3, ... — and each guest gets a unique physical card with their room number. registry can only execute explicit moves: either (a) shift every card up one room (the bus shift to make space), or (b) write down an explicit pairing rule and apply it card-by-card. management requests rearrangments but each move costs "energy" tokens; they start with 25. here's the injected twist: registry can call a "completion checkpoint" at any round. if they can physically point to every card sitting in its correct assigned room right then, management earns a bonus. but if any card is "in transit" from an infinite shift and can't be pointed to, registry loses points. i want the group to discover that accommodating one more guest is always mechanically possible, but that insisting every assignment is completed and verified in a finite number of steps makes the infinite rearrangment impossible to finish — and then they have to argue whether that failure means the infinity isn't "real" or just that verification is limited.

please include roles, rules, a timeline (maybe 10 min setup, 35 min rounds, 25 min debrief, 5 min for mess-ups), and a debrief that maps each round onto Cantor's argument vs. finitist objections (like the claim that infinite processes can't be completed, or that there's no "last" step). also tell me what's going to go wrong the first time — probably people will treat "all rooms" as a finished list, or the metaphysicians will reject the energy-token framing as question-begging, or someone will try to invoke quantum computation as a real analogy (my advisor mentioned that sounds like some quantum-gravity framing but i'm not sure that's a real thing — correct me gently if i'm inventing a reference). be honest about what the game can't reproduce: likely the formal consistency proof, since it's all card-moving, so you'll need to supply that in the debrief.
