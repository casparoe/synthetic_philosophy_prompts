I spent the better part of last evening arguing with a close friend about whether Dennett's Orwellian/Stalinesque framing of the color-phi phenomenon actually exhausts the explanatory space for the apparent retroactive coloring of perceptual experience, and to clarify my own position I drafted the following notes proposing a third option I am tentatively calling chronometric compression. I would be grateful for your comments on whether the distinction holds up conceptually, whether it genuinely avoids collapsing back into one of Dennett's two categories, and whether it sheds any useful light on the broader problem of the timing of conscious experience.

The core of the proposal runs as follows. In the standard color-phi display, a stationary red stimulus is followed by a stationary green stimulus at a nearby location, and subjects report seeing a single object that moves and changes color midway along the trajectory. Dennett frames the options as either Orwellian—subjects genuinely experience the first stimulus as red, but that experience is subsequently revised in memory in light of the second stimulus—or Stalinesque—the first stimulus never enters consciousness at all, and the brain constructs the narrative of motion and color-change entirely from unconscious processing. I want to suggest that both options share an implicit assumption: that there are discrete experiential contents assigned to correct temporal slots, which are then either preserved or altered. Against this, I propose that the perceptual system does not first assign red and green to separate moments and then edit them; rather, it compresses the physical interval between the two stimuli into a single synthesized phenomenological slice, so that the color-transition event is not a revised prior experience or a fabricated addition, but the only temporal location the binding mechanism can assign to the transition. On this view, there is no accurate prior conscious episode that gets overwritten, and there is no missing episode that was censored; instead, the temporal-indexing structure itself produces a unified perceptual object whose features are distributed across physical time but collapsed in experiential time. If this is coherent, I think it may also bear on Libet-style experiments about the timing of experience, since it implies that the time of experience is not a linear mapping onto physical time that can then be corrected, but a structurally reconstructed coordinate system that is already non-isomorphic to the physical sequence.

I am particularly unsure whether chronometric compression is genuinely distinct from a predictive-processing version of the Stalinesque account, in which the brain predicts the trajectory and suppresses the discrete inputs before they reach awareness. Could you comment on whether my idea avoids that collapse, whether it relies on implausible assumptions about temporal binding, and whether the argument about Libet-style timing actually follows? I am not asking for references or a reading list—only an assessment of the conceptual architecture and whether the argument holds together under pressure.
