I was chatting with my mate Dave at the pub after he lost his job, and he started going on about whether he even has a choice about applying for the new one. He laid out this chain that I can't break but it feels like it must break somewhere.

(1) Either Dave applies for the job tomorrow or he doesn't — there's no third option.
(2) Whichever one actually happens was true all along, even yesterday, because either 'Dave applies' or 'Dave doesn't apply' has to match reality.
(3) If something was true yesterday, then it's not up to Dave now whether it happens; the facts are already fixed.
(4) So if he applies, he couldn't have not applied, and if he doesn't, he couldn't have applied.
(5) That means for every little choice — applying for jobs, having a pint, calling his mum — it's already locked in.
(6) But Dave still feels like he's weighing it up, and if (5) is right then feeling like you're weighing it is just an illusion.
(7) So either Dave's feeling is fake (which seems mad) or there's a hole in the argument.

He says the weak spot is probably (3), but he doesn't know what rejecting it costs — like, do we have to say that true statements about tomorrow aren't really true yet? That sounds weird too, because we bet on football results and we're not wrong to say Man City will win if they do. I want to know which step is actually dodgy, and whether fixing it means giving up on the idea that the future is open in some realy sense, or if we can keep both. Also, is there a difference between 'it was always true he would apply' and 'he was forced to apply'? Dave thinks there is, but he can't quite say how without sounding like he's just playing with words.
