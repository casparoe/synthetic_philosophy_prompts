I was arguing with my friends from teh rationalist meetup about how museums should write causation. We're helping my cousin put together an exhibit for Marrow Creek — this tiny town that got hit by a fungal blight in the 1840s that killed the staple crop, then later a volcanic winter froze the river and cut off trade. The draft copy lists The Fungus, The Climate, and Mayor Hale as the three forces that collapsed the local economy. My cousin thinks it's cool to treat germs and weather like they have their own agendas, especially since there's this evolutionary biology idea that parasites literally manipulate host behavior to spread — so maybe the blight was kind of pursuing its own goals?

I feel like that's either giving plants and ash clouds too much personality, or else sliding into that view where weather just runs the show and people don't really matter. But I can't pin down exactly where the line is. If I'm writing teh plaque, is it fair to give the blight and the volcano equal billing with human choices like whether to quarantine or diversify crops? Or is there a middle way that doesn't treat them like actors but also doesn't pretend people were just free-floating minds? I'm not looking for generic museum-advice; I'm trying to see if there's a real philosophical tension here about how much we should blame nature versus people, and I need help seeing the structure without just getting a history lecture.
