I'm writing a dissertation chapter on moral worth and the conditions under which acting for the right reasons generates genuine credit, and in discussing it with my advisor I've become convinced that standard overdetermination cases miss a deeper conceptual problem. I'm interested in situations where an agent's recognition of a right-making feature—say, another person's dignity or urgent need—is causally sustained by an arbitrary, non-moral psychological filter that she does not recognize as arbitrary, even though the filter happens, in this particular instance, to direct her attention to the genuinely right feature. The wrong reason (perhaps self-interest or tribal loyalty) is present but causally secondary, so this is not the usual problem of redundant motives driving the action. Rather, the tension concerns the epistemic architecture of her moral cognition: whether her moral worth is undermined not because a bad motive is efficacious, but because her identification of the good reason depends on a contingent psychological pathway she would not endorse upon reflection.

Please give me a concrete example—vivid but not relying on obscure empirical psychology—that isolates this specific conceptual issue. The case should involve an agent who performs a morally worthy action genuinely motivated by a correct reason, yet her access to that reason is mediated by a hidden, arbitrary heuristic or association (for instance, a perceptual bias, a traumatic echo, or an idiosyncratic resemblance). Make explicit two competing intuitions: first, that moral worth requires not just the right motivational structure but also a non-arbitrary, reflectively endorsable pathway to recognizing the right feature; second, that if the agent is genuinely responsive to the right feature and the wrong motives are causally inert, the contingency of her epistemic access should not diminish her credit. Present the example in one paragraph of no more than 140 words, briefly naming the agent and situation.
