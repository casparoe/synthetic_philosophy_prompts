For several semesters I have used Dennett’s concept of the self as a center of narrative gravity as a case study in my philosophy of mind seminar, and I have observed a persistent stalemate that I suspect is not about the ontology of the self at all. One camp—let us call them the gravitational realists—treats narrative coherence as an emergent explanatory structure: the self is an abstract object analogous to a center of mass, genuinely predictive of behavior because the narrative patterns constrain the cognitive subsystems that generate them, in something like downward causation. The opposing camp—the dissolutionists—grants that narratives are useful predictive heuristics but denies that the center of gravity does any explanatory work that is not already done by the distributed sub-personal mechanisms; for them, invoking the center is either a harmless metaphor or a category mistake that reifies a summary statistic. The debate repeats without progress because both sides share the same empirical literature on confabulation, split-brain integration, and autobiographical memory, yet they read the same studies as supporting opposite conclusions about whether narrative unity is causally efficacious or merely post-hoc descriptive.

My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement is hidden beneath a verbal ambiguity in the term explanation: the realists are operating with a pattern-based, multilevel model in which higher-order structures can be genuinely causal, while the dissolutionists are committed to a mechanistic, micro-physical standard that treats any pattern-level claim as reducible without remainder.
