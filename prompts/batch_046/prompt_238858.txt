I'm on a clinical ethics committee at a mid-size hospital and we keep getting stuck on the same call: a patient wrote an advance directive five or ten years ago saying no artificial nutrition if they have severe dementia, and now they're here with moderate-to-severe dementia, sometimes asking for food, sometimes not recognizing family, and the family is split. We need a reusable decision procedre — something I can actually run in a 20-minute meeting with nurses and a social worker who don't do philosophy — for deciding whether the earlier self's directive still binds us, based on whatever the philosophy of personal identity and diachronic authority actually says that's usable. I want it grounded in the idea that authority isn't just "same body, same rules" but depends on whether there's still a continuous "normative architecture" linking the past decision-maker to the present patient — call it the endorsement-chain condition — but translated into concrete checks.

The deliverable should be a flowchart in words: the questions we ask in order, what counts as a yes/no/maybe for each, where the flow stops with "honor the directive" vs "defer to current expressed wishes" vs "this isn't for us, call the values panel." It needs to flag the steps that are pure judgment calls dressed up as boxes. Then run it on three cases: (1) the classic Alzheimer's patient who smiles at staff but can't understand the concept of a feeding tube; (2) a patient post-stroke who now has a radically different personality — cheerful, present-oriented, no memory of ever valuing autonomy over comfort — but can still say "feed me"; (3) a patient with fluctuating capacity who on Tuesday asks for a feeding tube and on Thursday says "stick to my plan," with family disagreeing about which Tuesday/Thursday counts.

For case 2 especially, I want the procedre to land on an answer the committee probably won't like, and then say why. Also tell me exactly what this procedre can't do — where it will misfire, what kinds of cases it should never be used for, and which distinction (e.g., preference change vs. value rupture) it simplifies away. No bibliographies, no "see Parfit" — just the steps, the tests, and the worked examples. Make it honest about where we're faking precision, and note that we recieve these calls about twice a month so speed matters more than philosophical elegance. Its not always clear where the line is, and thats okay as long as we mark it.
