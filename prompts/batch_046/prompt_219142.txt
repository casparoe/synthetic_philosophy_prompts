My study group has been trying to map Dennett's three stances onto current debates about machine cognition, and one of us constructed an argument that now looks like it proves far too much. Here are the steps.

(1) A pattern is real, in Dennett's sense, iff it supports counterfactual inferences that are robust across perturbations and improves predictive power over lower-level descriptions.

(2) The intentional stance is appropriate exactly when attributing beliefs and desires yields such a real pattern for an entity.

(3) For a sufficiently capable language model, attributing beliefs—say, that it "believes" Paris is in France—yields robust, counterfactual-supporting predictions about its outputs that are not captured by the physical stance of matrix multiplication.

(4) Therefore, by (1) and (2), these attributions describe real patterns in the intentional stance.

(5) Dennett insists that real patterns are stance-independent in existence but stance-relative in description: the same physical substrate can realize patterns visible only from the design or intentional stance.

(6) Consequently, if an LLM's beliefs constitute a real pattern, then the physical stance must be a complete but differently-described account of a world in which those beliefs exist.

(7) Yet the physical account of the LLM contains only numerical weights and activation functions, with no structural isomorphism to propositional attitudes.

(8) So either the intentional patterns are illusory despite their predictive success (denying (2)), or the physical stance is ontologically incomplete (denying physicalism), or human mental states are also illusory since they reduce to neurons.

(9) Hence Dennett's view collapses into either eliminativism about mind or a hidden dualism.

The conclusion feels wrong: I don't think Dennett is a dualist, and I don't want to be an eliminativist about my own beliefs. I'd rather keep (2) and (5) if I have to choose, but I'm willing to pay a price. Where is the slip? Is it an equivocation on "real," a scope error in moving from predictive success to ontological commitment, or something in the inference from (5) to (6)? Name the step, explain why it looked plausible, and price the alternatives: what does it cost to reject (2), (5), or (6) respectively?
