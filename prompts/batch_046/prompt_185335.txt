I am drafting a historical novel in which a post-war tribunal must decide whether statutes enacted under an overthrown regime retain any legal force, and I have become fixated on the Hart-Fuller debate not as an academic abstraction but as a problem about narrative continuity: can a legal system maintain its authority across a rupture if its own rules once licensed acts that the new order regards as monstrous? To explore this, I want you to analyze the well-known West German "grudge informer" cases of the late 1940s and early 1950s—where citizens who had denounced private enemies to Nazi authorities using technically valid statutes were prosecuted after 1945 for actions that had been lawful at the time—through the lens of legal positivism versus natural law. Rather than reciting the standard positions, I ask you to treat Fuller's eight conditions of internal morality not merely as procedural safeguards but as structural requirements for a legal system to tell a coherent, continuous story about itself across regime change, and to examine whether Hart's insistence on the separation of law and morality can be sustained if we understand legal validity as a kind of suspended disbelief rather than a moral endorsement. Specifically: map who the actors were and what options they faced; show how the informer's literal compliance with statute maps onto Hart's thesis and how the post-war court's refusal to recognize that statute as law depends on Fuller's claim that extreme immorality undermines legal existence; and then identify precisely where this framing breaks down—whether the "narrative coherence" metaphor obscures the fact that victims and judges understood themselves as characters in plots they did not author, and whether the episode ultimately supports, undermines, or simply fails to engage the positivist separation thesis in ways the canonical debate never anticipated. I want an honest account of what this lens illuminates and where it forces silence, particularly regarding whether a law's moral horror can ever be separated from the question of its narrative intelligibility.
