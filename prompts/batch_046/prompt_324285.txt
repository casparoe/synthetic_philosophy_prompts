Consider the institutional practice in psychiatric assisted-dying review boards in jurisdictions that permit euthanasia for mental illness, where boards assess requests using quality-of-life metrics that weigh projected future suffering against projected future well-being, yet they systematically reject cases in which suffering is severe but intermittent—even when the cumulative suffering exceeds that of accepted continuous cases—while requiring that suffering be both unbearable and without prospect of improvement. What philosophical theory of value does this evaluative pattern presuppose? Does it assume that pleasure and pain are fully commensurable on a single scale that permits intertemporal summation, or does it implicitly rely on a lexical-threshold view in which suffering above a certain intensity dominates any quantity of pleasure regardless of duration? Reconstruct the implicit axiological framework, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, identify whether the framework is consistent with treating severe intermittent and severe continuous suffering differently, and explain what changes to board procedure would be necessary if pleasure and pain were regarded as genuinely incommensurable rather than differently weighted.
