hey, phd student in moral phil here. i'm prepping a workshop on comparative ethics and cooked up an argument about ranking historical evils that now feels like a reductio ad absurdum, but i can't find the exit.

(1) if we rank atrocity A as worse than B, we need a comparison metric that applies to both.
(2) call the uniqueness-preservation condition: each atrocity's victim-suffering has a qualitative particuarity tied to its historical context, so suffering in A can't be mapped onto suffering in B without moral distortion.
(3) therefore any metric that ranks A over B violates that condition.
(4) so ranking historical atrocities is always obscene, not just risky or insensitive.
(5) but we routinely say the holocaust was worse than kristallnacht, and that feels morally required, not obscene.
(6) if (4) holds, then (5) is also obscene, which means we can't even distinguish genocide from a pogrom without disrespecting victims.
(7) that's clearly wrong.
(8) so one of (1)-(3) has to collapse.

i'd really like to save (2) -- it captures something about non-fungability that quantitative historians miss. but maybe i'm smuggling in an equivocation between "comparison requires identical units" and "comparison requires shared dimensions"? if i reject (1) instead, do i have to accept that all moral comparison is just power politics? what's the least costly exit, and what does giving up (2) actually cost me conceptually?
