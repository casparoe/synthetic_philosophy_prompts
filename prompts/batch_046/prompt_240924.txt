In political theory, the debate over populism's core commitment has stalled for years: critics charge that populists illicitly reify a heterogeneous electorate into a unitary "people" capable of possessing a single will that can be violated, while defenders insist they only demand democratic responsiveness to majority preferences, with no metaphysical agent required. Each side has standard rejoinders, yet neither camp has shifted. My working hypothesis is that the stall is neither a substantive dispute about ontology versus epistemology nor a merely verbal confusion, but a hidden disagreement about the speech-act structure of democratic claims—specifically, whether assertions of "the will of the people" function as constative reports (which can be false) or as performative utterances that constitute the collective agent they invoke, what might be called authoritative indexicals. Diagnose the impasse: are the sides answering different questions—one about representation, one about collective intentionality—under a single heading; is there a concealed empirical question about how group agency actually operates; or is the disagreement bedrock? Use what each side actually says about legitimacy and representation. And tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction between describing and authorizing a collective, a formal analysis of democratic authorization, or an argument that the performative framing collapses back into one of the two standard positions?
