In long-term technological preservation and interstellar messaging protocols—such as those governing self-replicating probe archives, deep-time storage systems, and physical message carriers—designers consistently embed operational logic in geometric and physical structures rather than in linguistic or purely digital formats, and they treat the survival of the machinery’s functional coherence as an independent institutional good, separable from the survival or welfare of the culture that built it. This design posture echoes Von Neumann’s framework for self-replicating automata that might persist and operate long after their creators disappear, yet the philosophical rationale behind it is almost never made explicit in policy documents or engineering standards. What philosophical commitments about value, continuity, and agency does this practice presuppose? Does it require that technology possesses an intrinsic telos, or that functional survival amounts to a form of non-biological flourishing? Reconstruct the implicit theory of technological survival at work, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say whether the same institutional goals could be achieved under a purely instrumental view that treats machinery as disposable tools with no standing of their own. The practice also seems to pull in opposite directions: we preserve and even encourage the machine’s capacity for autonomous replication, which suggests granting it a kind of agency, yet we deliberately design it with no capacity for moral deliberation or suffering, which seems to deny it moral status. Is the practice philosophically consistent, or does it depend on an unarticulated distinction between functional and moral agency? Finally, if the underlying view of technological survival were replaced with one that treats all artifacts as strictly subordinate to present human welfare, what concrete features of these preservation protocols would have to change?
