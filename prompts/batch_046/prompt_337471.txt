I am preparing a colloquium talk defending the thesis that shame—specifically a form I call "reflective exposure shame," defined as a reintegrative emotional practice centered on structured public acknowledgment of moral failure rather than private self-punishment—is structurally superior to guilt for sustaining collective moral repair, and that a liberal democratic culture can deliberately institutionalize shame-practices (for instance, structured accountability rituals) without collapsing into authoritarianism or essentialist identity-fixing, provided these practices are governed by what I term "reversible visibility": the affected party retains authoritative control over the duration, scope, and conditions of their exposure.

The audience is a joint philosophy and moral psychology colloquium. Roughly half are empirical moral psychologists who work on self-conscious emotions and are steeped in the standard guilt/shame taxonomies and in reintegrative shaming theory; they care about measurement, cross-cultural validity, and whether emotional practices can be intentionally designed. Roughly half are normative ethicists with broadly Kantian or liberal commitments who regard shame as heteronomous, dignity-threatening, and prone to moralized social control; they care about autonomy, equal respect, and the risk of majoritarian cruelty. There is also a cultural anthropologist in attendance who studies honor-shame dynamics in non-Western societies and is skeptical of universalizing Western emotional categories.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet. List the six or seven objections I will actually face, ordered from the most damaging to my thesis down to the merely annoying. For each objection, phrase it exactly as that kind of audience member would put it—using their vocabulary (e.g., "heteronomous affect," "reintegrative shaming," "essentialist identity-fixing," "Western emotional imperialism," "designability of affect"). Then give me a concise reply of about two to three minutes, and end each entry with a brief note on where that reply is weakest or where I should concede ground rather than over-defend.

In addition, identify the one question I am hoping nobody asks—the objection that targets the "reversible visibility" mechanism most directly—and advise me on whether to deflect, partially concede, or hold firm, with an eye to preserving the core thesis.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; I am not asking for literature I do not already have at hand.
