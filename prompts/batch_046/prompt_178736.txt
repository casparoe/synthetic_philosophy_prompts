In a seminar I am teaching on Enlightenment practical philosophy, I have been trying to isolate a conceptual tension that is often glossed over in standard accounts of Benjamin Franklin's project of moral perfection. Franklin proposes, in the autobiographical narrative of his self-improvement scheme, to reach moral perfection by cultivating thirteen specific virtues, tracking his failures each day in a private ledger with the rigor of a bookkeeper. The biographical fact of his admitted failure is well known; what is less clear is the conceptual model of moral development this method presupposes. Is Franklin practicing a form of Aristotelian habituation, in which repeated actions gradually reshape one's hexis, or is he engaged in a distinctly modern, instrumentalist regime of self-engineering, treating virtue as a discrete set of behavioral outputs susceptible to optimization? The question becomes sharper when one considers Franklin's own intellectual milieu: he was a deist with pronounced Stoic sympathies, yet the Stoic tradition distinguishes sharply between the prokopton—the one who makes progress toward wisdom—and the teleios, the fully perfected sage, who is essentially never encountered among living mortals. Franklin's language of arriving at moral perfection suggests an achievable endpoint, yet his ledger treats progress as cumulative and quantifiable, which seems closer to a calculus of merit than to the qualitative, transformative conception of character central to both Aristotelian and Stoic ethics. Could you explain the precise conceptual architecture of Franklin's project? In particular, clarify whether his notion of perfection functions as an asymptotic ideal—approachable but structurally unreachable—or as a state he genuinely believed attainable and personally failed to reach. Furthermore, address whether the ledger method implicitly commits him to a form of moral arithmetic—the idea that virtue can be decomposed into measurable increments of failure and success—such that virtues like temperance and industry, which may pull in structurally different directions, are forced onto a single metric of progress. Does this quantification conflict with a qualitative understanding of virtue, or can the two be reconciled within Franklin's framework?
