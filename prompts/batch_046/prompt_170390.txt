As a retired experimental physicist who has recently taken up the study of moral philosophy and pragmatics, I sit on the Governance Committee of the International Collaborative Research Consortium. We are drafting a Communication Integrity Protocol for multinational peer-review panels, and we face a philosophical deadlock that is also practical. Our Dutch members defend direct, unvarnished evaluative language as an epistemic virtue—arguing that clarity serves falsification and that softening criticism corrupts the error-correction mechanism of science—while our Japanese members maintain that indirectness, including strategic ambiguity and face-preserving formulations, is not a moral failure but necessary cooperative scaffolding that sustains the long-term relational conditions under which rigorous criticism can eventually be heard and acted upon. The specific point of dispute concerns so-called white lies in institutional evaluation: may a reviewer knowingly state something literally false—for example, describing a flawed methodology as promising—if the intent is to preserve morale or avoid rupturing collaboration? Some committee members propose formally prohibiting any evaluative utterance that deviates from the speaker's literal belief; others propose that such prohibitions destroy the social infrastructure of knowledge production.

Please draft the formal written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action—whether to mandate directness, protect indirectness, or institute a contextual framework—together with explicit criteria governing when white lies are permissible, prohibited, or structurally unnecessary within our evaluation process. The reasoning should treat the opposing position with full seriousness: specifically, the argument that politeness and strategic ambiguity are not ethical lapses but functional prerequisites for scientific cooperation, and that enforcing literal truth in evaluation risks collapsing the very networks that make correction possible over time. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need an argument that stands on its own reasoning. I will present this without access to external sources, so the memo must be entirely self-sufficient.
