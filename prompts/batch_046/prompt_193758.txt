I've been working through Avicenna's Kitab al-Shifa, specifically the psychology section containing the flying-man thought experiment. In the passage, he asks us to imagine a fully formed adult created in empty space, suspended in air, eyes covered, limbs spread apart and not touching even his own skin—so that he has no sensory experience whatsoever of his body or surroundings. Avicenna claims that even in this state the man would affirm his own existence, though he would deny having any particular limb or bodily part. I understand the standard Cartesian comparison, but I'm trying to see how the argument is supposed to function inside Avicenna's own hylomorphic framework. He treats the result as evidence that the soul is a substance in its own right, yet elsewhere he insists the soul is the form (perfection) of the body, not a separate substance that could exist independently. So my question is: if the flying man strips away every accidental bodily attribute and still affirms himself, what exactly is the object of that self-awareness? If it's not a Cartesian ego, could it be awareness of the substantial form itself—i.e., the soul knowing itself precisely as the form of this body? And if so, why does Avicenna present this as showing that the soul does not depend on the body for its self-knowledge, rather than as showing that the form knows itself even when abstracted from matter? I feel there's a subtle slide between "I exist" and "I exist as form of this body" that the argument exploits, but I can't locate the precise step. Can anyone help me parse the passage so that it supports the soul's substantial independence without collapsing into dualism?
