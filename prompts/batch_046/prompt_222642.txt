I teach a graduate political-philosophy seminar of 16 students in a single 90-minute session. I want them to physically experience the tension at the heart of sortition—specifically, what I call the “silent mandate” problem: election gives representatives a positive reason for being present (they were chosen), while lot removes that reason, and bodies selected by lot often invent substitute rituals of procedural legitimacy that can quietly undermine the randomness that justified their presence. Design a runnable in-class simulation/game in which half the group (8) is selected by a visible lottery and the other half (8) by a vote of the full class; both halves must deliberate the same concrete, emotionally specific policy case—whether to cut one of three small community programs just described in brief, realistic vignettes—and reach a binding decision within 30 minutes. The rules, roles, and stakes should be arranged so that the lot-selected group runs into the absence of positive authorization and begins generating compensatory rituals before anyone names the concept. Use only paper, pens, and a container for drawing lots; the stakes must not involve real money but should include public commitments or social consequences that make the decision feel real (for example, a written justification read aloud to the other half, with a symbolic collective reward at stake).
