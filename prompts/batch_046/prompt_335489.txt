I am designing a 90-minute graduate seminar on social philosophy for about twelve students, and I want them to physically experience why “tight versus loose” is not a single sliding scale but a calibration problem with at least three independent dimensions: rule density, enforcement visibility, and domain specificity. Most introductory treatments collapse these into one axis—strict societies versus permissive ones—so I need an in-class simulation that forces participants to discover the distinctions through conflict rather than through a lecture. I am particularly interested in the case of “domain-split tightness,” where a group is highly regulated in one area (say, resource allocation) and highly permissive in another (say, communicative style), and in the difference between high rule density with hidden enforcement mechanisms versus low density with hyper-visible social monitoring. The activity should run without real money; symbolic stakes—perhaps collective points that determine which group’s “charter” becomes the template for the merged society—are sufficient.

Please design a structured, two-phase classroom game. In Phase One, divide the students into three micro-societies—Alpha, Beta, and Gamma—each operating under a hidden “normative architecture” card that specifies its calibration on the three axes, but do not reveal that the other groups have different architectures. Give them a concrete collaborative task: drafting a governance charter for a hypothetical settlement using a limited pool of token resources, with rules that differ subtly across groups (for example, Alpha has many implicit rules about turn-taking but no designated enforcer; Beta has only three explicit rules but assigns rotating “monitors” who must immediately call out violations; Gamma applies rigid token-accounting rules but imposes no constraints on rhetorical style). In Phase Two, merge all three groups and require them to renegotiate a single charter under unified rules, while each subgroup retains its hidden calibration. The design should ensure that the friction they feel is not about the content of rules but about mismatched expectations regarding how much deviation is tolerable, how visibly correction should occur, and whether strictness in one domain licenses permissiveness in another.

Include precise rules and materials, a timeline for the 90-minute session, the symbolic payoff scheme, and a debrief that maps specific moments of the simulation onto the conceptual dimensions. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time—likely confusion about whether a conflict is substantive or meta-normative, and the risk that one group dominates the merged negotiation—and how to handle it without resolving the tension for them. Be frank about which feature of real-world cultural tightness the activity cannot reproduce (for instance, the historical inertia or institutional path-dependency that makes actual societies resistant to rapid recalibration) and note that the facilitator must supply that limitation in discussion. Write the response in American English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
