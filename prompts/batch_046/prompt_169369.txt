I have been comparing archaeological accounts of the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya lowlands, and Rapa Nui, and I keep noticing that in each case scholars speak of societal 'collapse' despite the fact that populations did not disappear but rather reorganised into smaller-scale settlement patterns with non-monumental economies. This leads me to ask whether 'collapse' operates here as a technical concept with a precise quantitative threshold—perhaps a 70 per cent reduction in large-scale construction coupled with the dissolution of centralised redistribution networks—or whether it is ultimately an aesthetic category applied whenever the kind of society that produces impressive ruins gives way to one that does not. If the latter, then treating the fragmentation of the Hittite imperial administration, which retained diplomatic correspondence and cuneiform literacy, and the abandonment of the Classic Maya ceremonial core, which saw a near-total regional loss of hieroglyphic monument production, as instances of one and the same phenomenon seems to commit a category error. Should we therefore retire 'collapse' as an analytical term, or is there a structural feature—independent of population persistence or the visibility of material culture—that genuinely distinguishes a societal collapse from a mere contraction in scale and institutional complexity?
