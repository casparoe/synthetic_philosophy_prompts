In metaethical debates, defenders of expressivism sometimes resist the Frege-Geach embedding problem by comparing moral sentences to institutional performatives — declaring a marriage valid, calling "strike," or issuing a ruling. On this view, "torture is wrong" functions like an umpire's call: it does not describe a pre-existing fact but registers or creates a normative status, and yet it can sit inside conditionals ("If torture is wrong, then encouraging it is also wrong") without paradox, because the embedding clause operates on the institutional effect rather than on a truth-value. Critics reply that this confuses the speech-act with its propositional content: the umpire's call can be reported, denied, and embedded precisely because there is an underlying record of what was called. Audit the analogy carefully. What in the source domain — authority conditions, rule-books, record-keeping, and counterfactual reasoning about institutional history — maps onto the target domain of moral discourse? Where does the mapping break, particularly regarding whether institutional performatives carry a descriptive trace that moral sentences allegedly lack? Does the analogy smuggle in the very propositional structure that expressivism denies? Can the expressivist consistently distinguish "If torture is wrong" as a hypothetical suspension of attitude from "If the umpire called strike" as a counterfactual about a record? And does the intended conclusion — that logical embedding poses no special difficulty for non-cognitivism — survive once the disanalogies between institutional records and normative attitudes are made explicit? If the analogy collapses, suggest whether a closer analog — perhaps instructions in a formal system or moves in a rule-governed practice — fares better against the embedding test.
