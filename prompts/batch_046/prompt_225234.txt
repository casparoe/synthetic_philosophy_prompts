I'm a phd student in moral phil and action theory, and lately I've been consulting for this neighborhood mediation collective that trains volunteers to step into tenant-landlord and neighbor disputes before things escalate. The board got obsessed with Kant after auditing a seminar, and now they want to know whether a mediator's intervention actually has moral worth — meaning the person is acting from respect for the moral law or duty, not just in accordance with it out of inclination, self-interest, habit, or because it feels good. They need an actual reusable procedure, not a lecture, because supervisors have to evaluate sessions quickly and they're not philosophers.

Write me a decision procedure — a flowchart in words with ordered questions — that a supervisor can run in about fifteen minutes using session notes and a brief post-interview. For each step say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a clear no-moral-worth finding, and where it must hand the call to a human because it's a values judgment not a fact. Build in two specific conceptual moves that go beyond the textbook summary: first, distinguish motive dominance (the moral reason is the strongest one present) from motive purity (no non-moral reasons are present at all), and second, include an audit-paradox check that asks whether knowing they're being evaluated for moral worth changes the mediator's motive structure. The procedure needs to handle mediators who genuinely care about justice but also know this boosts a law-school application; mediators who act purely from obligation with zero emotional pull and actively dislike being thanked; and mediators who have done this for years, act automatically and kindly, but can't articulate any particular reason for this specific case.

Then apply it to three worked cases. Case one: a mediator stops an illegal eviction because she believes the tenant's dignity demands it, but she's also up for a promotion at the collective and knows the board will hear. Case two: a mediator resolves a noise dispute entirely from a sense of obligation, feels nothing either way, and dislikes praise. Case three — and this should be the one where the procedure gives an answer I probably won't like — a mediator with ten years of service who acts automatically, has never sought credit or reward, but genuinely cannot point to any reason for this particular intervention beyond "it needed doing."

Please seperate what's settled about Kantian moral worth from what's genuinely contested — especially whether mixed motives destroy worth or just dilute it, and whether long-habituated right action retains any — and don't present one reading as the consensus. Also be explicit about what the procedure can't do, like detect unconscious motives or fully solve the audit-paradox, and flag any place where a supervisor's own values have to fill the gap.
