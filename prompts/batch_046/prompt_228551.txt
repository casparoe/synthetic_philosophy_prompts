The acquisition committee of the Meridian Art Foundation has decided five proposals. First, we approved acquiring a torn painting that conservators repaired with visible, fully documented techniques. Second, we rejected a painting executed in 2018 with period-appropriate materials and artificial aging, intended to deceive buyers into attributing it to 1890. Third, we rejected an honest pastiche painted in 2023 in the manner of a 19th-century master, clearly signed by its contemporary maker, after an auction house briefly mislrepresented its attribution—the error was corrected before our review. Fourth, we approved acquiring a photographic print made in 2010 by the photographer’s estate, using the original 1975 negative and the photographer’s own darkroom equipment. Fifth, we rejected a holographic reconstruction of a destroyed fresco, built from exhaustive archival measurements and presented with complete transparency about its digital origin.

We now face a proposal to acquire a canvas that Artist Z began in 1950 and left unfinished at death; in 2022 Z’s grandchild completed the work using Z’s preserved original pigments, brushes, and handwritten compositional notes, signing both names. The family endorses the completion, but the formal estate has issued no authorization. Recommend our decision if we want consistency with ourselves, identify the closest prior analog, state the principle we would be adopting either way—physical continuity with the artist’s materials versus formal authorization by the estate—and name the single further case that would distinguish these two rules. Respond in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introductory or concluding text, and take a committed stance with your reasoning explicit.
