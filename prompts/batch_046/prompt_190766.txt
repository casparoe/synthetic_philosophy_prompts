I have been trying to explain Molinism and middle knowledge to a friend who writes fiction, and we began wondering whether the grounding objection to counterfactuals of creaturely freedom could be dramatised rather than merely argued. With that in mind, I would like you to write a short story set in the Library of Unactualised Decisions, an institution maintained by a curator who possesses genuine middle knowledge: for every free agent in every possible circumstance, she knows precisely what they would freely choose, without her knowledge causing or constraining the choice.

The narrative should follow a junior archivist who catalogues entries for worlds that were never actualised, and who stumbles upon a counterfactual describing a specific moral decision made by an agent in circumstances that have never obtained. This discovery raises the grounding problem in concrete form: if neither the agent nor the situation exists, what serves as the truth-maker for the counterfactual? You should weave in the distinction between strong actualisation—where God directly brings about a world containing the circumstance and the free choice—and weak actualisation—where God merely permits the circumstance while leaving the choice free—showing how the archive's cataloguing system implicitly relies on one or the other. The story should remain unresolved; end with the curator arguing that the very absence of a metaphysical ground for these counterfactuals is what safeguards libertarian freedom, since a fully derived or causally explained counterfactual would reduce free choice to a necessary consequence of its conditions. Please write in a restrained, philosophical register, letting the architecture of the library—its branching corridors of possible worlds, its silent chambers for unchosen paths—carry the conceptual weight.
