I sit on the Technology and Labor Transition Committee of the Eastbridge Cooperative Federation, and we must vote Thursday on Project Loom, a pilot proposed by our largest logistics cooperative that would fully automate sorting and packaging using AI-driven robotics. The plan displaces roughly forty percent of current worker-members but promises to fund their retraining and reduce the workweek for retained members through a productivity-linked transition bond. Because our charter prohibits treating living labor as an appendage to dead labor—a formulation drawn from our founding ethical framework—the board is divided. Some members argue that collective ownership dissolves the Marxian critique of automation, since the machinery is not alien capital but self-administered past labor; others insist that competitive market pressure reproduces real subsumption regardless of formal ownership, turning the cooperative into a vehicle for extracting relative surplus value and generating an internal reserve army of displaced members. I need a formal written opinion for circulation before the vote recommending a concrete course of action—approve with conditions, reject, or approve within a modified framework—together with reasoning the dissenting side could actually respond to, and with an honest account of the strongest consideration on the losing side.

Please structure the memo exactly as follows:

1. Executive Summary (approximately 150 words): Open with the recommendation in one sentence, followed by the two or three premises that carry the decision.
2. The Case Through the Marxian Lens (approximately 250 words): Map Project Loom onto Marx’s analysis of machinery—specifically the subordination of living labor to dead labor, the drive to reduce necessary labor time, and the distinction between formal and real subsumption—while treating the cooperative-ownership structure not as an automatic resolution but as a complicating variable. Address whether the “general intellect” embodied in the automated system is captured for collective benefit or remains an external force governing the members.
3. The Strongest Counter-Argument (approximately 200 words): Present the best version of the opposing view, engaging it seriously rather than reducing it to a straw man; identify exactly where the Marxian framework is ambiguous about democratically owned automation.
4. Recommendation and Binding Conditions (approximately 250 words): State the concrete decision with a clear time frame—for example, approval contingent on a six-month democratic review clause, a hard cap on the displacement-to-retention ratio, and an enforceable mechanism by which productivity gains must first reduce working hours before funding external retraining. Specify what must happen if conditions are not met by a given date.
5. What the Episode Shows About the Theory (approximately 150 words): Briefly assess whether this case confirms, complicates, or reveals a gap in applying Marx’s automation analysis to organizational forms that lack capitalist ownership but remain embedded in competitive markets.

The memo should avoid bibliographic references, page citations, requests for further reading, or any quotation marks around passages from real texts. Write it as a document the committee could circulate, debate, and vote on.
