I teach an upper-level seminar on epistemology and philosophy of science and am drafting the final exam. Write one essay question designed to be answered in 90 minutes on Keynes’s account of probability and uncertainty. The question must not be answerable by summarizing the Treatise on Probability or the General Theory; instead, it should force students to apply Keynes’s concept of the weight of an argument and his conditions for the rational comparison of probabilities to a synthetic, concrete case.

Construct the case as follows. A regional planning board must choose between two mutually exclusive flood-defense strategies, Strategy Alpha and Strategy Beta. Three consultants present assessments. Consultant A assigns precise cardinal probabilities—0.75 to catastrophic failure under Alpha and 0.40 under Beta—and claims the evidential basis is extensive, giving the argument high weight. Consultant B refuses to assign any cardinal probabilities, arguing that the climate dynamics are structurally novel and that the evidential basis is too thin to support even an ordinal ranking; B concludes that rational comparison between the strategies is impossible. Consultant C proposes resolving the disagreement by averaging A’s probabilities with B’s refusal, treating B’s position as a uniform distribution over the failure range.

The exam question should ask students to do three things. First, using Keynes’s framework, classify A’s, B’s, and C’s positions with respect to risk, uncertainty, weight, and the comparability of probabilities. Second, explain whether Keynes’s view permits a rational choice between Alpha and Beta despite B’s skepticism, and if so, on what basis. Third, evaluate C’s averaging proposal: identify precisely which Keynesian condition for rational comparison it violates, and explain why treating non-comparability as a uniform distribution misrepresents the epistemic situation.

Provide a model answer of approximately 700–800 words. Then provide a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth (total 20), and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each wrong. The scheme should separate an A answer from a B answer by specifying the exact conceptual move each must contain—particularly the distinction between low weight and genuinely non-comparable probabilities, and the error of substituting a subjective Bayesian uniform prior for Keynesian uncertainty. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
