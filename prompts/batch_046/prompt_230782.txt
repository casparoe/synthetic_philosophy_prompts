After a long argument with a friend about museum restitution, I found myself returning to the opening museum scene in Ryan Coogler’s Black Panther (2018), in which Killmonger examines Wakandan artifacts—including a vibranium axe—displayed in a Western institution and directly challenges the curator’s authority to hold them. My friend insisted that debates over the Benin Bronzes and the Parthenon Marbles are essentially disputes about state sovereignty: either the modern nation-state of origin holds legal-moral title, or the encyclopedic museum retains it in the name of universal access and preservation. I countered that this framing misses a different wrong—the denial of a living culture’s self-determination over the meaning and circulation of its own creations—which is precisely what the scene dramatizes, since Wakanda is not a recognized state and Killmonger acts not as a diplomat but as a claimant on behalf of a culture that refuses mediation by foreign institutions. My friend replied that Wakanda’s monarchy is precisely the problem: using a fictional, authoritarian kingdom to model restitution romanticizes pre-colonial authority and ignores that real-world claims involve democratic governments, competing internal claimants, and the practical chaos of returning thousands of objects to unstable political contexts. I want a philosophical reading of that museum scene that takes both objections seriously. Read the scene through the tension between restitution-as-correction-of-state-sovereignty and restitution-as-restoration-of-cultural-self-determination, asking where the film genuinely illuminates the philosophical distinction—particularly in Killmonger’s refusal to accept the curator’s framing of benevolent preservation—and where it collapses important differences between fictional monarchy and actual post-colonial politics. Does the scene reveal something about the moral status of display, consent, and cultural continuity that standard arguments about the Benin Bronzes and the Parthenon Marbles miss? Or does it merely aestheticize a complex legal and historical problem? Be honest about where the film resists the philosophical reading and where the reading must concede that the drama simplifies what argument alone cannot resolve.
