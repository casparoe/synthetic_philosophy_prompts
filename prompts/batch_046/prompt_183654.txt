After a long argument with a friend about whether my animal-ethics views are actually consistent, I want an honest audit of the following package. Here are my commitments:

(1) A taxonomically invariant suffering-criterion for direct moral status: sentience is necessary and sufficient, and suffering of equal intensity is equally morally significant regardless of species.

(2) Relational amplification rather than replacement: dependency-creating relationships (domestication, sustained care) generate stronger positive duties, but they do not diminish the baseline moral weight of strangers.

(3) Ecosystem integrity as a non-instrumental side-constraint: intact wild ecosystems prohibit large-scale intervention against predation or disease, though this constraint is void in human-engineered environments.

(4) Defeasible cross-species analogical reasoning: in reflective equilibrium, if a practice is impermissible for humans with comparable cognitive and affective capacities, that creates a strong presumptive case against it for animals with those capacities.

(5) Non-linear harm of killing: the wrongness of killing scales with the psychological richness of the life interrupted, while suffering remains linear and invariant; so killing is worse for psychologically complex beings, but this is not species-based.

(6) A strict asymmetry between harming and failing to aid: causing suffering is always wrong, but there is no general obligation to prevent natural suffering, regardless of its aggregate scale.

Please examine which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. Rank the real conflicts by severity, specify the least costly revision for each, and identify which single change to the package would resolve the most tensions without undermining (1). Treat (1) as the fixed point. In particular, I am unsure how (3) and (6) interact with (1) in the context of wild-animal suffering, and whether (5) undermines the invariance claimed in (1).
