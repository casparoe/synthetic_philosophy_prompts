Consider the following argument, which I constructed for a seminar on moral psychology and now find suspicious because it seems to prove far more than its proponents intend.

(1) Virtue ethics requires that virtues are global, cross-situational dispositions: a genuinely courageous or just agent should manifest these qualities across diverse contexts—whether tired or alert, observed or anonymous, under time pressure or not.

(2) The situationist literature reports that correlations between trait ratings and moral behavior across contexts are weak—often below r = .30—while situational variables (framing, proximity, authority cues) account for substantially more variance.

(3) If a proposed psychological kind consistently fails to predict behavior in the very domain it is meant to govern, and if situational explanations do all the predictive work, then that kind should be eliminated rather than retained as an explanatory fiction.

(4) Therefore, global virtues and vices do not exist as real psychological traits.

(5) Now apply the same reasoning locally: if moral evaluation depends on attributing actions to an agent's stable character, and no such global character exists, then moral praise, blame, and education are all unfounded—agents are merely conduits for situational forces.

(6) Moreover, the criterion in (3) does not discriminate between moral and non-moral traits. If weak cross-situational correlation eliminates virtues, it should also eliminate conscientiousness, emotional stability, and other traits that underpin clinical diagnosis and organizational selection.

(7) So accepting (2) and (3) commits us not merely to rejecting virtue ethics, but to a sweeping eliminativism about trait psychology and moral agency that nobody—situationists included—actually endorses.

(8) Hence either (3) is false and we must retain global virtues despite the data, or (3) is true and we must abandon moral responsibility and clinical personality assessment altogether.

I find (2) compelling and would prefer to keep (4)—that global virtues are not psychologically real—but I want to reject the slide into (5) through (8). Where exactly does the reasoning go wrong? Is there an equivocation between global trait and localized dispositional cluster, a scope error in applying (3) to moral responsibility, or an overly stringent criterion for ontological commitment? If I must choose, I would rather preserve the empirical critique of global virtue than save the indispensability of blame, but I want to know what consistency demands and whether a middle position—perhaps accepting domain-specific localized dispositional clusters with high internal consistency but low cross-domain correlation—is available without paying the eliminativist price.
