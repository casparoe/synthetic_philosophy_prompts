You are a professional philosopher with expertise in American pragmatism, philosophy of language, and metaphilosophy. Before adjudicating the dispute below, please take a moment to outline the conceptual stakes: specifically, what antirepresentationalism denies, what pragmatic success is meant to replace correspondence with, and whether internal coherence or predictive failure can be fully accounted for without representational notions. Think step by step, and then proceed to your point-by-point verdict.

Two colleagues in a philosophy graduate seminar—call them Maya and Leo—were arguing about Richard Rorty’s antirepresentationalism and whether it can consistently explain scientific progress. I observed the exchange and remained neutral. Here is what they said.

Maya opened by saying that Rorty treats vocabularies as tools rather than mirrors, yet he still wants to claim that the Darwinian vocabulary is genuinely better than, for example, a creationist one. She argued that this judgment of “better” must mean more predictive and technologically effective, but predictive success is itself a representational achievement: to predict future events is to represent causal structures and future states accurately. So Rorty is smuggling representational accuracy back in through the back door of pragmatic efficacy.

Leo replied that prediction can be cashed out in purely behavioral terms. Organisms that behave in certain ways survive, reproduce, and build technology; there is no need to invoke mental representations of causal structures. Darwinism works because it reshapes our practices in ways that keep us alive and functioning. The standard of “better” is pragmatic efficacy—what helps us cope—not epistemic correspondence.

Maya then raised what she called the dissonance asymmetry. Some vocabularies are abandoned not because they fail practically—certain conspiracy theories, for instance, can be deeply comforting and practically sustaining for a community—but because they generate internal contradictions that feel like errors. She asked how Rorty can explain why internal coherence matters if vocabularies are not trying to match reality. Without representation, coherence becomes merely an aesthetic preference, and Rorty loses the normative authority of logic.

Leo answered that coherence is pragmatically important too. Incoherent vocabularies eventually cause practical friction when they clash with other tools we rely on for collective inquiry and action. The authority of logic is instrumental: it helps us avoid contradictions that disrupt cooperative projects. That instrumental value is sufficient to explain why we reject incoherent vocabularies.

Maya pushed back: reducing coherence to long-term utility collapses the distinction between “this vocabulary is incoherent” and “this vocabulary is inconvenient for our current goals.” She said Rorty thereby loses the ability to claim that logic binds us regardless of our contingent purposes. If logic is just another tool, then an authoritarian culture that finds contradiction useful for social control has no reason—within Rorty’s framework—to abandon it.

Leo closed by saying Rorty would accept exactly that conclusion. The authority of logic is indeed sociological and instrumental, tied to liberal democratic practices of free inquiry. There is no culture-transcendent fact that makes contradiction bad in itself; it is bad for us because of the kind of society we want to maintain and the kind of suffering we want to reduce.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of Maya’s and Leo’s contributions, rate whether it is clearly mistaken, mostly wrong, mostly right, or clearly right, and briefly justify your rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify at least one important issue or distinction that both interlocutors missed—for example, whether antirepresentationalism can distinguish vocabulary-change driven by genuine suffering from vocabulary-change driven by mere fashion, or whether Rorty’s own appeals to “solidarity” depend on a minimal representational commitment about human suffering.
