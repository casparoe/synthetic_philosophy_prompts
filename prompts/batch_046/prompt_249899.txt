I am working through the master argument in my early modern metaphysics course, and our professor has been giving consistent rulings on whether Berkeley's conceivability objection succeeds for particular entities. Her track record so far is: (1) a wooden chair in a dark, unoccupied room—she says the argument succeeds, so the chair cannot exist unperceived; (2) the sound of thunder propagating through a region with no ears or instruments—succeeds, since sound is essentially an idea; (3) the mind of another person who is alone in complete sensory deprivation—fails, because we can consistently conceive an active spirit without ourselves perceiving it; (4) the infinite divine mind that necessarily perceives all archetypes—she treats the argument as inapplicable, since God is not an idea but the perpetual perceiver.

The case she has not addressed involves a specific nucleotide sequence—say, a functional gene fragment from an organism extinct for roughly forty millennia—preserved in permafrost but never transcribed, never interpreted by any cellular machinery, and not currently being observed or conceived by anyone except now, in our discussion. The sequence carries informational content and has a definite structural order, yet it is not being "read" by any mind or biological system.

If she wants to remain consistent with herself, what should her verdict be on whether Berkeley's argument shows this unobserved genetic sequence cannot exist? Which of her four prior rulings is the closest analog—the chair, the thunder, the isolated human mind, or the divine archetype? If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, what principle would she be adopting with each possible choice? Finally, what further case—perhaps involving an artificial or computational system—would best distinguish the competing interpretations of the rule she is actually running?
