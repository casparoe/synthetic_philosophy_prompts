I'm a PhD candidate in history and philosophy of science, and next Thursday I'm defending a chapter thesis in our departmental seminar. I argue that historians of early modern science should abandon the aspiration of pure actors'-category reconstruction and instead adopt what I call reflexive anachronism: the disciplined use of present conceptual distinctions—such as the observation-theory boundary or the experiment-demonstration divide—to frame past scientific practices, provided every anachronistic frame is explicitly flagged and used solely to render conceptual discontinuities visible rather than to assign normative error. I claim this turns unavoidable presentism into a heuristic for tracking conceptual change, not a Whiggish bias.

The audience is precise: eight senior and junior social historians of science committed to the symmetry principle and to reconstructing past knowledge within actors' own schemes; one philosopher of science who regards normative evaluation as outside historiography's jurisdiction; and two graduate students writing on presentism in colonial medicine, who will hear any present-centered framing as an implicit moral judgment. They share a deep suspicion of teleology and of importing philosophical distinctions into historical description.

Please prepare my Q&A sheet. List the objections in the order they will likely arise—first from the social historians, then the philosopher, then the graduate students—phrasing each exactly as that interlocutor would put it, given their commitments. For each objection: say what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest; provide a concise reply suitable for a two-minute exchange; and note whether the reply relies on a distinction the audience will reject. Then identify the single objection I most hope is not raised—the one that targets the contested genealogy of the very presentist distinctions (e.g., observation versus theory-ladenness) that my heuristic requires—and draft a defense of that point, marking its softest spot. Do not include requests for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the prep should stand on the argument's internal logic.
