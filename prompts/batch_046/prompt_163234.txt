I am a novelist composing a manuscript about an archive that preserves every textual artifact through continuous physical and digital transformation, and in developing the narrative I have become fixated on a conceptual problem that seems to extend the classical Ship of Theseus into the ontology of stories, collective authorship, and reconstructed memory. Rather than seeking a survey of existing literature, I want to test whether there is a genuinely unified principle behind my own intuitive verdicts on the following concrete cases, even if that principle forces me to abandon one of them.

First, consider an original manuscript that is destroyed page by page and replaced, sheet by sheet, with transcriptions produced from the imperfect recollections of successive readers; the final bound volume shares no physical material with the original and contains minor narrative deviations, yet I judge it to be the same work, albeit corrupted. Second, imagine that same original is not destroyed but systematically rewritten so that every sentence is replaced by a syntactic equivalent with identical propositional content—no word, phrase, or punctuation mark is retained—yet the plot and characters remain intact; here my intuition reverses: I take this to be a different work that happens to retell the same story. Third, a collective novel is published under a single pseudonym over the course of fifty years, with every founding author gradually replaced by new members who adhere strictly to the stylistic charter and narrative bible established at the outset; the current authors have never met the originators, yet I feel the pseudonym designates a genuinely continuous entity, though I am unsure whether that continuity is grounded in institutional rules rather than in any textual or causal thread. Fourth, a solitary author suffers complete retrograde amnesia, then independently rewrites her earlier novel from scratch without ever consulting it; the result is remarkably similar. I think she has produced a replica, not continued the original—whereas had she retained her memories and revised the text incrementally across decades, I would have called it the same evolving manuscript. Fifth, a digital text is executed on servers that are entirely replaced, then translated into a new programming language that runs identically, then rendered as an audiobook narrated by synthetic voices with no human listener in the chain; I find myself genuinely uncertain at which link in that sequence the original work has ceased to exist, and whether functional equivalence alone can preserve identity.

Is there a coherent account—perhaps centered on causal-historical transmission, intentional-psychological continuity, institutional recognition, or some hybrid—that accommodates all five judgments without collapsing into ad hoc distinctions? If the answer requires sacrificing elegance, which of my verdicts ought I to overturn, and why? Finally, apply the principle you defend to a case I have not considered: the text is translated into a language with no semantic overlap with any human tongue, then reconstructed generations later by an entity that never knew the original, producing a version that preserves the emotional architecture of the narrative but not a single identifiable plot event. Does that reconstruction count as the same work, and what does your answer reveal about the boundary conditions of the principle?
