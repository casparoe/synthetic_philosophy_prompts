I am preparing a three-session seminar for intellectually curious non-specialists who have read the Grand Inquisitor chapter of The Brothers Karamazov but persistently reduce its argument to the commonplace that human beings prefer comfort and security to the burdens of liberty. My aim is not to rehearse the plot but to teach the underlying conceptual architecture: the difference between freedom understood merely as absence of external coercion and freedom understood as the terrifying responsibility of moral self-authorship, and why the Inquisitor claims that genuine happiness requires surrendering not just political liberty but the very capacity to choose evil. Please design the explanatory sequence: in what precise order must the three forces—miracle, mystery, and authority—be introduced in relation to the problem of freedom, so that the Inquisitor's final offer of bread rather than liberty emerges as an internal necessity rather than a rhetorical gesture? For the carrying example, propose one concrete parallel or constructed scenario—perhaps something analogous to a benevolent system of preference-optimisation that eliminates error and suffering by pre-empting choice—that can illuminate the specific burden of self-authorship without collapsing the theological frame into a generic modern dystopia; specify exactly what properties this example must have and what distortions it must avoid.

The participants will almost certainly arrive with three misconceptions: that freedom is purely a political condition, that the Inquisitor is merely cynical about human weakness rather than making a structural claim about conscience, and that Dostoevsky's implicit reply is sentimental rather than philosophical. Indicate at which point in the sequence each misconception should be anticipated and defused. Suggest a single closing question that would reveal whether they have understood the distinction between actively surrendering freedom for happiness and being constitutionally incapable of bearing freedom at all. Finally, state clearly which dimensions of the parable—the historical setting of Seville, the silent response of the Christ-figure, and the elder Zosima's later counter-argument—should be explicitly acknowledged as lying outside the scope of this explanation and deliberately set aside, rather than being glossed over. Please provide the plan in British English.
