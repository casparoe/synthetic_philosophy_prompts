Exam question:
Consider a rescue dilemma modified by a psychological fusion condition. A single rescue vessel can reach either Island A, where one isolated individual (Person X) is stranded, or Island B, where five people (Persons A through E) are stranded, but it cannot reach both. The five on Island B have lived for decades in a collective that practices radical identity fusion: each reports that the destruction of the other four would constitute, for them, a form of self-annihilation no different from their own death, and they do not conceive of themselves as separable welfare-bearers. An interlocutor argues that standard anti-aggregationist objections—of the kind that reject saving the greater number on the grounds that there is no cumulative moral weight across distinct lives—collapse in this case. The interlocutor claims that because the five constitute a single distributed welfare-unit rather than five separate units, the usual objection to counting heads is moot, and we should save the group because it preserves one large network of interdependent well-being rather than one isolated individual. Evaluate this argument. In particular, does the fusion condition actually undermine the anti-aggregationist framework, or does it misapply that framework? And if we grant the fusion premise, what should follow for the rescue decision?

Student answer:
The anti-aggregationist position holds that in forced-choice rescue cases, the fact that five people face death rather than one gives us no additional moral reason to save the larger group. The correct procedure, on this view, is to assign each stranded party an equal claim to rescue and decide by a fair random method, since any weighting by headcount treats lives as fungible commodities rather than as bearers of equal moral standing. Applying this to the fusion case, the interlocutor is right that the five on Island B do not present five independent welfare-bearers. Because their psychological continuity is collectively distributed, saving all five saves only one fused welfare-unit, while saving Person X saves one genuinely separate unit. Therefore the corrected headcount is one-to-one, not five-to-one. From this it follows that the anti-aggregationist prohibition on numerical aggregation no longer applies, because there is no larger number to aggregate. Once aggregation is removed from the picture, we are free to consider other morally relevant differences. The fused group represents a richer network of relational goods and shared projects, so saving them preserves more value overall even though the unit count is equal. Thus the fusion condition does not undermine the anti-aggregationist framework but rather clears the ground for a different kind of comparison—one that favors the group not because of numbers, but because of the density of the welfare-network being preserved. The random-selection procedure is therefore inappropriate here, since the parties are not symmetric: one is a solitary node, the other is a complex relational system with greater internal value.
