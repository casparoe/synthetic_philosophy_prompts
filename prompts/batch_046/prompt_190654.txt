I've been thinking on my own about whether remembering everything actually does justice to the past, especially after I spent an evening in a digital archive where every testimony from a decades-old tragedy was preserved in perfect, unchanging clarity, as if the grief itself had to stay exactly as intense for every future visitor. Next week I'm presenting to the board of a public memorial institution—mostly archivists, museum curators, and community historians—who oversee both a physical site and a massive online repository of survivor accounts and artifacts. They are committed preservationists: they see any reduction in access as a form of erasure, and several have personal stakes in keeping these records permanently visible. My thesis is that institutions have not only a duty to remember but also a duty to design structured forgetting, specifically by allowing digital archives of collective trauma to fade in accessibility over generations rather than remaining frozen in perpetual immediacy. I'm drawing loosely on the distinction Margalit makes between ethical memory and shared memory, and on Rieff's idea that some forms of grief require release rather than eternal preservation, but I'm applying this to institutional design through what I'm calling a "mnemonic half-life"—the claim that remembrance should have an intentional decay rate, not because the events become unimportant, but because the moral relationship a society bears to them ought to change over time.
