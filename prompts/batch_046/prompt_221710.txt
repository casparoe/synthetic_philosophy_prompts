In a recent session on scientific realism, the speaker introduced Karl Popper’s idea of verisimilitude and then kept returning to the line “truth is approached but never possessed,” using it first to characterize scientific progress, then—more aggressively—to dismiss a fellow attendee’s question about whether the BCS Hamiltonian for conventional superconductors should be regarded as literally true.

I want to come to the next meeting with a sharper conceptual toolkit. Could you treat that slogan as hiding a family of distinct precise claims and lay them out as an explicit menu? In particular, please distinguish: (a) an epistemic claim about the permanent inaccessibility of certainty; (b) a semantic claim redefining “truth in science” as proximity rather than correspondence; (c) a normative claim that the aim of inquiry is progressive approximation rather than final correctness; and (d) an ontological claim that reality lacks a stable structure that any theory could fully capture. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, defensible, false, or radical, and specify which—if any—would actually license dismissing the question about the literal truth of the BCS Hamiltonian. I am not asking for citations, bibliographies, or historical exegesis; I need only a conceptual disambiguation I can bring into discussion. Please keep your response to no more than 400 words, presented as a clearly numbered list with brief verdicts for each entry.
