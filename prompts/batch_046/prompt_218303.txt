I teach an upper-level seminar on consciousness -- 10 grad students, 75 minutes, one session. I want them to physically feel the split-brain problem, but not the tired "are there two minds?" version. Instead, inject this niche framing: treat the hemispheres as two committees inside one corporate person. The verbal/narrative committee (LH) holds a monopoly on public justification, but has zero direct access to the other committee's (RH) evidence base; they only get translated reports. The unity of consciousness, on this view, isn't given -- it's a regulatory fiction manufactured when one committee writes the official autobiography for actions it never authored.

Design a runnable in-class game with rules, roles, materials, and a small social-stakes payoff (no real money). Students work in pairs: one is LH (gets text-only rational descriptions of a forced-choice scenario), one is RH (gets only images/emotional cues with no text). They must reach a single unanimous "subject-level" decision and then co-author one first-person explanation of "why I chose this." Use a mediator or restricted communication channel -- maybe they can only exchange one sentence every 2 minutes. After they present, reveal that in the classic literature, the verbal side often confabulates reasons for actions driven by the non-verbal side. Have them identify which sentences in their joint explanation were likely "confabulated" by LH.

Include a timeline, what predictably goes wrong the first time, a debrief that maps game moments onto the philosophical idea, and an honest admission of which phenomenological feature of actual split-brain patients this can't reproduce (e.g., the actual absence of phenomenal access, not just infromation asymmetry). Write casually -- a typo or two is fine. Don't ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Don't quote anyone verbatim.
