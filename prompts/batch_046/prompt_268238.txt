I work as a senior compliance adviser for an international development programme that operates in jurisdictions with strong norms of communal reciprocity and kin-based obligation. We constantly face reports of local partnerships involving gift exchange, preferential hiring of relatives, or the routing of benefits through patronage networks. I need a reusable decision procedure my team can apply in about twenty minutes per case, without requiring legal research or citation of local statutes, to determine whether a specific practice crosses into corruption — not merely whether it violates our Western-derived code of conduct, but whether it corrupts an institutional role by diverting public-purpose goods to private or communal ends.

The procedure should translate philosophical distinctions — corruption as transactional quid pro quo, corruption as institutional diversion, and corruption as the distortion of legitimate reciprocal obligations — into an ordered sequence of practical tests. Please construct it with: the questions to ask in sequence; what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage; explicit stopping points where the procedure returns a clear negative or escalates; points where the question is irreducibly one of values rather than fact, requiring sign-off by a human rather than the checklist; and an honest account of where it will misfire or yield counter-intuitive results.

To keep the framework from collapsing into standard legal categories, inject a conceptual tool that is not part of conventional anti-corruption checklists: a three-layer model of obligation (personal reciprocity, communal solidarity, institutional fiduciary duty) combined with a test for whether an exchange is channel-contaminated — that is, whether an obligation that properly belongs to one layer is being routed through an institutional role to produce an outcome unjustifiable within that layer alone.

Apply the procedure to three worked cases:

1. A qualified engineer recommends her brother-in-law for a short-term technical consultancy. In her community, refusing to advocate for a capable relative when one holds influence is itself regarded as a moral failure, and the brother-in-law is genuinely the best available candidate.

2. Before signing a supply contract, local officials present our field director with an expensive ceremonial gift that, by local protocol, establishes a durable reciprocal bond. Refusing it would be interpreted as hostility and would likely cause the partner to withdraw, yet accepting it creates a personal indebtedness that will shape future negotiations.

3. One case where the procedure delivers an answer I will want to resist — either permitting an exchange that feels deeply wrong, or prohibiting one that seems harmless and culturally necessary — with an explanation of why the procedure produces that result despite the discomfort.

Conclude by stating clearly what this procedure cannot determine, particularly regarding the universality of Western anti-corruption norms versus context-sensitive evaluation, and flag every step that depends on a normative premise a reasonable practitioner could reject without irrationality. Write in British English, keep the tone formal, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
