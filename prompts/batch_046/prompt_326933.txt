You chair a hospital clinical-ethics committee that reviews cases twice weekly in which staff describe being torn between obligations. You need a reusable fifteen-minute decision procedure to determine whether a presented case is a genuine moral dilemma — defined here as a conflict in which at least two obligations genuinely apply, neither is hierarchically overridden, and at least one retains normative force (a residual claim) regardless of which course is taken — or only an apparent dilemma resolvable by empirical clarification, reconceptualization, or hierarchical ranking. The procedure must specify an ordered sequence of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, termination points for "not genuine" and "genuine — judgment required," explicit separation of factual investigation from value assessment, and a reversibility check (whether the conflict persists if the agents' roles are reversed) to distinguish structural conflict from contingent preference. Apply it to: (A) a competent patient refusing a low-risk, life-saving transfusion on religious grounds versus the duty to preserve life; (B) a legal proxy demanding continued ventilation for a patient with irreversible loss of consciousness, where staff invoke dignity; and (C) a single available ICU bed with two patients of equal clinical priority but different social roles (a parent of young children versus an elderly researcher with no dependents). Include one application where the procedure yields a counterintuitive result, flag every step where judgment is irreducible, and state clearly what the procedure cannot resolve. Separate what is philosophically settled about the possibility of genuine moral dilemmas from what specialists genuinely contest — including whether residual obligations are coherent, whether all apparent conflicts dissolve under correct analysis, and whether the reversibility check is defensible — and do not present any contested view as the consensus.
