In my environmental history course we have encountered a persistent disagreement about whether volcanoes, climate shifts, and pathogens should be treated as 'historical actors' rather than mere background conditions. Proponents point to cases such as the 1815 Tambora eruption, the anomalous climate of 1816, and the subsequent spread of cholera to argue that these forces actively reshape societies and should be granted explanatory agency alongside human institutions. Critics respond that this either stretches the concept of agency beyond recognition—since these forces lack intentions—or risks environmental determinism by reducing human choices to passive reactions to natural events. Despite widespread agreement that such forces have profound causal effects, the debate seems stalled: each side repeats familiar objections without convergence.

Is the impasse primarily verbal, with the two camps using 'agency' in incompatible senses—one causal, one intentional—or is it substantive, turning on different standards for what counts as a historical explanation? Might they in fact be answering different questions, one about narrative causation and another about moral responsibility, under the shared label of 'historical actors'? Please diagnose the structure of this disagreement by referring to what each side actually says, and indicate what kind of progress—perhaps a precise conceptual distinction, a framework for multi-causal explanation, or a change of question—would be appropriate. I am particularly interested in whether grouping climate disruptions and germ transmission together as a single class of 'non-human actors' obscures important differences in their causal mechanisms.
