Write the opening remarks for an interdisciplinary retirement workshop honoring Professor Elena Voss, titled “Looping Back: Self-Reference from Logic to Mind.” The audience is divided: logicians and philosophers of mathematics who know Gödel’s incompleteness theorems and Hofstadter’s analogy in Gödel, Escher, Bach are seated beside family members and cognitive-science graduate students who are not. Voss spent her career arguing that the Gödelian move—constructing a formal sentence that asserts its own unprovability—offers a genuine structural analogy for self-awareness, yet she always insisted that analogies are not identities: consciousness is not “a formal system containing its own Gödel sentence,” though any model of mind that lacks self-reference is structurally incomplete as an account of knowing oneself. She left one instruction for this occasion: the remarks must honor her life’s work without either reducing it to a decorative metaphor or claiming that minds literally execute diagonalization. In about four minutes, explain why that very distinction does real philosophical work—shaping what counts as an explanation of self-knowledge and what collapses into category error—using language that lands for the experts without excluding the family. Let the constraint govern what you can and cannot say: do not offer false comfort by treating mind as a theorem-prover, and do not abandon the analogy by treating self-reference as irrelevant to consciousness.
