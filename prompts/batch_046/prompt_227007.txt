You are a professional philosopher specializing in practical reason, decision theory, and intergenerational ethics. Before giving your final answer, take a few moments to brainstorm the main conceptual distinctions that could be in play—pure time preference, risk and uncertainty, personal identity over time, contractual or promissory obligation, and capital-productivity discounting—and consider how they might come apart in edge cases. Then think step by step and be precise.

Here are my intuitive verdicts about when and how future goods and harms should be weighted, and I want to know whether they trace back to one principle or to several that I am conflating.

1. I think it is rational for me to prefer $100 today over $105 in one year, just because the benefit is now rather than later. Call this pure time preference.
2. But I think it is irrational for me to prefer $100 today over $10,000 in thirty years on that same ground; that feels like a failure of long-range self-care rather than rational patience.
3. If I make a binding commitment to myself—say, an automatic savings contract—I think breaking it for present consumption is wrong, even though my present self still prefers the cash. Yet if I never made such a commitment and simply change my mind, that seems permissible.
4. I think a government should apply a near-zero pure discount rate to climate damages falling on people 200 years from now, because those people are just as real. But I also think that same government is right to spend less today on preventing a risk that only materializes in 500 years than on one in 50 years, even if the probabilities and magnitudes are held constant.
5. I think my concern for my own suffering at age 85 should not be discounted relative to my suffering at age 35, because it is still me. Yet I think it is reasonable to invest less in preventing a harm that might affect me at age 120, not because the suffering is less severe, but because I probably will not survive to experience it.
6. Now consider an injected case: imagine a technology that preserves full psychological continuity—memories, character, projects—by sequentially transferring consciousness into a biologically new body every 50 years. If I am the recipient of such transfers, should I apply pure time preference to harms that fall in the "body-period" 150 years from now? The calendar gap is large, yet psychological continuity remains unbroken. I feel I should not discount them heavily, yet I also feel the calendar distance should matter somewhat.

Is there a single principle—perhaps built on identity continuity, contractual obligation, or epistemic uncertainty—that captures all six verdicts? Or do I need separate principles for personal, political, and technologically mediated intertemporal choice? For each principle you propose, state it precisely, identify the case that motivates each clause, and give its verdict on the cases I have not listed. Then be honest: which of my six verdicts does the principle force me to abandon, and why?

Finally, apply your best principle to this unconsidered case: a community can vote today to bind its descendants 300 years hence to maintain a carbon-negative economy, but those descendants will have different values and no biological relation to current voters. Should the present community apply pure time preference to the descendants' welfare when deciding whether to impose the binding policy?
