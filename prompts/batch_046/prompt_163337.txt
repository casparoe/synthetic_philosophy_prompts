Exam Question 3: In the literature on free will, Frankfurt’s counterexamples are widely taken to undermine the Principle of Alternative Possibilities (PAP). Consider a modified scenario—call it the Gradual Intervention Case. Dr. Chen, a research scientist, must decide whether to sign a controversial data-sharing agreement. Unbeknownst to her, a hidden neural monitor tracks her deliberation. Rather than blocking a decision outright, the device applies progressive micro-stimulations that suppress dissenting first-order impulses while leaving her explicit reasoning and reportable mental states fully intact. The mechanism is calibrated continuously, not binarily, and in the actual sequence Chen never hesitates, so the monitor never activates. Analyze whether this scenario successfully undermines PAP. Your answer should address (i) whether Chen retains alternative possibilities in any relevant sense, (ii) whether Frankfurt’s hierarchical model of identification explains her moral responsibility, and (iii) whether the so-called flicker-of-freedom objection applies here.

Treat subtle missteps—such as conflations between levels of volition, misattributed objections, or unwarranted generalizations—as significant even when the overall response looks competent. Student Answer: Frankfurt’s counterexamples show that PAP is false: an agent can be responsible even if she could not have done otherwise. In the Gradual Intervention Case, Chen signs the agreement without ever hesitating, so the hidden monitor never activates. Because there is no actual intervention, her decision follows a single causal path, which means she lacks alternative possibilities in the relevant sense. Therefore PAP is false for Chen, yet she remains responsible.

Regarding the hierarchical model, Frankfurt holds that freedom of the will requires harmony between second-order volitions and first-order desires. Since the micro-stimulations only suppress dissenting impulses without disrupting Chen’s conscious reasoning, her second-order identification with signing remains intact. Thus the hierarchical model explains her responsibility: she genuinely wants to want to sign.

As for the flicker of freedom, Frankfurt himself argued that a valid counterexample must eliminate every trace of an alternative. Here the intervention is gradual rather than binary, so there are no discrete decision points at which Chen could have done otherwise—not even a minimal flicker. Consequently the flicker objection does not apply, and the case stands as a clean counterexample. From this we can conclude that determinism is compatible with moral responsibility generally, because if responsibility survives the absence of alternatives in this engineered scenario, it will survive in deterministic worlds as well.
