hey so i'm trying to work out my own stance on whether comparing nations across history ever actually gives us causal knowledge about wealth, or if it's all just storytelling with regression lines. here are my gut reactions to a bunch of cases, and they feel kinda inconsistent to me.

first, north vs south korea after 1953: same people, same mountains, split by war and politics. one gets rich, one doesn't. to me this is about as close to a controlled experiment as history allows, and i think we genuinely can say institutions and policy drove the divergence. geography and culture are basically held constant.

second, east vs west germany after 1949: same logic, same verdict. the wall gives us the treatment.

third, botswana vs zambia since independence: both landlocked, both sitting on diamonds, both poor british colonies at the start. one builds democratic institutions and gets rich, the other gets captured by a resource curse. i think here too we can isolate institutions.

fourth, haiti vs dominican republic on hispaniola: same island, same climate, different outcomes. but honestly i don't think this comparison works, even though it looks like korea. the colonial split wasn't exogenous—france took the west partly because there was already a brutal plantation slave economy there, spain took the east with different settlement patterns. so the "treatment" (colonial power) was assigned based on pre-existing economic structure. verdict: we can't cleanly infer causality here.

fifth, the needham question / great divergence—why britain and not china around 1800. i think this is just badly posed. coal location, colonial access, legal traditions, demographic patterns, all moving at once over centuries. there's no stable counterfactual "china with british institutions" that makes sense. verdict: we don't know, and probably can't.

sixth, but then compare japan and qing china both facing western gunboats in the mid-1800s. shared external shock, different institutional responses—japan centralizes reform, qing fragments. i actually think this comparison IS informative about institutional adaptability, even though it's east vs west.

seventh, the acemoglu-johnson-robinson reversal of fortune using settler mortality as an instrument for institutions. i buy this. clever identification, lets us claim long-run causality without relying on a single split.

so my pattern is: i accept natural experiments when the split is exogenous to economic potential (korea, germany, maybe botswana-zambia), i reject them when assignment was endogenous (hispaniola), i reject long-divergence comparisons with no shared shock (britain/china), but i accept shared-shock comparisons (japan/qing) and statistical instruments (ajr).

the problem is that korea and hispaniola both involve "same geography, different politics" but i treat them differently, and japan/qing involves comparing different societies over time just like britain/china but i treat them differently too. is there one principle that saves all seven verdicts? maybe something about exogenous assignment plus homogeneous pre-treatment conditions? but then why does japan/qing work—those societies weren't homogeneous at all. or should i just overturn one of these verdicts?

also, what would your principle say about comparing the economic trajectories of the two sides of baarle-nassau / baarle-hertog after the 1995 treaty finally locked in the enclaves, where borders literally run through houses and families? and about using synthetic control methods to build a "counterfactual korea" from other east asian economies?

please lay out the principle precisely, with which clause comes from which case, what it predicts for these two new ones, and be honest about which of my verdicts it has to throw out if any. and if there's more than one principle that fits, say so and tell me which one you'd pick.
