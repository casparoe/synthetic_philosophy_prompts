Construct a thought experiment in which a rational agent faces a medical treatment decision where she knows the objective physical chance of recovery is 0.7, but she also possesses epidemiological data showing that patients who form beliefs via her specific reasoning method—assessing side-effect narratives rather than statistics—recover at a rate of 0.4, with no causal link between reasoning method and physiology. The case must be ordinary (no demons, no perfect predictors, no exotic physics), and must be engineered so that the unrestricted Principal Principle fixes credence at 0.7, the admissible-evidence restricted version revises downward but not fully to 0.4 because objective chance retains weight, and an evidentialist who rejects chance-deference fixes credence at 0.4. After presenting the scenario in one paragraph, walk through the reasoning for each of the three positions, state exactly which feature—the non-causal correlation between belief-formation type and outcome in the reference class—drives the split, and explain how a defender of the restricted view would attack the case by arguing the epidemiological data is either admissible or inadmissible, and what each reclassification does to the verdict. Finish by noting what structural feature, if removed, collapses the disagreement back into a standard chance-deference problem.
