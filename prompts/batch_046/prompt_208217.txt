I've been working through Jackson's knowledge argument—the case of Mary, the neuroscientist who knows all physical facts about color vision but has only experienced black and white. Most discussions treat her release into color as delivering new propositional knowledge, but I'm drawn to a more specific reading: Mary's deficit isn't merely missing data, but a missing mode of presentation. Even if she could, from her physical theory, predict exactly which neural states she would enter upon seeing red, she still wouldn't possess the first-person acquaintance with the qualitative character—the subjective feel of red—that restructures her conceptual economy from the inside. The argument, on this reading, doesn't depend on a failure of physical prediction but on the claim that phenomenal acquaintance is a distinct cognitive achievement, not capturable by third-person descriptions however complete.

What would genuinely count against this qualified version? I want the defeaters ranked: which empirical findings—say, about blindsight, or about the neural correlates of qualitative experience being fully transparent to introspection—would undermine the claim that acquaintance adds a non-predictive structure? Which philosophical moves—such as the phenomenal concept strategy applied rigorously, or the claim that Mary's new knowledge is just old knowledge under an old mode—would need to succeed, and do they actually address the mode-of-presentation point or miss it? Also, is there a risk that this version has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if any physical explanation of Mary's reaction is compatible with her still lacking acquaintance, does the view risk becoming empty? I'd like an honest assessment of whether this is a live vulnerability or a legitimate insulation of the core claim.
