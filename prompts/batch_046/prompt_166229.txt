The disagreement I observed was between two technically sharp colleagues, neither of them philosophers, about whether Darwin’s theory of natural selection and Turing’s theory of computation represent the same kind of conceptual inversion: the idea that competence can emerge without comprehension.

Colleague A (Alex) opened by arguing that Darwin and Turing both overturned the same naive intuition: wherever there is well-adapted or intelligent behavior, there must be a comprehending agent behind it. Darwin showed that biological adaptation—eyes, camouflage, metabolic efficiency—arises through blind differential reproduction without any understanding of the outcome. Turing showed that a machine can solve problems, prove theorems, or play chess without understanding anything about the domain it operates in. So both are strange inversions: competence without comprehension.

Colleague B (Blake) replied that this collapses two structurally different cases. In Darwin’s case, there is no internal system that represents or selects for traits; selection is only a statistical summary of which organisms leave more offspring. There is no designer, and there is also no internal mechanism governed by syntactic rules. In Turing’s case, the machine executes precise internal rules—symbol transformations that are fully deterministic and syntactically tracked at the token level, even if the machine lacks semantic comprehension. The programmer supplies the design, and the machine supplies the execution. So Darwin eliminates the designer entirely and operates without internal representation, whereas Turing relocates design to the programmer and relies on internal syntactic representation. They are not parallel inversions; one is metaphysical (the absence of teleology), the other epistemological (behavior without mental states).

Alex pushed back: a universal Turing machine does not understand the program it is running any more than nature understands the organisms it produces. The machine shuffles symbols according to transition rules; it has no grasp of whether it is computing taxes or composing poetry. The programmer’s comprehension remains outside the system, just as there is no comprehending selector inside evolution. So at the system level—the entity that displays the high-level competence—both lack internal comprehension, and the parallel holds.

Blake then said that Alex is conflating absence of semantic comprehension with absence of representation altogether. Evolutionary processes do not contain representations; they are purely causal. Computers do contain representations—symbols that stand for values, addresses, and operations. That difference matters: Turing’s inversion is about the gap between syntax and semantics, not about whether a designer exists. Blake also pointed out an asymmetry Alex ignored: evolutionary algorithms are literally implemented inside computers. Darwinian selection can run as a subprocess on a Turing architecture, but a universal computer cannot be a subprocess of blind evolutionary selection without being pre-designed. So the relationship is hierarchical, not analogical.

Alex answered that this hierarchical point actually supports his view: if Darwin can be embedded within Turing, that shows the two inversions are compatible members of the same family—mechanisms that generate high-level competence without requiring the generating mechanism itself to understand the result. He also argued that both serve the same argumentative function: Darwin undermines the inference from biological design to a designer, and Turing undermines the inference that because a computer merely follows rules, it cannot think. Both separate the appearance of competence from the presence of a comprehending mind.

Blake concluded that functional analogy is not structural identity, and that calling both strange inversions of reasoning glosses over a crucial difference. Darwin’s process has no internal state that tracks goals, while Turing’s machine has internal states that precisely track syntactic goals set by the programmer. The inversion in Darwin is that order comes from disorder; the inversion in Turing is that meaningful behavior comes from meaningless symbol-shuffling. These are different enough that treating them as one idea risks misunderstanding both.
