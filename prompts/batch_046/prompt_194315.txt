Question: In the absent-mindded driver problem, the driver needs to take Exit B (the second exit) to get home; Exit A (the first) leads to a dangerous area. He can't tell the exits apart, and if he takes Exit A he forgets he did so. Payoff for B is +5, for A is -5, for missing B is 0, and he can pre-commit to a plan before leaving. First, explain why evidential decision theory (EDT) seems to reccomend continuing past both exits, and why causal decision theory (CDT) seems to reccomend taking the first exit you reach. Then evaluate a proposed fix: the driver installs a cheap GPS voice that says "this is Exit A" or "this is Exit B" with 90% accuracy. Does adding this device change the EDT analysis, the CDT analysis, both, or neither? Be specific about what changes and what stays the same.

Student answer:
EDT evaluates strategies by the evidence they provide about your location. If you adopt the strategy "take the exit", that correlates with being at Exit A, since you'd only reach Exit B if you continued past A. So EDT assigns lower expected utility to taking and recommends continuing, which gives 0 rather than the -5 you'd risk at A. CDT evaluates causal effects locally: at Exit A, taking causes -5 and continuing lets you reach B; at Exit B, taking causes +5 and continuing misses it. Because CDT allows planning, it recommends the plan "continue at A, take at B". But since the driver is absent-mindded he can't condition his action on location, so CDT defaults to taking the first exit you reach—the causal benefit at B outweighs the risk at A when you average over your 50/50 prior. With the GPS, EDT now gets direct evidence about location, so it can reccomend taking only when the voice says B, which solves it's problem. CDT doesn't care about evidence, only about causal effects, so the GPS doesn't change CDT's analysis at all—you still take if you're at B and continue if at A, except now you happen to know which is which, but that knowledge isn't part of the causal decision rule. So only EDT is affected.
