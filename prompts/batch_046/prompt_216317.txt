I have been trying to determine whether a purely tranquilist axiology—according to which only the absence of craving and suffering carries normative weight, whilst positive pleasure or achievement counts for nothing—can survive some intuitive comparisons I have been making. Here are my verdicts.

Case A: A being in deep, dreamless sleep with no mental content whatsoever, hence experiencing neither suffering nor craving, seems to occupy a maximally good state—better, at least, than someone who is mildly contented yet constantly desires more.

Case B: A person who has eliminated all first-order cravings through rigorous mental discipline, but who retains a subtle second-order anxiety about the possibility of relapse—what I am inclined to call meta-craving—seems worse off than the being in Case A, despite the identity of their first-order mental states. The lingering vigilance feels like a form of suffering, or at least like a contamination that pure absence ought not to include.

Case C: A world in which every inhabitant experiences intense creative joy and deep interpersonal love, but also a perpetual low-grade longing for these experiences to continue, strikes me as genuinely preferable to a world of universal tranquil non-existence, despite the ubiquitous presence of craving. This judgement appears to violate tranquilism outright.

Case D: Two individuals both suffer no physical pain. One achieves this through total sensory withdrawal—living in prolonged isolation with minimal stimulation. The other achieves it through active engagement in relieving others' suffering, which demands constant mental effort, occasional frustration, and a sustained desire for justice. I feel that the second life is more admirable and perhaps better overall, yet it clearly contains far more craving and agitation than the first.

My verdicts therefore pull in opposing directions: I want to maintain that the absence of craving is sufficient for the best possible state (A); that certain modes of absence are corrupted by meta-level tension (B); that craving can be compatible with, and perhaps even constitutive of, overall value (C); and that active striving can be preferable to passive tranquility (D). Is there a single principle—perhaps one that distinguishes first-order from second-order mental states, or that weights the manner in which absence is achieved differently—that delivers all four judgements? Or must one be abandoned?

Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause addresses which case, predict what it implies for a scenario I have not listed—say, a being that experiences pure aesthetic rapture with absolutely no desire for its continuation or repetition—and be explicit about which of my four verdicts it cannot accommodate without distortion.
