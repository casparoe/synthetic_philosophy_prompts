I've been discussing James's essay The Will to Believe and Clifford's essay The Ethics of Belief with colleagues in a seminar on epistemic normativity, and we've reached an impasse that neither canonical text seems designed to resolve. The standard framing treats the dispute as a clash between individual, existential belief-formation—choosing whether to believe in God or in moral progress when evidence runs out—and Clifford's universal evidentialist prohibition. But I'm interested in whether James's license for belief in live, forced, and momentous options can be extended coherently to collective, institutionally embedded beliefs—such as a democratic legislature's working presupposition that its authority derives from popular sovereignty—without licensing what we might call institutional epistemic closure. Clifford, by contrast, might seem to demand that every legislative premise be proportioned to available evidence, which raises the question of whether his view paralyzes democratic deliberation under conditions of unavoidable uncertainty.

Please give me your own credence—on a scale from 0 to 1—that James's framework can be applied to institutional belief-formation without collapsing into a partisan license for belief, decomposed as follows: first, the probability that James intended his argument to apply beyond individual psychology to collective agents; second, the probability that institutional beliefs can genuinely satisfy the live, forced, and momentous conditions without being strategically manipulable; third, the probability that a strict Cliffordian evidentialism applied to institutions would function as a purifying constraint rather than a paralyzing one; and fourth, the interaction term or combination rule you use to integrate these. For each component, provide the number and one line of justification. Then identify which component you are least confident in, and specify what kind of concrete institutional case—perhaps a constitutional court reasoning about democratic legitimacy under radical factual uncertainty—would most likely shift your number up or down.
