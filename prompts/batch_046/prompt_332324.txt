I am presenting a paper next month at an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-science colloquium defending a non-standard resolution of the Pasadena paradox. My central claim is that the paradox does not merely illustrate an exotic failure of expected-utility maximization, but reveals a structural gap in the von Neumann-Morgenstern framework when payoffs generate genuinely undefined expectation values through an oscillating weight structure rather than monotonic divergence. I argue that rational agents facing such games should not resort to arbitrary truncation or standard ambiguity-aversion heuristics, but should instead apply what I call a discontinuity-sensitive refusal principle: if a payoff sequence produces an undefined expectation due to non-convergent alternating weights, the agent treats the decision problem as categorically unplayable rather than as a high-risk gamble with an unknown value. I was developing this with a colleague in formal epistemology, and she pointed out that I keep sliding between a descriptive claim about rational preference and a normative claim about what agents ought to do, so I need to be sharp about that distinction in the defense.

The audience is precisely this: a philosophy of science and decision-theory colloquium composed mainly of formal epistemologists who work on Bayesian rational-choice models, two economists who specialize in ambiguity and risk but view philosophical framing with suspicion, and one philosopher of mathematics who is deeply skeptical of appeals to undefined values in applied contexts. They know the St. Petersburg literature well, but none are specialists in infinite-game formalization. They care intensely about whether a philosophical distinction can be given formal teeth, whether my proposal can be operationalized without hidden parameters, and whether I am illicitly importing normative conclusions into descriptive rationality theory. They will likely conflate my refusal principle with standard maximin strategies or with simple truncation, and I need objections phrased in their own vocabularies — the epistemologists in terms of representational coherence and Dutch-book vulnerability, the economists in terms of revealed preference and continuity violations, the mathematician in terms of whether undefined expectation is a well-formed concept at all.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. Order the objections by how damaging they are likely to be in this room, with the most destructive first. For each, phrase it exactly as that audience member would put it — include at least one from each of the three groups. For every objection, provide a concise reply I can deliver in about two minutes, note explicitly where that reply is weakest, and indicate what I should concede upfront rather than defend. I also want you to identify the single objection I am hoping nobody asks — likely the charge that my discontinuity-sensitive ordering either collapses into a disguised maximax rule or violates continuity axioms so severely that it cannot be approximated in any continuous decision problem — and sketch how I should respond without overclaiming. Finally, flag the softest point in my own argument: the move from the expectation being undefined to this licensing categorical refusal requires an intermediate bridge principle that I have not fully formalized, and I want to know how to acknowledge that gap honestly without letting the audience treat it as a fatal flaw.
