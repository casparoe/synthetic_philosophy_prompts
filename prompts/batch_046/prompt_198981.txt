Here are my verdicts on four decision problems that share one formal feature: the standard real-valued expectation of monetary payoff is undefined, because at least one tail diverges and the series fails to converge absolutely. I want to know whether a single principle explains the pattern, or whether I must abandon one judgment.

Case 1: The symmetric Pasadena game. A fair coin is flipped until it lands tails. If tails occurs on an odd-numbered flip, the payoff is 2^n dollars; if on an even-numbered flip, the payoff is -2^n dollars. The positive and negative contributions both diverge. Verdict: I feel genuinely ambivalent. I would play for free, but I would not pay a substantial entry fee, and I do not think there is a determinate fact about whether acceptance is rational.

Case 2: The weighted divergence game. The same flipping rule applies, but an odd flip n yields +4^n dollars, while an even flip n yields -2^n dollars. Both tails diverge, yet the positive tail grows faster than the negative one. Verdict: Despite the formal undefinedness, I clearly want to play, and I would pay a moderate entry fee.

Case 3: The nested-branch gamble. With probability one-third each, I face either the symmetric Pasadena game played for real money; the identical game played only for non-monetary points; or a certain payout of $50. Verdict: I am willing to enter the overall gamble when the undefined region involves only points or is diluted by the safe branch, but I want to opt out specifically when real money is at risk in Case 1. So my verdict depends on the stakes inside the undefined region, not merely on the formal expectation.

Case 4: The exit-option variant. I face the symmetric Pasadena game, but before each flip I may pay $1 to stop and receive $0. Verdict: The presence of an exit rule makes me willing to enter initially, whereas without it I am not; yet I have no precise stopping rule in mind. Thus a structural feature that does not resolve the underlying undefined expectation still reverses my entry verdict.

Is there a coherent decision principle—perhaps involving symmetry, dominance, bounded risk, or structural transparency—that delivers all four judgments? If the best principle saves three but forces me to overturn one, state which one and why. Then apply your principle to a case I have not listed: the symmetric Pasadena game evaluated with a logarithmic utility function over initial wealth, where the utility change from gaining or losing 2^n is proportional to n (so the expectation may converge). Would your principle recommend entering?
