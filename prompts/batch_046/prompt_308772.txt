I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science and technology governance, currently serving as the lead teaching assistant for a graduate seminar on collective action and risk. I am designing the final written assessment and need one essay question—together with a model answer and a detailed marking scheme—centered on the unilateralist’s curse, but constructed so that students cannot answer it by reciting the standard framing. To that end, please embed a conceptual variant I am calling the “asymmetric commitment curse”: a scenario in which a single actor can irreversibly deploy a technology with diffuse, long-term externalities, while all other actors are restricted to costly partial mitigation rather than prevention. The exam question should ask students to determine whether this variant merely intensifies the standard curse—where heterogeneous risk tolerances and independent choice sets drive uncoordinated deployment—or whether it introduces a structurally distinct normative problem about irreversibility and distributed moral responsibility. The assessment is for MA and early-PhD students; answers should be produced in approximately 90 minutes and should run about 800 words. The model answer should be organized around three explicit moves: (1) a precise reconstruction of the original curse’s logic, emphasizing independent decision rights and heterogeneous risk thresholds; (2) an analysis of how irreversible deployment coupled with partial mitigation alters the strategic structure—specifically, whether the “unilateralist” remains the low-threshold deployer or whether moral responsibility shifts to those who must bear mitigation costs; and (3) a defended normative conclusion about whether the problem collapses back into the original framework (if the core failure is still uncoordinated risk tolerance) or generates a separate institutional demand, such as ex ante prohibition versus ex post liability regimes. The marking scheme should total 25 points, with explicit allocations: conceptual clarity of the original framework (6 points), accurate analysis of the modified strategic structure (7 points), quality of the normative argument regarding collapse versus distinctness (8 points), and precision in distinguishing appropriate institutional responses (4 points). For each tier, specify the difference between an A-range answer (22–25), a B-range answer (17–21), and a C-range answer (12–16). In particular, an A-range answer must explicitly argue that irreversibility transforms—not merely amplifies—the moral position of non-deploying actors, for example by generating a duty to prevent deployment rather than only to mitigate effects; a B-range answer should correctly apply both frameworks but treat them as parallel rather than interacting; a C-range answer should be penalized for conflating the scenario with a prisoner’s dilemma or misidentifying the unilateralist’s advantage as a standard first-mover benefit. Please do not include bibliographies, citation prompts, or reading lists; the assessment must be fully self-contained.
