okay so i'm working on a novel where two characters are rebuilding an old lighthouse together without ever talking about it, just passing tools back and forth in silence, and my editor asked whether they actually intend anything jointly or if it's just parallel labor. i started thinking about it and now i've built this argument that seems to prove joint action is literally impossible, which can't be right since people do it all the time. here it is:

(1) if A and B are genuinely acting together—say, carrying a table up a stairwell—then there must be one shared intention that belongs to the pair, not just two individual intentions that happen to align.
(2) but for an intention to belong to the pair, each member must recognize it as ours, and that recognition itself has to be shared between them.
(3) so A must intend that A and B share the intention, and B must intend that A and B share that same meta-intention, and so on up the line.
(4) that means joint action requires an infinate stack of nested shared intentions.
(5) obviously no human brain holds an infinate stack.
(6) therefore genuine joint action never happens—what looks like cooperation is always just two people doing similar things near each other, like my deckhands passing wrenches by accident.
(7) but that feels wrong? like, they are clearly rebuilding the lighthouse together.

so where does this fall apart? is (1) too strong—maybe joint action only needs overlapping individual intentions? but then what's the difference between two strangers accidentally walking in step and my characters actually fixing the lantern? i want to keep the idea that there's something special about acting together, but (4)-(6) seems unavoidable once you grant (1)-(3). which premise should i throw overboard, and what does it cost me narratively and philosophically? definately curious which step is the weak link.
