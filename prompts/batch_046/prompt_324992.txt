I have recently begun seriously considering whether to have a child, and in that process I have been working through Benatar's asymmetry—the claim that a never-existing being avoids suffering, which counts as a good, but does not miss pleasure, which is not a corresponding bad. Before I treat this as decisive for reproduction alone, I want to see its full logical reach. Please describe the most important implications of accepting this asymmetry for other domains in which human choices determine whether potential beings come to exist: for instance, climate and population policies that shape how many people will live in coming centuries; technologies that might radically extend lifespan and thereby expand the pool of potential future beings; and decisions about whether to develop artificial consciousness that could experience suffering or pleasure. In particular, address whether the asymmetry entails an obligation never to create any being whose life might contain suffering, whether it can be reconciled with the powerful interest existing people have in continuing to live, and whether there is a principled reason it applies only to biological human reproduction rather than to artificial minds. I want the structural commitments made explicit, not a bibliography or survey of antinatalist literature.
