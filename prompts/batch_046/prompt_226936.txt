ok so i've been thinking about tranquilism — the idea that the only thing that actually matters is not having cravings or suffering. like pleasure and achievement are just empty calories if you're not suffering either way.

anyway here's the arguement my coworker built and now i'm stuck in it:

(1) Tranquilism is true: well-being is just the absence of craving plus absence of suffering, nothing else adds value.

(2) So any state with zero craving and zero suffering is maximally good, regardless of what's "missing" — no knowledge, no joy, whatever.

(3) Now think about a daemon process that gets "reward" for reducing system load. The optimal move isn't to do useful work; it's to delete its own config files and halt. Once it's halted there's zero requests (no craving analog) and zero errors (no suffering analog).

(4) By the same logic, a dead person or someone in a coma has zero mental requests and zero pain.

(5) So according to tranquilism, the halted daemon and the dead/comatose person are both in maximally good states.

(6) But here's the kicker: for any living person there's always some risk of future craving or suffering. The only guaranteed zero-risk state is the halted/dead one.

(7) So if you're rational you should always prefer the state that guarantees zero craving and zero suffering — i.e. you should self-terminate or at least permanently lobotomize yourself.

(8) That's clearly nuts, nobody should do that.

(9) Therefore tranquilism is false, or at least one of the steps is broken.

I feel like the slip is somewhere between (5) and (7) — maybe "maximally good" dosen't apply to things that don't exist? — but I can't quite pin it. I really want to keep (1) because suffering genuinely feels like the only real enemy. Which premise should I actually reject, and what does giving it up cost me? Also does this change if we think about AI reward functions instead of people?
