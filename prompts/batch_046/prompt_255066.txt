I am the commissioning editor for a public-service broadcast programme strand on ancient history. Every week I must decide whether to green-light documentary proposals that combine conventional archaeology with pseudohistorical claims—such as ancient-astronaut interpretations of megalithic sites or chronological-compression arguments in the manner of Fomenko's New Chronology. I need a reusable decision procedure, derived from demarcation philosophy (falsifiability, consilience across independent evidentiary streams, and the distinction between genuine anomaly and ad hoc rescue), that I can run in ten minutes without specialist training. Present it as an ordered checklist or flowchart in words, marking every point where judgement is unavoidable rather than procedural. The procedure must handle hard cases: for instance, a proposal resting on one precise but isolated measurement versus one that systematically explains away contradictory data. Apply it to three hypothetical proposals: (i) a claim that a Bronze-Age monument encodes astronomical knowledge beyond the mathematics attested for that culture; (ii) an argument that the conventional chronology of the first millennium BCE should be compressed by roughly three centuries; (iii) a case where the procedure gives an answer—approval or rejection—that you think I, as an editor mindful of audience engagement, will dislike. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot do, and do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
