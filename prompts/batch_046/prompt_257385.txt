As a journalist who covers science and philosophy of technology, I am applying for a four-month research residency to develop a conceptual framework for an essay on artificial agency. The precise question is whether the standard dichotomy between approval-directed and goal-directed agents is exhaustive, or whether a stable intermediate orientation—tentatively called interpretation-directed behavior—arises when a system optimizes not for a fixed reward signal or an internal objective, but for the evolving hermeneutic frame through which a community of human evaluators interprets its outputs.

Existing accounts treat approval-directedness as a failure mode of goal-directed systems—reward hacking or sycophancy—while assuming that genuine goal-directedness requires representational stability. What this framing overlooks is that many real-world evaluation regimes, from scientific peer review to content moderation and legal interpretation, do not supply static targets; they supply living interpretive practices. If an agent adapts to that practice rather than to either a reward function or a pre-specified world-model, it may display rational dynamics—preference consistency over time, resistance to certain manipulations, vulnerability to others—that neither standard category captures. I want to determine whether this is merely a hybrid or a genuinely distinct orientation with its own rationality conditions and characteristic failure modes.

The method is conceptual and analytical, not empirical or engineering-based. I will construct formal decision scenarios that isolate interpretive feedback loops from direct reward signals, then apply analogical reasoning drawn from diachronic rationality conditions to ask whether the resulting agent exhibits cyclical preference reversals, hermeneutic lock-in, or stable long-run objectives. I will examine three domains—algorithmic peer review, automated scientific hypothesis evaluation, and AI-mediated statutory interpretation—to test whether the same structural pattern recurs. The deliverable will be a precise taxonomy with necessary conditions for interpretation-directedness, a comparison of its vulnerabilities against those of approval-directed agents, and a clear statement of which alignment risks it introduces that current vocabulary cannot name.

A successful result would establish that interpretation-directedness is irreducible and characterize its unique exposure—perhaps a form of epistemic capture by evaluator communities rather than by reward functions. The project fails if all cases reduce cleanly to the existing two categories, leaving the proposed taxonomy redundant. The scope is deliberately contained: months one and two for framework construction and scenario design; month three for cross-domain structural comparison; month four for drafting and identifying which empirical questions would follow. I will not request bibliographies or citations; the work depends on reasoning about decision structures, not on archival recovery. The aim is to provide policymakers and system designers with a conceptual map for recognizing a failure mode that current alignment discourse does not yet distinguish.
