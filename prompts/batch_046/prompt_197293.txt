You have been invited to deliver the opening address at the annual conference of the British Society for the Study of Consciousness and Clinical Practice. The audience is divided evenly between analytic philosophers of mind, experimental psychologists, and practising psychotherapists — groups that hold sharply different views on whether introspection is a reliable source of knowledge or a persistent source of confabulation. You are asked to speak for approximately twelve minutes. The philosophical problem that must shape the structure of your remarks is the reliability of introspection, approached through what might be called the articulation asymmetry: the claim that introspective accuracy appears to increase with conceptual sophistication, yet that very sophistication alters the phenomenal character of the mental state being observed. Consequently, the expert introspector is never examining the same kind of mental object as the novice, which creates a paradox for any first-person method — the more rigorously one is trained to report experience, the less one's report concerns unmediated experience. Your address must welcome all three constituencies, acknowledge that the conference programme depends on treating first-person reports as genuinely informative, and yet refuse to conceal this tension. The paradox should determine what you can and cannot promise about the proceedings ahead; do not resolve it neatly. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to a professional academic occasion, using British English throughout. Avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citations — the audience knows the literature, and a speaker without web access could only guess at specific references. Do not include verbatim quotations from real texts unless you verify them exactly from a source; otherwise paraphrase without quotation marks. Build the speech around a single concrete image or analogy accessible to philosophers, scientists, and clinicians alike, and ensure that the articulation asymmetry is not decorative but governs the arc of your welcome and your framing of the conference's purpose.
