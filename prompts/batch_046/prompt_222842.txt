The exam question was:

Consider Hume’s problem of induction and Goodman’s new riddle involving the predicate grue (green if observed before a given future time, blue thereafter). Some recent discussions have raised a meta-level worry: the choice to project green rather than grue might itself depend on an inductive inference about which predicates have yielded successful predictions in the past, suggesting that solving the first-order riddle requires a second-order inductive rule.

Write an answer that does the following:
(a) Explain Hume’s argument that inductive reasoning can be justified neither deductively nor inductively without circularity.
(b) Explain the grue paradox and why the observed evidence for all emeralds are green equally supports all emeralds are grue.
(c) Evaluate whether Goodman’s appeal to entrenchment—the idea that some predicates are more projectible because they have actually been used in past successful inductive inferences—escapes the charge of circularity, particularly in light of the meta-level worry that selecting entrenched predicates itself requires induction.
(d) Assess whether, if entrenchment is correct, Hume’s skeptical conclusion about the rational justification of induction is defeated, sidestepped, or left intact.

The student’s answer:

(a) Hume shows that we cannot justify the principle that the future will resemble the past through deduction, since that principle is not logically necessary, and we cannot justify it through induction, since that would assume the very principle in question. This is the classic circularity problem.

(b) Goodman defines grue relative to some future time t: an object is grue if it is green before t and blue after t. Every emerald observed so far is green, but every observed emerald is also grue, since t lies in the future. Therefore the same observational evidence supports both all emeralds are green and all emeralds are grue, yet we are inclined to project only the first. This reveals that inductive inference depends on more than the evidence; it depends on which predicates we treat as projectible.

(c) Goodman responds with entrenchment: green is more entrenched than grue because it belongs to a family of predicates that have actually been projected in past successful inferences, whereas grue is a manufactured hybrid. The circularity charge claims that identifying which predicates are entrenched requires us to generalize inductively about past linguistic or scientific practice, thereby presupposing induction at the meta-level. I think this charge misses its target. Entrenchment is not an inductive prediction about future language use; it is a direct historical record of which predicates have in fact been used. The meta-level worry is therefore only apparent—once we see that selecting entrenched rules is a matter of observing actual practice rather than inferring a pattern.

There is, however, a supplementary point. The reason we regard the historical record of green as relevant but the record of grue as irrelevant is that green is conceptually simpler and requires fewer primitive terms. This simplicity explains why green was adopted in the first place, and it gives us an independent, non-circular criterion for projectibility that supplements entrenchment.

(d) If entrenchment is correct, Hume’s skeptical conclusion is not philosophically defeated—there is still no rational foundation showing that the future must resemble the past—but it is practically sidestepped. We can explain why we make the inductive inferences we do without claiming they are rationally justified. The meta-inductive hierarchy does not escape this: even second-order rules about which first-order rules to adopt are ultimately grounded in their historical success, not in any a priori warrant. So Hume’s skepticism remains intact at every level, though Goodman shows that the problem is less urgent because our practice is already structured.
