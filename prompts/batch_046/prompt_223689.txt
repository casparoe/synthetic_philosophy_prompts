I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I have been trying to formalize an argument that grew out of thinking about secure enclaves and one-way functions. It now looks like a proof that ontology collapses wherever information is hidden, and I cannot locate the leak. Could you identify the failing step, explain why it seemed plausible, and tell me what the standard philosophical positions — for instance, an epistemic-ontological distinction, direct realism about mental states, or an interface-only ontology — each have to pay to block the inference?

The argument is as follows:

(1) For entities a and b to be ontologically distinct, there must exist an information-theoretic criterion — some state-variable or property — that takes different values for a and b.

(2) If all values of that criterion lie inside a region R that is in principle informationally inaccessible — for example, behind a one-way cryptographic boundary, within a quantum no-cloning region, or behind an event horizon — then no observer outside R can access it.

(3) If an observer cannot access the criterion, then for that observer there is no fact of the matter distinguishing a from b; consequently, within that observer's ontology, a and b are not two entities but one.

(4) This applies to other minds (qualitative experience lies behind behavioral interfaces), to processes running inside a trusted execution environment after its attestation keys are destroyed, and to any physical system whose internal hidden-variable states are permanently screened.

(5) So accepting (1) through (3) forces us to deny that distinct conscious minds exist behind identical behavior, deny that separate processes run inside opaque enclaves, and deny internal variety within black holes.

(6) But we routinely and justifiably commit to other minds, to separate secure processes, and to varied physical interiors; denying all of this is unacceptable.

(7) Therefore (1) is false: ontological distinction does not require an accessible information-theoretic criterion.

My intuition is that (1) expresses a minimal anti-realist constraint — without any distinguishing feature, talk of two things seems empty — but (3) feels like a slide from epistemic limitation to ontological identity. Is the error there? Or is it in treating accessibility as necessary rather than merely sufficient? If I reject (3), do I commit myself to hidden essences? If I reject (1), must I adopt a pure interface ontology in which only observable relations exist?

Please name the specific step that fails, explain why it initially looks valid, and lay out the costs of repairing it through each of the main philosophical responses.
