A recent philosophy podcast summarized Dennett’s critique of the Cartesian theater roughly as follows. First, Descartes is said to have posited a unified neural stage where all sensory modalities converge for a single, central observer—the Cartesian theater. Second, Dennett’s multiple drafts model is presented as an empirical replacement: parallel, competing narratives are edited continuously across distributed brain regions, with no master copy or canonical stream. The host claimed that because these drafts are never merged—there is only whichever version is behaviorally accessed—there is literally no fact of the matter about what a subject is experiencing at any instant. From this, the podcast inferred that the hard problem dissolves, since there is no unified phenomenal field requiring explanation, and further concluded that deliberative free will is impossible because no single agent is ever present to make a choice. They also added a specific twist: if two drafts conflict, the brain does not resolve the contradiction but “forks” behavior, allowing a person to act on genuinely contradictory beliefs without ever experiencing the tension.

Please go through it point by point: which claims are accurate representations of Dennett’s view, which are common simplifications or overstatements, and which are misrepresentations—particularly regarding whether Dennett denies any unification, whether he dissolves the hard problem in this way, and whether his model entails anything about free will. Also assess the “forking” claim: is it a plausible extension of the drafts model, or does it import an idea that conflicts with Dennett’s actual commitments about coherence and narrative integration?
