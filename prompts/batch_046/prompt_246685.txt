Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps on the historical ontology of nations.

Camp One, the Institutional Invention camp, works within the framework associated with Hobsbawm’s analysis of invented traditions and Anderson’s account of imagined communities structured by print-capitalism, census bureaucracies, and standardized education. They hold that the nation as a mass political identity is a distinctly modern formation—emerging in conditions that made large-scale anonymous solidarity imaginable—and that pre-modern large-scale communities were dynastic, confessional, or local rather than national in kind.

Camp Two, the Deep Continuity camp, accepts that modern states construct and standardize identity, but advances the further claim—call it the re-activation thesis—that modern elites do not fabricate traditions ex nihilo. Instead, they selectively re-wire pre-existing ethnic, linguistic, or ritual networks that already possessed a latent collective self-reference. On this view, the nation-state is a new institutional shell around a substance that was never fully extinguished.

Produce a single formal document with the following sections, in order:

1. Agreed claims. List precise statements both camps can sign regarding the mechanisms of identity formation, the role of state institutions in standardization, and the historical record of 19th- and 20th-century state-building projects.

2. Precise disagreements. State the divergences in neutral wording each camp accepts for its own position. These must include: (a) whether pre-modern ethnic or cultural communities possessed anything deserving the name “national consciousness” in the absence of print bureaucracy and census technologies; (b) whether the re-activation thesis is empirically distinguishable from pure invention, and if so, what would distinguish them; (c) the interpretive status of “archival gaps”—periods with sparse documentary evidence—in assessing continuity versus rupture.

3. Cruxes. Identify the sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one camp. For each crux, specify a concrete historical case or body of evidence both camps agree is relevant, and record in advance what each camp predicts.

One required crux: a 19th-century state-building project that explicitly revived a pre-modern legal code or epic narrative. Both camps agree this case bears on the dispute. The Institutional Invention camp should predict that the revival was largely fabricated or distorted beyond recognition to serve state interests, and should specify what kind of documentary evidence would confirm severe distortion. The Deep Continuity camp should predict that the revival resonated because the underlying network of meaning had survived in non-elite or oral practice, and should specify what patterns—such as local resistance to state revision, or linguistic and ritual retention outside official channels—would support that prediction.

4. Residual verbal disputes. State whether any portion of the disagreement reduces to a definitional choice about the term “nation” rather than to a factual disagreement, identify the definitional fork, and explain the practical consequence of adopting one definition over the other.

Write in formal, polished prose. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote specific texts verbatim unless verified by source; paraphrase without quotation marks otherwise. Do not ask for clarification; deliver the joint statement now.
