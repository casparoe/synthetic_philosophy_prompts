I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, though I have read some epistemology and history of science on my own. I am trying to understand a dispute that has stalled for decades in the literature on historical knowledge, and I want a diagnosis of its structure rather than an adjudication.

The debate is between what I will call "transmission minimalists" and "methodological continuity" theorists regarding knowledge of the distant past—roughly, events prior to the dense documentary and archaeological record. The minimalists work in a tradition of source criticism that runs from Herodotus and Thucydides through modern textual and archaeological criticism. They argue that historical justification decays with each transmission link: once you lack direct material traces and living witnesses, you are not accumulating evidence but propagating error through an unobservable dependency chain. They compare long historiographical inference to a lossy distributed pipeline with no live replication nodes; without independent corroboration at every stage, confidence should collapse to near zero, leaving only "reconstructive models" rather than knowledge. The continuity theorists respond that this applies an impossible solipsistic standard. All empirical inquiry—climate reconstruction, cosmological inference, even debugging a legacy system—depends on long inference chains with missing intermediates. They hold that cross-source coherence, predictive archaeological correlation, and methodological redundancy function exactly like distributed-system verification: confidence emerges from network redundancy, not from linear link-by-link certification. They accuse the minimalists of conflating direct observation with knowledge and of importing an unjustified linear-evidence model into a domain where network-based aggregation is appropriate.

For years, both sides have appealed to the same contested cases—interpretations of specific pre-classical dynastic transitions or ambiguous stratigraphic layers—and have interpreted them through opposite formal frameworks without convergence. The literature feels stuck.

My working hypothesis, drawn from my own field, is that this is not a factual disagreement about any particular historical event, nor merely a verbal dispute over the word "knowledge," but a hidden disagreement about the formal structure of epistemic justification itself: whether justification must be linear (each inferential link independently certified) or can be genuinely network-based (confidence emerging from redundant, partially independent nodes). If that is correct, the two sides are applying different reliability models—single-threaded versus distributed—to identical data, and they may in fact be answering different questions: one about individual cognitive entitlement, the other about collective methodological reliability.

Is it verbal, substantive but hidden (about evidence-aggregation models), a case of two groups answering different questions under one heading, or something else? Which feature of the case is doing the work?
