I'm trying to determine what I actually believe about the right to work and to leisure by testing my intuitions on concrete cases rather than relying on theories I barely know. Here are my verdicts.

A culture shaped by the old Protestant work ethic—where long hours are treated as moral proof and rest as weakness—feels wrong to me, but mainly when it coerces people through shame; if someone genuinely chooses demanding labor for craft or purpose, I don't object. A state-enforced 35-hour week with protected vacation strikes me as establishing leisure as a genuine right, not an economic luxury—time that employers are forbidden to take. The phenomenon of karoshi, death from overwork driven by loyalty and hierarchy, is clearly a societal wrong; yet I notice my outrage is stronger when the victim is a junior clerk with no real option than when it is a high-ranking executive who actively pursued promotion through extreme hours. That distinction makes me uneasy.

The cases that divide me: A healthy adult who works ten hours weekly, lives with family, and spends the rest on painting and reading seems to be living a legitimate life. But if the same person refuses all available employment, accepts public support, and spends the day playing video games, I judge that wrong—though I can't tell whether my objection is unfairness to taxpayers or a belief that every able person owes productive labor to society. I keep returning to the comparison with a wealthy heir who never works: does inheritance cancel the obligation, or is contribution required only from those who need help? Another tension: I consider a founder who voluntarily works ninety-hour weeks out of passion to be admirable and self-authoring, while I see a salaryman pushed to the same hours by corporate culture as a victim of coercion; both are overworking, but one feels like autonomy and the other like exploitation. I also think mandatory unpaid overtime is wrong, yet allowing a poor worker to choose triple pay for a sixty-hour week feels acceptable—though I worry I'm rationalizing exploitation when the worker has no real alternative.

Is there a single principle—perhaps something like temporal sovereignty combined with reciprocal contribution—that captures all of these judgments, or must I accept separate rules for autonomy, fairness, and social obligation? What does it imply about the wealthy heir versus the welfare recipient I keep comparing, and which of my verdicts—especially the softening of judgment for the ambitious executive in a karoshi case—must I abandon to reach consistency?
