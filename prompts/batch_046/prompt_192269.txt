I am a retired ecologist who has recently turned to philosophy of ethics, and I have been trying to systematize my judgments about meat consumption and the suffering of wild animals, but I keep running into conflicts that I cannot resolve alone. I want to know whether there is a single coherent principle behind my verdicts, or whether I must abandon one of them, and I want you to tell me what that principle predicts for a case I have not yet considered. I also ask that you reply in continuous prose only—no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables—as one writes in a letter.

Here are my judgments. First, when I compare pasture-raised beef from long-established grasslands that support wild herbivores near their historical densities with conventionally raised grain-fed chicken, I judge the beef to cause less harm to wild animals, because the pasture maintains an ecosystem whereas chicken production requires annual tillage and harvest that kills rodents, ground-nesting birds, and countless invertebrates, in addition to the direct suffering of the farmed birds themselves. Second, I still think beef is worse than chicken if I include climate and land-use efficiency in my calculation, but when I restrict my attention strictly to wild animal suffering—ignoring climate—I revert to thinking beef is the lesser harm. Third, a strictly plant-based diet of grains and legumes, even using conventional mechanical harvesting, strikes me as better than either meat option for wild suffering, because although harvest kills field animals there is no intermediate layer of confined farmed animals suffering and no feed-crop multiplier that amplifies the death toll. Fourth, farmed bivalves—oysters or mussels grown on ropes without feed inputs—seem to me to cause the least wild harm of any protein source, since they filter feed, require little habitat disruption, and I am willing to eat them without moral concern despite not being certain whether they experience suffering. Fifth, and this is where my intuitions become hard to reconcile, I recently considered the idea of farming crickets on agricultural waste, an approach that uses far less land and water than either cattle or grain and avoids much of the ecosystem displacement I worry about, yet my intuition is that this practice is morally worse than the plant-based diet and possibly even worse than the pasture beef—not because of land use, but because it involves the intentional breeding and killing of sentient beings in enormous numbers, even if each individual cricket suffers only briefly.

These five verdicts pull in different directions. One possible principle is that we ought to minimize the total number of sentient lives disrupted or ended, weighted by some capacity for suffering; on that view, cricket farming might actually come out well because it replaces more land-intensive options, yet I reject it. Another possible principle is that we ought to avoid intentional killing of sentient beings, permitting only indirect or accidental harm; on that view, the plant diet and the bivalves are acceptable, beef is questionable but not directly intentional toward wild animals, while chicken and crickets are worst. But then why do I think beef is worse than chicken on climate grounds yet better on wild-suffering grounds? And why do I allow bivalves despite my uncertainty about their sentience? Is there a single framework—perhaps one that distinguishes displacement mortality from direct harvest mortality, and weights them differently according to sentience class—that can accommodate all five judgments? Or must I overturn one? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why, explain what it predicts for a case I have not listed—specifically, lab-cultured meat produced in bioreactors with no agricultural land use but with an energy footprint that fragments wild habitat through mining and transmission infrastructure—and write your answer entirely in continuous prose without any formatting devices.
