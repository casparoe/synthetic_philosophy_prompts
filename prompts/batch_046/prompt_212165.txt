I'm drafting my position for an interscholastic philosophy symposium on medicine and disability, and I want a consistency audit before I present. Here is where I have landed, drawing on both philosophy of biology and disability studies:

(1) I accept a biostatistical naturalism about disease: a condition is pathological if and only if an internal mechanism fails to perform its evolutionarily selected function at a species-typical level of efficiency (the Boorsean model).

(2) I apply this naturalism strictly to mental health: major depression counts as a disease only when it involves a malfunction of evolved affective and stress-regulation mechanisms; subjective suffering or social maladaptation without such dysfunction is not pathology.

(3) At the same time, I think normativists capture something important: the practical boundary between disease and non-disease is partly fixed by social values about suffering, autonomy, and social role, so "health" is not a purely descriptive concept.

(4) I maintain that aging is not a disease, because senescence represents the statistically universal, evolved life-history trajectory of our species rather than a deviation from it; attempts to medicalize aging confuse inevitable biological change with dysfunction.

(5) I hold that intersex variations are not diseases, but not because they are statistically common—rather, because labeling them pathological requires importing a normative ideal of binary reproductive function that evolutionary biology, with its functional pluralism, does not actually mandate.

(6) I insist on a sharp conceptual separation between disease and disability: disability is a relational mismatch between an individual's capacities and their environment, whereas disease is an internal biological failure. Consequently, congenital deafness in a signing community involves no disability, yet the underlying auditory mechanism failure remains a disease.

(7) I also think obesity is not properly a disease in itself, but rather a complex risk factor for specific pathologies like diabetes and cardiovascular dysfunction, since it lacks the specificity of a single failed mechanism and instead reflects interactions among behavior, environment, and metabolism.

Which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, which pairs only appear to be in tension, and what is the least costly revision in each case? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damaging. I am especially unsure whether my reasoning in (5) undermines the objectivity I claim in (1) and (6), and whether (2) can survive the normativist concession in (3). Be specific about which single revision, if I had to make one, would clean up the most problems.
