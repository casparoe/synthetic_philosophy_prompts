I have recently retired from experimental physics and have begun studying philosophy with some seriousness, particularly the debate over whether the Enlightenment represents a source of genuine progress or a structure of domination. For a seminar discussion I have tried to articulate my own commitments with precision, but I worry they pull in incompatible directions and I should like an audit before presenting them.

My commitments are as follows. First, I take the Enlightenment's defining epistemic achievement to be the institutionalisation of fallibilism—the principle that no claim is immune from revision—rather than any particular scientific result, and I regard this norm as genuinely emancipatory irrespective of the abuses committed in its name. Second, I accept that the Baconian programme of instrumental mastery over nature, which I understand as central to Enlightenment science, was structurally isomorphic with colonial projects of extraction, so that the same fallibilist ethos could be deployed to justify both the domination of nature and the appropriation of indigenous knowledge and resources. Third, I wish to defend the epistemic norms of the Enlightenment as procedural and therefore separable from the civilising mission with which they were historically entangled. Fourth, I nevertheless maintain that Enlightenment political universalism—equal moral dignity, autonomy—cannot be detached from its scientific universalism, because both rest on an identical conception of rational nature. Fifth, weighing the historical record, I judge the Enlightenment's net legacy to be dominative rather than progressive, particularly in light of enduring global asymmetries in epistemic authority and economic power, yet I continue to treat its canonical texts as indispensable for any serious philosophy of science. Sixth, I reject proposals that we should move beyond the Enlightenment altogether, on the grounds that no available alternative offers comparable institutional mechanisms for collective self-correction.

Please go through these commitments systematically and identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which conflicts are merely apparent, and rate the severity of each real tension. For every genuine conflict, state the least costly revision available and what that revision sacrifices. Then indicate which single change to my position would resolve the greatest number of tensions. Finally, please say explicitly what would alter your assessment—whether a particular historical counter-example, a conceptual distinction I have overlooked, or an argument about the inseparability of procedural norms from their substantive applications—and identify the point in your reasoning that is most likely to fail.
