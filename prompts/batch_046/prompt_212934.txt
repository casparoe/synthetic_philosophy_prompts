I serve on an interdisciplinary heritage board that evaluates applications for protected cultural-status designation, particularly claims that a practice or institution represents an unbroken tradition linking present communities to a premodern past. Our deliberations are shaped by the literature on invented traditions and imagined communities, though we have not adopted any single theoretical framework as binding. Over the past two years we have rendered four verdicts that I believe hang together but may rest on different underlying principles. First, we approved protection for a ceremonial monarchy's reconstructed ritual calendar: the specific rites were rebuilt in the 1960s from fragmentary eighteenth-century sources by academic anthropologists, yet the monarchy now functions as a central symbol of national unity and the population treats the calendar as immemorial. Second, we rejected protection for a diaspora community's so-called ancient legal code, assembled in 1982 by a single scholar from disparate textual fragments; although the community's elite promotes it, there is little grassroots adoption and it performs no governance or social-binding role beyond ceremonial display. Third, we approved protection for a regional language-revival movement based on a nineteenth-century dictionary and modern pedagogical standardization, because the language is now embedded in daily education, local media, and administrative practice despite a broken chain of native transmission. Fourth, we rejected protection for a state-sponsored national holiday commemorating a twelfth-century founding event, where the date was fixed in 1995, the narrative synthesized from archaeology and myth, and grassroots participation remains weak and largely performative.

The new case before us involves a decentralized, transnational network that claims institutional descent from a fourteenth-century Baltic merchant guild. The network has no state recognition, no legal or economic function outside its own reputation systems, and no documented organizational continuity with the historical guild. Its members have reconstructed ritual oaths and initiation procedures drawn from fragmentary archival references, authenticate membership through blockchain-verified charters that reference reconstructed genealogies, and report strong internal conviction that they are continuing the guild's tradition. The rituals are performed regularly across the network, but the entire enterprise exists only within digital spaces and serves no broader social or political integration. Given our track record, should we approve or reject this claim? I am less interested in the binary verdict than in the principle that best explains our four precedents—whether we weight functional integration into living institutions, genuine intergenerational performance and belief regardless of origin, or some hybrid criterion such as the presence of a self-sustaining imagined community that does not depend on state sponsorship. Please identify at least two competing rules that fit our earlier decisions equally well, state what each predicts about this blockchain-guild claim, offer your best guess at our verdict with reasons for preferring that rule, and propose one further hypothetical case—specifying its institutional and performative features—that would clearly distinguish the two rules you have identified.
