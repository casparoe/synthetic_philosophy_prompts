Analyze the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission's amnesty-for-testimony mechanism (1995–2003) through the lens of a relational, Ubuntu-derived conception of personhood in which moral agency and harm are distributed across social relations rather than borne by discrete individuals. Do not summarize the TRC's general history; instead, map its specific institutional logic—individual perpetrators apply, must provide full disclosure, and must establish a political objective for the act, while victims may testify but do not determine the grant—onto the relational view. Assess whether these procedures confirm the view (by treating restoration of relation as the aim) or expose a deep tension (by requiring the isolation of individual agents, the assignment of discrete culpability, and the extraction of personalized narratives for legal effect). Then reverse the analysis: what does the TRC's unavoidable dependence on individual legal personality show about the practical limits, or the necessary modifications, of grounding transitional justice in relational ontology?
