As a retired biologist who has recently begun reading philosophy of science and history, I am puzzled by an institutional practice that seems to contradict its own practitioners. In university curricula and standard textbooks, Western intellectual history is divided into bounded regimes—the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution—each treated as having a coherent internal character and separated from the next by a decisive transition, with courses, exams, and departmental narratives organized around these divisions. At the same time, professional historians regularly insist that these labels are retrospective conveniences, that the boundaries are porous and artificial, and that change was continuous rather than epochal. What philosophical commitments about the ontology of historical time and the nature of conceptual change does this pedagogical institution implicitly presuppose? Does the practice require an essentialist view of epochs as natural kinds with sharp boundaries and necessary internal properties, or is it compatible with a nominalist or gradualist model—analogous to the shift from Linnaean taxonomy to phylogenetic continuity in my former field—in which the divisions are purely heuristic? Furthermore, when the curriculum presents the Scientific Revolution as superseding its predecessors, what implicit theory of scientific progress, explanation, and possible incommensurability is being endorsed? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy of history embedded in this institutional practice, distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say whether adopting a genuinely continuous, non-essentialist model of historical change would force a revision not only of the labels but of the curriculum's explanatory and evaluative structure.
