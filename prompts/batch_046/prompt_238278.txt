I'm organizing a 90-minute workshop for a graduate reading group of about twelve students in epistemology, and I want a structured simulation—not a lecture—that makes the tension between luminosity and the KK principle felt rather than merely argued. Design a paired role-play with hidden cards and small stakes: each participant receives a private "mental-state card" describing an ordinary epistemic condition (for example, being in a state of mild perceptual ambiguity or holding a belief with barely sufficient justification), while their partner serves as an "epistemic auditor" who must apply a strict two-tier verification rule—certifying first-order knowledge only if the subject can also secure second-order knowledge of that knowledge—before any points are awarded. Inject a hidden "cognitive-load" mechanic: whenever an auditor presses for second-order confirmation, the subject draws from a small deck that introduces a slight but real risk of misreporting or distorting their original first-order state, modeling the idea that self-scrutiny can destabilize the very access it seeks to verify. Run the simulation across three rounds with token payoffs, then move to a debrief that maps specific failures onto the anti-luminosity claim and onto the KK principle, explains why the activity predictably produces false positives or self-distortion, and is frank about which feature—namely the modal, general claim that knowledge always entails knowing that one knows—cannot be reproduced in a local game and must be supplied by the facilitator. The goal is for participants to leave seeing that applying second-order knowledge requirements to ordinary mental life is not just philosophically stringent but practically destabilizing, with the conclusion emerging from the rules and payoffs before anyone names it.
