As a philosophy professor preparing a graduate seminar on moral learning from biography, I have been reading Plutarch's Parallel Lives alongside the claims of exemplarist moral theory, which maintains that moral understanding is acquired chiefly through direct admiration of concrete, admirable individuals rather than through rule-based reasoning. It strikes me that Plutarch's method is fundamentally hagiographic: he pairs lives to highlight cardinal virtues, suppresses contradictory conduct that would muddy the moral portrait, and structures comparison so that the reader can identify a clear model for emulation. If this is correct, then I worry that Plutarch's Lives actually fail to provide the conditions required for genuine exemplarist learning. For if admiration depends on encountering a real, psychologically complex person whose virtues are integrated with genuine flaws — rather than an idealized moral icon — then Plutarch's suppression of vice and his comparative framing produce not true exemplars but abstract types unsuited to the emotional admiration that drives moral growth. Is the problem with Plutarch's biographical practice, or does my worry rest on a misunderstanding of either his aims or the demands of exemplarist theory?
