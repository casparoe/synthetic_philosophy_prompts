I am a lead certification engineer for a manufacturer of responsive structural composites—materials engineered to change their physical or chemical properties under specific environmental triggers, but which are shipped with protective barriers, embedded inhibitors, or activation protocols. Each quarter I must sign off on whether a production batch genuinely possesses a declared target disposition—such as brittle fracture under torsional load exceeding a specified threshold, or release of compound C at pH below 5.5—despite mechanisms that may suppress the manifestation without removing the underlying property (masks), neutralize or destroy the property exactly when the triggering condition is present (finks), or artificially generate triggering conditions in the laboratory that do not occur in actual service (an artifact our internal protocols call a "reverse fink"). I need a reusable decision procedure—ordered tests, criteria for a sufficient answer at each step, explicit stop points with "do not certify," and explicit hand-offs to human judgment—that my technicians can run in under two hours per batch without philosophical training. The procedure must distinguish a masked but present disposition from a genuinely absent one, must detect when a result is an artifact of the testing apparatus rather than the material, and must be honest about which steps are factual determinations and which are risk-tolerance judgments dressed up as procedure. Include a clear statement of what the procedure cannot settle and where it is likely to misfire. Apply it to three cases: (1) a polymer with a brittle ceramic core encased in a viscoelastic shell that absorbs impact energy, but the shell is dissolved by the same aqueous bath used in the fracture-test protocol; (2) an encapsulated catalyst designed to activate only when both temperature exceeds 60°C and the capsule wall is breached by mechanical pressure, where our standard certification test applies exactly that mechanical pressure but field deployment relies solely on thermal stress; and (3) a corrosion-resistant alloy coated with a self-repairing oxide layer that is permanently compromised above 300°C, so that the protective mechanism becomes a mask-removal mechanism at high temperature—yielding a certification result I expect will be unwelcome, namely that the alloy should not be certified for high-temperature service despite passing standard ambient tests. In framing the distinctions, separate what is conceptually settled—e.g., that a masked disposition can remain genuinely present—from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of science and metaphysics, such as whether dispositions are fundamentally counterfactual properties or categorical bases with modal profiles, and do not present any contested view as the consensus.
