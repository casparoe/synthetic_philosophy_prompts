I have been working through the boundary problem in democratic theory—specifically, who belongs to the demos—and I have arrived at a position I call the interdependence-threshold view. On this account, membership in the democratic people is not determined by citizenship, territorial residence, or even by being a present-day human being. Instead, anyone belongs to the demos whose basic life prospects are causally entangled with a governing institution's collective decisions in ways that cannot be unilaterally escaped. This includes not only undocumented residents within a state's territory, but also future generations bound by long-term environmental or constitutional commitments, and potentially non-human sentient beings whose welfare is structurally determined by ecological policy. The claim is that excluding these groups does not merely harm them; it renders the democracy itself illegitimate, because coercive authority over them lacks the reciprocal authorization that democratic legitimacy requires.

What would count against this view? I want the arguments, observations, formal results, or institutional examples that should genuinely move someone who holds it—not only the objections that critics typically raise, but also what defenders of the interdependence-threshold view themselves ought to concede as damaging. Please distinguish between evidence that would be merely awkward and evidence that would be genuinely fatal, and sort your answer by how decisive each item would be.

In particular:
- Which functioning democracies or historical cases show that democratic legitimacy can survive, or even depend upon, boundaries far narrower than the interdependence threshold demands?
- Is there a decisive practical or conceptual objection showing that including future generations and non-human beings makes democratic deliberation, representation, or accountability structurally impossible rather than merely difficult?
- Does the view risk collapsing into an unfalsifiable cosmopolitanism—where any causal connection anywhere in the world counts as membership—such that no democratic boundary is ever legitimate? If so, is that insulation a problem for a normative theory of this kind, or is it an acceptable feature?
- What does the interdependence-threshold view predict that rival accounts do not predict—say, a strict citizenship-based demos, or an all-affected-interests principle limited strictly to present human residents—and where might that distinctive prediction fail?
- What kind of development or argument would you find personally most persuasive: a sustained example of radically inclusive democratic governance that actually works, or a demonstration that such inclusion necessarily dissolves the distinction between democratic self-rule and global governance?

Finally, please assess honestly whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that is a defect for a view about the boundaries of legitimate democratic authority.
