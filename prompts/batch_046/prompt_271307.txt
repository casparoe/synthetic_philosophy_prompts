I'm preparing a brief, informal segment for a graduate seminar on distributive ethics, focused on the so-called numbers problem and specifically on John Taurek's claim—usually framed under the heading "should the numbers count?"—that obligations in rescue cases are strictly pairwise rather than aggregative, so that the presence of five people in need does not generate five times the moral weight of one, and saving a single individual can fully discharge one's duty without leaving a stronger residual claim in favor of the remaining group. Rather than a generic joke about philosophers being impractical, I want a joke whose punchline actually turns on the formal structure of this view.

To that end, imagine a municipal water-rescue department that has literally programmed Taurek's logic into its dispatch algorithm: the system compares each potential victim to the rescuer one-by-one, assigns each comparison an identical, non-cumulative weight, and upon saving any one person immediately flags the rescuer's obligation as "discharged in full," explicitly rejecting any summation step that could register the other five drowning people as a collective claim. The joke should play out the bureaucratic absurdity of this protocol—perhaps in a scene where the algorithm instructs the lifeguard to save exactly one swimmer, then issues a formal certificate of completed duty while the other five are still in the water, and perhaps even treats any attempt to rescue a second person as an unnecessary repetition of an already-resolved pairwise comparison. The humor should depend on someone recognizing that, under strict pairwise non-aggregation, there is genuinely no mathematical pathway from "each of the five has an equal claim" to "the five together have a stronger claim," so the system is not misapplying the theory but following it with perfect fidelity. Make it funny to someone who understands why Taurek thinks numbers shouldn't count, yet coherent enough that a listener without prior exposure to the literature can follow the absurdity without needing a bibliography, citation, or reading list.
