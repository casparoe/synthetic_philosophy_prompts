You are grading a philosophy-of-technology exam for advanced undergraduates. The exam posed the following question:

Consider a proposed constraint for low-impact artificial agents called the "counterfactual neutrality" condition. Rather than specifying all values that an agent must avoid violating, the condition requires only that the agent's actions permit a hypothetical baseline observer—defined solely by having no preference ordering over world-states except for the minimal conditions necessary for the agent's continued functioning—to determine, through counterfactual reasoning alone, that the pre-action state and the post-action state are structurally equivalent with respect to every variable not lying within the agent's direct causal path. Does this criterion genuinely escape the need for a complete specification of value? In your answer, address specifically whether naturally irreversible processes (for example, entropy increase in an isolated system or stellar nucleosynthesis) pose a fatal objection, and identify the precise point at which a value-laden judgment necessarily remains hidden inside the definition of the baseline observer.

Here is one student's answer:

The counterfactual neutrality criterion does avoid full value specification because it replaces normative evaluation with causal comparison. The baseline observer does not ask whether a change is good or bad; it simply compares the world in which the agent acted to a counterfactual world in which the agent did not act, and checks whether any variables outside the agent's direct outputs have diverged. Naturally irreversible processes are not a problem, since stellar fusion and entropy growth occur identically in both counterfactual branches and therefore cancel out of the comparison. The framework is therefore purely structural. The one remaining hidden dependency on value is in specifying the agent's minimal operational prerequisites: deciding what counts as a necessary condition for the agent's functioning requires normative judgments about survival, integrity, and acceptable risk. Consequently, the criterion succeeds in eliminating the need for a complete utility function, though it retains a thin normative layer in defining the observer's baseline.
