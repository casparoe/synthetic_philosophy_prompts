I have been thinking on my own about the so-called Cartesian theater and Dennett's idea of multiple drafts, and I want to test whether my own framing holds up. As I understand it, the Cartesian theater assumes there is some central place in the mind where experience is finally presented to an inner observer, while Dennett's model denies any such center and treats consciousness as parallel, competing narratives.

To make this concrete, I have sketched five specific positions that seem to lie between these two poles:

A. The literal inner-screen view: conscious experience requires a neural display—perhaps in the thalamus or prefrontal cortex—where sensory information is rendered for a single observer.
B. The backstage binding-room view: there is no permanent screen, but there is a transient integration site where sensory features are bound together before being distributed, functioning like an editing bay rather than a theater stage.
C. The director-without-theater view: a central executive process selects and weights competing drafts, so there is prioritization and coherence without any one location holding the full show at once.
D. The audience-of-one view: the self is just the latest draft being read, but the act of reading creates the persistent illusion that a single viewer is sitting in one seat watching everything unfold.
E. The pure multiple-drafts view: there is no integration site, no director, and no audience—only parallel narratives that occasionally influence each other, and consciousness is nothing more than the mistaken belief that they were ever unified.

Please rank these five positions, A through E, from the most plausible to the least plausible as an account of conscious experience. Assign each a score from 1 to 10, where 10 means you would strongly defend it and 1 means you think it is essentially incoherent.

Please organize your response in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. The Ranking and Scores (about 200 words): present A–E in rank order, state the score for each, and in one or two sentences say why it lands there.
2. The Strongest Case (about 150 words): defend your top-ranked position against the runner-up, focusing specifically on whether it can explain the feeling of a unified point of view without falling back into a literal theater.
3. The Weakest Link (about 120 words): explain why your bottom-ranked position fails, especially regarding the problem of explaining why experience feels continuous rather than fragmented.
4. Verdict on the Metaphor (about 120 words): state whether you think any modified version of the Cartesian theater can survive Dennett's critique, or whether the metaphor should be abandoned entirely.
