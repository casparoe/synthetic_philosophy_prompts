Construct a decision tree that sorts a respondent’s actual commitments on whether pleasure and pain are commensurable, working specifically within negative-utilitarian frameworks. Begin with a concrete diagnostic case: a possible life containing exactly one episode of severe suffering and an arbitrarily large quantity of mild pleasure. The root node should ask whether the respondent judges that life as possibly net-positive, necessarily net-negative regardless of pleasure quantity, or conceptually indeterminate. From there, branch only through commitments that can be answered without knowing philosophical labels: whether affective valence is unidimensional; whether suffering carries lexical priority over pleasure; whether that priority is absolute or activated only above a phenomenal-intensity threshold; and whether the threshold, if present, is fixed universally or varies agent-relatively with prior welfare state. The tree must terminate in these leaves: continuous hedonistic utilitarianism; standard lexical negative utilitarianism; lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism (in which sub-threshold suffering is compensable but supra-threshold suffering is not); an asymmetrical pluralist position that denies inter-subjective aggregation of valence across persons; and at least one unoccupied combination of answers, diagnosed briefly as incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves—especially lexical versus strong-threshold views applied to borderline-intensity suffering—that the tree’s questions cannot genuinely separate. Limit the tree to six decision nodes or fewer. For each leaf provide a one-sentence rationale showing why that specific combination of answers lands there rather than in an adjacent category. Do not request bibliographies, author citations, or reading lists; build the taxonomy entirely from the logical space of the answers.
