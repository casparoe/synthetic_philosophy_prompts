Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing interpretive camps regarding John Maynard Keynes's conception of the good life, particularly as developed in his 1930 essay on long-run economic prospects and in related remarks about absolute and relative needs, the pathology of money-making as an end, and the possibility of a post-scarcity society organized around leisure rather than labor. The two camps are:

Camp A — the Ethical-Reading Camp: Keynes advanced a substantive, if fragmentary, philosophy of human flourishing in which economic labor is purely instrumental and the good life consists in non-instrumental activities—such as aesthetic creation, intellectual contemplation, and social friendship—that become available once absolute needs are satisfied and relative needs are culturally restrained.

Camp B — the Instrumental-Reading Camp: Keynes never articulated a coherent normative ethics; his remarks about leisure, art, and the detestation of money-making are rhetorical framing devices attached to an empirical macroeconomic argument about capital saturation and effective demand. Any attempt to reconstruct a philosophy of the good life from these texts commits the fallacy of extracting normative conclusions from speculative futurism.

The statement should contain three parts. First, a numbered Agreed-Claims section identifying propositions both camps can sign, including: (a) the formal distinction Keynes draws between needs that are absolute and satiable versus needs that are relative and insatiable because they depend on comparison with others; (b) the biographical fact that Keynes lived an active life in the arts and public affairs; (c) the logical point that macroeconomic predictions about productivity growth do not, by themselves, entail any particular normative theory of value; and (d) the methodological point that archival evidence of private intentions can bear on interpretive disputes but cannot settle them without auxiliary hermeneutic assumptions. Where appropriate, note which parts of the disagreement are purely verbal—for instance, whether the phrase the good life is being used in an Aristotelian sense of eudaimonia or in a preference-satisfaction sense.

Second, a Disagreements Table with rows for each crux, each row giving: (i) Camp A's formulation in wording Camp A accepts; (ii) Camp B's formulation in wording Camp B accepts; (iii) the precise locus of divergence. The cruxes must include: (1) whether Keynes's absolute/relative needs distinction is intended as a descriptive psychological claim, a normative evaluative claim, or both; (2) whether Keynes's prediction of a substantially reduced working week implies that he regarded economic compulsion as the principal obstacle to human flourishing; and (3) whether the introduction of synthetic relative needs—needs that originate as positional comparisons but become culturally embedded as non-negotiable baseline expectations—undermines Keynes's optimism that satiation is achievable within roughly a century, and if so, whether this undermines Camp A's ethical reading or Camp B's empirical reading.

Third, an Evidence and Predictions section. For each crux, specify the argument, case, or empirical finding both sides agree would bear on it, with each camp's explicit prediction recorded in advance. Examples both sides should accept as relevant include: comparative historical analysis of post-war leisure and consumption patterns in high-productivity economies; behavioral-economic studies on positional goods and adaptation effects; and close textual analysis of Keynes's correspondence with figures in his artistic and literary circle regarding work and value. Each camp should state what result would weaken its own position.

The statement should be written in formal, precise prose, should not request bibliographies or external citations, and should treat both camps as good-faith interlocutors seeking exact disagreement rather than rhetorical victory.
