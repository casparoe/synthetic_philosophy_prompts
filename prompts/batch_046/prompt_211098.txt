I'm drafting an essay for a translation-theory workshop and would like comments on the argument's coherence, particularly whether I'm conflating two different levels of constraint. The working title is "Functional Isomorphism and Epistemic Asymmetry in the Translation of Self-Referential Form." The central case is Clément Marot's poem "Le Ton beau de Marot," a text that simultaneously laments the difficulty of preserving its author's distinctive tone across languages and demonstrates that tone through an intricate AABBA rhyme scheme. My thesis is that conventional evaluations of translations of this poem—such as those that ask whether an English version replicates the rhyme scheme—operate at the wrong level of abstraction. Instead, I propose that a translation should be judged by whether it preserves what I call the "epistemic asymmetry" of the original: the tension between the poem's explicit claim that Marot's tone is essentially untranslatable and the implicit demonstration that formal elegance can survive linguistic transfer. To make this concrete, I consider a hypothetical English rendering that abandons rhyme entirely but imposes a strict isosyllabic constraint—eight syllables per line, with a caesura pattern mimicking the original's rhythmic pauses—arguing that such a version might better reproduce the reader's cognitive conflict between message and medium than a rhymed version that sounds natural but loses the structural irony. I then extend this to a broader claim: in poems where form is part of the semantic content, the correct formal constraint for translation is not morphological equivalence but the reproduction of a specific cognitive dissonance profile. My questions are: (1) Is "epistemic asymmetry preservation" a genuinely distinct criterion from functional isomorphism, or am I just renaming the latter? (2) Does my isosyllabic example actually reproduce the asymmetry I describe, or does it merely substitute one arbitrary constraint for another? (3) Am I ignoring an obvious objection—that any translation that succeeds in producing the dissonance thereby undermines its own explicit content, and that this paradox is unavoidable regardless of formal choices? Please address these in order, and flag any place where the essay seems to smuggle in a false equivalence between form and cognition.
