I have been revisiting Hofstadter's discussion of the MU-puzzle in Gödel, Escher, Bach, specifically the claim that resolving it requires jumping out of the system. As I understand the argument, the MIU formal system generates propositions that are undecidable within its own axioms—deriving MU is impossible unless one steps into a meta-system where the rules governing M and U are recognized as arbitrary syntactic patterns rather than logical necessities. This seems structurally parallel to Gödel's first incompleteness theorem: any sufficiently powerful formal system produces true statements that are unprovable within the system, and grasping them requires cognition that is not computably bound by the base rules.

This framing raises a problem I have not seen cleanly resolved in rationalist discussions of AI cognition. If jumping out of the system is essentially non-computable meta-level access—cognition that violates the closure conditions of its own substrate—then recent demonstrations of analogical reasoning in large language models become puzzling. When a transformer rejects the implicit framing of a prompt and reconstructs the problem at a higher level of abstraction—what some researchers call distributional boundary crossing—should we classify this as genuine JOOTS? The dilemma I see is binary and unsatisfying: either transformers perform genuinely non-computable steps, which undermines physicalist computationalism about mind, or what appears to be JOOTS in models is merely complex intra-system pattern matching, which implies that human JOOTS is also just intra-system matching and Hofstadter's distinction collapses. Is there a coherent intermediate position—perhaps that JOOTS is computable but requires dynamic self-modification of the rule set rather than static meta-access? And if such a position holds, does the MU-puzzle actually establish anything distinctive about human cognition, or does it only illustrate the representational flexibility available to any sufficiently recursive architecture?
