As a doctoral candidate in political theory specializing in the ethics of administrative governance, I am constructing a reusable institutional audit protocol for my dissertation research committee—composed of legal scholars and policy practitioners without specialist philosophical training—to determine whether a given organizational practice exhibits the conditions Hannah Arendt associated with the banality of evil. The instrument must be executable within ninety minutes, must yield a documented determination suitable for sign-off by non-philosophers, and must clearly flag every point at which judgment rather than observation drives the conclusion. I want the procedure to translate Arendt’s analysis of Eichmann—specifically her account of thoughtlessness as a failure of moral imagination, her observation of bureaucratic role-absorption, her identification of functional euphemism in administrative language, and her later argument that judgment requires representative thinking or an enlarged mentality—into a practical flowchart in words, while incorporating a distinction I treat as necessary: that between first-order thoughtlessness, a failure to think about the concrete meaning of one’s actions, and second-order thoughtlessness, a failure to think about the institutional conditions that make one’s own thinking possible.

Please design the protocol as follows. First, a preliminary filter that distinguishes practices lacking malicious intent but potentially exhibiting structural moral failure, with an explicit stop condition that refers practices manifesting radical or demonic evil outside Arendt’s category to legal or theological adjudication rather than to this audit. Second, an ordered sequence of empirical tests for bureaucratic role-absorption, linguistic cliché, and organizational distance—which I am terming moral spacing—specifying for each what observable evidence counts as sufficient, what counts as ambiguous, and what counts as absent. Third, a judgment-stage for representative thinking: a structured standpoint-exchange test in which the auditor determines whether the institutional design permits agents and affected parties to occupy one another’s concrete perspective without abandoning their functional roles; this stage must be explicitly marked as an irreducible value judgment that cannot be collapsed into checklist compliance. Fourth, a hard-case branch for practices mediated by algorithmic abstraction or contractual delegation, where causal chains are fragmented and responsibility diffused across automated systems or subcontractors; this branch must specify how to weigh the absence of direct human contact against the presence or absence of structural feedback loops that restore standpoint accessibility.

For each stage, indicate where the procedure yields a definitive negative determination, where it yields a provisional positive requiring committee deliberation, and where it must halt because the question exceeds institutional audit and belongs to moral philosophy or legal culpability assessment. Then apply the procedure rigorously to three cases. Case A: a government welfare-eligibility algorithm that assigns denial scores through predictive risk modeling, where caseworkers describe adverse outcomes as optimization adjustments and possess no protocol for reviewing individual narratives. Case B: a multinational supply-chain audit framework that contractually delegates labor-inspection obligations to subcontractors, thereby removing direct observation of factory conditions from the primary firm’s compliance reports. Case C: a municipal climate-adaptation mandate that requires public testimony but channels it through standardized digital forms that strip contextual narrative, coupled with a mandatory stakeholder perspective session for officials; I anticipate that your procedure will likely classify this case as falling outside the banality framework because the standpoint-exchange mechanism is formally present, yet my own intuition is that the formal presence of exchange is vitiated by prior linguistic and procedural filtering, and I want to see precisely where the judgment-stage either captures that tension or collapses into the very bureaucratic rationality it is meant to diagnose—so include the result that I will not like if that is where the logic leads.

Finally, state precisely what this procedure cannot do: for example, whether it can establish legal guilt, whether it can distinguish Arendtian evil from ordinary institutional negligence, whether it can prescribe reform rather than merely flag conditions, and whether the distinction between first-order and second-order thoughtlessness can be preserved in practical use or must be collapsed. Identify the exact boundary between audit and moral philosophy, and specify which conceptual distinctions must survive the translation into procedure and which may be safely dropped for the sake of usability by practitioners who lack philosophical training. Write the response in formal, precise language appropriate for inclusion as a methodological appendix.
