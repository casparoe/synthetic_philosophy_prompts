The philosophical debate over privacy's nature and value has been stalled for decades between two positions that agree on nearly every empirical claim yet never converge. One camp treats privacy as informational sovereignty: surveillance wrongs are expropriations of data that belong to the individual, and privacy's value is instrumental to autonomy through control over personal information. The other camp treats privacy as a structural condition for democratic personhood: the harm is not theft but misrecognition, reducing persons to calculable profiles that preempt spontaneous political action, and its value is collective and constitutive rather than proprietary. Both condemn identical surveillance practices, cite the same research on chilling effects, and propose overlapping regulatory remedies, yet neither has persuaded the other. Is this disagreement substantive—about whether harm requires an individual rights-holder or can exist at a systemic level—or verbal, with the word "privacy" covering two distinct goods, one distributive and one constitutive? Are the sides answering different questions—one about justice in data allocation, one about the conditions for authentic public life—under a single heading? Or is a hidden formal question doing the work: does one side assume that privacy harm is aggregative, arising only when discrete data points are combined, while the other assumes it is immediate, with the predictive inference itself causing harm regardless of storage?

Diagnose this using what each side actually says about harm, autonomy, and the state's role, not by surveying privacy law. In particular, examine whether the impasse reflects bedrock intuitions about whether persons are fundamentally owners of information or beings whose freedom requires strategic opacity. Then propose what would count as progress. Would introducing a distinction between archive privacy—protection of recorded past facts—and horizon privacy—protection against predictive inference about future behavior—dissolve the disagreement by showing each camp is correct about a different temporal mode? Or does progress require abandoning the attempt to unify these concerns under one concept altogether? Please answer in American English.
