I'm trying to resolve a tension I keep running into in discussions of moral demandingness. The standard critique—going back to Wolf's moral-saints argument—seems to show that a life entirely organized around moral optimization is unattractive because it crowds out humor, personal projects, and non-instrumental relationships. But I wonder if that critique actually presupposes a false dichotomy. Couldn't one reply that there's simply an optimal point on the production-possibility frontier between moral achievement and personal flourishing, and that going past it is instrumentally self-defeating (burnout, reduced long-term impact) rather than conceptually wrong? If so, then the objection isn't to maximal moral goodness itself, but to an unsustainable strategy for pursuing it—much like running a factory past its efficient scale. What I want to know is whether this reframing saves the ideal of being maximally good, or whether it still misses something essential in the original critique—perhaps that certain non-moral goods aren't fungible inputs but are constitutive of a life that can be called good at all.
