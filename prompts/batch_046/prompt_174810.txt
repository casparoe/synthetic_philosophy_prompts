I'm constructing a taxonomy for a graduate value-theory handout on tranquilism—the view that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, while positive goods such as joy, achievement, love, or knowledge are valuable at best instrumentally. Rather than sorting people by the philosophers they cite, I want a branching taxonomy built from the logical space of answers to concrete diagnostic questions.

Please construct a decision tree with the following properties. Start from the root commitment: "Positive mental states and achievements have no independent intrinsic value; only the absence of craving and suffering matters." From there, build four successive splits, each phrased as a direct question that a respondent could answer without knowing philosophical labels:

1. Scope of absence: Does the value of tranquility apply only to conscious, experienced states of calm, or does it extend to non-conscious and even non-existent states (so that permanent unconsciousness or death can be positively good)?
2. Source of badness: Is craving or suffering bad because it frustrates a prior desire (a relational, desire-dependent account), or is it intrinsically bad regardless of prior mental content?
3. Hierarchical depth: Must second-order cravings—wanting to want something, desiring recognition for one's tranquility, or craving the absence of craving—also be eliminated, or do only first-order bodily and psychological cravings count?
4. Scale and prohibition: If an action produces widespread tranquility for many at the cost of one person's intense but brief suffering, does the scale of net tranquility determine the right choice, or is causing any suffering categorically prohibited regardless of outcome?

Locate in the resulting space the following named or constructed positions: a Schopenhauer-inspired view treating will-cessation as the sole good; Negative Utilitarianism; Stoic apatheia (only if it genuinely belongs in the tranquilist family); a synthetic position combining tranquilism with Frankfurt-style hierarchical desire-elimination (call it "Hierarchical Tranquilism"); and at least two unnamed combinations of answers. For every leaf, provide one sentence describing its most awkward practical or theoretical commitment.

Then identify any pair of leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate, and flag them explicitly. Also list the branch combinations that remain unoccupied, with a brief diagnosis for each: is the combination incoherent, merely unexplored, or unattractive for some reason?

Please organize your response in these sections, in this order:

1. The Root and Branch Questions (approx. 300 words): Present the tree as nested questions, explaining briefly what concrete case or commitment each split tests.
2. Leaf Profiles (approx. 250 words): For each leaf, state its answers to the four splits, name it if appropriate, and give the one-sentence awkward commitment.
3. Unoccupied Branches and Separation Failures (approx. 200 words): List empty combinations with diagnoses; flag indistinguishable pairs.
4. Design Rationale (approx. 150 words): Explain why these four splits were chosen, what they capture about the tranquilist family, and which important distinctions they omit.

The point is to sort respondents by what they actually believe about cases—for example, "Is a permanently unconscious being in a state of value?" or "Should a meditator suppress her desire to be admired for her calmness?"—not by which label they prefer. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not frame the deliverable as a scored quiz; use a branching taxonomy only.
