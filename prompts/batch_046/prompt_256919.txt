I'm working through some material for a philosophy-of-mind reading group and we've been trying to apply Dennett's framework of real patterns to phenomena that sit awkwardly between the design stance and the intentional stance. The standard taxonomy is familiar enough—the physical stance explains via laws and structures, the design stance via functional organization and teleology, and the intentional stance via rational-agent predictions—but I'm trying to understand the ontology that underwrites the claim that some patterns are 'real' in a way that makes them more than useful fictions. Specifically, I'm interested in cases where a system's behavior is predictively tractable only if we attribute beliefs and desires, yet the underlying architecture is best characterized in purely functional or algorithmic terms without any explicit representation of those propositional attitudes—think of a distributed market mechanism or an adaptive control system whose equilibrium properties are describable only through game-theoretic rationality conditions, even though no module in the system encodes anything like a belief state. Does Dennett's criterion for real-patternhood—robust, projectible, non-redundant predictive compression—apply stance-by-stance, so that a pattern can be real at the intentional level but ontologically absent at the design level? Or is there a single, stance-transcendent pattern that is merely differently accessed? I want a careful conceptual explanation of how pattern-reality is supposed to be identified across these stance boundaries, particularly whether predictive utility at one stance can confer ontological weight even when the corresponding structural description at another stance is either intractable or nonexistent, and what this implies for the claim that the intentional stance is an ontologically neutral predictive strategy rather than a commitment to the existence of mental states.
