In evolutionary biology and its philosophy, Gould and Lewontin's spandrel analogy is routinely extended well beyond its original scope: the triangular spaces between the arches of San Marco are geometric necessities—byproducts of placing round arches on rectangular columns—that only later received decorative mosaics, and they have no independent structural purpose. Critics of strict adaptationism invoke this to argue that many human traits—language, numerical cognition, or certain affective dispositions—are similarly non-adaptive byproducts of selection for other capacities, with evolutionary-psychological narratives serving as the post-hoc mosaics. The analogy is used to license a methodological stance: that searching for direct adaptive functions for such traits is conceptually confused, like asking what load-bearing problem a spandrel was designed to solve.

Audit the analogy in detail. Provide an explicit correspondence table: what in evolutionary explanation plays the role of the arch, the column, the spandrel itself, the mosaicist, and the structural engineer? Then identify the precise points of breakdown: for instance, whether evolutionary byproducts possess the geometric determinism that makes architectural spandrels inevitable, and whether the distinction between a true spandrel and an exaptation—a feature originally a byproduct but subsequently co-opted for function—collapses once the analogy is pressed. Examine what the analogy quietly imports about intentional design, the separability of form and function, and the legitimacy of reverse-engineering purpose, and assess whether these imports are defensible in a process that lacks a designer. Determine whether the methodological conclusion—that adaptationist hypotheses should face default suspicion for certain classes of trait—survives once these disanalogies are made explicit, or whether the analogy was carrying the burden of proof all along. Finally, consider whether an alternative analogy drawn from distributed or emergent systems—rather than from static structural geometry—would better capture the relation between selection, developmental constraint, and evolutionary byproduct, and say precisely what that substitution would change about the anti-adaptationist argument.
