I am currently in a graduate seminar on normative ethics, and a recent disagreement with a colleague about whether moral conflicts can be genuinely unavoidable—rather than merely practically messy—has left me stuck on a conceptual knot I cannot untie alone. Specifically, I want to understand the argument that some dilemmas are structural, meaning they are generated by the normative framework itself rather than by contingent circumstances that sharper reasoning or a better world could dissolve. Could you explain, in an intuitive but conceptually precise way, why accepting structural dilemmas seems to entail that an agent can act entirely correctly and yet rationally bear what is sometimes called moral residue—a lingering guilt or regret that is not a psychological malfunction but an appropriate response to an unrecoverable loss of value? I am especially interested in whether this conclusion requires a strong thesis of incommensurability among moral reasons, or whether it can survive on a weaker claim about the non-substitutability of particular obligations. As you work through the dependency, I would like to see exactly where the line falls between rational regret, which tracks a real moral cost, and self-deceptive rationalization, which merely alleviates discomfort without tracking anything normative. I do not need a historical survey, bibliography, or citation of specific texts; I want to understand how these conceptual pieces fit together, and whether the case for genuine moral dilemmas collapses if one rejects incommensurability but retains non-substitutability.
