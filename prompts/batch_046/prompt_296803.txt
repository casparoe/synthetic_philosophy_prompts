You are a novelist with deep familiarity with political philosophy, specifically the problem of dirty hands—the idea that those who govern may be required to commit morally wrong acts for the common good and thereby incur a lasting moral stain. Before you begin, take five minutes to brainstorm: identify three structural conditions that distinguish genuine dirty hands from ordinary corruption or self-serving rationalization, and sketch one concrete mechanism by which moral residue might fail to adhere to the agent who intended to absorb it.

Now write a short story of approximately 800 words. Set it in the capital of a fictional post-revolutionary republic during the first winter of a fragile transitional government. The protagonist is Dr. Lena Voss, a physician who served on the transitional council and who, six months earlier, authorized a targeted deception—fabricating evidence of an armed militia's strength—to convince a neutral faction to suppress a coup attempt without bloodshed. The deception worked; the coup was averted; but it required publicly framing innocent civilians as collaborators. Voss believed she was taking the stain onto herself so that others—especially the neutral faction—would remain morally clean.

The story should open with Voss entering the National Archive of Moral Residue, a newly created institution that does not record victories or crimes, but assigns "debts" to specific individuals according to who holds institutional power to have their actions interpreted as sacrifice rather than error. She expects to find her entry marked as a debtor's record, confirming that she alone carries the cost. Instead, she finds the debt distributed across the names of the civilians who were framed—those without the authority to have their suffering recognized as necessary—while her own name appears in a separate annex labeled "collective strategic wisdom," with no stain attached.

Dramatize the moment she reads these entries. Do not allow her to speak in philosophical abstractions; let her understanding emerge through physical detail—the texture of the ledger paper, the silence of the archivist, the sound of the council celebrating outside. The narrative should make clear that the concept of dirty hands she relied upon assumed a kind of personal moral accounting—one agent takes the dirt, the community stays clean—that the archive reveals to be structurally false: the dirt settles not on the one who chooses it, but on the one who lacks the power to have it named as choice.

End the story not with redemption or suicide, but with Voss closing the ledger and recognizing that the theory she trusted was not wrong about the necessity of wrong action, but wrong about who bears the mark. The final image should suggest that the "hands" in question were never hers alone, and that the dirt was never meant to be washed—only assigned.

Write in a formal, polished literary style, with precise attention to setting and psychological texture. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to specific scholarly texts by title or author. The story should be accessible to readers unfamiliar with the philosophical debate, yet accurate enough in its structure that someone familiar with the classic formulation of dirty hands and its critiques—particularly the challenge that moral residue is distributed by power rather than intention—would recognize the argument being dramatized.
