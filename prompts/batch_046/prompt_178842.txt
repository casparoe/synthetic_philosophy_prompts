After a long and exhausting argument with a friend last night, I want to press a position that I think is defensible but rarely articulated with precision: that microbial pathogens, multi-decadal climate oscillations, and major volcanic events should be recognized as exercising what we might call interventionist agency in historical explanation. By this I do not mean intentional agency, but a structured, mechanism-specific capacity to redirect demographic regimes, state fiscal architectures, and trade networks in directions that human actors alone could not have generated—without this recognition collapsing into environmental determinism. My friend insists that attributing agency to a bacterium or an eruption is only a metaphor, and that all real explanatory work is done by describing contingent human institutional responses to these stimuli; she worries that treating non-human forces as actors erases human choice and revives crude geographical determinism in new language. Please argue that these non-human entities genuinely possess a form of causal agency that shapes historical outcomes irreducible to background conditions, but that recognizing this agency actually protects human autonomy in historical explanation rather than undermining it—because it forces the historian to model the interaction between structured non-human interventions and contingent human responses, rather than treating either pole as a deterministic baseline. In making your case, distinguish this view sharply from both Latour-style symmetry and from older deterministic frameworks, and explain why the refusal to grant any causal standing to these forces produces a distorted, overly intentionalist narrative.
