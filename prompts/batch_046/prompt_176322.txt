Covering the archaeology of belief, I've noticed that efforts to reconstruct ancient religious institutions from material remains have reached a durable methodological impasse. Cognitive archaeologists read standardized burial orientations and storage architectures as traces of shared cosmological representations, while materiality scholars treat identical patterns as emergent properties of practice-networks in which objects are active agents—making the attribution of beliefs an unwarranted internalist projection. Both camps rely on the same stratigraphic sequences and ethnographic analogies, yet the divide has not narrowed since the post-processual turn. Is this a substantive dispute about whether mental representations are necessary to explain institutional regularity, a verbal divergence over what counts as an institution, or a hidden empirical question about practice transmission? Given your diagnosis, what—if anything—would actually move it: a neuroarchaeological result, a conceptual distinction between representation and scaffold, or an argument that the question is ill-posed?
