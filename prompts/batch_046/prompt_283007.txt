My thesis is that post-commissurotomy patients shouldn't be interpreted as housing "two minds" or as retaining a fully unified single mind with a disconnected module; instead, they instantiate a single conscious system running with a degraded inter-hemispheric consistency protocol—essentially the mental analog of a distributed database that has lost its synchronous replication link and fallen back on eventual consistency. I borrow the engineering vocabulary deliberately: the corpus callosum functions like a consensus channel, and its severance doesn't spawn a second processor so much as it forces the remaining architecture into a partition-tolerant mode where local hemispheric states diverge and only slowly, if ever, reconcile. The audience will be a mixed philosophy-of-mind and cognitive-science reading group—mostly phenomenologically inclined graduate students and one faculty member in philosophy of neuroscience who is sympathetic to materialist unity-denial. They know the classic Sperry and Gazzaniga results well, they are deeply suspicious of importing technical computer-science analogies into first-person phenomena, and they tend to treat "distributed system" talk as a category error unless it earns its keep phenomenologically. Prepare my Q&A defense sheet: list the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way these people would actually phrase them (not generic "gotchas"), rank them by how damaging they are to my proposal, and for each give me a concise reply I can deliver in about two minutes plus an honest note on where that reply is weakest. I need to know what I should concede up front—probably something about the analogy's limits—what I should hold firm on—probably that "unity" is a functional property of synchronization rather than a metaphysical given—and I want you to flag the one objection I'm hoping nobody asks: the one that targets whether my model can distinguish split-brain divergence from ordinary inattention or confabulation without circular appeal to behavioral reports. Keep everything in conceptual terms; don't ask me for bibliographies or citations, and don't frame answers as literature surveys.
