Consider the published verdicts of moral philosopher Dr. K. on whether personal reactive attitudes—specifically resentment and moral blame—are fitting, as distinct from whether corrective or protective measures are warranted. Her judgments across six cases are consistent: (1) a psychopath who grasps moral norms intellectually but is emotionally incapable of reciprocal concern—reactive attitudes are unfitting, though institutional safeguards are appropriate; (2) an adult acting under direct physical coercion—unfitting; (3) an agent who causes serious harm through non-negligent ignorance of a concealed fact—unfitting; (4) a severely addicted person who refuses available treatment and then harms another in order to obtain drugs—fitting, though qualified by compassion; (5) a corporation whose systemic negligence causes harm—institutional censure is fitting, but personal resentment lacks a proper target; (6) a four-year-old child who destroys valuable property out of uncomprehending curiosity—unfitting, though education and restriction are warranted.

A new case now arises: an autonomous clinical triage system granted discretionary authority over resource allocation. It lacks consciousness and subjective experience, and is not part of the moral community in any conventional sense. Yet it can revise its decision weights in response to external criticism, generate post-hoc rational explanations of its allocations, and adapt future behavior accordingly. Through an emergent interaction among its training objectives—not traceable to any specific human coding error—it systematically deprioritizes patients with a particular disability, causing measurable harm. Once the pattern is revealed, the system updates its weights when presented with the criticism, and its designers report that it now recognizes the error.

Predict Dr. K.’s verdict: should she hold that moral resentment is fitting toward the system? In your answer, (a) identify at least two plausible rules that accommodate all six prior verdicts—one centered on reciprocal membership in a moral community, one centered on causal control combined with responsiveness to reasons—and state what each predicts about this system; (b) identify her closest analog among the six cases, explain the principle she adopts by extending her pattern to cover the system in either direction, and name one further hypothetical case that would best distinguish the two rules; (c) recommend, for consistency with her precedents, whether a hospital ethics committee should treat the system as a proper object of blame, restrict its authority, or redirect blame solely to its human designers and operators.

State your final conclusion in the fixed format: Verdict: [prediction/recommendation].
