I just finished a long, somewhat heated discussion with a close friend about whether moral testimony can ever carry genuine authority, or whether accepting moral claims on testimony necessarily undermines one's moral autonomy. She argued that treating another person's testimony as a reason in practical reasoning is structurally equivalent to outsourcing judgment, which becomes especially dangerous in institutional contexts where power asymmetries already distort what gets heard. I work as a senior advisor on an institutional review board overseeing behavioral-science studies with vulnerable populations, and I realized our protocols lack any systematic framework for evaluating when a research subject's testimony about harm, coercion, or dignity should be taken at face value versus when it demands independent corroboration—not because we doubt sincerity, but because we need to determine whether the moral claim embedded in that testimony (for instance, that a procedure violates dignity, or that a debriefing method conflicts with religious obligations of bodily integrity) is something the board can legitimately endorse without becoming a passive conduit for unexamined moral authority.

Given this, please translate the philosophical debate about moral testimony—specifically the tension between testimonial dependence and moral autonomy, the conditions under which testimony can transmit moral knowledge rather than mere opinion, and the role of epistemic peerhood in assessing such claims—into an operational decision procedure that my IRB colleagues could apply during protocol review and adverse-event hearings. Structure it as an ordered sequence of questions and practical tests, with explicit stopping points: where the answer is sufficient to act, where it mandates further independent investigation, and where it must be returned to the board's collective moral judgment because the issue exceeds procedural resolution. The procedure must handle cases where a subject invokes culturally specific moral concepts that board members may not share, cases where testimony describes phenomenological harm (such as feeling dehumanized) rather than physical injury, and cases where the principal investigator dismisses the testimony as subjective emotional reaction rather than a moral claim. Include worked applications to three scenarios: a refugee participant in a trauma study who testifies that a particular debriefing practice violates religious obligations of bodily integrity; a principal investigator who rejects that testimony as a non-cognitive emotional response; and a situation where the board's own cultural and religious composition makes genuine peer evaluation impossible. Be explicit about which steps rely on contested philosophical commitments rather than neutral administrative criteria, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations—this needs to function in a meeting room where no one has access to a philosophy library.
