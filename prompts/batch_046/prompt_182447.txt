I am a science and ideas journalist preparing a long-form feature on whether moral blame can genuinely attach to belief itself, and I have constructed an argument that brings William James’s conditions for legitimate belief into direct tension with W.K. Clifford’s evidentialist prohibition by introducing a premise neither author foregrounds: doxastic involuntarism. The argument proceeds as follows. First, Clifford holds that it is wrong always and everywhere to believe anything upon insufficient evidence, treating belief as an action with moral weight. Second, James permits belief without sufficient evidence only when the option is live, forced, and momentous, framing such belief as an exercise of will. Third, both accounts implicitly assume doxastic voluntarism—that belief is something we can directly choose to adopt or withhold. Fourth, if doxastic involuntarism is correct—if we can influence inquiry only indirectly and cannot will belief itself—then Clifford’s moral condemnation misses its target, since blame requires control, and James’s will-to-believe thesis is impossible in any direct sense, since there is no voluntary act of believing to license. Fifth, therefore, if involuntarism holds, neither prohibition nor permission applies to belief proper; both must be redirected to voluntary practices of inquiry. Sixth, yet the very conditions James identifies—liveness, forcedness, and momentousness—are precisely those in which belief is least susceptible to voluntary control, because high stakes and limited alternatives overwhelm rational agency. Seventh, consequently, James’s exception applies exactly where doxastic voluntarism is weakest, meaning his defense operates only in the domain where belief is least like an action. Eighth, so either we reject involuntarism and preserve both Clifford’s universal blame and James’s universal permission, or we accept it and discover that the ethics of belief collapses into an ethics of trivial, voluntary assent—rendering the classic debate about momentous conviction moot.

My editorial instinct is that a subtle slide occurs somewhere between the fourth and seventh steps—perhaps an equivocation between direct and indirect voluntarism, or an unexamined assumption that moral responsibility requires direct control rather than merely accountable agency—but I cannot isolate the fault line with confidence. Which numbered premise should I reject, what makes it initially plausible, and what does abandoning it cost for the broader project of holding people ethically responsible for their convictions?
