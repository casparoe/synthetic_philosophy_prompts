I am running a postgraduate seminar in political philosophy—twelve students, ninety minutes—on the epistemic structure of institutional reform. I want an in-class game or structured role-play that makes the group feel the specific force behind Chesterton’s fence, but not as generic risk aversion. Instead, they should encounter the asymmetry between the observable burdens of an institutional rule and the permanently hidden protective functions it may supply, combined with the irreversibility of social experimentation once a rule has been abolished.

Please design an activity around a fictional community—call it Meridian—governed by six apparently obsolete or burdensome rules (for instance, restrictions on building near a riverbank, mandatory rotation of communal land use, a prohibition on consolidating small workshops, and so on). The rules should each carry an immediately visible cost: inefficiency, inconvenience, or lost autonomy. The students should be divided into small deliberative bodies with authority to vote on retention or abolition.

Crucially, arrange the information and incentives so that the hidden protective functions—if any—are unknown to the participants. Once a rule is abolished, reveal its hidden purpose only through an irreversible crisis card: for example, the abolished riverbank restriction allows a development that is then destroyed by a predictable flood, and the destroyed district cannot be rebuilt. Reinstating an abolished rule should carry a severe collective penalty—representing institutional disruption, eroded trust, and administrative cost—so that restoration is technically possible but practically ruinous. For rules that are retained, reveal nothing at the end: the students should never learn whether those rules were genuinely protective or merely useless dead weight. The payoff scheme should be non-monetary—perhaps collective welfare points that determine whether Meridian “thrives” or “collapses” at the close of the session.

The sequence should allow students to experience the intuitive pull to tear down fences in the first phase, then suffer the consequences of irreversible removals in the second, and finally confront the silence surrounding the rules they kept. The debrief should map these moments precisely onto the Burkean/Chesterton argument: the visibility of costs versus the invisibility of counterfactual benefits; the problem of negative institutional knowledge; and the asymmetry between destruction and preservation. Include an honest account of what will predictably fail the first time—likely an over-cautious group that refuses to abolish anything, or a competitive dynamic that encourages reckless removal for dramatic effect—and how to manage it. Also state frankly which feature of the position—perhaps the normative claim that accumulated wisdom deserves deference regardless of present understanding—the activity cannot reproduce and that you, as facilitator, must supply.

Please write in British English. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not require students to recall specific texts or authors by name during the activity itself. Inject into the framing one specific niche concept—such as the institutional equivalent of “unknown unknowns” in epistemology, or the idea that institutional rules function as tacit-knowledge kludges—so that the prompt is not something a model could answer purely from common training patterns.
