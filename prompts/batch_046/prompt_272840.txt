I am composing a cycle of historical novels that adopt, in each volume, the internal epistemic framework of a particular community—monastic, scientific, bureaucratic—rather than imposing a contemporary omniscient perspective. To maintain philosophical consistency across scenes, I require a reusable decision procedure I can apply whenever a character holds, questions, or acts upon a belief. Specifically, I need to classify any given belief as either a hinge proposition—part of the world-picture that makes empirical doubt about other matters intelligible—or as an ordinary claim open to verification, falsification, or revision within the narrative world. The procedure should be grounded in hinge epistemology and in Wittgenstein’s account in On Certainty: the notion that certainty functions not as the outcome of accumulated evidence but as the scaffold within which evidence operates, together with the riverbed image in which some beliefs form the stable channel whilst others flow over them, with the bed itself capable of gradual shift but never of wholesale simultaneous replacement.

Please provide the procedure as an ordered checklist of questions, designed for repeated use without theoretical training. For each stage, specify what response allows the analysis to continue, what response forces classification as a hinge, and what response requires the decision to be referred back to me on artistic or ethical grounds rather than philosophical ones. The procedure must accommodate three hard cases: beliefs that serve as hinges for one community but not for another; beliefs currently in transition from bedrock to flowing water; and beliefs that appear empirical but in fact underpin an entire form of life.

Then run the procedure on the following three narrative proposals.

First, a monk in 1240 accepts the geocentric ordering of the cosmos not through astronomical observation but because it is woven into his liturgical calendar and sacramental practice; a novice asks whether the spheres might be arranged differently. Second, a Victorian engineer treats the pressure thresholds of a steam valve as measurable and correctable, whilst treating the moral superiority of industrial progress over agrarian life as beyond serious question. Third—a case where I expect the result to conflict with my artistic intentions—a protagonist in a contemporary liberal democracy holds that individual consent must always take precedence over collective welfare; within her world-picture this functions as a hinge, yet I had hoped to portray her eventual dissent from that principle as fully rational without destroying the intelligibility of her entire framework.

Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot settle, and mark clearly the boundary at which philosophical analysis ends and my authorial judgement must take over.
