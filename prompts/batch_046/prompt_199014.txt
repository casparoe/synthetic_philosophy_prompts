To avoid both well-worn split-brain commentary and generic extended-mind debates, I am adopting a specific conceptual framing: extending Hofstadter's strange-loop model of the self, I treat the "I" not as a single brain-bound symbolic pattern but as a self-referential loop that could, in principle, span multiple cortical architectures linked by direct, high-bandwidth neural interfaces. Call this the multi-loop problem. Given that framing, which open questions, implications, or lines of work actually deserve sustained intellectual and scientific attention over the coming decade, and which are overrated or likely dead ends? I want a defended prioritization, not a survey. Specifically, address three clusters—though you may reorder, replace, or subdivide them if your reasoning demands it. First, boundary conditions: under what conditions does shared working memory and mutual self-reference constitute one distributed self rather than two tightly coupled but separate ones? Second, asymmetry and moral status: if one node in the network can be edited, disconnected, or suffer focal damage, does the remaining system inherit obligations, rights, or a continuous personal identity, and what does that imply for responsibility? Third, tractability: are there foreseeable experimental or modeling approaches—such as coupled neural dynamics simulations or distributed cognitive architectures in non-human models—that could yield meaningful constraints, or does the issue remain permanently speculative? Rank these clusters by importance for guiding serious public and scientific discourse, using explicit criteria: for instance, how many other questions depend on resolving this one, what ethical or practical stakes hang on it, whether it is currently neglected relative to its importance, and what empirical or conceptual finding would cause you to reverse the ranking. I am not seeking a bibliography, a reading list, interview suggestions, or citations; I want an agenda that tells me where conceptual clarity would genuinely reshape our understanding of identity and neural engineering, and where effort is better directed elsewhere.
