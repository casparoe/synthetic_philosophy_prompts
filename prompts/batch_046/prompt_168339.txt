My friend and I just spent the evening arguing about pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism, specifically whether a peer reviewer’s belief that a statistical result is robust counts as knowledge differently when she is writing a journal report versus advising a regulatory body. She advanced three objections: first, that institutional norms fix the epistemic standard to the peer-review context, so the belief’s status does not shift with downstream practical stakes; second, that theoretical assessments are not action-guiding in the sense pragmatic encroachment requires; and third, that my view absurdly implies she simultaneously knows and fails to know the same proposition when she occupies both roles. I replied that institutional norms are just aggregated stakes, that peer-review judgments guide funding and citation decisions, and that contextualists already accept context-relative shifts rather than simultaneous contradiction. Please adjudicate our exchange move by move: rate each of her three objections and each of my three replies from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification. Then tell us what both sides are missing: is there a version of pragmatic encroachment that blocks my objection without appealing to institutional fixity, and is there a principled distinction between theoretical and practical belief that does not collapse into an ad hoc defense?
