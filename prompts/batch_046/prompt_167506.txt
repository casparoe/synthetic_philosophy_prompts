I was listening to a podcast about the quest for the historical Jesus and the host tried to lay out how scholars actually filter real sayings from ones the church made up later. He said there are four main criteria: multple attestation means if Matthew and Luke both record a saying it's probably authentic since they worked indepedantly; dissimilarity means if something doesn't sound like what a first-century rabbi or the early Christians would invent, then Jesus must have said it himself; coherence means you build a core profile of Jesus first and then keep other sayings that match that vibe; and embarrassment means if a detail is awkward for the church—like Jesus being baptized by John—then nobody would have fabricated it. He claimed that stacking all four works like an algorithm, and if a saying clears every hurdle historians can call it ipsissima verba with about 90% confidence, which is why we can settle that Jesus was an apocalyptic prophet who rejected family ties. He also said the standard criticism is that the whole thing is circular: you already have to assume Jesus was weird and discontinuous with Judaism to apply dissimilarity, so you're just confirming your own bias. I think he's mostly right but my roommate, who sat in on a seminar about this, said coherence and embarrassment aren't really independant checks at all, they're more like post-hoc adjustments you apply after you've already picked your favorites, which would break the algorithm idea if true. Can you go through his summary sentence by sentence and tell me what's accurate, what's sloppy or overstated, and what's flat wrong? I especially want to know whether treating the criteria as an algorithm that can settle who Jesus was is a misrepresentation of how historians actually use them, whether the circularity objection hits all four criteria or mainly just dissimilarity, and if my roommate's point about coherence and embarrassment being post-hoc is something serious scholars actually worry about or just a clever-sounding complaint.
