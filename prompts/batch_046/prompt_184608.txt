I'm a senior editor at an interdisciplinary journal that publishes comparative history, and my desk editors keep receiving submissions framed as "Why Britain and not China?"—the Great Divergence, the Needham question, and analogues asking why the Scientific Revolution consolidated in Europe rather than in the Islamic Golden Age or the Ming court. I want a reusable decision procedure, runnable in about fifteen minutes without philosophy training, that determines whether a contrastive "why X rather than Y" framing is well-posed enough to send out for peer review or should be returned for reconceptualization. Ground it in what philosophy of explanation actually delivers—explanatory contrast classes, causal symmetry, the distinction between selecting an explanandum and selecting a comparison class, and the conditions under which a counterfactual baseline can be stabilized—not in historiographical convention. The procedure should list ordered tests, state what counts as a passing answer for each, flag the steps that are value judgments disguised as procedure, and specify exactly where it halts with "reframe" versus where it hands the decision to me. Apply it to three submissions: (1) "Why did sustained per-capita growth begin in Britain around 1750 rather than in the Yangtze Delta?"; (2) "Why did the Ming court halt long-distance voyages rather than expand them, as Portugal did?"; and (3) a case where the procedure returns "reframe required" but a competent historian would still defend the comparison—make that tension explicit and say how an editor should resolve it. Also state what the procedure cannot do, especially regarding counterfactual histories that lack stable baselines. If the task rests on a confused premise—say, that well-posedness is binary rather than graded, or that any "why X not Y" can be repaired by widening the comparison class without changing the explanatory target—push back plainly rather than complying politely.
