After a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a close friend about medieval Islamic philosophy, I want an accuracy audit of how he summarized Avicenna's flying-man thought experiment. He put it to me roughly as follows: Avicenna asks us to imagine a fully rational human being created in an instant, suspended in empty space, blindfolded, with limbs spread apart so that he feels no tactile contact, and lacking all sensory experience. My friend insisted that Avicenna's point is precisely that such a being would still immediately affirm his own existence—"I am"—while remaining entirely ignorant of whether he possesses a body. From this, my friend concluded that Avicenna had demonstrated the soul is a Cartesian-style thinking substance wholly separable from the body, and that this single argument already refutes any form of physicalism about the mind, since if self-awareness requires no bodily mediation then consciousness cannot be a neural process. He went further and said that Avicenna took this to prove the soul's immortality: because the soul's primary act of self-affirmation does not depend on the body, the destruction of the body at death cannot extinguish the soul. He also mentioned, almost in passing, that this is essentially the same cogito argument Descartes rediscovered in the Meditations, only a thousand years earlier and without the methodological doubt.

I have the vague sense that this is mostly in the right neighborhood but that several moves conflate distinct claims Avicenna actually separates—perhaps the inference to separability, perhaps the slide from self-awareness to immortality, perhaps the assimilation to Descartes. But I am not well-versed enough in Avicenna's psychology or in the precise structure of the argument in his psychological writings to say where the line should be drawn. Which parts of my friend's summary would a careful reader of Avicenna accept without reservation? Which parts are defensible simplifications that a scholar might allow in popular exposition? Which are subtle misrepresentations—conflations of separate arguments, anachronistic framing, or overstatements of what the thought experiment actually establishes? And do the conclusions he draws—that physicalism is refuted and that immortality is secured—follow from the accurate parts of the summary, or do they depend on the errors?
