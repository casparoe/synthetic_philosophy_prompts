I am preparing questions for a podcast interview with a philosopher who defends what she calls a "Keynesian leisure ethic." Her position is that Keynes's prediction of radically reduced labor was not a forecast of hedonistic consumption or individual self-cultivation, but a normative vision of "structured idleness"—a form of life that can only be sustained by collective institutions such as public cultural infrastructures, amateur artistic societies, and civic education programs, without which abundant free time collapses into anomie rather than flourishing. She argues that this reading is anchored in Keynes's distinction between absolute needs and relative needs, and she insists that post-scarcity societies must suppress relative needs through deliberate social norms rather than through market mechanisms. Crucially, she maintains that this ideal is neither Aristotelian flourishing, which requires virtuous activity, nor standard utilitarian welfare maximization, but a distinct category of cultivated leisure that depends on institutional scaffolding rather than on individual choice.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the actual content of this view rather than asking her to restate Keynes's familiar essay. Include at least one question challenging how her institutional account differs from conventional welfare-state liberalism that also supplies public goods, with a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that welfare states provide income security but not formative practices. Include at least one question asking whether Keynes's association with the Bloomsbury Group—whose members often treated aesthetic experience as an elite pursuit—introduces a class-biased distortion into his economic argument, with a follow-up anticipating her dismissal of biographical context as philosophically irrelevant. Include at least one question asking what patterns in actual post-industrial societies—without requesting specific bibliographic references—would count against her claim that unstructured leisure produces anomie, with a follow-up keyed to her probable citation of Scandinavian leisure structures; in that follow-up, press whether those examples genuinely demonstrate institutional necessity or merely correlate with pre-existing cultural capital. End with a question about whether a society that achieves material abundance but refuses to build these formative institutions has thereby failed morally, not merely failed practically, and briefly indicate how she might distinguish moral failure from simple inefficiency.
