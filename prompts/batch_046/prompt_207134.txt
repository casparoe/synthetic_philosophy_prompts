As a novelist who has spent years constructing characters from scattered memories and unreliable narration, I keep returning to an institutional practice I encountered while researching a novel about displacement: in asylum tribunals, adjudicators routinely require applicants to deliver a continuous first-person autobiographical account spanning years of trauma, and they treat temporal inconsistencies or moments of third-person dissociation not as psychological sequelae of violence but as indicators of deception. What metaphysics of the self does that evidentiary protocol silently presuppose? Must it assume a substantive, diachronically unified ego that owns its memories like possessions, or can it be reconciled with Dennett's center of narrative gravity, according to which the self is an interpretive construct that can fragment without vanishing? Reconstruct the implicit theory of personal identity embedded in the protocol, say whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined—compatible with rival accounts that do not treat fragmentation as deception—and tell me exactly which credibility rules would have to change if the tribunal genuinely adopted the narrative-gravity view rather than treating it as a decorative metaphor.
