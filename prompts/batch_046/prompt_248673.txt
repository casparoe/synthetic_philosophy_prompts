Exam question: Consider a listener in an isolated linguistic community who receives testimony about local botanical taxonomy from a speaker who is present and apparently sincere. The listener has no prior observational experience of these plants, no statistical basis for assessing the speaker's reliability, and knows that the speaker's belief derives entirely from prior testimony in a chain that extends back to an original observer who is now permanently inaccessible. Does the listener acquire testimonial knowledge? In answering, distinguish reductionist from anti-reductionist frameworks, and specifically address whether Burge's acceptance principle and Moran's assurance view can accommodate this case of testimonial isolation without collapsing into one another.

Student answer: Reductionists, in the tradition of Hume and developed by Coady, maintain that testimonial justification must be reducible to other epistemic sources—perception, memory, or inductive inference. Because our listener lacks any independent access to the botanical facts and possesses no statistical track record for this speaker, reductionism correctly withholds knowledge. Anti-reductionists reject this reduction. Burge's acceptance principle grants a default entitlement: in the absence of specific defeaters, a listener may accept what is presented. Here the listener detects no insincerity or contradiction, so she is prima facie justified in believing the speaker. Moran's assurance view goes further by treating testimony not as information transfer but as an interpersonal commitment: the speaker offers her word and thereby becomes answerable to the listener. This normative bond gives the listener a direct, non-inferential reason to believe. Crucially, this assurance travels with the content even through mediated chains, so the listener need not trace belief back to the original observer. One might object that, since the chain never terminates in an accessible observational source, assurance eventually requires some non-testimonial foundation—thereby collapsing Moran's view back into reductionism. But this misreads Moran. His account implicitly requires that the speaker herself possess an observational or inductively supported basis for her assurance; if she lacks one, her commitment is empty. In our case, the speaker's belief is purely testimonial, so she cannot genuinely offer assurance. Consequently, the listener's belief is not fully justified. Thus, while Burge's principle supplies initial entitlement, Moran's view actually shows that pure anti-reductionism fails in isolated chain cases, and knowledge is ultimately denied.
