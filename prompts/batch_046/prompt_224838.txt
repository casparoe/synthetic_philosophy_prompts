I work in distributed systems and have been trying to map some philosophical problems onto error-handling models, but one argument I constructed about perception keeps collapsing in a way I cannot debug. I have no formal philosophy background, so I am likely misusing terminology.

Consider the following chain about disjunctivism and the argument from illusion:

(1) In both a veridical perception of an oak tree and a perfect hallucination of an oak tree, the subject possesses an experience with identical phenomenal character: the same visual shape, color saturation, and spatial presence.
(2) Disjunctivism holds that these are not the same kind of mental state—one is direct awareness of the tree itself, the other is awareness of no real object—despite that identical phenomenal character.
(3) If two mental states share every feature accessible from the first-person perspective, then a rational subject has no internal basis to classify them into different ontological kinds.
(4) Yet disjunctivism requires that the subject can, in principle, correctly identify which kind of state they are in; otherwise the distinction between veridical and illusory is purely external and epistemically useless to the subject.
(5) Therefore disjunctivism must allow for some hidden internal feature—call it a phenomenological checksum—that distinguishes the two states even when phenomenal character is identical.
(6) If such a checksum exists and is part of the mental state, then in veridical perception the subject is aware not only of the tree but also of this internal marker.
(7) That violates the directness of perception: the subject’s awareness is mediated by an internal feature, which is precisely what representationalist theories claim.
(8) So disjunctivism either denies the subject any epistemic access to the distinction (rendering it useless) or admits an internal mediator (collapsing it into representationalism).
(9) Hence disjunctivism is either incoherent or redundant.

I want to preserve (1) and (2), because they seem to capture what disjunctivists actually claim, and I would like to keep the intuition that perception can be direct. But (9) feels too strong—if disjunctivism collapsed this easily, it would not have serious defenders. Which step contains the bug? Is it (3), on the grounds that first-person access is not required for kind-distinction? Is it (5), because I am inventing a “checksum” that disjunctivists never committed to? Or is there a modal slide between (6) and (7) that illegitimately equates being part of a state with being an object of awareness? If I must pay a cost, I would rather pay in (4) than in (2), but I want to know exactly what I am buying and what I am giving up.
