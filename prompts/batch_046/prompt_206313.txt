You are a senior philosopher of value with deep familiarity with absurdism, naturalist theories of meaning in life, and reflective equilibrium methods. Before answering, please take a moment to brainstorm the logical structure of the position I outline below: identify hidden premises, watch for equivocations between cosmic and terrestrial significance, and consider whether any of my commitments rely on unstated metaphysical assumptions.

This question has become personally urgent rather than merely academic. After a sudden career restructuring and my father's recent serious diagnosis, I have been forced to ask whether my life is actually meaningful or simply well-organized. I have arrived at the following commitments, and I want a rigorous consistency audit before I try to live by them.

(1) I accept an absurdist thesis: the universe provides no inherent purpose, and human life possesses no cosmic significance. Yet I deny that this renders life tragic or not worth living; the absence of external purpose is a descriptive feature of the human condition, not a normative defect.

(2) I reject contemporary naturalist accounts that ground meaning in objective value or in relations to goods that transcend the subject, because they seem to presuppose a kind of metaphysical importance that absurdism denies is available.

(3) At the same time, I think reflective equilibrium is the correct method in this domain: I should revise my intuitions and my principles about meaning until they cohere with my actual cares, commitments, and emotional responses.

(4) From (3), I conclude that terrestrial meaning is constructed through projects and relationships to which I am genuinely attached, regardless of whether an external, impartial observer would deem them important.

(5) Nevertheless, I want to resist the conclusion that any sincerely pursued project is equally meaningful. I believe there are substantive constraints: for instance, a life spent solely on incrementally improving one's personal speed at solving a particular kind of abstract puzzle, with no connection to other people, to knowledge, or to broader human concerns, strikes me as deficient in meaning, even if the agent cares about it intensely.

(6) I also hold that the awareness of cosmic pointlessness creates what I call a residual dissonance—a persistent, low-grade cognitive friction that does not disappear merely because one has built satisfying terrestrial projects. This dissonance is not despair, but it is real, unreconciled, and not fully dissolved by cheerful project-pursuit.

My questions: Which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, which pairs are only apparently in tension, and what is the least costly revision to restore consistency? Specifically, does (2) undermine my ability to defend (5)'s constraints without smuggling objective value back in? Does (6) contradict (1)'s claim that the absence of cosmic purpose is not tragic? And does the combination of (3) and (4) commit me to a form of subjectivism that (5) tries to block? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally damaging, and indicate which commitment, if any, should be abandoned or qualified.
