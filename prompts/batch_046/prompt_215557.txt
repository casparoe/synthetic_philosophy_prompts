I'm working through my own position on reproductive ethics for a seminar paper, and I'd like a consistency audit before I commit to it in writing. Here are the commitments I've settled on: (1) I endorse procreative beneficence in a qualified form—parents have a defeasible moral reason to choose, among feasible reproductive options, the future child whose life is expected to be best, but only within a "threshold pluralism" framework that treats any life well above a baseline of severe suffering or profound deprivation as good enough, rather than demanding welfare maximization; (2) I defend liberal eugenics—the state should remain neutral among reasonable conceptions of a good life and should not mandate genetic selections, though it may regulate against selections that predictably cause serious harm; (3) I accept that the disability rights objection carries real weight—routinely selecting against disability-related traits can express and reinforce devaluation of existing disabled persons, and this expressive harm is a genuine moral cost that must be weighed, not dismissed; (4) I think the non-identity problem undermines person-affecting harm arguments in embryo-selection cases, since selecting for trait profile A over B typically brings a different person into existence, so the "unselected" child is not made worse off by not existing; (5) I maintain that reproductive autonomy is weighty but not absolute, and it can be overridden when a selection decision risks serious harm to the future child or to others, including through the expressive effects noted in (3); and (6) I believe that "best expected life" is sufficiently intersubjective—grounded in widely shared goods like health, cognitive capacity, and social connection—to guide parental choice without collapsing into a single perfectionist blueprint.

The tension I'm unsure about is whether these hang together. In particular, (1) and (6) seem to pull in different directions: if there's an intersubjective ranking of lives, why allow pluralism about what's "good enough"? Meanwhile, (4) and (5) look like they might conflict—if the non-identity problem blocks person-affecting harm in selection, what exactly is being overridden when autonomy yields to harm-prevention? At the same time, (2) and (3) might only appear incompatible: one might think liberal neutrality lets parents select for "normal" traits, but perhaps the expressive-harm constraint can be built into liberal regulation without violating neutrality. Could you go through these pairs, rank the tensions by severity, say for each whether it's genuine or only apparent, specify the least costly revision in each case, and identify which single change would resolve the most conflicts? Please provide your answer in American English.
