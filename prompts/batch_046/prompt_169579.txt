I have been thinking for some time about why certain historical defeats become what people call "lost causes" and why these stories remain so powerful long after the events themselves. I am particularly struck by four cases that seem to share a structure but operate very differently: the American Confederacy, Masada, Kosovo, and Dunkirk. In the first two, the defeat seems to become a kind of origin story for a group identity; in the third, it functions as a perpetual claim to territory despite demographic change; and in the fourth, it appears to be a story about survival through withdrawal rather than about loss at all.

What I find puzzling is that discussions of these myths tend to stall in a very specific way. Historians disagree about facts, but the deeper disagreement seems to be about whether a defeat can generate a continuing moral or political entitlement for people who never experienced it. Some people treat the myth as a dangerous falsehood that distorts present obligations; others treat it as a legitimate form of collective memory that binds a community together. But neither side seems able to persuade the other, and I wonder whether they are actually disagreeing about the same thing.

My own hypothesis is that the disagreement is partly verbal: one side uses "myth" to mean a false narrative that should be corrected, while the other uses it to mean a shared framework of meaning that does not need to be factually accurate to be valuable. But I also suspect there is a hidden substantive disagreement about whether collective identity can be inherited in the same way that property or obligations can be inherited. Is the stall really about the semantics of "myth," or is it about whether defeat can create a kind of moral debt that survives across generations?

Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? I want to know whether the sides differ about historical facts, about what counts as a legitimate reason for collective identity, about the conditions under which a past event can bind present people, or about something else entirely. And if it is a hidden disagreement about inheritance or entitlement, is there a conceptual distinction—perhaps between commemorative obligations and political claims—that could move the discussion forward? I am not asking for a survey of all four cases, but rather for a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement and an honest assessment of whether anything short of abandoning the concept of inherited collective identity could resolve it.
