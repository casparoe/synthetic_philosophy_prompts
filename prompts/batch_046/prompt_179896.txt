I've been revisiting Michel Gondry's Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind after a long discussion on a rationalist forum about whether personal identity survives radical memory editing, and I'm trying to work out whether the film is actually a stealth argument against the Buddhist doctrine of anatta. The standard philosophical reading treats Joel and Clementine's erased memories as a narrative-identity case study—something like MacIntyre's unity of life disrupted by technology—but I want to push further. In the Buddhist skandha analysis, what we call a self is just five aggregates, including sankhara (volitional formations and habitual dispositions) and vijnana (consciousness). The Lacuna procedure wipes explicit autobiographical memory, yet Joel and Clementine are drawn back together; their emotional patterns, defensive dynamics, and even the specific choreography of their relationship seem to persist as traces independent of recollection. Is the film dramatizing a hidden "substrate self"—not a Cartesian ego, but a persistent pattern of clinging (upadana) and karmic residue—that survives the destruction of narrative? Or does it instead show that what remains is precisely not a self but an empty, selfless repetition of suffering and attachment?

Read the film through the lens of anatta and the skandha framework: where does it treat identity as a constructed story that can be deleted, and where does it resist that by suggesting that something deeper—perhaps the aggregate of sankhara—persists? Focus on the train scene, the collapsing beach house, and the final meeting where they agree to begin again despite knowing the outcome. Does the film's romantic framing smuggle in an essentialism about "true love" that anatta undermines, or can a Buddhist reading accommodate the repetition as the working out of unwholesome habit-energy without anyone who owns it? I want a reading that takes both the cinematic logic and the philosophical doctrine seriously, and that ends by saying what the film adds to the debate about personal identity—say, between Parfit's reductionism and a narrative account—that pure argument alone doesn't capture.
