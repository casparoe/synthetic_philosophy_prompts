I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but after encountering Libet's readiness-potential studies and the subsequent fMRI experiments that appear to predict simple decisions several seconds before subjects report conscious awareness, I started seeing structural parallels with distributed system design that I find hard to dismiss. I now hold what I think of as a distributed-authority interpretation: voluntary action is not initiated by a centralized conscious executive that is merely slow, but rather emerges from a neural commitment race in which pre-conscious subsystems compete to lock in a motor plan, with the conscious self-model functioning more like an audit log that assigns authorship only after consensus has already been reached. On this view, the readiness potential is not just an early precursor to awareness; it is evidence that there never was a unified point of authorization in the loop. The phenomenology of "I decided" becomes a post-hoc narrative reconstruction by a monitoring module that receives the outcome rather than directing it—analogous to how a logging service records a distributed transaction only after the underlying nodes have committed. If this framing is correct, then free will in any robust sense—whether agent-causal or even standard compatibilist—is structurally absent, not merely delayed, because the conditions for genuine conscious initiation are never satisfied.

What would count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure: which empirical findings—perhaps about the precise temporal dynamics of veto power, about whether continuous decision trajectories undermine discrete commitment points, or about cases where conscious deliberation clearly reshapes an emerging action—would actually damage the commitment-race framing rather than being accommodated by redescribing them as post-hoc narration or late-stage suppression? Which conceptual distinctions—say, between initiation and veto, between predictive coding and genuine authorship, or between distributed processing and unified agency—would defenders of the neuroscience-based illusion view themselves concede as genuinely damaging if they resolved differently? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, include what proponents of this view would admit counts against them rather than only what critics say, and address whether the framework risks being insulated into unfalsifiability: if every apparent counterexample can be reinterpreted as either retrospective attribution or a veto applied after the race is already won, is that insulation a feature that makes the view robust, or a defect that empties it of empirical content?
