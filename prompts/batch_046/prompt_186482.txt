I've been in this philosophy of science / esthetics reading group for like a year now and we've been circling the problem of verisimilitude — what makes a representation actually feel true-to-the-world rather then just being useful. The debate has totally stalled. One camp says verisimilitude is basically structural correspondence plus predictive success: a climate model is verisimilar because its dynamics map onto real dynamics, end of story. The other camp insists there's an irreducible phenomenal layer — the representation has to present itself to an observer in a way that can't be fully cashed out in terms of structure or prediction alone. They keep bringing up the same cases — MRI scans, hyperrealist portraits, those high-res galaxy simulations — and everyone agrees those are verisimilar, but they can't agree on why. It's been months and nobody's moved.

My working theory is that this isn't really about representation at all but about what counts as a good explanation — one side wants a third-person functional story, the other wants a first-person phenomenological one. But I'm also wondering if there's a hidden empirical question about how human visual systems parse similarity that's doing all the work, and neither side realizes it. Someone also threw in this idea about 'perceptual availability' — that verisimilitude depends on whether the representational format can be directly experienced by a specific kind of observer, not just decoded — which was supposed to break the deadlock but just made everyone more confused.

So what's actually going on here? Is this a verbal disagreement about the word verisimilitude, a substantive clash about whether phenomenology can be reduced, or two groups answering different questions — one about epistemic virtue, one about perceptual experience — under one heading? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that would actually move it — a conceptual distinction, an empirical result from vision science, or maybe just admitting the question is ill-posed? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of theories, and an honest answer about whether progress should even be expected.
