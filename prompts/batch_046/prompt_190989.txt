Consider the membership rules of the Rivermouth Participatory Budgeting Assembly, a municipal institution that allocates part of the local tax yield through direct vote. Its charter contains the following provisions, which have never been amended as a set:

1. Any adult who has maintained a registered primary residence inside the mapped district boundary for ninety consecutive days may propose projects and vote on all expenditure categories.
2. Non-citizens holding temporary work visas who meet the same residency threshold receive full voting rights on infrastructure and education allocations, but are excluded from ballots concerning policing and immigration-enforcement expenditure.
3. Citizens who have permanently relocated outside the district, yet retain freehold property within it, may cast absentee votes on all tax-related measures, irrespective of the length of their absence.
4. Inhabitants of the neighbouring unincorporated riverside settlements—who share the district's water and sewage infrastructure and whose children attend district schools under a reciprocal agreement—are invited to non-binding consultation forums but are denied any formal vote.
5. The assembly's digital verification platform issues "presence tokens" that lapse after thirty consecutive days of absence; simultaneously, it grants perpetual "heritage tokens" to descendants of families named in the 1921 municipal charter, entitling them to vote solely on cultural-preservation matters even if they have never lived in the district.

What conception of the demos—of who rightfully belongs to the body of democratic decision-makers—does this composite practice presuppose? Can a single coherent principle, such as affected interests, territorial co-presence, citizenship, or shared fate, license all five provisions together, or do different rules quietly invoke rival and incompatible political ontologies? Reconstruct the implicit theory of democratic membership embedded in the charter: distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely happens to fit it, identify where the provisions are philosophically underdetermined, and specify what institutional arrangements would have to change if Rivermouth adopted either a consistently all-affected-interests principle or a strictly territorial-co-presence principle.
