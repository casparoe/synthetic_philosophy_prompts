I was working through some problems for a seminar on the philosophy of perception, and I constructed the following argument that now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to collapse the distinction between seeing and thinking entirely. Here are the steps: (1) A perceptual experience E is cognitively penetrated by a cognitive state C just in case C causally influences E and the influence is semantically coherent—meaning the representational content of E varies with the semantic content of C, not merely with C’s non-semantic properties. (2) Take a case of belief-guided attention: I believe my lost keys are crimson, so I deploy attention selectively toward crimson-colored regions. This attentional state is a cognitive state with the content <the keys are crimson>. (3) Because of this attention, the crimson regions in my visual field are represented with higher precision and greater salience than they otherwise would be; the representational content of my experience—<there is a crimson object there>—is thus shaped by the semantic content of my belief. (4) So belief-guided attention satisfies both conditions in (1): it is causally efficacious and semantically coherent, hence it counts as cognitive penetration. (5) Now, conscious perception never occurs without some form of attentional selection; subliminal processing may occur, but anything that reaches awareness is filtered by attention, and attention is always guided—whether by explicit beliefs, tacit expectations, or practical goals—rather than being a purely sensory reflex. (6) Therefore, every conscious perceptual experience is cognitively penetrated. (7) But if every conscious perception is penetrated, then perceptual justification is necessarily circular: we invoke perceptual experiences to justify beliefs, yet those experiences are already shaped by beliefs (or other cognitive states) with related content. (8) Yet we plainly do have non-circular perceptual justification—when I see rain falling and form the belief that it is raining, my belief is justified by the experience without prior commitment to rain. (9) Hence, if (8) is true, the chain must fail somewhere between (1) and (7). The conclusion that all perception is theory-laden looks like a reductio.

My instinct is that the error hides in the move from (3) to (4) or from (4) to (6), but I cannot precisely locate the equivocation or scope mistake. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? In particular, if I want to preserve (8)—that some perceptual justification is genuinely independent—do I have to deny that attention ever penetrates, or can I keep penetration for explicit beliefs while excluding attentional modulation? And if I draw that line, what principle distinguishes permissible attentional influence from illegitimate penetration? Please lead with your bottom-line diagnosis in the first sentence, then lay out the reasoning with no preamble and without restating the question.
