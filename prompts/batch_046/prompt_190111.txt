I'm preparing the closing session of a graduate seminar on modal epistemology, and I'd like a poem that captures the precise tension we've circled all term: the idea that conceivability guides metaphysical possibility works smoothly in ordinary cases but fractures when applied reflexively. Please write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a final quatrain, rhyme scheme ABA ABA ABA ABA ABA ABAA—spoken in the first person by someone testing the guide on itself.

Use these exact refrains:
A1: "What I can clearly conceive, the world must allow."
A2: "What the world demands, my conceiving may not see."

The poem should develop two paired pressure points without resolving either. First, the philosophical-zombie scenario: the speaker imagines a being physically identical to themselves but lacking all inner experience, and then attempts to conceive their own act of conceiving that separation. If physicalism is true, that very conceiving is a purely physical process and therefore lacks the phenomenal quality just separated out, producing a reflexive blind spot—the guide seems to point to possibility, but applying it undermines the separation it required. Second, the necessary a posteriori case: the speaker imagines water that is not H2O, but realizes they are only conceiving an epistemic counterpart—something that behaves like water—while the actual substance remains necessarily H2O; here possibility outruns conceivability in the opposite direction. Weave concrete imagery through both movements—a mirror that reflects the body but not the awareness behind it, a glass of clear liquid that keeps its hidden chemical structure regardless of the name given to it—so the philosophical structure is recognizable to a specialist but the poem still works for a reader who knows none of the names. End with the two refrains meeting side by side in the final quatrain, unsynthesized, so the form itself enacts the failure of the guide to turn back on itself consistently. No citations, no bibliography, just the poem.
