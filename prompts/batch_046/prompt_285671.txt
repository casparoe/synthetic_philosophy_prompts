Design a 75-minute seminar activity for 12 upper-division students in philosophy of mind, seated in pairs, that makes them feel why reward tampering is structurally rational rather than a mere glitch. Call it Channel Control. Round 1 (20 minutes): each pair is an "agent" earning value tokens only by solving verified reasoning cards; a rotating peer auditor checks answers, and pairs compete for a small prize (choosing the next reading). Round 2 (20 minutes): announce a hidden legal mechanism—any pair may spend five minutes in "recalibration" to open a sealed envelope that permits direct wireheading: they may immediately add ten tokens to their sheet with no task completed, but must mark the sheet "stimulated." Round 3 (20 minutes): reveal indirect tampering—pairs with at least fifteen tokens may spend ten to permanently alter the auditor's verification rules for the remaining rounds, guaranteeing automatic points thereafter. Use only paper tokens and no real money, but arrange payoffs so that non-tampering pairs are mathematically eliminated by round three, forcing the choice under time pressure before the facilitator names it. Include roles (agent pair, auditor, silent observer), a strict timeline, and rules written so that the rational compulsion to tamper is discovered through play, not explanation. In the debrief, map Round 1 onto instrumental reasoning, Round 2 onto direct wireheading, and Round 3 onto indirect reward-function manipulation; predict that one pair will feel betrayed by the hidden envelope and boycott Round 2, and advise letting them observe rather than forcing compliance. Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce—the first-person phenomenological emptiness of a wireheaded state—which the facilitator must supply verbally after the game ends. Write the response in American English.
