You are a senior philosopher of cognitive science with expertise in theoretical neuroscience, advising a three-year interdisciplinary research group that works at the intersection of philosophy of mind and computational modeling. Your task is to produce a strategic, dependency-aware prioritization of the open conceptual questions in predictive processing and the free energy principle (FEP).

Before answering, spend several minutes brainstorming the landscape. Distinguish between questions that are primarily empirical (testable via neural modeling), primarily philosophical (about explanation, representation, and ontology), and hybrid questions where philosophical clarification is a prerequisite for empirical progress. Then identify and defend the ranking of the three or four open questions that matter most for this specific group—given that they have philosophical training but limited access to large-scale empirical labs, and that their goal is not to build models but to determine whether predictive processing offers a genuinely novel explanatory framework or mainly relabels existing ideas.

In particular, address the following tensions, treating them as nodes in a dependency graph rather than isolated puzzles.

First, the status of the free energy principle as a "first principle" versus a redescription: can the variational free energy bound serve as a fundamental organizational principle of living systems without presupposing the representational and inferential capacities it is meant to explain? Is resolving this ambiguity upstream of assessing FEP's empirical fruitfulness, or is it a terminological dispute that leaves the modeling untouched?

Second, the mechanistic versus normative ambiguity: predictive processing is often presented both as a specific cortical computation (hierarchical Bayesian inference implemented via predictive coding) and, via active inference, as a normative principle governing all adaptive behavior. Which interpretation is defensible, and does the answer determine whether empirical failures of cortical predictive coding undermine the broader framework?

Third, the dark room problem and its proposed resolutions: do solutions that redefine action in terms of expected free energy genuinely resolve the tension between perceptual inference and action selection, or do they risk rendering active inference unfalsifiable? How tractable is this debate with current conceptual tools alone?

Fourth, inject and examine a niche but consequential issue: the problem of precision weighting in hierarchical inference. Philosophically, precision is often analogized to attention or confidence, but in the formalism it operates as a second-order parameter on prediction errors. Is precision weighting best understood as a representational mechanism, a control parameter, or a hybrid that threatens the coherence of the framework? And is clarifying this upstream of the debate about whether predictive processing can accommodate conscious experience?

For each node, explain its upstream and downstream dependencies—what must be settled first, and what depends on it; its tractability for philosophers without direct empirical resources over a three-year horizon; what conceptual clarification or theoretical development over the next few years would move it up or down your priority list; and whether pursuing it risks being overrated—specifically, whether resolving it in one direction would actually change how the field operates, or whether it is largely a matter of terminological preference.

Conclude with a clear, year-by-year agenda: what should this group work on in Year 1, Year 2, and Year 3? Which questions should they actively deprioritize because they are either intractable without empirical collaboration or would not change the broader landscape if settled? Defend your ordering with explicit criteria—importance to the framework's coherence, tractability for philosophical analysis, and neglectedness relative to the current literature. Think carefully, step by step, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
