I've come around to thinking pure time preference is rational, not just tolerated—call it diachronic agent-relativity. The idea is that my present self has direct practical authority over tomorrow's outcomes, but only mediated authority over outcomes thirty years out, since every intermediate self in the chain can veto the plan. So discounting tracks fading control, not fading value, and treating my future self like a stranger misses the point: authority weakens with mediation, not with identity. What would count against this? I don't want the usual you're-being-partial line—I actualy think I can absorb that. I want the harder hits: do formal decision-theoretic results show that any mediated-authority model collapses into exponential discounting with weird parameters, making the story redundant? If personal identity turned out to be conventional rather than real, does that strengthen the authority-fade view or blow it up? And is there empirical work on intertemporal choice that can actualy distinguish control-tracking discounting from simple hyperbolic impatience? Rank these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a committed defender would concede matter, and be honest about whether this position risks anything real or has just been qualified into unfalsifiability.
