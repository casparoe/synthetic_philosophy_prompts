Below is a draft essay outline on whether global history can be told from everywhere at once. I propose a distinction between synchronic polycentrism—treating every node in a network, such as the 13th-century Mongol relay routes linking the Black Sea, Central Asia, and East Asia, as equally causally generative at a given moment—and diachronic polycentrism, which holds that over long durations the narrative center can shift continuously without ever fixing on one region. I argue that diachronic polycentrism collapses into synchronic polycentrism because any temporal sequencing requires a metric of significance (what counts as a turning point), and that metric is always drawn from an archival tradition with its own historiographical priorities. Using the Mongol case as an illustration rather than proof, I note that the same relay trade can be narrated as Eurasian integration, Chinese tributary extension, or Islamic commercial-law expansion, with no neutral meta-criterion to rank them. Please comment on three things: first, whether the synchronic/diachronic distinction is coherent or merely restates the problem; second, whether my claim about metrics of significance confuses narrative structure with causal ontology; and third, whether there is a more elegant framing of the hidden-center problem that does not rely on invoking archival privilege. I am not asking for citations or a reading list—only conceptual criticism.
