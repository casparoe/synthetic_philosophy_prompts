Here are my verdicts on rescue obligations, and I want to see whether they cohere around one principle or whether I must abandon some.

If I can save a child drowning in a shallow pond at the cost of muddying my clothes, I must do it. If I can save a child from a burning building by breaking a window and suffering minor injuries, I must do it. If I can send fifty dollars that will certainly save a distant stranger's life, I should, but if I spend it on a concert ticket instead I do not commit the same wrong as the pond bystander; the failure feels less direct and less grave. Yet if I am enrolled by default in an automated 0.5% income levy that saves hundreds of lives, and I actively click to opt out and keep a barely noticeable sum, that opt-out feels seriously wrong—much closer to walking past the pond. I also think that if I must choose between saving one child now or taking a small personal risk to save five, I should take the risk for the five. But if saving the five requires using one innocent person as a means—say, pushing them onto a track to stop a vehicle—then I must not, even though five live and one dies in both scenarios. On distribution: if two children are drowning in ponds exactly equidistant from me, flipping a coin seems acceptable. If one pond is ten meters closer, saving the nearer child also seems acceptable, even though need is identical; but if the nearer child is less severely injured and the farther one is closer to death, I think I should go to the farther one, which seems to undermine any simple proximity justification.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps combining proximity, the structure of agency, risk allocation, and the doing-allowing distinction—that delivers all of these verdicts? If not, which should I overturn? Please state the principle precisely, identify the concrete case that motivates each clause, predict its verdict on a case I have not listed—specifically, whether I may remotely trigger a drone rescue that saves ten strangers at the cost of destroying property I value at roughly that fifty-dollar donation amount—and be explicit about which of my judgments above the principle must reject.
