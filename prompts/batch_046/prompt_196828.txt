I have been wondering about the legal protection of geographical indications—rules that reserve names like Champagne or Parmigiano-Reggiano for products made in specific regions, even when laboratory analysis shows that an identically composed product made elsewhere is chemically indistinguishable. The practice seems to treat the name as referring not merely to a set of internal properties shared by both items, but to something essentially tied to origin, history, and environment. Does that institutional practice presuppose a form of semantic externalism—the idea that what a term denotes depends on causal and environmental relations rather than on intrinsic characteristics or mental representations? Could one justify the same rules without committing to that philosophical picture, for instance by appealing solely to economic fairness or to preventing consumer confusion? And if the practice does carry that commitment, is it philosophically consistent with parallel institutions—such as pharmaceutical regulations that approve generic substitutes precisely because chemical identity is all that matters—where we appear to treat internal properties as sufficient?
