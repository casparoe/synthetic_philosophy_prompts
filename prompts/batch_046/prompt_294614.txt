Analyze the Fischer controversy—the debate sparked in the 1960s by the historian Fritz Fischer's argument that German leadership bore primary responsibility for the outbreak of the First World War through deliberate risk-taking and expansive war aims (the September Program)—as a case study in collective national blame. Rather than treating the nation-state as a distributive aggregate of individual citizens, apply a framework of institutional diachrony: a collective body may be held retrospectively responsible across generational turnover only if its governing institutions maintain continuous normative memory, preserved through strategic doctrines, archival practices, and commemorative rituals, that sustain the identity conditions of the agent over time.

Proceed in both directions. First, use this framework to examine whether the Weimar Republic's formal rupture with imperial structures—combined with the documented persistence of General Staff strategic culture and the suppression of war-aims records—breaks or preserves the conditions for attributing blame to Germany in 1914 versus Germany in 1961. Address specifically what Fischer's documentary evidence regarding Bethmann Hollweg's war aims and the decision-making architecture of the July Crisis implies for the philosophical claim that collective blame requires present-tense institutional endorsement of past actions.

Second, allow the historical episode to test the framework itself. Does Fischer's demonstration that responsibility was structurally embedded in bureaucratic planning—rather than distributed across the German population—support the institutional-diachronic view, or does it reveal that such frameworks mistakenly reify organizational charts into moral agents? Identify precisely where the theory explains salient features of the controversy, such as why West German historians resisted Fischer's conclusions despite accepting comparable documentary methods, and where it remains silent—particularly regarding whether a nation can be blamed when its institutional memory has been deliberately fractured, as occurred during the transition from Empire to Republic. Conclude by stating whether the Fischer episode strengthens, weakens, or transforms the claim that holding a nation responsible requires more than causal contribution: it requires a living institutional thread that connects present citizens to past decisions.
