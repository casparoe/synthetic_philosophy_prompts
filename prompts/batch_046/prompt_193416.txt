Compare the normative framework of Dutch communicative directness — in which explicit, unsoftened disagreement is typically interpreted as respect for the listener’s rational equality — with that of Japanese communicative indirectness — in which the strategic avoidance of direct refusal is typically interpreted as protective care for the interaction’s stability. Focus your comparison on the moral status of a deliberately softened falsehood, such as expressing enthusiasm for a colleague’s impractical proposal: does each framework treat this “white lie” as ethically permissible, obligatory, or blameworthy? Then apply both frameworks to an identical concrete case: a junior researcher must respond to a senior scholar’s publicly presented but methodologically flawed argument during a workshop Q&A session. Which framework yields the more internally consistent judgment about what the junior ought to say, and does either framework implicitly depend on an unstated assumption about whether politeness is fundamentally a duty to the listener’s autonomy or a duty to the relational conditions that make future cooperation possible?
