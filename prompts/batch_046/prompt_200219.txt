The debate over whether the Anthropocene should be ratified as a formal geological epoch — and, if so, whether it began around 1610 with colonial ecological exchange, around 1784 with fossil-fuel industrialization, or around 1950 with the Great Acceleration and plutonium fallout — has persisted for decades across geology, history, and the environmental humanities. Stratigraphers insist on a Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point anchored to a globally synchronous, durable physical signal in rock, ice, or sediment; historians and critical geographers often treat the term as a diagnosis of socio-ecological power and responsibility, for which a precise lithological marker is at best incidental. Despite ever-more-precise measurements of radionuclides, microplastics, and nitrogen isotopes, disagreement over both the start-date and the authority to decide — whether that belongs to stratigraphic commissions, Earth-system scientists, or the affected communities being named — remains completely stalled.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not mainly empirical but concerns epistemic jurisdiction and explanatory purpose: the geologists are asking for a chronostratigraphic boundary comparable to the Pleistocene-Holocene transition, while the humanists are asking for a normative-historical concept comparable to modernity that happens to leave geological traces. Is that right? Or is there a hidden formal or empirical question — perhaps about whether human impacts have produced a globally synchronous signal genuinely distinguishable from regional noise — that actually divides the sides?

I want a diagnosis supported by what stratigraphers actually say they require (observable, durable, synchronous markers) versus what critical scholars actually say they require (a framework that distributes causal responsibility and captures institutional power), not a survey of interpretations. Specifically: are these two groups answering different questions under a single heading? Is the disagreement verbal, turning on whether "epoch" must denote a formally ratified geological unit? Is it substantive but masked by bedrock intuitions about whether natural and human history can share one periodization? Or is the question itself ill-posed? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a refined formal criterion for anthropogenic signals, a conceptual distinction that separates stratigraphic description from historical diagnosis, or an argument that the two projects should be disentangled entirely and that no further resolution should be expected?
