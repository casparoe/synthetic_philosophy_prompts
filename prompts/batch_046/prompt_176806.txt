For a 90-minute graduate workshop with ten students in history and philosophy of science, design a structured simulation—not a lecture—through which participants experience both the explanatory power and the conceptual cost of treating pathogens, climate anomalies, and volcanic winters as historical agents rather than mere background conditions. The central tension to be felt rather than named is what you should call “agency without intentionality”: attributing causal efficacy to non-human forces seems to require treating them as actors, yet treating them as actors seems to demand anthropomorphic properties that drain the explanation of content.

Construct a role-based deliberation with rules and small stakes (for example, a shared evaluation rubric or a required public commitment at the end). Divide participants into three rotating roles—Policy Architects, Microbial Dynamics, and Climate/Volcanic Systems—each holding evidence cards drawn from stylized, anonymized case vignettes (a city’s demographic collapse, an empire’s agricultural contraction, a trade-route shift). The payoff structure should initially reward groups for reaching consensus on a single “dominant cause” using only parsimony and predictive accuracy, so that environmental explanations tend to win early rounds. In later rounds, however, the rules should suddenly require the group to assign retrospective responsibility, narrative coherence, or counterfactual contingency to the outcome they have selected; at this point participants should discover that the environmental explanations they converged on erase precisely the distinctions they now need.

Participants must not hear terms such as “environmental determinism,” “non-human agency,” or “agency without intentionality” until the debrief. The idea should emerge only from the friction between the simulation’s phases.

Provide the full protocol: setup, card distribution, turn structure, the moment the rule-shift occurs, and the required public defense. Then provide the debrief, mapping each simulation phase onto a specific conceptual tension—causal selection versus overdetermination, enabling conditions versus triggering conditions, and the normative residue left when human actors are displaced. State explicitly what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run (for instance, a dominant speaker collapsing roles, or participants conflating correlation with mechanism), and how the facilitator should handle it without taking over. Be frank about which feature of the idea—likely the normative implication for how we assign praise and blame to human decision-makers—the activity’s mechanics cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator directly. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not invoke specific scholarly works by title or date.
