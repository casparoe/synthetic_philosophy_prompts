I am preparing a novel drawn from my grandmother's private papers. Among them is what I have come to call a negative testimony: a letter in which she refuses to contribute her account to a public archive commemorating an event she survived. She argues that institutional remembrance demands a shared language that would translate her particular grief into a collective narrative, remembering her loss wrongly—not falsely, but in a form alien to how it was lived. Publishing this refusal honors her resistance to assimilation; withholding it respects her silence, yet allows the specific texture of her experience to vanish. Is there an ethical distinction between remembering with someone, preserving the form in which they lived a loss, and remembering for them, extracting a usable past? And if obligations of memory vary with the thickness of the relation between the one who remembers and the one remembered, does my position as her descendant create a duty of fidelity to her private form of remembrance, or does the public importance of the event override that fidelity?
