The exam question was: Explain the Platonia dilemma, where twenty people are each told that if exactly one of them sends a telegram by noon the next day that person gets a billion dollars, with no cooperation allowed. Explain why Hofstadter says the superrational move is to send with probability one-in-twenty, contrast that with standard game-theoretic reasoning, and then apply both frameworks to the actual 1980s magazine contest where readers mailed postcards bearing entry numbers and the prize was divided by the total. Here is what the student wrote: In Platonia, twenty people face the telegram rule and standard rationality predicts a Nash equilibrium where nobody sends, since sending risks being among many senders and getting nothing. Superrational agents recognize they all share identical reasoning, so each should randomize with p equals one-twentieth, giving an expected payout of fifty million dollars per person, far better than zero. For the Luring Lottery, Scientific American offered a million dollars split by entries, and Hofstadter proposed that each reader roll a twenty-sided die and send one entry only if they roll a one, which should yield roughly a single postcard and the full prize. Instead contestants submitted astronomically large numbers like a googolplex, ruining the pool. This failure proves standard rationality was right all along: individuals should maximize personal expected value regardless of others, and superrationality only works in small transparent groups, not anonymous crowds.
