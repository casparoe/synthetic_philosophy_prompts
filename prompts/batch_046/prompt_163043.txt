I'm giving the opening remarks at a two-day workshop I'm convening on cross-cultural folk morality and its implications for moral objectivity. The room will be split: half are moral philosophers worried about whether WEIRD psychology undermines realism, the other half are cognitive anthropologists who think philosophers have been treating Western college-student intuitions as the human baseline. I need about five minutes of remarks — roughly 600 to 750 words — that do real structural work: they have to set an agenda both sides can share without either side feeling the framing has already answered the question in the other side's disfavor.

The philosophical idea I want running through it, not as decoration, is the distinction between moral-concept divergence and moral-judgment divergence — that is, between populations having different thick moral concepts (different extensions of harm, purity, loyalty, etc.) versus applying the same underlying concept with different weights, salience orderings, or boundary conditions. This distinction should shape what I can and can't say: I must not declare that cross-cultural variation automatically refutes objectivity, nor that objectivity requires perfect cross-cultural convergence, because the remarks need to stay genuinely neutral on that. Instead, the distinction should organize the workshop by showing why both camps have often been talking past each other — one treating surface divergence as concept replacement, the other treating it as noise around a shared signal.

To keep it from being a generic interdisciplinary welcome, inject one somewhat niche framing device — not an obscure named theory or citation, but a conceptual move a well-read model could construct without regurgitating a standard intro. Specifically, frame the workshop's central task as deciding whether moral objectivity is better modeled on the analogy of physical measurement (where inter-instrument calibration is required and divergence indicates error) or on the analogy of linguistic translation (where surface divergence can preserve deep structural invariance). Use that analogy to organize the three concrete questions the workshop will tackle: first, whether there is a shared "deep grammar" of moral cognition across populations; second, whether the WEIRD pattern represents a calibration extreme rather than the unbiased human mean; and third, whether objectivity claims in morality are parameter-sensitive to cultural context in something like the way color perception is parameter-sensitive to ambient lighting conditions — that is, whether the same objective property can be correctly tracked by different calibrated responses.

The audience is mixed. Philosophers should catch the analogy's debts to measurement-theoretic and structuralist traditions without needing them named; anthropologists should recognize that I'm not treating their data as "noise around the real moral signal." Don't include a bibliography, reading list, or citations — this is conceptual architecture, not scholarship display. The tone should be formal but not lecture-stiff; I'm speaking as someone who has read too many rationalist forum threads and is tired of both the "morality is just evolved intuition" and the "moral realism is obviously true" defaults. Make it polished, precise, and a little in the weeds — the kind of opening that makes people lean in rather than settle back.
