As a novelist preoccupied with how institutions silently encode philosophies of the good life, I have been studying a particular design movement in secular retreat centers and clinical wellness programs that administers what its directors term subtractive evaluation frameworks. Instead of measuring success through conventional well-being metrics—positive affect, life satisfaction, social connection—these programs audit outcomes exclusively through the reduction of self-reported craving and the verified absence of suffering, treating elevated positive emotion as an unreliable indicator that may even signal hidden attachment. The physical spaces are engineered to match: stimulating color palettes, commercial signage, and communal gathering points are systematically removed, not to cultivate joy or belonging, but specifically to prevent the arising of desire itself. I am trying to understand the implicit metaphysics of value at work here. What philosophical commitments about welfare, consciousness, or human flourishing must be presupposed for such a practice to appear rational rather than merely ascetic? Does it necessarily entail tranquilism—the position that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value—or might a framework that weights the elimination of suffering far more heavily than the production of pleasure license the same institutional design? Please reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what is strictly required by the practice from what is only contingently associated with it, and indicate whether substituting a straightforwardly eudaimonist or positive-hedonic view would demand a total restructuring of both the evaluation protocols and the architectural logic, or merely a cosmetic revision.
