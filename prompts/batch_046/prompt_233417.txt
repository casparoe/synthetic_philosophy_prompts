question: "Distinguish moral particularism from generalism with respect to defeasible moral principles. Then discuss whether particularism can account for cross-case resonance—the idea that grasping a reason in one situation can non-inferentially sharpen one's sensitivity to a structurally different reason elsewhere, without relying on an implicit rule."

student answer (paraphrased from there script, not quoted):

Particularists deny that moral reasoning needs principles at all, even defeasible ones, since reasons are completely context-bound. Generalists think defeasible principles still govern reasoning. Cross-case resonance is a problem for particularists: if reasons are totally context-bound there's no mechanism for case B learning from case A unless you smuggle in a generalization. So particularists should reject cross-case resonance and say moral expertise is just accumulating isolated judgments. Generalists can explain it easily through implicit principles covering both cases. The shape/weight distinction helps particularists—shape shifts, weight stays calibrated—but that still doesn't explain why case A should help with case B if the reasons have different shapes.
