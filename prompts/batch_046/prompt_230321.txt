I run a philosophy-of-ethics graduate seminar—fourteen students, one eighty-minute session. Design a runnable in-class simulation that makes participants feel the structural tension between Buddhist agent-evaluation and consequentialist act-evaluation, specifically through the Abhidharmic taxonomy of root mental factors (mūla) and secondary defilements (upakleśa) that determine karmic weight. The group should come away seeing that two actions with identical welfare outcomes can diverge radically in moral assessment once intention is decomposed into primary and secondary mental states, and that consequentialist aggregation has no internal mechanism for tracking this decomposition.

Structure: Give each student a sealed envelope containing one private “intention card” and one public “outcome card.” The cards describe five hypothetical monastic administrative decisions—e.g., distributing alms to a wealthy but exploitative donor, expelling a disruptive novice whose departure improves communal meditation but causes him homelessness, accepting a polluted but life-saving medical supply—each paired with a precise welfare calculus (lives saved, suffering reduced) and with an internal mental-state description using the root/secondary taxonomy: for instance, “root: non-attachment and compassion; secondary: subtle pride in being seen as generous,” or “root: aversion; secondary: disciplined speech masking hostility.”

Round One (Consequentialist, 20 minutes): Students rank the five cases from best to worst using only the visible outcome cards; discussion must cite welfare numbers only. Payoff: each student earns seminar-participation points proportional to how closely their individual ranking matches the group median, incentivizing convergence on aggregate welfare.

Round Two (Buddhist Intention-Ranking, 25 minutes): The same five cases are displayed, but now students may reveal—at a cost of one point per disclosure—their private intention card. The group must re-rank the cases by inferred karmic weight, defined in the rules as the moral quality of the root mental factor modified (not replaced) by the presence of secondary defilements. Crucially, students may challenge another player’s inference: if Student A claims Case 3 was rooted in compassion, Student B can force a “mental-factor audit” in which A must defend whether the root was genuinely non-aversion or whether aversion was merely displaced into a secondary factor like concealed resentment. Successful challenges earn points; failed challenges cost points.

Debrief (15 minutes): Map Round One’s convergence pressure onto the consequentialist assumption that moral information is fully captured by outcome states. Map Round Two’s disclosure costs and audit conflicts onto the Buddhist claim that karmic evaluation requires access to mental architecture that is not publicly observable, and that the presence of upakleśa can diminish or corrupt the karmic value of an otherwise wholesome root. Note specifically where the simulation breaks down: it treats karmic weight as ordinal and interpersonally comparable, whereas classical Abhidharmic accounts treat karmic result (vipāka) as not coextensive with worldly welfare and as operating across lifetimes; the activity therefore cannot reproduce the Buddhist view that a wholesome root intention producing catastrophic worldly harm is still, in the relevant sense, not a moral failure for the agent.

Predictable failure and remedy: In the first run, students will treat Round Two as “Round One plus hidden motives,” attempting to weight outcomes by some coefficient of purity. Stop the round, remind them that in this framework the root factor determines the basic moral kind of the act, and secondary factors only modify within that kind—there is no “weighted sum.” Also, expect one student to hoard disclosure points and free-ride on others’ audits; build in a rule that any student who never discloses must defend their silence in the debrief, turning free-riding into a visible analog of concealed mental states.

Finally, be explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce: the temporal extension of karmic causation, the distinction between mundane (laukika) and supramundane (lokottara) paths, and the possibility that a root factor is present without being fully conscious to the agent—so the facilitator must supply these limits verbally rather than expecting them to emerge from the rules.
