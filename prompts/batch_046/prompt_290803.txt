I just finished a long argument with a friend about whether history can be objective, and I want to see whether my intuition survives scrutiny. Consider the standard institutional architecture of Anglophone history doctoral programs: candidates must pass an archival-methods examination that treats provenance verification, paleography, and forgery detection as purely technical skills separable from narrative framing, yet the same faculty will reject a dissertation that offers impeccable new primary-source findings but lacks an interpretive argument, and they will reject a bold interpretive thesis that offers no original archival documentation. The institution therefore demands both, but structures them as independent modules. What view of the relationship between historical fact and interpretation does this bifurcated practice presuppose? Does it presuppose Ranke's ideal of recovering the past exactly as it happened, with interpretation applied only after the facts are secured? Or does it presuppose something closer to Carr's scepticism or Hayden White's emphasis on narrative construction—namely that bare facts do not exist—while maintaining an institutional fiction that separates them? Is there a coherent account of historical knowledge that licenses both requirements at once, or does the practice quietly exclude one of the rival positions? And if the underlying view were replaced by a radically constructivist one in which every document's evidential authority is itself an effect of narrative framing, which parts of the examination and dissertation-evaluation process would have to change, and which could remain intact? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
