I am preparing a feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on whether historians can ever genuinely collaborate across the objectivity divide, and I have been discussing the problem with colleagues who hold sharply opposing views. One group remains committed to a rigorous, modified Rankean position: they believe that the past possesses a determinate structure recoverable through disciplined source criticism, and that any narrative imposed on documents must be subordinate to the evidentiary constraints those documents supply. The other group works from the tradition of E.H. Carr and Hayden White, maintaining that historical facts are constituted rather than discovered—that the questions a historian asks in the present determine which traces become significant, and that emplotment is not an optional literary veneer but a necessary condition for historical meaning. Rather than letting them rehearse familiar slogans about spurious objectivity versus reckless relativism, I want them to address a concrete, somewhat underexplored problem I have been calling negative institutional traces: archival series—such as unanswered petition logs, census categories that remain unpopulated, or bureaucratic forecasts of events that never materialized—in which the record's most salient feature is a systematic absence rather than a presence.

Please draft the adversarial-collaboration document that these two camps could genuinely co-sign after sustained, good-faith argument. Structure it as follows: first, a numbered list of agreed claims about the epistemic status of negative traces, the proper limits of source criticism, and the unavoidable role of the historian's framing questions; second, a disagreements table in which each camp's precise position is phrased in language that side itself would accept, isolating the genuine cruxes—particularly whether a documented absence counts as an objective structural fact or as an artifact of categorical imposition, and whether narrative coherence functions as a distorting overlay or as the very mechanism that renders absence intelligible; and third, for each crux, specify a concrete hypothetical case or line of reasoning both sides agree is relevant, recording in advance what interpretation each camp predicts the case will support. The aim is neither adjudication nor dialogue, but a constructive text both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement rendered exact rather than rhetorical.
