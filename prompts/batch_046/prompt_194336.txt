You are a moral philosopher with particular expertise in the sociology of normative change and the history of ethics. Before you answer, take a moment to brainstorm the main lines of resistance to honor-centered explanations of moral revolution, and flag any hidden assumptions in the framing I am about to present.

I have come to accept what I will call the honor-first thesis about moral revolutions, inspired by Bernard Williams's writings on honor and shame and Anthony Appiah's historical analyses of practices like dueling, foot-binding, and Atlantic chattel slavery. On this view, the collapse of a deeply entrenched moral practice is rarely driven in the first instance by moral argument or by shifts in material interest; rather, the decisive mechanism is a restructuring of the social economy of honor—who can be shamed, who can confer status, and which behaviors signal membership in a respected group. Arguments and economic incentives matter, but mainly as post-hoc rationalizations or as accelerants that only become effective once the honor code has already destabilized. The thesis is not merely that honor plays a role, but that it is structurally primary: if you hold the honor distribution constant, neither compelling argument nor altered interest is sufficient to overturn the practice.
