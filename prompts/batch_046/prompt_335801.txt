I'm trying to map Bradley's regress onto the problem of propositional unity, and I think there's an overlooked middle path. Bradley's argument, as I understand it, only generates an infinite chain for relations that are external: they leave the intrinsic nature of the relata untouched, so that linking a relation to its terms requires a further relation. For an internal relation, by contrast, each term is essentially modified by its connection, so the unity is already contained in the terms themselves and no further link is needed. Applying this to propositions, I take a statement like Socrates is wise to involve an internal relation: being-wise-of-Socrates is part of Socrates' nature in this context, and being-Socrates'-property is part of wisdom's nature here. If that's right, then Bradley hasn't undermined propositional unity at all; he's only shown that it can't be achieved by an external, reified copula. But modern predicate logic treats predication exactly as such an external application — the predicate applies to the subject without altering either term's essence. So doesn't this mean that standard logical form is precisely the structure Bradley's regress renders impossible? Or can Frege's notion of unsaturated concepts be read as capturing Bradley's internal-relation insight without collapsing into his monism?

My question is whether this hybrid view — that Bradley correctly undermines external relational unity for propositions, but that we can reject monism and preserve modern logic — is coherent at all.
