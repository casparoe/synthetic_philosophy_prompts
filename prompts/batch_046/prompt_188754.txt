I'm in an upper-division political philosophy seminar focused on Enlightenment theories of public life, and a fellow student offered this summary of Rousseau's argument in his Letter to d'Alembert on the Theater: Rousseau holds that theatrical spectacle corrupts a republic by substituting artificial emotional bonds—especially the spectator's identification with fictional vice—for genuine civic solidarity. The remedy, on this reading, is not censorship but the replacement of theater with participatory public festivals in which citizens are both performers and audience, thereby eliminating the dangerous passivity of the spectator. The summary further claims that Rousseau's objection is structurally identical to Plato's critique of poetry in the Republic: both condemn representation for appealing to irrational passions and for creating a false image of virtue that undermines moral discipline.

I think this captures part of Rousseau's view, but I worry it flattens several distinctions he actually draws—perhaps between the moral danger of theatrical identification and the political danger of spectator passivity, or between the corruption of women on stage and the corruption of male citizens in the audience. Could you audit this summary sentence by sentence? I want to know which claims a scholar of Rousseau's political writings would accept, which are common simplifications that obscure the particular mechanism of corruption he describes—something about pity and the weakening of moral resolution—and which are straightforward errors, especially regarding the comparison with Plato. Does the conclusion that Rousseau and Plato share the same structural objection actually survive closer inspection?
