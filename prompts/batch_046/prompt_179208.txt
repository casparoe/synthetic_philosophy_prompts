Question: Subsidiarity is standardly defended on two distinct grounds: an epistemic-efficiency claim (lower levels possess better local information) and a dignity claim (local communities and individuals possess a right to self-determination that remote decision-making violates). Analyze whether these justifications can come apart, using a concrete case in which a local majority adopts a policy that is objectively inefficient by standard metrics yet is deeply valued as an expression of communal identity. Then address the "reverse subsidiarity" objection: if the principle is applied consistently downward, it should ultimately locate all legitimate decision-making authority in the individual, which appears to collapse federal or communal structures into a form of radical libertarianism. Is this a successful reductio of subsidiarity, or does it instead reveal that the principle must be constrained by an independent theory of legitimate authority?

Student Answer: The two justifications can indeed come apart. Consider a small agricultural community that bans mechanized farming despite its inefficiency, because manual practices are tied to religious identity. Here the dignity claim supports local control, while the efficiency claim actually favors centralized standardization.

However, I think the dignity claim is ultimately procedural rather than substantive: what matters is that the decision was made locally, not what the decision is. So even if the local choice harms minority members within the community, subsidiarity is satisfied as long as the procedure was local.

As for reverse subsidiarity, this objection misunderstands the principle. Subsidiarity applies only to relations between authorities—federal, state, and local governments—not to relations within an authority. Since the individual is not an authority over anyone else, the principle simply stops applying once we reach the individual level. Therefore there is no collapse into libertarianism; the principle just does not govern the individual's private choices.

Thus subsidiarity is safe from the reductio, though it needs the procedural interpretation of dignity to avoid internal conflicts.
