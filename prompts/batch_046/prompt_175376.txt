I was arguing with a friend about whether archives merely preserve the past or actively construct it, and she shared a reconstructed excerpt from an 1883 provincial ministerial circular on municipal record triage. The directive states that deeds, tax rolls, and sealed official correspondence are to be retained permanently, while petitions from local artisans, unsealed labor grievances, and records of informal arbitration are to be destroyed after ten years, leaving only a numerical index of their prior existence. The justification offered is not limited storage space, but a conceptual distinction between acts that alter the legal condition of persons or property and those that express transient discontent lacking juridical form.

I want to understand how this classification scheme operates as more than an organizational tool: it appears to embed a specific ontology of the state, one in which only actions already recognized by legal form merit institutional memory. Can you help me parse the internal logic by which juridical form becomes the criterion for historical endurance? Specifically, does the circular treat the resulting silence as an accidental byproduct of practical selection, or does the very category of transient discontent indicate that the archive is actively producing a particular kind of legible subject—one whose grievances matter only once they are translated into state-recognized form? Please work through the reasoning on its own terms; I am not asking for a summary of modern archival theory, though a brief comparison with contemporary accounts of structural silence might help clarify the mechanism if it illuminates the bureaucratic reasoning rather than replacing it.
