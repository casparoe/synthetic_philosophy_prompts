I teach an upper-level philosophy-of-mind seminar and am drafting the final exam. I want one essay question built around a modified version of the thought experiment familiar from The Mind's I by Hofstadter and Dennett, in which Einstein's brain is replaced by an exhaustive functional description rendered as a physical book whose pages are turned and annotated to mirror neural activity, with the philosophical question being whether such a book could be conscious. For the exam, I want a deliberately altered variant that introduces structural complications rather than merely scaling up the original.

In this modified case, the book is a distributed, asynchronously maintained database composed of independent chapters, each corresponding to a functional brain region. Thousands of separate agents update individual chapters, with update latencies ranging unpredictably from one to forty-eight hours. There is no central reader and no unified supervisory mechanism; contradictions between chapters are resolved by a majority-vote algorithm. Over any given month the system's outputs converge statistically on Einstein's behavioral dispositions with perfect accuracy, but there is no single spatiotemporal trajectory of information flow, no direct causal interaction between the agents maintaining different chapters, and no unified temporal now at which the entire state is integrated.

Please write the exam essay question, a model answer of approximately 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 marks. The essay question should ask students whether this distributed system could be conscious, and should require them to do three specific things: (i) evaluate whether standard functionalist arguments for consciousness survive the loss of synchronous, direct causal interaction between subsystems; (ii) assess whether the absence of a unified temporal present undermines arguments specifically about phenomenal binding; and (iii) distinguish and defend a position on three separate notions—consciousness as behavioral disposition, consciousness as integrated information, and consciousness as subjective presence—saying which, if any, the system satisfies and why.

The marking scheme should identify five specific analytical moves that an A-level answer must contain, allocating marks to each so that the total is 25. For each move, include a one-line note describing the characteristic way B-level answers fail on that point—particularly highlighting the distinction between answers that treat the latency and distribution problems as merely quantitative, i.e., slower or larger-scale, versus those that recognize them as structurally defeating the causal-integration conditions that functional equivalence requires. Specify that the exam allows 90 minutes and that answers should be about 700-800 words. The question should be unanswerable by simply summarizing the original thought experiment or reciting standard functionalist slogans; the difficulty should lie in seeing exactly where the structural modifications break the analogy to neural integration.
