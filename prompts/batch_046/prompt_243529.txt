I have recently faced a sequence of high-stakes decisions about allocating scarce research resources among competing hypotheses in my own field, and the experience exposed a gap in the framework I usually present to students: standard Bayesian updating treats each belief revision as isolated, but in practice being systematically wrong in a line of inquiry compounds in a way that feels geometrically destructive. I want to design a fifty-minute seminar session that introduces Kelly betting—not in its conventional financial or gambling framing, but specifically as a normative model of geometric rationality for distributing epistemic capital across uncertain propositions when errors accumulate multiplicatively. The audience is a small group of second-year philosophy-of-science graduate students who are comfortable with basic probability but skeptical that formal decision theory applies to belief formation or scientific commitment. I do not want the explanation itself; I want the teaching architecture. Specify the dependency order in which the components must be introduced and why (for instance, whether the geometric-mean objective must precede the derivation of the Kelly fraction, and whether logarithmic utility should be presented as a derived constraint rather than an assumed preference), identify the single carrying example that should bear the entire conceptual load (it should involve a researcher distributing a fixed laboratory budget across three competing, mutually exclusive climate-feedback hypotheses with quantified but uncertain payoffs—explicitly not a horse-race or stock-market scenario—and say precisely what structural features it must possess and what distracting features it must lack), map the predictable misconceptions in the order the students will form them—I anticipate they will conflate geometric rationality with ordinary risk aversion, interpret the Kelly fraction as a measure of subjective confidence rather than optimal stake size, and assume the criterion applies only to infinitely repeatable gambles rather than to one-off, irreversible research allocations—and indicate exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted, propose one diagnostic question I can ask at the close that distinguishes whether a student grasps that the criterion maximizes the long-run growth rate of capital across sequences rather than the expected value of a single trial, and state clearly what should be omitted at this level and why (for example, whether the full optimality proof, the information-theoretic interpretation, or references to continuous-time extensions should be left out). The plan should be formal enough for me to adapt directly, and it should treat the idea as genuinely normative for belief management rather than as a mere mathematical curiosity.
