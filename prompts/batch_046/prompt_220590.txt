I am preparing a seminar that pairs nineteenth-century fiction with contemporary philosophy of social policy, and I want a careful reading of Charles Dickens's Oliver Twist through the lens of workfare conditionality and the administrative production of suspicion around benefit fraud. The novel's workhouse board treats Oliver's request for additional food not as evidence of need but as a sign of moral deficiency and probable deceit; modern conditional welfare regimes operate with a parallel logic of suspicion, requiring claimants to prove deservingness against bureaucratic and sometimes algorithmic scrutiny. Read the novel as dramatizing the structural dynamics of this suspicion: which specific scenes reveal the production of fraud or undeserving status where the claimant has done nothing wrong? Compare the board's epistemic situation—what they take themselves to know about Oliver's intentions, and on what basis—to that of a modern caseworker assessing eligibility under strict conditionality rules. Where does the novel suggest that any system of proof-based deservingness will generate false positives? Conversely, where does Oliver Twist resist being read as direct commentary on the modern welfare state—what historical or institutional features of the Poor Law setting break the analogy, and what does that resistance reveal about whether the undeserving poor is an inevitable administrative category or a contingent product of particular institutional designs? Finally, what does Dickens's portrayal add to the philosophical debate about conditionality and fraud that analytical argument alone tends to miss?
