I'm a novelist delivering tomorrow's opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop for senior diplomatic historians and fiction writers. The session asks whether political leaders can learn from history without being misled by analogy, and I want to frame the talk around a distinction I've developed in my novels: "plot rhyme," where events mirror each other narratively—Munich, Vietnam, the so-called Thucydides trap—creating an illusion of inevitability, versus "causal rhyme," where underlying mechanisms actually align. The philosophical constraint must do structural work: because plot rhyme is seductive but causally hollow, I should not claim that history offers transparent lessons; yet because causal rhyme is rare and hard to verify, I should not dismiss analogy as mere fiction either. The room is split—half are experts who will spot every lazy historical comparison, half are writers attuned to narrative form but not to statecraft—so the speech must land for both without lecturing either. Please write about six minutes of formal, polished remarks that use this tension to explain what can and cannot be said about the past guiding the present, mentioning the three analogies only as instances of plot rhyme that obscure divergence, and closing without resolving the tension. No citations, no reading list—just the speech itself.
