I'm taking a graduate course on international humanitarian law and keep arguing with friends over news headlines: when does an atrocity earn the word genocide before any tribunal has spoken? I'm now drafting an internal protocol for the copy desk of the international-news section where I volunteer, because editors keep asking for rules they can apply in under twenty minutes without legal training. I need a reusable decision procedure that produces either a clear recommendation to use the term, a qualified formulation—such as describing acts consistent with genocide or a genocide risk—or a prohibition on its use, with explicit checkpoints where the editor's judgment cannot be eliminated and must be signed off.

Ground the protocol in Raphael Lemkin's original conception rather than treating the 1948 Convention as exhaustive: genocide is a coordinated, multi-dimensional attack on a group's existence encompassing cultural, economic, political, biological, and other destructive techniques, not merely mass killing with individually targeted intent. At the same time, build in the politics of naming—applying the label activates legal and diplomatic machinery, shapes victim recognition, and can either pressure intervention or harden resistance. Your procedure should therefore separate genocide as a retrospective legal verdict from genocide as a present moral-political designation, and take a committed position—defended with reasons—on which frame should dominate in rapid-turnaround news reporting.

The deliverable should be a sequential checklist for practitioners: establish targeted-group identification and pattern coordination; map which destructive dimensions are present; assess whether intent is distributed across bureaucratic structures rather than concentrated in individual orders; weigh the communicative effects of labeling in this specific conflict; and render a qualified recommendation. Include worked applications to two scenarios: one where the procedure clearly permits the term, and one—perhaps involving systematic forced assimilation through language suppression and child removal without widespread lethal violence—where it yields an unwelcome answer, either permitting a legally risky headline or forbidding a morally urgent one, and explain why that tension is structurally unavoidable. Clearly flag what the protocol cannot do: it is not a substitute for judicial findings, it cannot predict state behavior, and it does not resolve whether cultural destruction alone warrants the term in headline form. Keep the conceptual architecture intact, but translate it into editorial tests that survive the loss of legal expertise.
