You work as an ethics auditor for an international development agency operating in regions where clientelist exchange and ceremonial gift-giving are structurally embedded in social life. Your agency has adopted a specific principle: an act counts as corruption only when it involves a breach of an institutional role obligation that is not justifiable within any recognized local normative framework governing that role. Write a reusable, step-by-step decision procedure that a non-specialist auditor can run in about thirty minutes: list the questions in order, specify what counts as a sufficient answer to each, provide tests for distinguishing a gift that reinforces social reciprocity from a payment that displaces institutional duty, indicate where the procedure yields a clear determination and where it must stop and hand the case to a senior reviewer because the conflict is one of values rather than observable facts, and flag the points where the procedure is likely to misfire. Apply it to two proposals: first, a local official accepts ceremonial livestock after approving a community water project, in a context where refusal would cause serious social rupture; second, a contractor provides regular cash envelopes to procurement officers, locally framed as relationship maintenance, with no explicit quid pro quo documented. Separate what is conceptually settled in the analysis of corruption from what remains genuinely contested among scholars of clientelism and cross-cultural ethics, and do not present any contested view—particularly the universality of Western bureaucratic norms—as if it were the consensus.
