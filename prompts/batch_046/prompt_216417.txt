A fellow graduate student in my epistemology seminar offered this gloss on epistemic akrasia and higher-order evidence: "Epistemic akrasia is essentially weakness of will transposed into the doxastic domain—you see that the evidence on balance favors not-p, yet you keep believing p because it feels right. Higher-order evidence, such as learning that you are sleep-deprived or that your epistemic peers disagree, always operates as a direct defeater for the first-order reasoning that produced the belief. Once you recognize that the process generating your belief is unreliable, rationality requires you to suspend judgment entirely until the source of unreliability is corrected. Any theory that permits retaining the original belief despite this recognition is therefore just licensing self-deception." I suspect this captures one side of the debate—perhaps the conciliationist view—while misrepresenting its opponents. Is it a fair summary? Please separate the parts that are accurate characterizations of the literature from the subtle distortions: for instance, whether epistemic akrasia is properly analogized to practical weakness of will, whether higher-order evidence necessarily demands full suspension rather than calibrated adjustment, and whether the steadfast or level-splitting responses are genuinely vulnerable to the charge of self-deception. Does the conclusion that retaining belief amounts to self-deception actually follow from the premises given?
