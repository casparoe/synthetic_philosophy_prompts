As a novelist drafting a scene in which my protagonist interviews a metaphysician for a literary podcast, I am preparing questions for Dr. Silas Vane—an invented but rigorously defined defender of what he calls gradient mereology. In his recent (fictional) monograph, Vane argues that the special composition question rests on a false binary: he replaces the yes-or-no relation of composition with a graded property he terms cohesive entailment, measured by the density of reciprocal causal and modal constraints among parts. At one limit—maximal cohesive entailment—he locates organisms and tightly integrated artifacts as paradigm wholes; at the opposite limit, scattered collections such as a table and a distant moon possess the relation only with a coefficient approaching zero, which he treats not as absence of composition but as composition at negligible intensity. He denies that this introduces vagueness, insisting that the gradient has a precise mathematical structure, and he claims that any practical need for sharp boundaries—say, in biology or law—is satisfied by conventional taxonomy rather than by metaphysical fact.

Please write me a formal, polished sequence of colloquium-style interview questions that press this view where the literature on special composition actually presses. Each question should target a specific commitment of gradient mereology: the arbitrariness of thresholds, the sorites risk across intermediate cases, the threat of collapse into either universalism or nihilism, and the status of our intuitive judgments about tables versus table-moon aggregates. Anticipate Vane’s standard replies—especially his appeals to cohesive entailment as primitive, to mathematical precision, and to conventional taxonomy—then embed the follow-up that makes each reply harder to maintain. Avoid generic biographical or career questions; every item should force him to defend a structural feature of the framework. Conclude with a question asking what empirical or conceptual scenario could drive an organism’s cohesion coefficient toward zero without destroying its constituent parts, and whether that outcome would vindicate gradient mereology or instead undermine our reason to treat organisms as the central case of composition.
