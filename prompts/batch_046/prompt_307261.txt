I serve on a three-person allocation committee for a neighborhood mutual-aid collective that assembles emergency supply kits and assigns delivery routes every Friday; we have roughly fifteen minutes to sign off on both the kit rules and the route plan before volunteers leave. I am not an academic, but after listening to a podcast discussion of John Rawls I have been trying to apply his veil of ignorance and his difference principle — the notion that just institutions are those we would choose without knowing our social position, and that inequalities are permissible only when they work to the advantage of the least well off — to our actual decisions. I need a reusable, step-by-step procedure that my committee can run with the limited data we actually have: an intake form listing income bracket, household size, mobility limitations, and vehicle access. The procedure should instruct us to evaluate each proposed rule by placing ourselves behind a partial veil — we know we are either committee members, volunteers, or recipients in the pool, but we do not know which specific position we occupy — and then apply the difference principle to determine whether an inequality in kit size or route burden genuinely improves the position of the worst-off group rather than merely raising the average.

Please build the checklist in explicit stages — identifying the worst-off cohort in our pool, testing whether the proposed inequality is necessary for that improvement, checking for collective-collapse risks if the burden on middle groups undermines volunteer retention, and marking every point where a value judgment is unavoidable, such as weighing immediate nutritional security against long-term network sustainability or defining "advantage" when the worst off include both immobile elderly households and working parents with unreliable transportation. Apply it concretely to three cases: first, a proposal to double kit portions for households below the poverty line while reducing portions for households just above it; second, a proposal to assign the longest, most fuel-intensive routes to volunteers who own reliable vehicles, knowing that several of those volunteers are themselves low-income and depend on our fuel stipend; and third, a proposal to prioritize delivery to households with disabled members, which delays delivery to a non-disabled household with an infant and no vehicle. I anticipate resisting the answer in the second case — it feels like we are exploiting our own members — so I want the procedure to deliver a definite verdict on whether that resistance is consistent with Rawlsian justice or a failure to apply the principle correctly, and I want you to defend that verdict rather than present both sides. Finally, state precisely what this procedure is incapable of resolving and where it will misfire if we treat it as more exact than it is. Give me one committed, coherent version of the procedure with its judgments exposed, not a menu of interpretations.
