I am preparing a podcast interview with a historian of early modern science who defends a position she calls calibrated presentism. She argues that historians can never fully suspend the normative frameworks of their own era, but she insists this is not a failure of method—rather, it is an unavoidable condition that can be turned into an explanatory tool. To operationalize this, she proposes what she terms a retrograde calibration matrix: a structured comparison between the evidential and argumentative norms operative in present disciplinary practice and the recoverable decision-rules documented within past scientific communities. By explicitly mapping where these two frameworks diverge, she claims historians avoid hidden anachronism, because present standards are not silently projected onto the past but are used to locate precise points of incommensurability. Critics reply that the matrix is circular, since selecting which past decision-rules are relevant for comparison already requires present-day criteria of significance, and that in practice the method collapses into a refined Whig history—particularly when applied to abandoned research programs such as phlogiston chemistry, where the present framework treats the past theory as a straightforward failure rather than an alternative rationality. Draft eight to ten interview questions that press her beyond restating the view. Include at least one question on the circularity objection regarding how past rules are selected for inclusion in the matrix, one on how she applies the matrix to a concrete case like phlogiston without importing presentist victory conditions, and one on whether her claim that all interpretation is unavoidable but auditable actually distinguishes her position from standard reflexive historiography. For each of these three, provide a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give—such as that the matrix is purely descriptive, that phlogistonists shared enough procedural norms with modern chemists to permit non-triumphalist comparison, or that auditability requires a transparency standard she has not yet fully specified.
