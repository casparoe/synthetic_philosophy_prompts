I just finished a long, somewhat heated argument with a friend about whether consequentialist reasoning collapses into conceptual incoherence—rather than mere practical difficulty—when institutions face radical uncertainty about distant outcomes, the problem often called consequentialist cluelessness. The disagreement was sharp enough that I want a formal institutional recommendation rather than more informal debate. I sit on our university’s Interdisciplinary Research Governance Board, and we must vote next Thursday on whether to authorize a 25-year observational study tracking emergent cognitive architectures in distributed synthetic systems, where second- and third-order societal spillovers are genuinely unmodelable with present tools. My friend insists that if we take cluelessness seriously, we should reject the protocol outright: any attempt to justify approval through expected-value reasoning is epistemically illegitimate when the outcome space is that opaque, and institutional approval should require calculable welfare impacts or not be granted at all. I believe that position overreaches, but I need the board’s written opinion—addressed to members, circulated before the vote—recommending either approval, conditional approval with specific safeguards, or rejection. The memo should propose a concrete decision criterion and timeline that does not pretend to compute unknowable utilities, should treat the cluelessness objection on its own terms rather than reducing it to generic risk aversion, and should include an honest, non-dismissive acknowledgment of the strongest consideration on the losing side.
