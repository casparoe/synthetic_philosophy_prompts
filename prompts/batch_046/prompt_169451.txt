I've been thinking about whether we can genuinely compare how badly different people suffer or flourish, not just in abstract theory but in the concrete judgments I find myself making when I try to rank misfortunes. I don't have any formal philosophy training, but I've tried to notice exactly where my intuition says "this person is clearly worse off than that one" and where it refuses to rank them at all, and the pattern seems messy in a way that bothers me.

Here are the cases as I see them. If two strangers both break the same bone but one has a lifelong high pain threshold and reports only mild discomfort while the other reports agony, I think the second is suffering more, but only because I implicitly correct for their different "baseline sensitivities"—if I don't make that correction and just compare the raw reports, the comparison feels hollow. When I imagine a concert pianist losing her hearing versus a painter losing her sight, I think I can rank the harm if I take into account how each person's "life architecture" is built around those senses, but without that contextual adjustment the comparison seems arbitrary. On the other hand, if I picture a naturally cheerful person falling from a 9-out-of-10 life satisfaction to a 7, versus a chronically depressed person rising from 3 to 5, I feel strongly that the first person's drop is not "more suffering" despite the larger numerical fall—here my intuition seems to reject any simple baseline-correction and instead looks at something like "distance from the life trajectory they were meant to have," though I can't quite articulate what that means. I also think comparing the grief of losing a child with the anxiety of perpetual low-grade dread is possible, but only if I translate both into how severely they disrupt a shared human "intended functioning"; without that translation, the two feel incommensurable, like comparing colors by weight.

So I'm trying to see if there's one coherent principle behind all of this, or if I'm just patching together excuses after the fact. Is there an account that explains why baseline-correction works for physical pain but fails for life-satisfaction drops, why "life architecture" matters for sensory loss but not for emotional states, and why "disruption to intended functioning" can bridge grief and anxiety? And if such a principle exists, what should it say about the case I keep returning to: a person born without the capacity for joy above a 4-out-of-10, who lives a stable, unambitious life at that level, compared to a high-achiever who crashes from 9 to 6 due to a career failure? Which one is worse off, and does that verdict force me to abandon one of my other judgments?
