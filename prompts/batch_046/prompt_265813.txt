As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy systematically, I wish to explain Jain anekāntavāda—the doctrine that reality possesses an infinite number of irreducible aspects and that any single assertion captures only a conditional, partial truth—to a seminar of fellow retired laboratory scientists (physicists, biologists, and engineers) who have no background in Indian philosophy but are deeply accustomed to model-dependent measurement and to the fact that different instruments reveal genuinely different properties of the same sample. I require a teaching plan, not the lecture itself. Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order, with approximate lengths:

1. Dependency sequence and order of introduction (approx. 250 words): Lay out the exact order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced—beginning from what the audience already grasps about multi-modal observation and ending with the metaphysical claim that reality is ananta-dharmātmaka—explaining for each step what prior idea it depends upon and why reversing the order would cause the explanation to collapse. Identify precisely where syādvāda, the logical form of conditional predication with its characteristic non-absolute qualifier, must be introduced relative to anekānta, and justify that placement.

2. The carrying example: design, properties, and deployment (approx. 300 words): Propose one concrete, non-standard scenario—not the conventional blind-men-and-elephant parable—that can carry the full weight of both anekāntavāda and syādvāda. Specify exactly what the example must possess (for instance, a single material object that genuinely exhibits apparently contradictory properties under different conditions of observation without those properties being merely subjective or ambiguous) and what it must lack (for instance, simple epistemic limitation, disagreement between observers, or ambiguity of language). Explain how you would walk the audience through applying the seven modes of predication (saptabhaṅgī) to this object, and state which specific misconception this example is engineered to block.

3. Misconception map and defusion points (approx. 300 words): Catalog the predictable misconceptions this particular audience will bring or form—especially the conflation of anekāntavāda with scientific perspectivism, with epistemic humility, with modern cultural relativism, or with a straightforward violation of the law of non-contradiction—and specify at which point in the sequence from section 1 each misconception should be anticipated and neutralized. For each, state the precise distinction that must be drawn (for example, metaphysical multiplicity of aspects versus epistemic limitation of the observer) and the brief demonstration or phrase that defuses it.

4. Check for understanding (approx. 150 words): Design a single diagnostic task—preferably one that requires the audience to construct, rather than merely recognize, a conditional predication about the carrying example—that would reveal whether the idea has actually landed. Specify what a correct response must contain and what a common wrong response reveals about which misconception remains active.

5. Boundaries and omissions (approx. 150 words): State explicitly what must be left out of this twenty-five-minute explanation—such as the full taxonomy of naya-standpoints, the historical dialectic with Buddhist śūnyavāda, and the technical derivations of Jain syllogistic logic—and say so openly, explaining for each why its inclusion would derail the dependency sequence or confuse the audience without adding necessary clarity.

The plan should treat anekāntavāda as a structured logical and metaphysical doctrine rather than as vague pluralism, should avoid any request for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should be usable by someone with no access to external sources.
