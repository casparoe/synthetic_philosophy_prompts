I'm constructing an argument for a workshop on atrocity naming and now distrust it because the conclusion seems to dissolve the very category I'm defending. Here are the steps.

(1) The 1948 UN Genocide Convention defines genocide by specific intent (dolus specialis) to destroy, in whole or in part, a protected group, through acts such as killing or serious bodily harm, and it deliberately excludes what was then discussed as "cultural genocide"—the systematic erosion of a group's language, religious practices, or social institutions without physical extermination.

(2) The reason activists, jurists, and states fight so intensely over whether an atrocity receives the label "genocide" rather than "war crime" or "crime against humanity" is that the label is treated as carrying unique, non-derogable moral and legal weight—an absolute condemnation that triggers obligations to prevent, intervene, and prosecute that other categories do not carry.

(3) If this special weight is justified by the severity of harm to victims, then excluding cultural destruction is morally arbitrary: the forced erasure of a group's collective identity, language, and institutions can produce suffering, dislocation, and loss of meaning comparable in magnitude to physical destruction, and it can be pursued with equal deliberateness.

(4) Therefore, consistency demands either that we extend genocide's special status to include cultural destruction, or that we deny genocide has any special status and treat all mass atrocities as morally equivalent.

(5) Denying special status contradicts the actual practice and justification of the politics of naming, which treats genocide as sui generis; so we must extend the category.

(6) But if cultural destruction counts as genocide, and if "destruction of the group" is interpreted structurally—as eroding the conditions for the group's continued existence as a distinct collective—then numerous ordinary state policies risk falling within the definition: compulsory schooling in a dominant language, urban renewal that disperses ethnic neighborhoods, or suppression of minority religious practices, whenever pursued with awareness of their erosive effect.

(7) One might object that these policies lack the requisite "intent to destroy," but on a structural reading, sustained knowledge of destructive effects combined with refusal to alter course can constitute intent; and liberal states have historically pursued assimilationist policies with precisely such knowledge.

(8) Thus, either we must classify standard state-building and assimilationist practices as genocide—trivializing the category—or we must retract the claim that genocide has special moral weight—undermining the politics of naming—or we must maintain the arbitrary exclusion of cultural harm, violating the consistency demanded in (4).

(9) Hence, the Convention's narrowing is not merely a pragmatic legal compromise but a deep moral contradiction: it tries to preserve genocide's unique status while excluding harms comparable in severity, and any attempt to repair the exclusion collapses the distinction between atrocity and ordinary governance.

If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (2) and (3) than accept (9), but I want to know which step is actually doing the illegitimate work and what giving it up costs.
