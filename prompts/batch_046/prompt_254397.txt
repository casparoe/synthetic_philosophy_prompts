I am a senior software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I serve on the oversight board for my employer's synthetic-research division. We are deadlocked over a concrete classification decision that seems to turn on a deeper conceptual issue I can describe but not fully resolve.

Our R&D team has engineered synthetic structures they call autopoietic polymer lattices—fully inorganic, algorithmically specified systems that metabolize ambient energy, maintain dynamic equilibrium, and replicate with heritable error-correction, yet every boundary condition and catalytic pathway was intentionally designed. The division petitions to have these lattices classified as living organisms under our institutional biosafety charter, which would trigger containment protocols and ethical-review requirements intended for natural biological entities. One faction on the board argues that life denotes a natural kind defined by an underlying causal structure—functional autonomy and homeostatic regulation—regardless of historical origin, so the lattices qualify. The opposing faction argues that a kind only carves nature at its joints when its unity is discovered rather than imposed; because the lattices' replication and metabolism are executed by engineered algorithms rather than evolved biochemical pathways, classifying them as living commits what an internal science advisor termed the designer fallacy, conflating intentional architecture with an independently existing causal structure.

Please draft the board's formal decision memo—recommending approval, rejection, or conditional approval—with reasoning that the research team could actually respond to in practice. The memo should engage the conceptual question directly but without invoking obscure named doctrines or assuming specialized philosophical literacy; work from the structure of the case itself. It must include an honest treatment of the strongest consideration from the side you do not take. Do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or reading lists.
