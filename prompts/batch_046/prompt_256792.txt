As a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading in philosophy of history and science studies, I find myself caught between two instincts whenever I encounter explanations that invoke pathogens, climatic shifts, or volcanic events as forces in human affairs—say, the 1815 Tambora eruption and its subsequent famine and political aftershocks, the role of Yersinia pestis in the Black Death, or the influence of the Little Ice Age on early modern agricultural crisis. My scientific training treats these as mechanistic causal factors embedded in larger physical systems; yet historiographical writing often describes them as “actors” possessing explanatory weight comparable to human decision-makers. I want a taxonomy—specifically a decision tree in which each branch is labeled by the concrete claim or case judgment that separates it from its siblings—to sort out the philosophical positions on whether, and in what sense, non-human forces should be granted the status of “historical actors.”

Please begin with a concrete diagnostic scenario rather than an abstract definition: consider a historical narrative stating that a nineteenth-century cholera outbreak “reshaped” urban sanitation policy. The first node should ask whether a respondent takes this actor-language to be purely metaphorical, or whether it tracks a distinctive explanatory role that cannot be reduced to background conditions.

From there, the tree should branch on the following splits, each posed as a question answerable without knowing philosophical labels:

1. Is causal efficacy alone sufficient for actor-status, or is some form of responsiveness, goal-directedness, or minimal intentionality also required?
2. Does granting actor-status to these forces necessarily entail environmental determinism—the view that human moral and political agency is systematically undermined by non-human causes—or can a coherent multi-causal framework accommodate both human and non-human “actors” without collapse?
3. Is there a distinct epistemic peril—perhaps call it “narrative displacement”—in which the dramatic structure of historiography systematically overweights visible, discrete non-human events (a volcano, an epidemic) at the expense of slow, diffuse human structural processes, and does this peril operate independently of determinism?
4. Given one’s answers above, is the appropriate philosophical stance eliminativist (all actor-language is a category error), instrumentalist (retain it for narrative economy without ontological commitment), or realist (distributed or non-human agency is a coherent category)?

Each leaf should carry the name of the position if one is standard (e.g., strong environmental determinism, co-constitutive multi-causalism, critical instrumentalism), or be marked “unnamed/unoccupied” if the combination is not prominently defended. Every leaf must also include one sentence stating its most awkward commitment—for example, the view that accepts actor-language but rejects determinism must explain why the eruption’s “intervention” does not compete with the state’s “response” in the same explanatory currency.

Please flag any pair of leaves that the tree genuinely cannot separate—positions that share identical answers to every diagnostic question but differ only in rhetorical emphasis or disciplinary vocabulary—and briefly diagnose whether that unoccupied logical space is incoherent, merely unexplored, or occupied by views that collapse upon inspection.

The questions should be phrased so that a respondent answers by reflecting on concrete judgments about cases, not by recognizing labels. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citation; I want an instrument that guides reflection without requiring access to specific texts.
