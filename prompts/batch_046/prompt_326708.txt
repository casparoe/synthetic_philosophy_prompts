I'm preparing a presentation for my advanced high-school philosophy seminar on whether genuine moral dilemmas can actually exist—situations where an agent is rationally required to do two incompatible actions and cannot avoid wrongdoing. Rather than asking for a list of named theories, I want a decision tree that sorts real philosophical positions by concrete answers to diagnostic questions, so that someone could work through the branches without already knowing the labels. The first split should ask whether moral reasons are always commensurable in principle, meaning every conflict can be resolved by weighing them correctly. The second split should ask whether unavoidable emotional residue—such as lasting remorse or guilt after one acts—is a reliable sign that a true dilemma occurred, or merely evidence of a difficult but resolvable choice. The third split should ask whether a dilemma disappears if there is objectively one best action, even when the agent can never be certain which action it is. At each leaf, place the corresponding view—strict Kantian or deontological denial, tragic-choice acceptance in the style of Bernard Williams, particularist endorsement, deflationary prima-facie-conflict reduction, or any unnamed combination—and briefly note whether empty branches seem incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. Include a concrete case, such as a judge who must violate one binding oath to prevent catastrophic harm, so the tree classifies people by what they say about the case rather than by which theory name they recognize. I do not need citations, bibliographies, or reading lists—just the logical structure, the reasoning at each node, and an honest flag for any pair of positions the tree cannot cleanly separate.
