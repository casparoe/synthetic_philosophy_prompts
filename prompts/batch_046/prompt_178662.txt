I'm working through the philosophical debate over whether individual actions can carry meaningful moral or causal weight in large-scale collective harms—climate emissions, industrial animal agriculture, and mass democratic voting—when any single agent's expected marginal impact is statistically swamped. Two camps have argued past each other for years, and I want you to draft the adversarial-collaboration document they should have produced. One camp, the inefficacy objectors, holds that once causal contributions are diluted across collective dynamics, attributing moral responsibility or demanding individual behavioral change is conceptually confused; they treat institutional and collective agency as the only appropriate loci of evaluation and regard individual-focused prescriptions as a category error. The other camp, the structural-participation theorists, maintains that individual actions matter through non-linear threshold effects, norm-expressive coordination, and constitutive roles in sustaining systemic patterns—even when linear causal impact approaches zero—so that framing the issue solely in terms of expected marginal harm misses the relevant moral and causal structure.

Please produce the joint statement both sides could sign. First, a numbered section of agreed claims about the empirical and conceptual landscape: the mathematics of expected marginal contribution in large populations, the causal opacity that separates individual consumption or voting choices from specific harmful outcomes, the distinction between causing harm and participating in a harmful structure, and what is actually known about feedback loops and institutional responses to individual-level shifts. Second, a disagreements table stating each camp's position in wording its own proponents would accept, with divergences made precise—not merely "individuals matter" versus "they don't," but whether moral complicity requires counterfactual dependence, whether expressive or coordinative value can ground obligations without linear causal efficacy, and whether the inefficacy argument illicitly applies expected-utility reasoning to deontological or virtue-ethical frameworks. Third, identify the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side—including whether naturalness or institutional feasibility should constrain evaluations of individual agency, whether documented cases exist of sub-critical individual actions triggering systemic cascades in supply chains or electoral dynamics, and whether the concept of "structural complicity" collapses into standard causal contribution upon analysis. For each crux, specify a concrete case or empirical finding both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts about that case.

Write this with commitment: do not present the disagreements as a menu of equally plausible interpretations. Choose formulations that are genuinely neutral and precise, state clearly which disagreements are substantive and which are verbal or framing-dependent—such as whether "responsibility" should be analyzed causally, structurally, or expressively—and defend those judgments with reasons rather than hedging. If part of the dispute dissolves upon inspection into a disagreement about which conceptual framework is appropriate, say exactly which part and why. I want the residual disagreement made exact, not smoothed over, and I want the document to read as something both parties could put their names to without feeling misrepresented.
