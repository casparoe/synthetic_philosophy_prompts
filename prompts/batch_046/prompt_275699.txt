So I was arguing with my roommate about what actually makes something alive, and I think I might be mixing up two different things but I can't tell which. He mentioned this idea from like the 70s called autopoiesis, where living things are systems that produce their own boundaries and maintain themselfs, which sounds right for cells but then he said that means a synthetic protocell built from scratch doesn't count because it didn't "make itself" in the evolutionary sense. But then I read that the NASA working definition uses Darwinian evolution as the key marker, which seems like a totally different criteria — one is about self-production, the other is about historical lineage. So here's where I'm stuck: if autopoiesis is the real principle, then shouldn't viruses be dead since they don't maintain their own metabolism? And if Darwinian evolution is the principle, then shouldn't synthetic minimal cells like JCVI-syn3.0 be alive once they start replicating with mutation? Or are these just two competing definitions with no way to choose?

But then there's this other case that breaks both for me. There's these things called viroids that are just naked RNA, no protein coat, and they replicate inside plants using host machinery. They're simpler than viruses but we don't usually call them alive. Meanwhile, dormant bacterial spores can sit for centuries with basically zero metabolism and then wake up, and everyone agrees they're still alive. So my question is: is the reason we keep these categories straight that there's some deeper physical principle — maybe something about dissipative structures or thermodynamic openness — that just hasn't been named well yet? Or am I wrong to think there even needs to be one unified answer, and "life" is just a practical label we use for seperate clusters of phenomena? I feel like there must be a correct view but every time I pick one it fails on some weird edge case.
