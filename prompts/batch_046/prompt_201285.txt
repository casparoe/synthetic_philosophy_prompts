I'm trying to pin down a pattern I keep seeing in memory-and-rationality puzzles—mostly the absent-minded driver, but also in other planning setups. There's this occurence where an agent's best strategy requires them to maintain a false belief about what they'll do at a key step, and if they correct that belief the reasoning behind the later move collapses into contradiction with the reasoning behind the earlier one. For instance, the driver who rationally intends to stay on the highway but then takes the correct exit; the reasoning that makes taking it rational at that moment can't be held together with the reasoning that made going straight rational at the start, unless there's a gap in his memory about having reached that point. Or take a doctor who sets a treatment protocol that depends on her forgetting the initial diagnosis that triggered it—if she remembers the diagnosis clearly, she revises the protocol and the treatment fails. In both cases there's this particularlly narrow band where partial amnesia isn't just a handicap but a structural requirement: fill the blind spot and the plan becomes incoherent from the inside, not just changed. I've heard people call this dynamic inconsistency, but that seems broader—this is specifically about self-knowledge destroying the coherence of the plan itself. Is there an established term for this necessary-blind-spot configuration? And is it a real feature of rational planning, or just an artifact of how we model forgetting? Please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested, don't present either side as the concensus, and write in American English.
