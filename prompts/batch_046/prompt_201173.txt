As a retired physicist recently drawn to social philosophy, I have agreed to lead one session of an interdisciplinary retirees' group on the thesis that modern nations are constructed rather than primordial—specifically, on the notions of invented tradition and imagined community linked to Hobsbawm and Anderson. The audience are mostly engineers and applied scientists who treat borders and flags as evidence of deep historical continuity and who are likely to hear "constructed" as "fictitious." I need an explanatory plan, not a script.

Set out the conceptual dependencies in order: which distinction—perhaps between a long-standing cultural practice and a formally institutionalized tradition—must be secured before the central claim can even be formulated, and which elaboration should come only after that. For the carrying example, use a case in which an administrative census category suddenly hardens a previously fluid group identity—explain exactly what this example must include and what it must omit to do the pedagogical work. Then map the predictable misconceptions in the sequence they will arise: that "invented" means fabricated out of nothing; that this denies all pre-modern collective loyalty; and that it applies only to states formed outside Western Europe. Specify at which point in the sequence each should be intercepted and by what move. Provide one closing question that reveals whether the audience has grasped the structural argument rather than adopted a merely cynical stance. Finally, identify one region of the historiography—perhaps the granular chronology of nineteenth-century state-building rituals—that I should explicitly exclude and declare out of scope, so that the session does not collapse into a list of dates.
