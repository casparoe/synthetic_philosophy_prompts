As a novelist who regularly constructs hypothetical scenarios to test the moral architecture of fictional characters, I have become increasingly skeptical of philosophy's reliance on intuition pumps—particularly in philosophy of mind, where devices such as Mary's black-and-white room and Searle's Chinese Room are often presented as if they were decisive experiments rather than carefully staged narratives. Write an essay weighing whether these thought experiments genuinely advance conceptual understanding, or whether they systematically mislead by exploiting narrative techniques—focalized perspective, emotional identification, and the artificial closure of plot—to manufacture an illusion of philosophical clarity. In developing your argument, distinguish between diegetic pumps, which immerse the reader as an agent inside the scenario, and expository pumps, which observe a subject from an external vantage; assess whether this structural difference explains why certain pumps generate widespread consensus while others fracture sharply along disciplinary lines. You should also examine what I will call focalization bias: the tendency of such experiments to achieve analytical precision by isolating a single mental state, mechanism, or qualitative episode, thereby obscuring the contextual, embodied, and temporally extended dimensions that narrative art knows to be inseparable from consciousness. Reach and justify a clear conclusion about whether intuition pumps, despite these narrative liabilities, retain a legitimate methodological role in philosophy, or whether they ought to be replaced by more structurally explicit forms of conceptual modeling.
