You are a senior philosopher of ethics and logic with experience writing fellowship and workshop proposals. Before drafting, spend a few sentences brainstorming: what makes a philosophical proposal genuinely tractable rather than merely ambitious, and what hidden ambiguity in the standard framing of this problem makes it resistant to the usual historical surveys? Then write a concise research proposal—about two pages, suited for a one-semester independent study or a small philosophical workshop—addressing the following.

The is-ought gap and the naturalistic fallacy are usually taught as a settled warning: descriptive premises cannot entail normative conclusions, and any inference that tries to do so collapses into either circularity or category error. But this framing may obscure a more specific structural mistake. Your proposal should target this precise question: is the fallacy best understood as a failure of logical entailment between fact-statements and value-statements, or is it specifically a failure of modal scope—an illicit shift from stating that X is the case to prescribing that X ought to be maintained, one that skips the intermediate claim about what agents have practical reason to pursue?

State the question with enough precision that it has a determinate answer, not just a broad theme. Explain why the canonical responses—Hume's separation of reason and passion, Moore's open-question argument, and the modern attempts to bridge the gap through practical reason or institutional design—leave this particular reconstruction unsettled. Describe your actual method: will you formalize the logical structure of normative inferences, examine how policy-science arguments implicitly manage the gap, or reconstruct the implicit modal logic in key historical texts? Defend this method against the obvious alternatives, explaining why a straightforward historical survey or purely conceptual analysis would not settle the issue. Specify what success looks like—perhaps a taxonomy that distinguishes legitimate normative derivations from fallacious ones without requiring a sharp metaphysical divide between fact and value—and what failure looks like, including the risk that your distinction collapses back into the standard framing or proves inapplicable to real-world arguments. Finally, assess scope honestly: is one semester sufficient for both the conceptual reconstruction and the application to actual reasoning, or does one part threaten to dominate the other? Write without a bibliography, literature review, or citation requests; mention thinkers and positions by name where necessary, but do not ask for specific sources, page references, or reading lists.
