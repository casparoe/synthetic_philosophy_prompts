Everyone treats his integrity objection and the 'one thought too many' bit as seperate complaints about utilitarian-style reasoning, but I want to actually compare their structures rather than just listing them. On one hand there's the integrity objection: Jim in the jungle with the Indians, where killing one to save nineteen violates his ground projects and makes him an agent of horror he can't live with. That's about not being forced to alienate yourself from your core commitments for the aggregate good. On the other hand there's 'one thought too many'—the husband saving his wife should think 'it's my wife', not 'she's a person with moral value and I have duties to people'. Both seem anti-calculative, but are they the same kind of anti-calculative? The integrity objection feels like it's defending agent-relative reasons and the importance of not betraying what makes you you. The 'one thought too many' thing feels more like it's about the phenomenology of love and intimacy—adding a moral layer corrupts the relationship itself. So my ask: compare the two objections precisely—what kind of reasoning failure each targets, whether they share a common root in Williams's view of moral psychology, and whether they're actually consistent. Or is there a clean boundary between reflecting on your projects (allowed) and reflecting within intimate action (forbidden)?
