I've noticed a recurring asymmetry in how people deploy observer-selection reasoning, and I'm unsure whether it has a specific name or is simply an unremarked inconsistency. The pattern runs like this. In cosmological debates, someone will argue that a theory predicting vastly more observers—say, across a multiverse or in distant future civilizations—receives support from the bare fact that I exist, on the grounds that I am more likely to be one among many than one among few. This is treated as a serious, if contentious, inference. Yet when the same logic is applied closer to home—say, to the question of why I am a human in the twenty-first century rather than an ant in the Cretaceous, or why I am not among the far more numerous future generations that might exist if civilization survives—the inference is usually dismissed. The dismissal typically appeals to some hidden restriction on the reference class: "I'm not randomly drawn from all time," or "my identity is fixed independently of population size," or "this is a different kind of indexical uncertainty." What strikes me is that the restriction is introduced precisely where the conclusion is unwelcome, not derived from an independent principle about reference-class selection. I have seen this happen with multiverse arguments, with simulation hypotheses, and with long-term demographic projections, always with the same shape: the sampling principle is expanded to cover large populations when it flatters a preferred ontology, then contracted to protect an intuitive self-location.

Is there an established concept for this selective expansion and contraction of the observer reference class? I know it sits near the debate between self-sampling and self-indication assumptions, and near the presumptuous philosopher problem in anthropic reasoning, but it seems distinct from either: it is not merely choosing one principle over the other, but applying whichever one yields the desired result in different domains without justification. Does it have a name—perhaps in the literature on anthropic bias, or on motivated reasoning in philosophy of probability? If not, how should one characterize its boundaries: is it a fallacy of reference-class gerrymandering, a failure of indexical consistency, or something else? And where exactly does it differ from ordinary equivocation on "I"? I am especially curious whether the boundary cases I mentioned—treating temporal location as susceptible to population-weighting while treating species membership as immune—genuinely belong to the same pattern or to different ones.
