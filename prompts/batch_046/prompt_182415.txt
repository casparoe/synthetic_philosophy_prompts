I have been working through the literature on the philosophy of medicine, particularly the tension between naturalist and normativist accounts of health and disease. I keep encountering a particular conceptual move that I do not have a precise label for, and I am unsure whether it is recognised under a specific name in the field.

Consider how naturalist theories — especially those that define disease in terms of statistical deviation from a biologically specified reference class — present themselves as avoiding explicit value judgments. Yet in practice, selecting that reference class involves choices about which populations are standard: whether to use age-stratified cohorts, sex-specific distributions, or broader demographic aggregates. These selections are rarely defended on purely biological grounds; rather, they seem guided by implicit normative assumptions about which functional capacities matter for a good human life. Once the reference class is fixed, the resulting statistical deviations are then treated as objective discoveries of dysfunction, as though the values had not entered through the selection of the baseline. I have noticed this pattern in discussions ranging from metabolic syndromes to cognitive ageing, where the naturalist framing promises neutrality but the operationalisation of "normal" depends on prior judgments about desirable functioning.

Is there an established concept for this specific failure mode — the smuggling of normative commitments into a naturalist framework via the back door of reference-class selection — and how does it differ from the general problem of theory-ladenness in observation, from the standard epidemiological reference-class problem, and from Wakefield's explicit incorporation of harm into the concept of dysfunction? If there is no dedicated term, could you propose one, together with clear boundary conditions that distinguish it from ordinary auxiliary hypotheses and from straightforward conceptual equivocation? I should be grateful for an answer in British English.
