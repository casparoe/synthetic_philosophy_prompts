I have recently been revisiting some older discussions on decision-theoretic paradoxes in rationalist forums, and I find myself stuck on a point about the centipede game and backward induction. As I understand the standard account, two players take turns deciding whether to continue or stop, with payoffs increasing if they cooperate, yet each has an incentive to defect just before the other would; reasoning backwards from the final round, common knowledge of rationality should drive both to stop immediately. The result is usually presented as a normative puzzle: rational agents ought to defect at once, yet in experiments they do not, which invites explanations in terms of bounded rationality, social preference, or flawed reasoning.

However, I have been experimenting with a conceptual variant—let us call it the truncated-induction framework—in which we do not treat rationality as an all-or-nothing property extending across every round, but rather as a resource that can be exhausted. Specifically, suppose that each player knows the other can perform at most n steps of backward reasoning, where n is far smaller than the total length of the game, and suppose further that this cognitive ceiling is itself common knowledge. Under these conditions, the induction chain does not reach round one at all; it halts at round n, and both players can rationally continue up to that boundary without facing any logical pressure to defect earlier. This suggests to me that the classic paradox may depend not on cooperation being irrational, but on an implicit assumption that agents possess an unbounded capacity for recursive common-knowledge reasoning—an assumption that seems psychologically unrealistic.

My question, then, is whether I am correct in thinking that backward induction requires an infinite (or at least complete) chain of mutual knowledge in order to generate defection at the first move, and that if this chain is truncated by realistic cognitive limits, the paradox dissolves for all practical purposes. If so, why do philosophers and economists continue to treat the centipede result as a deep challenge to cooperative behaviour rather than as a reductio ad absurdum of the unbounded-rationality model? I worry that I am conflating the logical structure of the argument with its psychological implementation, but I cannot identify precisely where the confusion lies.
