I am putting together materials for a graduate seminar on sentimentalist moral psychology, and my students have been debating not whether Adam Smith belongs in the sentimentalist camp, but exactly what normative authority his impartial spectator is supposed to carry and by what mechanism sympathy delivers it. Rather than asking for a literature review, I want a decision tree—usable as a classroom sorting instrument—that maps the logical space of interpretive positions from the ground up, so that someone can be sorted into a view by answering concrete questions about sympathy and judgment without already knowing the scholarly labels.

Start by asking whether sympathy, in Smith's account, requires an imaginative exchange of situations—actively placing oneself in the other's circumstances—or can operate through direct emotional resonance without such role-taking. From that root, branch to the nature of the spectator: is it an internalized accumulation of actual social judgments, an idealized rational observer detached from particular passions, or a phenomenological structure that only crystallizes in the moment of judgment itself? Next, split on whether the spectator's verdict derives authority from being a corrected emotional response—sympathy regulated by distance, information, and perspective—or from approximating a non-emotional, reason-based standard of propriety. Each leaf should carry a label such as social-internalized sentimentalist, rationalist-ideal spectator, phenomenological-structural, corrected-proximity view, or unnamed, followed by one sentence on the position's most awkward commitment—for example, that it must treat moral disagreement as perceptual miscalibration, or that it cannot distinguish moral error from mere social nonconformity.

Please phrase every node as a concrete claim or case a student could accept or reject without knowing the scholarly label it points toward, so the tree sorts people by belief rather than by vocabulary. Where the logical space allows a combination that no named view occupies, include it as an empty leaf and briefly diagnose whether the gap stems from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Explicitly flag any pairs of named interpretations that your tree cannot separate—such as views that share both the mechanism of sympathy and the source of the spectator's authority—and explain why they collapse onto the same branch. The audience is students who have read Smith's major moral-psychological writings but are not specialists, and the instrument should stay reconstructible from conceptual analysis of sympathy, distance, and judgment rather than from obscure secondary debates.
