As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I have been following an online discussion group that has spent months circling the classic epistemic regress problem without anyone conceding ground. The positions are familiar: foundationalists insist that justification must bottom out in basic beliefs that need no further support; coherentists maintain that beliefs are justified by their mutual integration within a holistic web, with no privileged base; and infinitists argue that justification requires an infinite, non-repeating chain of reasons, and that this is not vicious but structurally acceptable. Despite exhaustive rehearsal of the standard objections—arbitrariness of the foundations, circularity in coherence, and the alleged impossibility of completing an infinite chain—the participants remain locked in place. Foundationalists treat coherence as disguised circularity; coherentists treat basic beliefs as epistemically arbitrary; infinitists are dismissed as describing an unattainable ideal. I am trying to understand whether this is genuinely a deep disagreement or whether the conversation has stalled because the participants are answering different questions under the single heading of "justification."

My working suspicion, shaped by thinking about verification architectures in distributed systems, is that part of the impasse is hidden in a mismatch between local and global notions of support—something like the difference between a root certificate in a trust hierarchy, a consensus protocol with no single root node, and a lazily evaluated proof tree that has no base case but remains well-founded. I wonder whether the foundationalist is implicitly demanding a locally grounded termination condition for every belief-token, the coherentist is treating justification as an emergent global property of the system, and the infinitist is treating it as a potential infinity that is tractable only if the chain is well-ordered rather than actually traversed. If that architectural analogy captures something real, then the debate may not be about whether knowledge is possible at all, but about which formal properties—termination, consistency, well-foundedness—are required for an epistemic architecture to be rationally acceptable. Is the disagreement therefore verbal, turning on what "justification" means in different contexts? Is it substantive but hidden behind a failure to distinguish local from global norms? Or is there a genuine empirical or formal question—perhaps about the cognitive tractability of infinite reasoning chains—that neither side has addressed?

Please diagnose the structure of this stalemate with reference to what each of the three positions actually commits to, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a formal result about well-foundedness or consistency, a change in the framing question, or an argument that the disagreement should be expected to remain permanently unresolved. I am not looking for a survey of the literature or a list of authors; I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, and an honest assessment of whether anything—conceptual, formal, or empirical—could actually move it.
