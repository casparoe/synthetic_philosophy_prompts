In my philosophy of decision theory reading group we've went round in circles for months about whether transformative experiences actually break rational choice, specifically in cases like deciding on gender-affirming hormone therapy or deep brain stimulation for OCD where the intervention might flip your core values. Everyone basically accepts Paul's point that you can't know what it's like beforehand and that your preferences can invert afterwards. The rational-choice camp says you just maximize expected utility over possible future selves; the skeptics say there's no fact of the matter about which self's preferences should govern the decision. Zero movement. Is the stall because we're smuggling in different theories of personal identity without admitting it? Or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about how common these value-flips actually are post-transition or post-DBS? Or are the two sides literally answering different questions -- one about decision-theoretic rationality at a single time slice, one about diachronic agency and authority? I want an actual diagnosis that engages what each side says, not a literature survey. And tell me what kind of move could break it -- a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding, a formal model -- or whether we should just accept that some choices are rationally opaque and stop expecting progress.
