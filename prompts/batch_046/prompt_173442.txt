I'm preparing a long-form feature on rationality in systems that can alter their own decision criteria, and I've become fixated on a philosophical stalemate that seems to resist every formal tool thrown at it. The absent-minded driver problem—the scenario in which an agent must pass the first highway exit to reach a farther destination, yet knows that passing it will trigger a memory lapse that makes her take it anyway—has been analyzed through causal decision theory, dynamic epistemic logic, and models of bounded rationality since it entered the literature. Still, philosophers of action and decision theorists remain divided: one camp insists that rationality demands forming the present intention to drive straight through, treating the future self as an unreliable instrument to be managed; the other camp argues that any plan contingent on forgetting one's own reasons is already incoherent, rendering the "drive straight" intention self-defeating.

What I cannot determine is whether this deadlock is substantive—rooted in genuinely incompatible commitments about the unity of agency over time, the normative force of present intentions against predicted future irrationality, or the proper formalization of absent-mindedness—or whether it is partly verbal, with each side answering a different question (one about descriptive cognitive failure, one about normative planning) while using the same scenario. Could you diagnose the disagreement with reference to what each camp actually says about intention-formation and self-binding, identify whether a hidden empirical claim about human prospective memory or a formal result in dynamic choice theory is doing silent work, and tell me what kind of evidence or conceptual distinction—perhaps a clarification of the boundary between strategic self-deception and mere cognitive limitation—would actually move the debate? I am particularly interested in whether we should expect resolution at all, or whether the case functions as a stable limit case that exposes where decision theory stops being normative and starts describing psychology.
