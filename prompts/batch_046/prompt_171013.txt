You are advising a philosophy research group with three years, two postdocs, and no lab facilities, working at the intersection of metaphysics and philosophy of science. Their focus is dispositions, finks, and masks—specifically, whether standard counterexamples defeat realism about powers, or merely show that dispositional ascription requires contextual parameterization.

Before ranking priorities, consider two framing distinctions that are not yet standard in the literature but should shape how the group evaluates open problems.

First, the double-mask problem: cases in which not only is a manifestation blocked (first-order mask), but the very conditions that would remove the blocker are themselves obstructed by a second-order interference. For example, a compound that would restore solubility is present but chemically neutralized by an environmental factor that itself requires a specific catalyst to be removed. The question is whether a theory of dispositions needs a recursive or hierarchical account of masking conditions, or whether first-order solutions—ceteris paribus clauses, ideal conditions—already absorb these cases without additional metaphysical machinery.

Second, dispositional inertia: the idea that some dispositions possess temporal momentum, so that brief finking or masking does not cancel the underlying power, but sustained interference over a threshold duration transforms the property into something else—perhaps a different disposition, or the absence of the original one altogether. This raises a boundary problem about when masking ends and property-change begins, with potential relevance to how we model capacities in biological systems and engineered artifacts.

Given these frames, the field currently has several live lines of work:

A. Formalizing the logic of fink-resistant dispositions—developing modal or conditional accounts that specify exactly which interfering conditions fall within the scope of a dispositional ascription.

B. Determining whether masks are always extrinsic to the disposition bearer, or whether internal state-changes (e.g., a structural alteration that prevents dissolution) should count as intrinsic finks rather than external masks.

C. Assessing the implications of unresolved mask and fink problems for scientific lawhood—specifically, whether ceteris paribus laws in biology and psychology can survive if dispositions are systematically masked in observable conditions.

D. Applying the distinction between brief masking and sustained interference (dispositional inertia) to mental capacities and artifact functions—asking, for instance, whether a mental disorder can be modeled as a sustained mask on a disposition toward well-being, or whether an AI system with an active safety filter is best understood as a masked agent or as an agent with different dispositions altogether.

E. Developing a taxonomy of nested or recursive masking (the double-mask problem) and testing whether it requires new metaphysical primitives or collapses into iterated applications of first-order theories.

Your task is to advise this group on where to direct its limited resources. Which two or three of these lines (A–E, or any others you think belong in the top tier) deserve the bulk of their effort, and which should be treated as secondary or overrated? Defend your ranking with explicit criteria—tractability given their resources, neglect in current literature, dependency (how many other questions hang on resolving this one), and potential payoff for philosophy of science or philosophy of mind. Also specify what evidence or result would cause you to reorder the ranking: for example, if a formal account in A turned out to be incompatible with the internal/external distinction in B, or if empirical work in neuroscience showed that sustained interference never transforms mental dispositions but only suppresses them. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; focus on delivering a defended agenda with clear trade-offs.
