I'm attempting to reach reflective equilibrium on how virtues of character ought to be attributed, testing whether my judgements cohere around a single principle or whether some must be sacrificed. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

First, a senior manager is unfailingly courteous and supportive in face-to-face team meetings, yet under an anonymous internal feedback system she submits repeatedly cruel and dismissive assessments of junior staff. I judge that she does not genuinely possess the virtue of professional kindness; the anonymity removes performative constraints and reveals that the trait was never present.

Second, an emergency surgeon displays calm decisiveness during well-structured, high-stakes operations governed by clear protocols, but during a chaotic multi-casualty incident with ambiguous command structures she freezes and fails to assist. I judge that she remains genuinely courageous; the freeze is a situational failure that does not undermine the underlying virtue.

Third, a public official applies regulations with perfect impartiality in every audited, transparent procedure, but routinely exploits discretionary, unrecorded channels to favour personal allies. I judge that he lacks the virtue of justice; the formal fairness is institutional performance that masks a defective character.

Fourth, a philanthropist donates a substantial portion of her income to effective causes while she is affluent, but during a period of genuine financial precarity that threatens her basic security she reduces giving to negligible levels. I judge that she still possesses the virtue of generosity; virtue does not require self-destruction.

Fifth, a schoolteacher is celebrated for patience in well-resourced classrooms with clear schedules, but in an underfunded, severely overcrowded setting where he works voluntary overtime he repeatedly loses his temper. I judge that he lacks patience; the adverse conditions explain but do not excuse the failure, showing the trait was never robust.

These verdicts pull in different directions. In the first, third and fifth cases I treat situational change as revealing the absence of virtue, whereas in the second and fourth I treat situational difficulty or high cost as excusing failure without removing the trait.

Is there a coherent principle that captures all five? I am particularly drawn to a view that ties virtue attribution to conditions of epistemic transparency and unambiguous role-definition: a trait counts as genuine only if it persists when the agent clearly recognises the moral demand and faces no structural ambiguity about her responsibility. If that principle works, state it precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, say what it predicts for a case I have not listed — for instance, a volunteer who demonstrates exceptional compassion in high-cost, clearly defined rescue work but becomes indifferent in low-cost, highly routinised administrative support — and tell me honestly which of my five verdicts it cannot accommodate.

If no single principle saves every verdict, present the two most plausible competing accounts, state the clause each is forced to drop, and say which revision you think is least damaging to our ordinary practice of praising and blaming character.
