Having spent thirty-five years in experimental physics before turning to philosophy in retirement, I have been struck by the persistence of the conciliationism versus steadfastness debate in epistemology. For nearly two decades, the exchange has revolved around canonical cases—disputes over restaurant bills, horse-race predictions, and complex scientific judgments—yet neither camp appears to have conceded ground, and the arguments now feel well-rehearsed on both sides. I am inclined to think that part of the stagnation comes from treating "epistemic peer" as a uniform relation, when in practice scientific collaboration distinguishes between peers in competence, peers in information access, and peers in methodological commitment—distinctions that the philosophical literature tends to collapse. Could it be that conciliationists and steadfastists are not disagreeing about a single rational norm, but about which of these peer-relations should govern belief revision? Or is the disagreement deeper—perhaps about whether rationality requires intersubjective calibration at all, analogous to the difference between adjusting an instrument to match another versus trusting one's own calibration curve? I ask you to diagnose the structure of this impasse: determine whether it is substantive, verbal, or a case of two research programs answering different questions under one label; examine whether hidden formal or empirical assumptions—say, about the dynamics of belief pooling or the measurement of evidential weight—are driving the stalemate; and specify what would count as genuine progress given your diagnosis, whether that be a formal model, a conceptual distinction among peer-types, or an argument that no further resolution should be expected. Do not merely adjudicate the dispute; tell me what kind of disagreement it really is, supported by what each side actually says about private evidence, the normative force of disagreement, and the conditions under which peerhood obtains.
