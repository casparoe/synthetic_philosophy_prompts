You are advising me, a senior science editor at a general-interest magazine who must evaluate whether to publish, downgrade, or reject a news story based on a single behavioral-genetics study, typically within thirty minutes, without access to a specialist, and with the story already assigned a headline. I need a reusable editorial procedure—a sequential checklist with explicit pass/fail conditions and decision nodes—that translates the statistical meaning of narrow-sense heritability (the proportion of phenotypic variance in a specific population explained by additive genetic variance at a given time) into concrete editorial judgments, without allowing the common collapse into genetic determinism or immutability. The procedure must explicitly incorporate three conceptual constraints that survive simplification: heritability is a property of a population-environment pair, not of a trait or an individual; it can shift when the environment shifts; and indirect genetic effects—genetic nurture, in which parental genotypes shape the child’s environment—can inflate twin-based estimates by attributing environmentally mediated influences to the child’s genotype. It must also state the points where numerical thresholds are unavoidable judgment calls disguised as rules, and it must be honest about what it cannot adjudicate—such as whether a trait is ethically appropriate to study, or whether a finding should influence policy.

The stakes are high: an erroneous pass can propagate genetic fatalism to the public; an erroneous fail can suppress a legitimate result. The procedure should be usable by an editor without statistical training, should require no literature search, and should not depend on bibliographies or specialist consultation.

Deliver the procedure in ordered steps. Then apply it to three hypothetical incoming studies: (1) a Scandinavian twin registry reporting h² ≈ 0.75 for educational attainment, with no discussion of indirect effects; (2) a cross-population study finding h² near zero for the same trait in a high-inequality environment; and (3) a widely circulated claim that “genes explain 60% of obesity,” paired with an explicit policy argument that anti-obesity programs are therefore inefficient. For case (3), I want the procedure to yield a verdict I will not like: specifically, that the study should not be rejected on purely statistical grounds, but that the correct framing undermines the policy conclusion drawn from it. Finally, take a committed position and defend it: should a heritability coefficient ever appear in a headline without an explicit population-context caveat? Answer with reference to what the procedure reveals about the gap between variance partitioning and practical intervention, and mark exactly where your own judgment—not the procedure—enters.
