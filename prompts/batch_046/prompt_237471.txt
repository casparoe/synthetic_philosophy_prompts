In my dissertation work at the intersection of philosophy of history and collective moral responsibility, I have been tracking the Fischer controversy and its philosophical afterlife. Among the historians and philosophers of war with whom I work, there is broad agreement on the archival facts: the Schlieffen Plan existed, the German war council of December 1912 documented an appetite for preventive conflict, and the July 1914 crisis involved calculated risk-taking by Berlin that other powers did not initiate. Yet the debate over whether Germany bears primary responsibility for the outbreak of the First World War has remained structurally unchanged for decades—one camp cites intentionality and the failure to restrain Austria; the other insists that alliance structures, mobilization timetables, and reciprocal escalation distribute culpability so diffusely that attributing primary blame to a single nation-state is either empirically unsupported or conceptually incoherent. I am trying to understand why this disagreement has stalled despite such shared factual ground. Is it a hidden empirical dispute about counterfactual robustness—whether a restrained German posture in July 1914 would have contained the conflict—or about the normative conditions under which a collective agent can be said to bear blame? Might the two sides be answering different questions—one about causal contribution, one about moral accountability—under the single heading of "holding a nation responsible"? Or is there a bedrock divergence in how each side weighs intentional agency against structural constraint, such that no further archival discovery could resolve it? I ask for a diagnosis grounded in what each camp actually argues, together with an account of what would count as progress: a formal framework for collective agency, a counterfactual test with agreed-upon parameters, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the dispute, or an argument that the question is ill-posed for modern state systems. Please distinguish clearly between what is historically settled about the July crisis and what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any contested interpretive framework—whether Fischer's thesis, the structuralist revision, or a particular theory of collective responsibility—as if it were the consensus view.
