I am designing the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in epistemology. The students have worked through Nozick’s tracking conditions—sensitivity and adherence—together with the canonical discussions of epistemic closure and skeptical alternatives. I want a single essay question that cannot be satisfied by reciting the familiar line that tracking denies closure because we do not track “I am not a brain in a vat.” Instead, the question should embed a constructed, non-standard case that forces students to apply the counterfactual conditions precisely to negative knowledge and logical inference.

Please construct the following: (1) an exam question suitable for a ninety-minute essay expecting roughly 800–1,000 words; (2) a model answer of about 700 words; and (3) a marking scheme that distinguishes an A answer from a B answer by naming specific moves and specific mistakes. To keep the problem in the weeds without relying on obscure literature, inject this niche scenario: an agent S knows, via a highly reliable but not infallible laboratory monitor, that a specific toxic gas is absent from her workspace. The monitor tracks absence correctly in nearby worlds, but in very distant worlds where the gas is present the monitor malfunctions and still reports absence. S then reasons: “If the gas is absent, then either the ventilation is functioning or I am not conducting the experiment; I know the gas is absent; therefore I know that either the ventilation is functioning or I am not conducting the experiment.” The exam question should ask students to determine, using Nozick’s conditions exactly, whether S knows the disjunctive conclusion, whether she knows either disjunct separately, and whether closure holds or fails in this case—particularly addressing whether successful tracking of the absence can transmit through inference to a conclusion that embeds a counterfactual claim about the monitor’s own reliability in distant worlds. The question should also require students to explain how, if at all, this case differs structurally from the standard brain-in-a-vat closure failure. The model answer must correctly apply the counterfactual formulation of sensitivity to the disjunctive belief, identify that S may adhere to the conclusion without separately adhering to either disjunct, and explain why the monitor’s distant-world malfunction blocks or permits transmission of knowledge. The marking scheme should list the analytical moves required for an A—such as distinguishing adherence to p from adherence to q via p, recognizing that tracking failure for negative existentials behaves differently under closure, and avoiding the conflation of reliability with tracking—while also listing errors that should cost marks, including treating closure as an all-or-nothing logical principle rather than a condition-sensitive transmission relation, or misapplying the counterfactual to the disjunction as a whole rather than to its components. Do not include requests for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the answer should depend only on the conceptual apparatus of tracking theory and the constructed scenario.
