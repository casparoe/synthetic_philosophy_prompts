I'm investigating a recurring move in evolutionary-biology debates that I haven't seen named precisely. A researcher argues that trait T is a direct adaptation shaped for function F. Critics respond that T is structurally inevitable given feature S—like the triangular spaces beneath an arch that exist because of the architecture, not because anyone designed them to serve a purpose. Instead of conceding that T is a non-adaptive byproduct, the defender often makes a second move: they accept that T necessarily arises from S, but then claim that S itself was selected because it generated T, and that T's presence in turn stabilized S through feedback. The result is that what began as an admission of a spandrel-like origin is folded back into an adaptive story, now routed through indirect selection for the byproduct itself. I have noticed this pattern in discussions of traits such as the human chin and certain design features of language capacity, though my interest is in the logical form rather than those specific cases.

Is there an established term for this particular two-step defense—admitting the structural-byproduct origin but then "re-adapting" the byproduct into the selective history? How does it differ, exactly, from standard exaptation accounts, in which a trait arises for one reason and is later co-opted for another? And is it genuinely distinct from the Panglossian paradigm that Gould and Lewontin criticized, or is it merely that same paradigm rendered more resilient by allowing an intermediate concession? If no specific concept captures it, could you propose a term together with boundary conditions that separate it from both ordinary exaptation and from flat denial of the spandrel? Please state how confident you are in mapping this pattern onto the existing literature, and identify the specific point about which you are least certain—especially whether the pivot I describe actually occurs as a recognizable strategy in published arguments or whether I am over-reading a few scattered defenses.
