I am a journalist who writes on neuroscience and philosophy of mind, and in a fortnight I shall present at an interdisciplinary symposium on consciousness and clinical neurology. The thesis I intend to defend is that pain asymbolia reveals not a simple dissociation between sensation and affect, but a separable third layer in the structure of pain's badness — a normative-binding component — through which a nociceptive signal acquires its aversive force. I argue that this coupling can be disrupted while both sensory discrimination and emotional appraisal remain intact, which undermines both the IASP affective-definition of pain and representationalist accounts that reduce badness to misrepresentational content alone. Instead, I propose a triadic model comprising sensory representation, affective response, and this normative-binding relation.

The audience is precisely composed of: clinical neurologists who have worked with asymbolic patients and who defend the IASP framework; phenomenologists of embodied cognition for whom affect is constitutive; predictive-processing neuroscientists inclined to recast my claim as a precision-weighting failure; philosophers of mind suspicious of an apparently mysterious third factor; and a medical-ethics chair concerned with analgesic policy implications. Each group will bring its own vocabulary and its own vulnerability.

Please construct my Q&A preparation sheet. Order the objections by how damaging they would be in this room — not generic philosophical puzzles, but the specific formulations these interlocutors will use. For each objection, indicate whether I ought to concede a point, where I must hold firm, a concise reply suitable for a two-minute exchange, and an honest note on where that reply is softest. Include the one question I am most hoping is not raised — the objection that strikes at the weakest joint in the triadic model — together with a reply that avoids overclaiming. Finally, identify the common misconception about asymbolia that I should pre-empt in my opening remarks so that it does not corrupt the subsequent discussion.

Please reply in British English.
