My opening move is as follows. Imagine a reflexive legislative framework—the Triad Constitution—structured in three tiers. Tier One provides that any amendment to Tier One requires unanimous consent of a constitutional council. Tier Two authorizes suspension of any Tier One provision by simple majority, but only when such suspension is necessary to preserve the rule of law. Tier Three contains an entrenchment clause that reads: "This clause is suspendable if and only if its suspension is necessary to preserve the rule of law," and it further stipulates that no other tier may modify Tier Three. I argue that Tier Three instantiates a direct normative analog of the liar paradox: should the clause be suspended, its own conditions authorize that suspension; should it stand, the assessment of whether suspension is "necessary" must be conducted within the very clause under evaluation, producing an undecidable normative status. Your model places reflexive authority in stratified tiers, yet Tier Three appears to collapse back upon itself without ascending to a superior tier. How does your account of hierarchical meta-rules resolve the logical structure of this specific clause, and what authority relation between Tier Two and Tier Three prevents the paradox from re-emerging at the apex?
