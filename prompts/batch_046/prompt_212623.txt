I'm building a decentralized governance protocol in which participants vote on both intermediate factual claims and a final policy conclusion that logically depends on them. I have no formal philosophy training, but I've encountered what is called the discursive dilemma in judgment aggregation: a group can agree consistently on individual premises yet disagree on the derived conclusion, or agree on the conclusion while holding contradictory premises. To adapt this to software, my system lets voters submit binary judgments plus continuous confidence weights between zero and one for each premise, and it applies a tunable threshold—premises are treated as collectively binding only if their aggregated confidence exceeds that cutoff; otherwise the protocol falls back to direct aggregation of the conclusion.

Given that architecture, please rank the following three resolution strategies into tiers according to their suitability for implementation: (A) pure premise-based aggregation, where the conclusion is always derived mechanically from the threshold-accepted premises and direct conclusion votes are ignored; (B) pure conclusion-based aggregation, where premise votes are discarded and only the direct conclusion is aggregated; and (C) a hybrid threshold-premise rule, which uses premise-based derivation when the confidence-weighted premises clear the cutoff and otherwise defaults to conclusion-based aggregation. For each tier, justify its placement in one or two sentences, focusing specifically on vulnerability to strategic manipulation—such as a voter inflating confidence on a false premise to force a desired conclusion—and on whether the strategy risks producing collectively irrational outputs like accepting contradictory premises or deriving a conclusion that conflicts with the direct majority. I am not looking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want a conceptual evaluation grounded in the logic of the dilemma itself and in how each strategy behaves under partial confidence weighting and abstention.
