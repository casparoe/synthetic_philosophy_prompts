I have been working through the foundations of welfare comparison with a small reading group, and we keep returning to a problem that standard textbooks treat too quickly: whether it is still meaningful to rank people as better or worse off when their internal affective scales have been systematically distorted—whether through lifelong adaptive preference formation under deprivation, profound neurological differences in affective range, or hypothetical "hedonic calibration" procedures that deliberately rescale subjective satisfaction. For a seminar handout, I want a decision tree—not a list of named theorists—that classifies positions on interpersonal utility comparison by the answers students give to concrete diagnostic questions. The tree should begin with one commitment: "There is a fact of the matter about whether person A is genuinely better off than person B, even when their self-reported satisfaction metrics are structurally incommensurable." From there, the branches should split on four issues: whether comparison requires a shared biological or evolutionary substrate for affect; whether objective-list goods (such as health, autonomy, or knowledge) can bridge incommensurable subjective states; whether adaptive preferences formed under constraint should be corrected, respected, or discounted as unreliable; and whether aggregation across such pairs must be linear, lexicographic, or non-aggregative altogether.

Each leaf should name a coherent position—or mark it "unnamed/unoccupied"—and state its single most awkward commitment in one sentence, for example that it must deny cross-cultural welfare judgments, or that it permits comparing human well-being with that of a hypothetical synthetic mind that shares no evolutionary affective architecture. Please flag any pair of leaves that your tree's logic cannot actually separate, and identify one branch-path that seems logically coherent but is apparently empty in the literature, with a brief diagnosis of whether its emptiness is due to incoherence, obvious unattractiveness, or mere neglect. The audience is graduate students in philosophy who can reason through concrete cases but should not be expected to recall specific authorities; accordingly, do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and phrase every node as a question answerable without prior knowledge of the label it leads to.
