I'm preparing to moderate a colloquium Q&A with the author of a recent monograph that extends Davidson's framework of radical interpretation to large language models. She maintains that the principle of charity applies to LLM outputs in exactly the same way it applies to human utterances: if a system's generated text can be rendered mostly true and largely rational by an appropriate assignment of beliefs and meanings, then that assignment is correct, regardless of whether the system has ever causally interacted with the world it describes. She has a habit of deflecting worries about missing causal-world anchoring by saying that "triangulation describes the causal conditions under which interpretation begins, not a constraint on what can count as interpreted content; charity governs the assignment, causation governs only the occasion." Write me a progressive sequence of audience questions that makes this separation of charity and triangulation increasingly difficult to maintain. The sequence should start by pressing Davidson's own claim that belief and meaning are interdependent and that fixing content requires shared causal interaction with objects and events, then move to the problem of distinguishing an LLM's "massive error" from its "radical irrationality" when there is no shared environment to anchor disagreement, and anticipate her likely reply that the interpreter can always preserve rationality by revising auxiliary hypotheses about the LLM's background beliefs. Conclude with a question that asks what specific, reproducible pattern in an LLM's outputs—something that cannot be absorbed by revising background beliefs or translation manuals—would actually force an interpreter to abandon charitable attribution altogether rather than simply redescribing the system as holding bizarre but consistent beliefs.
