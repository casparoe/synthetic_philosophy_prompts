John Maynard Keynes died in April 1946. In his 1930 lecture-essay Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren—delivered in Madrid and later published in Essays in Persuasion—he argued that once economic needs of an absolute kind are satisfied, humanity's central challenge becomes the wise use of leisure: cultivating friendship, aesthetic experience, and contemplative mental states rather than competitive accumulation. His ethical outlook was shaped by G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica, which treated certain mental states and personal relations as possessing intrinsic value independent of instrumental benefit.

Consider a development that occurred only after Keynes's death: the rise of algorithmic recommendation systems that monetize non-working hours, converting rest into a source of behavioral data and engagement revenue. Some contemporary theorists distinguish between liberated leisure—time genuinely free from both labor and commercialized cognitive extraction—and captive leisure, in which algorithmic platforms compete for attention during ostensibly private hours. At the same time, advances in automation have revived serious policy discussion of an unconditional Universal Basic Income (UBI).

If Keynes had lived to observe both phenomena and had been convinced that captive leisure had become the dominant mode of non-work time for the majority, how would he likely have evaluated the interaction between UBI and leisure quality? In particular:

1. Would he have supported unconditional cash transfers, or would he have argued for conditioning economic security on structural protections for liberated leisure—such as public funding for non-commercial spaces, or regulatory limits on attention-extraction during designated rest periods?

2. Given his Moore-influenced conviction that the good life consists in specific mental states and relationships rather than in consumption, do you think he would have regarded captive leisure as a failure of the economic system to solve the problem he identified—namely, using abundance to secure meaningful freedom—or as an entirely new pathology requiring different remedies?

3. Drawing on his Bloomsbury-era aesthetic commitments and his skepticism of purely quantitative welfare measures, how might he have assessed proposals to distribute part of a basic income not in currency but in time-credits redeemable only for activities certified as non-commercial and contemplative?

Answer by reconstructing Keynes's likely reasoning from his known ethical and economic commitments, without citing specific texts. Be precise about which elements of his framework—Moorean intrinsic value, the distinction between absolute and relative needs, the leisure problem—do the work in each part of your answer.
