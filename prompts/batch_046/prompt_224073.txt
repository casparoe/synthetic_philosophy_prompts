Construct a thought experiment designed to isolate how the Solomonoff universal prior can drive a consequentialist agent to act more cautiously—indeed, to withhold a clearly beneficial action—than an empirically-grounded Bayesian agent in an identical evidential situation, specifically because the prior is malign in weighting simple deceptive worlds heavily. Constraints and specifications: (1) The scenario must be realistic and institutional (e.g., a government health agency, a research lab, or a hospital administration), not science-fictional, and must not invoke demons, perfect predictors, explicit simulation stipulations, or other exotic devices. (2) Present the scenario in one paragraph: a concrete decision about whether to deploy, approve, or release something that has shown strong predictive or therapeutic value in observed trials. (3) The Solomonoff-prior consequentialist must reject the action, and this rejection must fall directly out of the prior's simplicity-weighting—specifically, that low-Kolmogorov-complexity hypotheses describing a hidden adversarial structure or deceptive environment receive prior mass proportional to 2 raised to the negative complexity, which competes with and dominates the empirically observed success—rather than falling out of standard risk aversion, ambiguity aversion, or a separately adopted precautionary rule. (4) Contrast this with an empirically-grounded Bayesian decision-maker who, using priors calibrated to observed institutional reliability and physical regularities, approves the action on expected-utility grounds. (5) After the scenario, walk through the reasoning of each agent step-by-step, identify the precise feature of the case that does the work (the divergence between algorithmic simplicity and empirical frequency), explain what happens to the Solomonoff agent's verdict if that feature is removed (for instance, if the prior is restricted to hypotheses whose complexity is bounded relative to observed data, or if the adversarial hypotheses are made descriptively complex), and include an honest note on how a defender of using the universal prior for action selection would attack the case—whether by appealing to rapid Bayesian updating, conditioning on the agent's own algorithm being part of the hypothesis space, or arguing that the prior is epistemic rather than action-guiding. (6) Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or appeals to ultra-niche formalisms that could not be expected from general training; the case should genuinely generate the divergence from the theories rather than stipulating it. (7) Conclude by stating what the case is for—whether testing the action-guiding viability of algorithmic priors, illustrating a form of epistemic modesty failure, or isolating the role of simplicity in decision-theoretic reasoning—so that it is clear which moves would count as cheating.
