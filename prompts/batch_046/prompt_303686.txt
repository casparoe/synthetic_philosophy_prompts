I'm working through my position for a graduate seminar on moral psychology and want a consistency check before I present. I keep returning to the same cluster of commitments about moral worth and acting for the right reasons, and I cannot tell whether they form a stable package or whether I am helping myself to incompatible motivations in different parts of the view. Here is where I stand:

(a) Moral worth is determined entirely by the agent's operative motivating reasons, not by the consequences that happen to result; any difference in assessment that tracks outcomes rather than motives is either an error or, at best, a pragmatic adjustment that must not be confused with a judgment of worth. (b) To act for the right reason is to act from a direct recognition that the action is morally required, where that recognition functions as the sufficient motivating cause—not merely as a background belief or as one desire among others. (c) There are no degrees of "acting for the right reason": either the recognition of duty is the operative motive, or it is not. Mixed motives, partial alignment, or acting from sympathy that happens to converge with duty all fall on the "not the right reason" side of the line. (d) Moral luck is therefore impossible in the domain of worth: two agents who share the same operative motive and exercise the same care deserve identical moral evaluation, regardless of whether external factors cause one's action to succeed and the other's to fail. (e) Nevertheless, I think it correct that we should praise a successful rescue more enthusiastically than a failed one, and that the successful rescuer deserves greater social honor, provided we understand this strictly as an assessment of contribution rather than of moral worth. (f) I also hold that an agent who saves a life solely for financial reward possesses more moral worth than an agent who attempts to save a life out of duty but fails completely due to unforeseeable circumstances—because the reward-driven agent at least produced value, and producing value is a necessary condition for any positive moral assessment, even if it is not sufficient.

My intuition is that (f) is the weak point, but I am unsure whether it conflicts more severely with (c) or with (a), or whether (e) is actually smuggling outcome-dependence back into my view of worth despite my attempt to quarantine it. I also wonder whether (b)'s demand for "direct recognition" as sufficient cause is compatible with the idea that non-conceptual agents—say, a young child acting from an inchoate sense of justice—can ever genuinely act for the right reason.

Could you audit this package? For each tension, indicate whether it is genuine or only apparent, rank them by severity, and specify what the least costly revision would be and what exactly it costs me—whether it forces me to abandon the binary in (c), the outcome-independence in (a), the social-honor distinction in (e), or something else. Please be precise about which commitments are actually in conflict and which pairs can be kept together through careful reformulation rather than abandonment.
