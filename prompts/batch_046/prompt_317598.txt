I'm working through debates about whether culture or values can be genuine causes in social explanation, particularly arguments that take Weber's thesis on the Protestant ethic seriously rather than treating it as a historical curiosity. I've noticed a recurring argumentative configuration that I can't place precisely, and I want to know whether philosophy of social science or methodology already has a name for it.

Consider the pattern in two moves. First, a researcher claims that an ascetic value-orientation toward worldly labor was not just a post-hoc rationalization of emerging capitalist practices but actually shaped economic behavior. When reductionist critics reply that material conditions and market incentives did all the causal work, the researcher insists that values are not competing with material causes on the same level; instead, they supply the interpretive frame or the meaning-structure that makes certain material opportunities actionable for agents. At this stage, values sound like a mediating lens or a condition of intelligibility, not an independent causal force.

Then a different critic observes that many societies faced structurally similar material conditions without developing the same economic system, and asks why values should be credited with any explanatory weight at all. The researcher now shifts register: values are presented as an independent causal selector. Given a roughly comparable material base, societies that possessed the ascetic orientation went in one direction, and societies that lacked it went in another. The values are no longer merely framing conditions; they are doing differential causal work.

The result is a persistent oscillation. Under pressure from materialist reduction, values retreat to being non-competitive framing devices; under pressure from correlational skeptics, values advance to being autonomous causal variables. The researcher never specifies what ontological status would allow values to occupy both roles consistently—whether they are causes in the same sense as material conditions, causes in a distinct meaning-causal category, or something intermediate like an elective affinity that operates across levels.

Is there an established concept for this exact oscillation? I am familiar with Weber's own distinction between causal explanation and meaningful interpretation, with debates over methodological individualism, and with discussions of supervenience in social theory, but none of these seem to name the strategic back-and-forth itself: the deployment of values first as causally inert interpretive schemes and then as causally active selectors, without resolving the tension between those two roles. If there is no specific term, how should the pattern be classified—is it a form of equivocation, a defensible feature of multi-level explanation, or a methodological ambiguity with identifiable boundary conditions? And does it occur only in arguments about cultural causation, or is it a broader feature of explanations that invoke what we might call soft or framing causes?
