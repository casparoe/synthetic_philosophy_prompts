The philosophy-of-emotion literature has been deadlocked for decades between judgmentalists, perceptualists, and feeling theorists, with each camp recycling the same counterexamples—fear that outlasts the corresponding danger-belief, anger that feels like direct perception of wrongness, bodily arousal lacking any clear intentional object—and none conceding ground. My working theory is that the disagreement is not about ontology but about explanatory priority: whether the primary task is accounting for intentionality, for phenomenological transparency, or for motivational efficacy. I also suspect a hidden empirical question about how the brain integrates bodily signals with evaluative representations may be doing unrecognized work. I want a diagnosis, not a survey: are the sides answering different questions—say, about brief emotional episodes versus enduring emotional dispositions—under one name? Is the stalemate verbal, substantive, or a sign that the category itself is ill-posed? Describe the positions concretely, say whether any conceptual distinction—such as separating affective framing from evaluative commitment—or any empirical result could actually resolve the dispute, or whether permanent disagreement should be expected. Finally, separate what is genuinely settled (that emotions involve both bodily change and some form of evaluation) from what remains contested among specialists, and do not present any contested view as the consensus.
