I sit on the advisory council for our county public library system's reference services committee, and by our meeting next month we must decide whether to approve a pilot program that integrates an AI assistant into all reference consultations, where the system not only delivers answers but also lays out intermediate reasoning steps in a conversational format that users can accept without ever holding the chain of reasoning in their own minds. I do not have an academic background in philosophy or technology studies; I have been thinking about this on my own, and what concerns me is not the risk of wrong answers, but a deeper erosion of understanding: when people come to depend on a tool not just for facts but for the shape of their own thinking, they may eventually be unable to verify or reconstruct that thinking independently, not out of laziness, but because the interaction is designed so that the steps are visible yet not internalizable. I ask you to draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the meeting, recommending a concrete course of action—approval with specific restrictions, conditional approval for a limited trial, or rejection—with a clear time frame attached. The memo must treat seriously the strongest opposing view, which is that this dependence is no different from using a card catalog, a calculator, or a trusted librarian, and that insisting on unassisted mental labor is a form of elitism that ignores how knowledge has always been socially distributed. Please write entirely in continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, as if it were a letter to fellow council members. In developing the reasoning, please distinguish between instrumental dependence, where a tool extends one's capacities, and structural dependence, where the format of the tool replaces the user's own reasoning pathways in a way that makes independent reconstruction practically impossible, but do not cite any specific scholars, books, or studies; develop the point in language accessible to non-specialists.
