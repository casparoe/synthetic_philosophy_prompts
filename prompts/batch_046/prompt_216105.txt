I'm applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship and need a two-page proposal on the conditions for valid consent. My half-formed idea is that the standard triad—deception, coercion, and competence—misses a distinct failure mode I want to call structural misalignment. In many institutional settings, a person can understand all the relevant physical and procedural facts, face no explicit threat, and possess ordinary rational capacities, yet the administrative framing of the interaction silently transforms the normative kind of act being authorized. For example, a patient might consent to a procedure fully aware of its risks and benefits, but if the institution has reclassified the procedure's purpose—from direct therapeutic care to observational data-generation—without altering the patient's experience of the interaction, she may be authorizing an act of one normative type while the system records it as an act of another. The existing philosophical literature leaves this open because it generally assumes that once factual disclosure is accurate and voluntariness is uncoerced, the only remaining question is whether the agent meets a threshold of cognitive competence. I want to ask a precise question: does structural misalignment invalidate consent independently of deception, coercion, or incompetence, and if it is not reducible to any of these three, should it be treated as a fourth necessary condition?

The approach would combine conceptual analysis of institutional case studies with a normative framework that distinguishes authorizing an act from authorizing a role within a system. Month one: refine structural misalignment and defend its independence from mere deception about facts. Months two through four: examine whether dominant accounts of consent—those that center on adequate information and uncoerced choice—have the resources to absorb this failure mode or whether they systematically overlook it. Months five through seven: investigate competence, specifically whether competence should include not only understanding of facts and risks but practical authority over the normative framing of one's consent—what I will call framing-competence. Months eight through ten: apply the framework to concrete domains, such as clinical research enrollment and administrative contracting, where framing shifts are common but invisible to standard consent checklists. Months eleven and twelve: write up results and articulate failure conditions. The project fails if structural misalignment collapses into deception (because the institution withheld a material fact) or into incompetence (because the agent lacked the cognitive capacity to understand the framing), but even then it may yield a sharper taxonomy of consent failures. A successful result would be a principled distinction that allows institutional review bodies to recognize invalid consent in cases that current disclosure and voluntariness protocols miss. The main risk is that the distinction proves too subtle to do practical normative work; I will guard against this by tying every conceptual claim to observable differences in institutional practice and decision-making.
