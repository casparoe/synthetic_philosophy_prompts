I am preparing an interdisciplinary seminar on persistence and narrative form, and I want to work through the 1993 film Groundhog Day as a case study in the endurantism/perdurantism debate, but I am trying to avoid the standard same-person-or-different framing that dominates popular discussions. Instead, I want to examine whether the loop's ontology is better captured by an endurantist model—where Phil Connors is one three-dimensional object that successively bears contradictory properties across iterations—or by a perdurantist stage-theoretic model in which each day's Phil is a distinct temporal part or counterpart stage, with psychological continuity serving as the relation that binds them into a single career rather than strict identity holding them together. The film complicates this because the physical environment resets every morning, which suggests that only Phil's mental states persist, yet his moral development—his shift from cynicism to virtue—seems to require that he accumulate experience and character across loops as one enduring subject. I am especially interested in the scene with the homeless man: Phil repeatedly attempts to save him and eventually accepts the inevitability of his death. On an endurantist reading, this is the same enduring self learning through repeated failure; on a stage-theoretic reading, each rescuing Phil is a different temporal part, and the moral wisdom belongs to the series or to the counterpart relation rather than to any single enduring agent. I want to know where the film genuinely illuminates this metaphysical distinction—perhaps by dramatizing the tension between spatiotemporal continuity and psychological continuity—where it risks conflating epistemic repetition with ontological persistence, and where its narrative logic resists reduction to either ontology, particularly given that the loop's closure seems to reward Phil only after he has achieved a kind of atemporal virtue. I also wonder whether the loop structure implicitly supports a branching four-dimensionalist picture, in which each iteration initiates a new world-line that diverges from the previous one except for Phil's psychological trajectory, and whether breaking the loop therefore represents not the endurance of a single object through time but the termination of one world-line and the inception of another. This is not for a bibliography or citation exercise; I need a conceptual analysis that respects both the metaphysics and the film's moral architecture, one that does not reduce the narrative to a mere illustration of either view but asks what the work adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone cannot provide.
