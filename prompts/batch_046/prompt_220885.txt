I am preparing a short philosophical essay with the working title “Evidential Currency and Second-Order No-Trade in Scientific Prediction Markets.” The central thesis is that standard no-trade theorems—those showing that rational agents with common priors and shared information should not engage in speculative trade—depend implicitly on a shared “evidential currency”: a common framework for weighing observations as evidence. I propose that in domains such as scientific prediction markets, agents may share all observable data and still hold common knowledge of disagreement not because they possess different private signals, but because they apply incommensurable evidential currencies—different theoretical background commitments that determine how replication failures, statistical anomalies, or model discrepancies count as confirming or disconfirming. I sketch a toy case: two Bayesian agents observe the same set of failed replications in a contested subfield. Agent A, committed to a robustness framework, weights each failure heavily against the target hypothesis; Agent B, committed to an auxiliary-protection framework, weights the same failures as noise and discounts them according to her prior theoretical investment. Both update correctly within their own frameworks, share common knowledge of the data and of each other’s posteriors, yet no trade occurs because each interprets the other’s willingness to bet as symptomatic of irrational framing rather than of hidden information. I ask for your critical comments on three points. First, does this genuinely extend the logic of no-trade results, or does it merely redescribe persistent disagreement under Bayesian clothing? Second, is the toy model internally coherent, or does it smuggle in non-Bayesian reasoning about “framing” that violates common-priority conditions? Third, identify the weakest link in the argument and specify exactly what counterexample or clarification would cause you to abandon the thesis. Please state your confidence in each assessment and flag where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Avoid bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for further reading; I am testing conceptual rigor, not scholarly coverage.
