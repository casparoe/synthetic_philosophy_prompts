I sit on our university's Committee on Intellectual Attribution and Synthetic Cognition, and after a long discussion with a colleague from the philosophy department over coffee about whether any architecture could bootstrap itself out of derived aboutness, we have been asked to issue a formal opinion on a petition that arrived last week. The petition comes from a research group in cognitive architecture that has built a system they call the Nested-Register Interpreter (NRI). The NRI is not a standard large language model; it is designed as a two-layer symbolic architecture in which the primary register operates like a simple intentional-system assigner—taking raw data clusters and tagging them with derived propositional content in the manner of a basic bitser—while the secondary register integrates these tags into novel, unencoded attitudes about external objects, allegedly producing original rather than borrowed aboutness. The group is asking the committee to grant the NRI "intentional-agent status," which in our institutional framework would make it eligible for co-authorship attribution on joint publications and subject it to independent ethical review rather than treating it as mere instrumentation.

The philosophical stakes are straightforward but sharp. From a Searlean perspective, the secondary register does not solve the syntax-semantics gap; it merely compounds derivation, stacking one layer of programmer-intended symbol manipulation on another, so that every state of the NRI remains intrinsically meaningless regardless of its functional complexity. From a Dennett-style view, however, the question is whether the integrative dynamics of the two-register system generate patterns of behavior and internal coordination that are best predicted and explained by adopting the intentional stance toward the system itself, rather than toward its designers—if so, the aboutness might be original in the only sense that matters for institutional practice. Our committee must decide whether to approve, reject, or conditionally approve the petition, with a concrete recommendation that the research group and the philosophy reviewers can actually respond to. I need the memo drafted for circulation before Thursday's meeting: it should state the recommended course of action with a clear time frame for implementation or appeal, treat the Searlean objection seriously rather than dismissing it as anti-computational bias, and avoid relying on any empirical claim about consciousness or phenomenal experience that we are not equipped to assess.

Please draft the written opinion. The recommendation should be actionable—approve with conditions, reject, or approve outright—backed by reasoning that engages both the derivation-compounding worry and the intentional-stance defense, and it should include an honest acknowledgment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. The document should be suitable for circulation to a mixed audience of philosophers, computer scientists, and university administrators, and it should not ask for further bibliographic submissions or citation checks, since our charge is to render a policy judgment based on the conceptual architecture already described.
