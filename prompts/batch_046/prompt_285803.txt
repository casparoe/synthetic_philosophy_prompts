I've kinda bought into this reading of Hofstadter's frame/outer/inner message thing from GEB — specifically that the frame message doesnt just guide interpretation, it actually *makes* the inner message meaningful. Like, strip away the framing instructions and there's literally zero semantic residue left; the inner content is just syntactic noise until the frame activates it. This even applies to weird self-referential loops where the frame refers back to itself. I think it explains how formal systems can seem to talk about numbers without actually containing any intrinsic mathematical meaning.

But I want to know: what would count against this? Don't give me the soft "symbols feel meaningful" objection. I want the serious defeaters. Which formal results about self-reference or undecidability actually depend on inner messages carrying some intrinsic content? Are there real-world cases — in language use, or maybe cognitive science — where meaning clearly survives when the frame collapses or gets contradicted? And what about translation: if meaning is entirely frame-constituted, shouldn't communication between people with different framing systems be impossible, yet it obviously happens? Rank these from fatal (the view implodes) to merely akward (needs a patch). Also, what would someone who holds this frame-constitutive view honestly concede as a real problem, versus what they'd dismiss as missing the point? I want an honest exposure map, not just a list of critics' complaints. Does this position risk being unfalsifiable — and if so, is that actually a problem for a view about meaning?
