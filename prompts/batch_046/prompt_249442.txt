I'm currently writing an essay for a philosophy-of-mind programme that concentrates on constitutive panpsychism and its rivals. During a recent seminar on the combination problem, a fellow student kept invoking the line: "Consciousness is fundamental, so all we need is the right account of how micro-subjects combine; emergence is a red herring." They used it to shut down discussion about whether panpsychism genuinely solves the hard problem, implying that anyone who still worries about macro-consciousness is simply confused about composition rather than facing a substantive objection.

Could you separate out the distinct precise theses hiding in that one formulation? For instance, does it commit one to phenomenal bonding rather than phenomenal summation? Does it tacitly assume that cosmopsychism is off the table, or can it remain neutral between constitutive and cosmopsychist versions of the view? Which of the possible readings are near-tautologies (for example, that panpsychism treats consciousness as basic), which are substantive but defensible (for example, that combination mechanisms can be specified without invoking brute emergence), and which are actually false or question-begging (for example, that solving combination automatically dissolves the explanatory gap for macro-subjects)? I'd also like you to say which interpretation is being invoked when someone deploys the line to end an argument about whether panpsychism offers a real solution to the hard problem, and whether that interpretation is genuinely available to both the phenomenal-bonding and the subject-summing camps. Please present the options as a menu rather than settling on one, and indicate which readings would actually support the rhetorical move my classmate was making.
