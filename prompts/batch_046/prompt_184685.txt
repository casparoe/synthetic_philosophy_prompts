As a novelist constructing an interior monologue for an eighteenth-century character, I seek help understanding a passage from David Hume's essay on suicide. Paraphrasing the argument: Hume replies to the objection that suicide violates divine providence by noting that human beings constantly redirect natural processes—diverting rivers, altering courses—without impiety; ending one's own life is therefore only another rearrangement of matter within the universal mechanism, not a unique affront to cosmic order. He further contends that the duty to preserve oneself dissolves once life becomes an unmitigated burden, since rational beings owe themselves no obligation to endure purposeless suffering.

My difficulty is conceptual. The analogy treats life as a hydraulic flow that may be turned aside, yet Hume elsewhere denies any substantial, enduring soul, reducing the self to a bundle of perceptions. Does this mechanistic image of the self as a redirectable stream not sit awkwardly with the idea that one can have a duty to oneself—a notion that seems to require a persisting subject who bears obligations? Or does Hume's denial of substantial identity precisely dissolve the problem, leaving only utilitarian calculation? I should welcome your clarification, and please write in British English.
