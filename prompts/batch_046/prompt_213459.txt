Consider the argument Newton gives in the Scholium to the Definitions in his Principia, where he asks us to imagine a bucket suspended by a twisted cord and filled with water. At first, as the vessel spins rapidly, the water inside remains flat and apparently at rest relative to the bucket; gradually, however, the rotational motion is communicated to the water, which then climbs the sides and becomes concave. Newton treats this change in shape as proof that the water is undergoing true circular motion, and he insists that such motion cannot be defined merely in relation to the bucket or to other nearby bodies, since those same relative configurations obtain both when the water is flat and when it is concave.

My confusion is about whether Newton is advancing an empirical observation about fluid dynamics, a conceptual claim about the criteria for attributing rotation, or an inference to the best explanation that relies on unstated premises. I am unsure whether this is a fair reading of Newton's text or whether it imports later Machian and Einsteinian concerns into an argument intended to be local. Could you help me understand exactly what Newton is doing in this passage—whether he offers a reductio of relational motion, an observational inference, or a definitional stipulation—and clearly separate what is settled among historians and philosophers of physics about his reasoning from what remains genuinely contested?
