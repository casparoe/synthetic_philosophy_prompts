I'm preparing to defend a modified Hartian position against Devlin-style legal moralism at next month's interdisciplinary workshop on law, philosophy, and platform governance. My specific thesis is that democratic legislatures may regulate the algorithmic visibility of certain expressive content only when that content violates genuinely shared normative expectations—what I call institutionalized moral conventions—not merely when its presence aggregates private feelings of revulsion or social unease among a statistical majority; I apply this to recent legislative proposals that would mandate algorithmic down-ranking of expressions of non-normative gender identity on the grounds that their public visibility erodes social cohesion. The audience is a seminar of twelve: six analytic legal philosophers, among them one who has published defenses of the view that law may legitimately enforce shared morality to prevent social disintegration, and others with broadly Rawlsian commitments to the separation of legal and moral norms; three constitutional lawyers for whom the harm principle is settled doctrine and who will treat any moralistic rationale with suspicion; and three philosophers of technology who regard the Hart-Devlin framework as anachronistic for mediated digital environments and who will ask whether harm can be individuated when damage flows through recommendation architectures rather than direct interpersonal action.

Please prepare me for the Q&A by drafting a prep sheet, not a defense. First, list the eight objections I will actually face, ordered from most to least damaging, and phrase each in the vocabulary of the subgroup that will raise it—legal philosophers speaking in terms of institutional integrity and the separation of legal from moral norms, constitutional lawyers invoking content-neutrality and the impossibility of defining harm without circularity, and technology philosophers questioning whether collective intentionality can exist in algorithmically curated spaces. For each objection, identify its source subgroup, rate its damage potential, provide a concise two-minute reply that concedes whatever must be conceded, and flag the weakest point in that reply—specifically where I am stretching Hart's original framework beyond its explicit commitments, or where I am importing an unstated premise about collective intentionality that my argument requires but has not independently defended. Second, include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks: the challenge that my distinction between aggregated private disgust and institutionalized moral convention is itself circular, because identifying which moral responses constitute a genuinely shared convention rather than a mere statistical aggregation already presupposes the very harm principle I am using to separate permissible from impermissible regulation. For this hidden objection, sketch the reply I must have ready, including the upfront concession that Hart never explicitly endorsed this distinction, and identify the single argumentative move—drawing on the difference between a statistical aggregation of individual preferences and a genuinely shared normative expectation that shapes institutional practice—that must land if the proposal is to survive. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not frame any objection around familiarity with a specific recent paper; ground every point in the conceptual structure of the debate itself and in the institutional context I have described.
