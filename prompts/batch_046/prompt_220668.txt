Consider the following summary, drawn from an unnamed essay that applies Dewey’s philosophy to contemporary governance: “Dewey treated democracy as a collective laboratory in which policies are proposed as testable hypotheses, implemented by the state, and then evaluated against empirical predictions; unsuccessful policies are falsified and discarded. On this view, democratic legitimacy depends not on popular consent but on the experimental verification of outcomes. Dewey therefore rejected both tradition-bound authority and mere majority preference, arguing that only rigorously tested social experiments produce genuinely democratic results. Consequently, in technologically complex societies, the experimental function ought to be delegated to specialized agencies equipped with predictive modeling and large-scale data, since ordinary citizens lack the technical competence to design or interpret rigorous social experiments.” How fair is this characterization? Examine it claim by claim: identify where it captures Dewey’s analogy between scientific inquiry and democratic problem-solving, where it conflates participatory social intelligence with technocratic hypothesis-testing, and whether the conclusion about delegating democratic experimentation to expert algorithmic bodies follows from Dewey’s actual commitments. In particular, address whether Dewey’s notion of “experimental method” requires the kind of controlled falsification implied here, and whether his account of democratic inquiry allows the separation of experimental design from the direct participation of those affected by the results.
