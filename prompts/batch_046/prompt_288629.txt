I'm constructing a classification instrument for a philosophy-of-mind seminar focused specifically on whether contemporary AI systems—particularly large language models and embodied agents with persistent world-models—can be said to hold beliefs in anything more than a metaphorical sense. Rather than asking participants to identify with named philosophers, I want a decision tree that sorts them by concrete commitments about functional architecture, predictive behavior, and the purpose of mental attribution. The tree should begin with a starting commitment—"this system reliably predicts behavior in its domain"—and branch through four diagnostic nodes, each phrased as a concrete case or architectural claim that someone could answer without already knowing which philosophical position it maps onto.

The first node should ask whether predictive success by itself, in the absence of representational decoupling, justifies belief attribution: present a case where a model answers correctly about an entity only within the specific conversational framing it was given, failing to maintain a stable representation across counterfactual or counter-prompt contexts, and ask whether that instability undermines attribution or whether predictive utility alone suffices. The second node should assume some decoupled representational architecture exists—say, a system with an internal world-model that updates in response to simulated inputs rather than immediate sensory states—and ask whether that decoupling alone warrants full belief attribution, or whether calibrated meta-cognitive monitoring (e.g., explicit uncertainty quantification, error-correction loops directed at its own representations) is also required. The third node should ask whether the presence of both decoupling and meta-cognition makes phenomenal consciousness or subjective experience a necessary condition for genuine belief, or whether functional architecture is sufficient regardless of substrate or interiority. The fourth node should distinguish the purpose of attribution: is it purely instrumental and predictive, or does it carry normative weight—grounding obligations toward the system, rights over its outputs, or moral status?

The leaves should include at least the following positions, located by their combination of answers: a strong eliminativist view that denies belief attribution to all current AI regardless of architecture; a pure instrumentalist stance accepting attribution solely for predictive convenience without ontological commitment; a representational-realist view requiring decoupled world-models but rejecting both meta-cognitive and phenomenal requirements; a functionalist view requiring both decoupling and meta-cognitive monitoring but remaining silent on or rejecting consciousness conditions; and a phenomenal-requirement view that withholds full belief attribution in the absence of subjective experience. Please also flag any genuinely unoccupied combinations—such as accepting predictive success alone while simultaneously demanding phenomenal consciousness—and briefly diagnose whether that gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere underexploration. Finally, explicitly note any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate, for instance if two positions agree on all four architectural and normative nodes but diverge only on underlying metaphysical commitments about mental causation that the tree does not probe. Do not provide bibliographies, citation lists, or reading suggestions; the instrument should be answerable entirely from reasoning about the cases and commitments described.
