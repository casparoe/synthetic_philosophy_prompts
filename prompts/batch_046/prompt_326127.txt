I'm preparing a twenty-minute defense of a position I call interventional infinitism for next Tuesday's philosophy of science seminar. The thesis is that in scientific practice, the regress of justification for measurement standards is not halted by foundational calibration artifacts, as the foundationalist claims, nor dissolved into holistic coherence, as the Quinean coherentist maintains, but is practically truncated by what I term interventional closure—the point at which an experimental manipulation breaks the calibration chain not by providing an epistemically basic endpoint, but by shifting the burden of justification onto causal efficacy rather than representational accuracy. The audience is eight philosophers of science—six with coherentist leanings who think holism already handles instrument calibration, two formal epistemologists who regard infinitism as psychologically implausible and logically arbitrary, and one historian of metrology who knows that no physical standard is ever foundational in practice. I need a Q&A prep sheet: the objections they will actually raise, ordered by how damaging they are, phrased in their vocabulary (coherentist talk of webs of belief, formal talk of arbitrariness and infinite descent, historian talk of material practice and artifact biography), with for each a concise reply deliverable in about ninety seconds, a note on where the reply is weakest, and a clear marker of what I should concede versus hold. Include the one objection I'm hoping the formal epistemologists don't ask—whether interventional closure collapses into disguised foundationalism once causal efficacy is treated as epistemically basic—and a strategy for deflecting it without sounding defensive. I also want identification of the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss, plus the single upfront concession I should make about scope: that my claim applies specifically to instrumental calibration chains, not to all empirical justification.
