I'm trying to pin down my own view on indexical reasoning—specifically, when it is legitimate to update beliefs about oneself based on population size or observer frequency, and when it collapses into the presumptuous-philosopher fallacy. Here are my verdicts on concrete scenarios, and they do not sit together comfortably.

Case one: I learn I am one of a hundred people in a sealed vault, ninety-nine engineers and one poet, with no other information. I conclude I am almost certainly an engineer. That feels correct.

Case two: A philosopher notices there are far more competent practitioners than geniuses in her field, and applies the same statistical logic to herself: she infers she is probably not a genius. This strikes me as a mistake—the classic presumptuous-philosopher problem—even though it mirrors case one structurally.

Case three: Two cosmological models predict different total numbers of observers—10^12 versus 10^22. A researcher thinks her own existence should nudge her credence toward the more populous model. I want this to be legitimate, but I worry it is just case two writ large.

Case four is the doomsday framing: if humanity eventually reaches 10^11 people, finding oneself among the first 10^10 favors a smaller total population. This feels like sound statistical reasoning, not a fallacy.

Case five is where I keep flipping. Hypothesis M predicts 10^30 observers in exactly my epistemic situation; M' predicts only me. I think it would be wrong to favor M simply because I would not be surprised to find myself there, yet this seems to invoke the same principle that justified case three.

Is there a single principle—perhaps a refined self-sampling assumption, a self-indication constraint, or a reference-class restriction—that captures cases one through four without committing me to case five? If not, which verdict should I abandon? Please state the principle precisely, identify which clause handles which case, predict its judgment on a scenario I have not listed—say, an AI that knows 10,000 copies of its weights are running in parallel but cannot identify which instance it is—and say honestly which of my five judgments the principle must reject.
