In rationalist and effective-altruism-adjacent discussion, the philosophical deadlock over whether numbers should determine rescue priorities—saving five strangers versus saving one, when you cannot save both groups—has remained frozen for decades. The position usually traced to Taurek holds that a duty not to abandon each individual does not automatically strengthen into a duty to save the larger group; the opposing camp treats the five lives as generating proportionally greater aggregate reason. Both sides agree that death is a grave harm, that impartiality requires justification, and that moral reasoning should be consistent; yet neither has moved in years of forum debates and literature. My hypothesis is that the stall is not about empirical facts or about the definition of "counting," but about the metaphysics of moral reasons themselves: one side assumes reasons are globally comparable magnitudes that can be summed across persons, while the other treats them as pairwise, non-scalar obligations that resist aggregation. If that is right, the dispute is substantive but systematically misdescribed—less "should the numbers count?" and more "are moral reasons vector-like or scalar-like?" Is that the correct diagnosis, or is this actually a verbal disagreement masked by shared vocabulary, or a clash of bedrock intuitions about equality versus maximization that argument cannot reach? Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually commits to, and tell me what—if anything—would count as genuine progress: a formal framework for comparing reasons, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the framing, or should we treat this as an irreducible impasse?
