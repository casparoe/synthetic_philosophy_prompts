In discussions of synthetic biology I keep encountering the slogan "DNA is a code," most recently when a colleague used it to reject the idea that an engineered bacterium with a reassigned stop codon produces a "different meaning" from its wild-type counterpart: if DNA is a code, he argued, the sequence itself carries the message regardless of which decoder reads it. I am skeptical that the slogan does that work, but I want a precise diagnosis. Lay out the distinct, precise theses that "DNA is a code" could be expressing — including, but not limited to, claims about syntactic structure, about a fixed one-to-one mapping analogous to a cipher, about semantic content that depends on an interpreting system, about algorithmic execution by cellular machinery, and about the independence of message from decoder. For each thesis, indicate whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false, and specify which of these readings, if any, actually licenses dismissing the engineered-ambiguity objection. Finally, say whether the slogan, on its most plausible interpretation, requires a canonical decoder such as the ribosome-tRNA apparatus to fix meaning, or whether the mapping relation alone is sufficient.
