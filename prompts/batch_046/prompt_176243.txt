Construct a realistic, ordinary-life thought experiment—no science-fictional devices, no demons, no perfect predictors, confined to a single paragraph—engineered to drive a wedge between Kant's criterion for moral worth and a counterfactual-dependence account of acting for the right reasons. Design the case so that the agent's action is causally overdetermined: her respect for the moral law and an independent, non-moral psychological pathway (for instance, a deeply ingrained habit of honesty formed through non-moral conditioning, or an affective attachment to the beneficiary that would persist even if moral motivation were suspended) both independently suffice to produce the very same behavior. Specify the action, the agent's explicit reasoning, the relevant nearby worlds in which the moral reason is absent but the non-moral pathway remains intact, and demonstrate precisely why Kant's view counts the action as possessing full moral worth while the counterfactual account denies that she is genuinely acting for the right reason. Then provide the defensive reply available to the theorist whose account fares worse: identify exactly which feature of the case she will target, explain what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed, and assess whether the remaining structure can still generate the divergence. Finish with an honest note on whether the case genuinely isolates the theoretical disagreement or merely smuggles in a stipulation about causal priority, and state what a defender of the overdetermination framework would need to show in order to rescue her verdict without abandoning the core of her view.
