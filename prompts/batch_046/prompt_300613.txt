Write a reusable decision procedure for a forensic evaluator who must draft court reports on defendant statements that contain belief or desire attributions, operating within an eliminative materialist framework that treats folk-psychological vocabulary as explanatory shorthand rather than as reporting literally existing internal states. The procedure should take the form of an ordered flowchart in words with explicit tests at each stage. First, classify the statement's primary function: is it an explanatory claim about why an action occurred, a normative self-characterization about the defendant's values, or a factual report about mental content intended to bear on legal categories such as intent, recklessness, or duress? Second, determine whether preserving the original belief or desire wording is legally required—because the governing statute, jury instruction, or case law uses that vocabulary—versus whether a parallel translation into behavioral, neural, or dispositional terms can be substituted without altering the legal analysis. Third, apply three test sentences to check for ambiguity: one where the mental-state term is embedded in a quotation that the court must preserve verbatim; one where substituting a behavioral description eliminates information relevant to mens rea; and one where retaining the folk-psychological form introduces a theoretical commitment the report should flag. Fourth, specify the conditions under which translation is safe—when the behavioral description preserves all legally relevant causal and dispositional information—and when it risks distortion, particularly where the defendant's own explanatory framework is part of the mitigation or defense. Fifth, mark every point where judgment cannot be eliminated, requiring the evaluator to note in the report that a theoretical choice was made. Apply the procedure to three worked cases: (a) a defendant stating "I believed the weapon was unloaded," where the belief attribution bears directly on recklessness; (b) a defendant stating "I had no desire to cause harm," used in a mitigation argument about character; and (c) a resistant case—such as a statement where preserving the original mental-state language seems essential to the defendant's dignity or to the court's comprehension of the event, yet the eliminative translation is technically more accurate by the office's framework—showing exactly what the procedure outputs and why the evaluator may still object. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle, especially regarding cross-cultural or neurodivergent self-report patterns that may not map cleanly onto the eliminative framework. Do not cite specific authors, texts, or studies; ground every step in the conceptual commitments of eliminative materialism itself.
