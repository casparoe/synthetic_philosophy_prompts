I've been chewing on the conceivability argument for philosophical zombies, but through the lense of fiction writing. My position is this: I can write a character so convincing that readers weep for her, yet she's literally just syntax and white space -- a kind of narrative zombie with no inner life. If that's possible, then conceiving of a physical duplicate with no consciousness isn't special; we do it every time we write a person. So the zombie intuition doesn't point to a metaphysical gap, it just points to our skill at constructing convincing surfaces. What would actually count aginst this view? I want it sorted: the fatal ones versus the ones that are just awkward. Would it be if someone showed that reader empathy requires attributing real mental states (not just simulated ones)? Or that fictional characters depend on a conscious reader in ways physical zombies don't? Or that my view makes consciousness unfalsifiable because I can always say 'that's just surface'? I want to know what defenders of the conceivability argument would concede hurts them, and whether my reversal risks the same problem. Don't give me a reading list -- just the map of where this breaks.
