A philosophy blog I read summarized Kant's universalizability tests like this: Kant gives two checks—the Formula of Universal Law and the Formula of Humanity—but they're not really separate. The Formula of Universal Law has two contradictions, logical and practical, and both are just ways of seeing if your maxim can belong to a universal system of nature using the Typic of pure practical judgment. The contradiction-in-will case (like refusing to help anyone) is just a practical version of logical inconsistency in that system. Meanwhile the Formula of Humanity—treating people never merely as means—is completly redundant, because if your maxim treats someone as a tool, it already fails the universal-law check; dignity just names the failure from the agent's side. So Kant really has one deep test, coherence of maxims within a universal system of nature, and the famous murderer-at-the-door case is solved by seeing that lying introduces a practical contradiction into that system. The blog concludes that modern readers overstate the humanity formula because they miss how the Typic collapses everything into consistency. Something feels wrong about reducing the humanity formula to the Typic, and also about calling contradiction-in-will just logical inconsistency in disguise. Is this summary fair?
