My colleague in formal epistemology has given clear verdicts on whether Jeffrey conditionalization—revising beliefs by adopting precise probabilities over an exhaustive partition without conditioning on a specific observed event—is appropriate in the following five scenarios:

1. A meteorological forecast assigns exactly 70% to rain and 30% to no rain; you update your belief about carrying an umbrella without stepping outside to observe the weather. Verdict: Jeffrey conditioning is correct.

2. You observe a specific instrument reading D and update beliefs about hypotheses H via a known likelihood function P(D|H). Verdict: This is standard Bayesian conditioning on D, not Jeffrey conditioning.

3. You receive ambiguous verbal testimony that makes one claim feel “probably true” and its negation “probably false,” but you cannot assign sharp numerical weights; you experience a smooth, graded shift in confidence. Verdict: Jeffrey conditioning applies in a qualitative form—you redistribute credence in proportion to the felt evidential force—even without precise numbers.

4. You learn that theoretical Model A is twice as probable as Model B, but you do not learn which specific world-state holds within either model; you update your beliefs about states conditional on each model. Verdict: Jeffrey conditioning applies at the model-selection level.

5. You learn that an expert assigns 80% to hypothesis H, but you possess an independent prior over whether the expert is well-calibrated, overconfident, or underconfident. Verdict: This is not Jeffrey conditioning; you should treat the expert’s report as observable data and apply hierarchical Bayes.
