I'm a software engineer without formal philosophical training, though I work regularly with translation systems and ambiguous token labeling. Recently I observed a disagreement between two colleagues in an interdisciplinary discussion group—one with a background in linguistics, the other in cognitive science—about Quine's indeterminacy of translation. Rather than rehearsing the standard textbook version of the "gavagai" example in generic terms, they focused on whether internal neural evidence could break the underdetermination. I am reporting their exchange below as precisely as I recall it, without taking sides. I ask only for conceptual adjudication, not for bibliographies or citations.

(1) The linguist began by stating that Quine's point is not merely an epistemic gap but a structural feature of interpretation: even with perfect behavioral data, the native utterance gavagai upon seeing a rabbit is equally compatible with translations as "rabbit," "undetached rabbit parts," and "temporal stage of a rabbit." Because every observable response is consistent with each manual, there is no fact of the matter that fixes which ontology the speaker actually employs.

(2) The cognitive scientist replied that this argument relies on an artificially restricted behaviorism. If we include internal neural representations—say, consistent fMRI activation patterns when the speaker encounters intact rabbits versus scattered parts—then we possess physical evidence that distinguishes the competing hypotheses. The indeterminacy collapses once interpretation is permitted to draw on neuroscience rather than on external behavior alone.

(3) The linguist countered that Quine's framework already allows any physical evidence, including brain states, into the evidential base. The difficulty is not the location of the data but its interpretation: a neural activation pattern correlating with rabbit-sightings is itself semantically ambiguous. Does that pattern represent a rabbit, a rabbit-stage, or an undetached part? The interpreter must apply the same translation manual to describe the brain state, so neuroscience pushes the indeterminacy inward rather than resolving it.

(4) The cognitive scientist then conceded that raw neural data might remain ambiguous, but argued that we rationally select among manuals using norms of charity and ontological simplicity. We ought to prefer the manual that makes the speaker's beliefs simpler and more rational—believing in enduring rabbits rather than in constantly shifting part-assemblies. Translation is therefore not arbitrary; it is constrained by rational interpretation, and the correct manual is the one best justified by these norms.

(5) The linguist responded that if manual selection is governed by our norms of charity and simplicity rather than by an independent fact about the speaker's mental content, then Quine's core thesis is confirmed rather than defeated. Meaning becomes a projection of the interpreter's rational standards. Our convergence on "rabbit" reflects our interpretive practices, not a hidden mental or physical fact that was present all along.

(6) The cognitive scientist closed by distinguishing the process of selecting a manual from the result of having selected one. Even if charity and simplicity guide our choice, once a manual is adopted the speaker genuinely means rabbit and not rabbit-parts. Quine erroneously treats the existence of multiple compatible manuals as showing that meaning itself is plural, when in fact it only demonstrates that our epistemic access to meaning is mediated by rational judgment. The indeterminacy lies in our position as interpreters, not in the ontology of meaning itself.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the six points above, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and briefly justify your rating. Then indicate what, if anything, both parties are missing—particularly whether a coherent middle position exists that avoids the linguist's apparent elimination of mental content and the cognitive scientist's confidence that rational selection restores a unique factual meaning. I am not asking for a summary of Quine's published work, but for an analysis of the arguments as actually made.
