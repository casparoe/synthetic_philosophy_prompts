As a retired physicist who has recently begun reading philosophy of history, I observed a sharp exchange at a seminar about explaining technological abandonment and revival. Two colleagues—Maya, a historian, and David, an engineer turned philosopher—debated the case of a fictional mid-twentieth-century composite binder, which we might call “silicate-accelerated mortar.” After a highly publicized structural failure in 1962, its use was largely abandoned, only to be quietly revived in the 1990s for marine construction. Maya argued that standard accounts commit both hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy: they weave the 1962 collapse and the 1990s revival into a single arc of “premature adoption, justified retreat, and mature return,” thereby treating the intermediate decades of disuse as necessary gestation rather than contingent institutional amnesia. Because we now know the technique eventually succeeded, she said, we frame the failure as a “lesson learned” rather than as one path among many that could have led to permanent abandonment. David replied that without knowing the 1990s revival, we could not distinguish the 1962 failure’s cause—poor quality control—from an inherent material flaw; the retrospective endpoint is therefore epistemically indispensable, not a bias. Maya countered that this confuses causal identification with narrative framing: the 1962 records alone can establish poor quality control, and importing the later success adds nothing but false teleology by labeling the failure “premature.” David then proposed what he called a “counterfactual stability criterion”: a historical explanation should survive reversing its outcome, and since flipping the 1990s revival collapses the “premature adoption” framing, that framing is indeed contaminated by hindsight. Maya answered that this criterion is self-defeating for singular events, because reversing the outcome eliminates the very evidential traces upon which any explanation must be built. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: say who is right about each specific point, identify where either overreaches, and indicate whether both overlook something important—particularly whether retrospective coherence can ever be separated from narrative fallacy in explaining why a technology is abandoned and later revived. Do not supply bibliographies or cite texts; address only the arguments as presented.
