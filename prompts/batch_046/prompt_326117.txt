I am a high school student in an advanced philosophy seminar, and we recently discussed what my instructor called the paradox of deontology—the apparent tension between absolute moral prohibitions and the demands of rescue. To understand the structure better, I invented a thought-tool I am calling a deontic ledger: a bookkeeping framework that registers moral failures symmetrically, so that allowing a preventable death creates an omission-entry of the same weight as a commission-entry for causing a death, differing only in causal direction rather than moral magnitude.

Using this ledger, I built the following argument, but its conclusion—that any consistent deontology must collapse into a form of consequentialism once the stakes are high enough—strikes me as suspiciously neat. (1) A standard deontological framework includes both agent-centered prohibitions against killing the innocent and positive duties to rescue others when the cost is not extreme. (2) Under the ledger model, failing to rescue N people generates N omission-entries, each carrying the same moral weight as the single commission-entry generated by killing one innocent person to save those N. (3) Consequently, when N is sufficiently large—say, one hundred lives—the total omission-debt of inaction is one hundred times greater than the commission-debt of the lethal rescue. (4) To avoid the greater moral debt, a rational agent operating within deontology ought to perform the lethal rescue. (5) Yet if an absolute prohibition against killing can be overridden whenever omission-debt exceeds it, then the prohibition functions not as an absolute side-constraint but as a weighted factor in an overall calculus. (6) Therefore, deontology either permits mass death through inaction, violating its rescue commitments, or it surrenders its absolute character and becomes indistinguishable from rule-utilitarian optimization.

Which step is the weak link? I want to keep the intuition behind (1) and I find (5) hard to abandon, but I need to know whether the error lies in the symmetry assumption built into the ledger, in the aggregation of omission-debt across many victims, or in the move from debt-avoidance to permission. Please identify the specific premise or inference to reject, explain why it initially seemed sound, and discuss how defenders of deontology—whether through the doing-allowing distinction, agent-relative restrictions, or some version of threshold deontology—would respond. Also tell me whether discarding the ledger's symmetry actually resolves the paradox or merely pushes it into a different part of the framework.
