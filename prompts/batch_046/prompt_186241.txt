I'm researching a long-form piece on the hidden philosophy inside contemporary urban-planning metrics, and I need a rigorous conceptual analysis for the draft. Over the past decade, several municipalities in Northern Europe and East Asia have adopted institutional evaluation frameworks—called, in planning documents, "negative-capacity welfare audits"—that assess the quality of public spaces exclusively through indicators of absence: lowered stress biomarkers, reduced exposure to craving-stimuli, elimination of noise and advertising, and declining complaint rates about psychological overload. Notably, these audits do not register social connection, aesthetic experience, or recreational engagement as positive goods; where such activities generate excitement or desire, they are classified as risks to be minimized rather than values to be cultivated. The implicit rationale seems to treat tranquility—the absence of craving and suffering—not merely as one good among others, but as the sole intrinsic measure of a successful environment. What philosophical commitments about well-being does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory with precision: does the audit commit to a strict tranquilist view according to which only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, with all positive states being at best instrumentally useful and at worst sources of future disturbance? Or does it allow for a hybrid position? Identify any inconsistencies between how the audit treats sensory deprivation (valued) and social isolation (sometimes an unintended side effect, apparently tolerated). Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, assess whether the parts hang together philosophically, and tell me what specific institutional changes—what metrics, what permitted activities, what design priorities—would have to be altered if the underlying framework were replaced by a eudaimonist or hedonist account that assigns independent, positive weight to flourishing, pleasure, or communal intensity.
