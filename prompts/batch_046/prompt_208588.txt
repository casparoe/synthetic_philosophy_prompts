I hold the following normative thesis in agricultural ethics: if welfare-based restrictions on rodenticide use are justified by evidence of prolonged suffering in targeted mammals, then parallel restrictions ought to apply to neurotoxic insecticides employed in crop protection, since decapod crustaceans and several insect orders exhibit nociceptive and stress-related behavioral and neurochemical responses that are functionally analogous—not merely homoplastic—to vertebrate pain pathways. I take the standard exemption of invertebrate pest control from animal-welfare regulation to rest on an unjustified phylogenetic essentialism rather than on any defensible difference in the capacity for experiential harm, which undermines the coherence of any ethics that permits lethal rodent control only under conditions of minimal suffering while allowing unrestricted aerial application of neonicotinoids. What would genuinely count against this position? I want the defeaters that should move a committed defender, sorted from fatal to merely awkward: which empirical findings about invertebrate neurobiology—for instance, rigorous distinctions between nociception and integrated subjective experience, or the absence of specific neural architectures—would be decisive rather than accommodating? Which ethical arguments—such as those appealing to ecological replacement, relational harm, or the moral irrelevance of indirect killing—survive the analogy, and which collapse once it is pressed? Does the view make distinctive predictions about the moral status of particular agricultural practices—say, regarding biological versus chemical pest control—that its rivals do not, and would the falsification of those predictions damage the core analogy or only its peripheral applications? Would a proponent concede that if targeted invertebrates lack integrated subjective experience, the position is fatally undermined, or could they retreat to a broader relational or ecological account of harm? And does the view risk being insulated by ad hoc qualifications—restricting the analogy only to "higher" invertebrates, for example—such that it becomes unfalsifiable, and if so, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a vice for a normative claim of this kind? Please distinguish the liabilities that defenders ought to acknowledge from the objections they can legitimately dismiss, and be frank about whether the position has been qualified into invulnerability.
