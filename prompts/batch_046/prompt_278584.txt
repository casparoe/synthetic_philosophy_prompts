In discussions of institutional commitment races—where actors irreversibly bind themselves to escalate, deter entry, or lock in policy—people frequently invoke the proverb 'burn your bridges behind you.' I recently encountered it deployed to dismiss the idea that a credible commitment can be maintained purely through reputation or contractual penalties: the speaker insisted that unless retreat is physically or legally impossible, rational opponents will always anticipate defection, so only literal irreversibility creates credibility. Can you separate the precise claims hiding in that one sentence, arranged as a menu? For each reading, specify whether it is a psychological claim about motivation, a game-theoretic claim about payoff structures and rational expectations, or an institutional claim about observable design; mark which are tautologies, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false. In particular, distinguish (i) the claim that raising the cost of retreat strengthens one's bargaining position, from (ii) the claim that rational agents can commit only through technically irreversible mechanisms, and from (iii) the claim that reputation effects are always swamped by reversibility in repeated interactions. Then say which of these readings the speaker was actually invoking to reject reputation-based commitment, whether that reading holds up in models of commitment races with incomplete information and observable but costly exit, and identify the further case—say, a credible threat sustained solely through a history of costly punishment—that would tell the competing readings apart.
