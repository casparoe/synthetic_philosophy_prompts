After taking on an advisory role with a regional planning board in an area experiencing prolonged depopulation and the abandonment of centralized infrastructure, I have been reading comparative histories of societal discontinuity to understand what “failure” actually looks like at different scales. In the literature on the Classic Maya lowlands, the Late Bronze Age Eastern Mediterranean, and Rapa Nui, I keep encountering a specific narrative shape that I do not have a precise word for, and I want to know whether one exists.

The pattern is this: scholars document the cessation of elite-driven institutional projects—monument construction, palace economies, long-distance trade networks, and in some cases writing systems—along with demographic contraction in administrative centers. They then label the overall process a “collapse.” Yet the same accounts routinely include evidence that household-level subsistence practices, local exchange, and kin-based social organization persisted, and in some cases reconfigured rather than disappeared. The term “collapse” therefore seems to slide between two different referents: the dissolution of a particular political-economic superstructure, and an implied judgment that the society as a whole failed or ended. Meanwhile, “transformation” feels too broad to capture the real loss of institutional capacity, and “resilience” misses the fact that something specific and important did disappear. What I am trying to isolate is the disjunction itself—the analytical move in which the failure of an institutional layer is treated as synonymous with the failure of the social formation beneath it, even when the archaeological record shows substantial continuity at the household and local levels.

Is there an established concept that names this exact configuration—the gap between superstructural dissolution and substructural persistence—rather than treating it as merely an ambiguous use of “collapse”? If not, I would like a term and a boundary: what counts as this phenomenon, what distinguishes it from ordinary political decentralization, and where its edges lie relative to genuine societal extinction on one side and mere regime change on the other. I am not looking for a literature survey; I want the carving.
