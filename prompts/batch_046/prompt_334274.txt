I have been working through the ethics of collective remembrance, particularly the tension between an obligation to preserve historical injustices in living form and the case that strategic forgetting serves moral and social recovery. I am drawn to a strong version of the duty-to-remember view, one that goes beyond the claim that archives must be maintained or that citizens should know the basic facts. The position holds that a community owes the victims of past wrongs not merely propositional preservation—that is, true records and historical knowledge—but what I will call an indexical mnemonic commitment: an active, institutionally embedded, affectively sustained orientation in which the present community structures its practices (education, ritual, public space, legal framing) around an unmediated relation to the event, so that the victims remain present as living interlocutors rather than as settled historical entries. On this view, propositional memory without such indexical commitment is a form of moral failure, because it converts an open claim into a museum piece; conversely, strategic forgetting is permissible only if it leaves the indexical link intact, and any dissolution of that link constitutes not therapeutic release but a second moral injury.

This framework borrows from the ethics of memory tradition associated with thinkers like Margalit and stands in contrast to defenses of forgetting advanced by figures such as Rieff, yet I want to treat it not as a survey item but as a specific thesis with conceptual teeth. The claim is that the duty is non-dischargeable: no generational turnover, no change in political circumstances, and no demonstrated harm to descendants can fully release the community, because the obligation is indexed not to the psychological needs of the living but to the standing of the dead as co-participants in an unfinished moral relation. What I want is an honest map of this position's exposure. What observations, conceptual arguments, or empirical patterns would genuinely count against it? For instance, if societies that maintain intense indexical remembrance show systematically worse outcomes—perpetuation of intergroup hostility, psychological damage to descendants who never experienced the original wrong, or the instrumentalization of victimhood for present political ends—does that defeat the view, or only complicate it? Would a conceptual critique showing that any institutionalization of remembrance necessarily mediates and therefore destroys the very "unmediated" relation the view demands be decisive, or merely awkward? And what should defenders themselves concede: if direct descendants of the victims collectively demand that the indexical practices cease, is that a legitimate dissolution of the obligation, or does the view have resources to absorb it by redefining such demands as products of false consciousness? Finally, assess whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—for example, by treating every successful case of forgetting as "merely propositional" and every failed case of remembrance as "insufficiently indexical"—and whether, if so, that insulation is a philosophical virtue or a debilitating weakness.
