As a journalist preparing a long-form essay on the philosophy of administrative violence, I keep returning to Arendt's well-known shift between her study of totalitarianism—where she describes evil as radical, in the sense of being root-deep and world-annihilating—and her report on the Eichmann trial, where she calls evil banal, apparently meaning it lacks ideological depth altogether. Given that she specifically notes Eichmann's claim to have lived by a distorted version of Kant's categorical imperative, and given her emphasis on his sheer inability to imagine any standpoint other than his own, doesn't her later framework actually rest on a hidden psychological thesis: that thoughtlessness is a stable, cross-cultural personality trait rather than a situational product of bureaucratic structure? And if that is the case, why do scholars and commentators continue to treat the banality thesis as primarily a structural critique of modern organizations, rather than as an individual-level diagnostic that should apply only to specific cognitive failures?
