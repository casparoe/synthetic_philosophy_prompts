ok so I'm prepping a short statement for a seminar on biography and moral learning and I want a consistency check before I open my mouth. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) I think the point of reading lives—Plutarch, hagiograpy, modern biography—is moral calibration, not imitation. You're seeing what a human being can actually do, not picking a role model.

(2) At the same time I think Plutarch's parallel structure (Alcibiades with Coriolanus, etc.) undermines the whole exemplar idea, since virtue ends up looking civic-specific rather than portable.

(3) I think hagiography should be read as aspirational literature, not failed history, and that its value is in the gap between the reader and the saint.

(4) But I also think that gap only works if the writer includes failures and temptations in detail—like the desert fathers—otherwise the figure is just inhuman and useless.

(5) I reject the balance-sheet approach to biography (virtues minus vices) because moral character is a unified gestalt, not an accounting exercise.

(6) And I think this applies to fictional lives just as well—there's nothing special about having actually existed for this kind of calibration.

So my tensions seem to be: (1) needs some shared ground with the subject, but (3) and (4) are about preserving distance and including failure, which might collapse the calibration I'm after. Then (2) looks like it conflicts with (5): if virtue is always indexed to a city, can there be a unified character across contexts? And (6) feels like it kills (3)—if fiction works too, why defend hagiography's special aspirational status?

Are these real or am I inventing them? Rank by severity, say what has to give in each case, point out any tension I'm missing, and tell me which single commitment I should drop if I want the cleanest package. Don't worry about bibliographies, I just want to know if my own house is in order.
