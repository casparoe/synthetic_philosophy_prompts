I am revising the syllabus for an advanced seminar on post-positivist philosophy of science, and I want an exercise that forces students to separate genuine philosophical disagreement from terminological stalemate. Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps regarding the fate of the analytic-synthetic distinction after Quine's critique in Two Dogmas of Empiricism. The first camp, the Quinean demolitionists, maintains that Quine's two central arguments—the circularity involved in defining analyticity through synonymy or definition, and the failure of reductionist verification for individual statements—jointly demonstrate that no principled, non-circular, and explanatorily substantial distinction between analytic and synthetic truths survives. They further hold that subsequent attempts at rehabilitation either confuse epistemic privilege with semantic determination, or smuggle the distinction back in through an unacknowledged appeal to pre-theoretic linguistic intuition. The second camp, the reconstructivists, accepts that Quine's critique is decisive against the specific logical-empiricist package of reductionism and definitional circularity, but contends that a non-reductionist distinction—grounded perhaps in the asymmetry between explanations that invoke linguistic convention and those that invoke empirical causal mechanisms, or in the structural properties of conceptual competence—can be articulated without the metaphysical commitments Quine attacked, and that this rehabilitated distinction retains sufficient normative weight to guide theory revision and conceptual analysis.

The document should be formatted as a numbered section of agreed claims followed by a disagreements table. In the agreed-claims section, include shared descriptions of Quine's arguments, shared acknowledgment of the historical trajectory from logical empiricism to contemporary naturalism, and any shared assessments of specific conceptual stress tests—such as whether the retention of mathematical framework principles during empirical revision demonstrates a genuine analytic core or merely reflects pragmatic conservatism in belief revision—without resolving their interpretation. The disagreements table should present precisely formulated residual disagreements, phrased so that each camp would endorse the wording of its own position, particularly concerning: whether any distinction that survives Quine's critique is genuinely continuous with the original analytic-synthetic distinction or instead a distinct conceptual tool; whether the failure of statement-by-statement reduction entails the failure of all kind-based semantic distinctions or only of a particular verificationist variant; and whether an appeal to explanatory asymmetry can be sustained without implicitly presupposing the very distinction it aims to ground. Then identify the cruxes—sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one party—including whether conceptual-role theories can avoid circularity when specifying the roles that distinguish analytic from synthetic concepts, and whether pragmatic considerations in theory-choice can be rigorously separated from semantic considerations. For each crux, specify the argument or hypothetical case both sides agree is probative, with each side's predicted outcome recorded in advance: for instance, both might agree that a detailed analysis of how physicists retained the mathematical structure of general relativity while revising its physical interpretation bears on the issue, yet they should predict opposite conclusions about what that retention demonstrates. The prompt should not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, but it should be written at the level of detail appropriate for philosophers who have studied the primary texts and are now testing whether they can articulate their disagreement without talking past one another.
