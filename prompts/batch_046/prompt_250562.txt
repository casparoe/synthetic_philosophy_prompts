My friend Sam and I agreed to organize our neighborhood’s annual winter supply drive “together.” We never wrote anything down. For the past three years, Sam handled donation outreach and I handled distribution logistics, but we always checked in before making big changes. This year, Sam unilaterally switched our drop-off partner from the community center to a private church without telling me. When I found out, I said we had violated our agreement. Sam replied that “together” just meant we both contributed labor, not that we had veto power over each other’s domains. Meanwhile, the church coordinator has been emailing both of us as a single unit—“you two decided to partner with us”—and donors have started addressing checks to “Sam and Jordan’s drive.” I feel like Sam broke something real, but I can’t articulate why it isn’t just hurt feelings or poor communication.

Is Sam’s interpretation of “together” defensible? Does the fact that outsiders treat us as one agent matter for whether we actually are one? And if we really did form a joint agent, what does that imply about who gets to correct the course now?

Please identify the philosophical structure here—what kind of shared mental state or social arrangement, if any, was created by our agreement—and use it to evaluate Sam’s move, the significance of the third-party recognition, and what repair would actually restore the joint status rather than just patching the logistics. Also consider a variant: suppose Sam and I had explicitly divided domains in writing, but donors and partners still treated us as a single unit. Would that external framing alone create a collective agent where none existed internally?
