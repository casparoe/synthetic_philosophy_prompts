I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who, after leaving the laboratory, have begun auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of science and logic, and I find myself preoccupied with induction in ways that my former colleagues rarely discussed. In conversation with a fellow student recently, I expressed dissatisfaction with the standard treatments of Hempel's raven paradox: not because the Bayesian, projectibility-based, or pragmatic resolutions are technically wrong, but because they all treat the observing agent as a featureless logical calculator with uniform epistemic access. From my own experience running experiments, observation is never neutral—it is expensive, domain-specific, and tied to prior investment in instrumentation and theoretical framing. I want to turn this intuition into a polished, two-page research proposal for a six-month independent-study fellowship. The precise question is whether the raven paradox survives, transforms, or collapses once we replace the universal logical agent with a resource-bounded observer who possesses domain-specific evidential entitlements. Specifically, can we formalize a framework in which observing a white shoe genuinely fails to confirm "All ravens are black" not merely because of low prior probabilities or non-projectible predicates, but because cross-domain evidential transfer requires an explicit theoretical bridge that the observer has not constructed? And if so, does this framework distinguish Hempel's asymmetry from ordinary scientific induction in a principled way, or does it merely smuggle psychological limitations into logic?

For the proposal document, I need four things articulated with conceptual precision but without unnecessary jargon. First, a sharp statement of the problem and an explanation of why existing answers—Bayesian conditioning, Goodman-style projectibility constraints, and pragmatic appeals to background knowledge—leave precisely this gap open. Second, the method: what I would actually do month by month over six months, working alone without students or laboratory resources, likely adapting resource-rational or decision-theoretic formalisms rather than pure logic, including intermediate milestones that would prevent the project from drifting into mere psychologism. Third, clear success and failure conditions: success might be a formal condition for legitimate cross-domain evidential block; failure might be demonstration that the framework collapses into standard Bayesianism with an ad hoc parameter added. Fourth, a realistic scope that respects my solitary, non-institutional setting. Please do not include a bibliography, citation list, or references to specific page numbers or editions—I will compile all references myself, and I want to avoid anything that requires verification beyond standard philosophical knowledge. The tone should be formal and suitable for a fellowship committee in philosophy of science, but it should retain the voice of someone whose primary training was in empirical science rather than professional philosophy.
