Question: Kuhn's thesis of incommensurability is frequently reduced to the claim that rival paradigms share no common ground whatsoever. Clarify what incommensurability actually entails for Kuhn, with particular attention to local lexical taxonomy and the status of shared scientific values; distinguish this precisely from Feyerabend's stronger methodological anarchism; and evaluate whether incommensurability renders rational paradigm choice impossible during a period of crisis.

Student answer:

Incommensurability, for Kuhn, means that scientists working in different paradigms literally inhabit different worlds because their conceptual taxonomies are entirely disjoint. There is no neutral observation language that both sides can appeal to, which means empirical anomalies are also interpreted differently within each framework. Feyerabend takes this further by arguing that not only are observations theory-laden, but that there is no rational basis whatsoever for preferring one theory over another—everything is permitted. Kuhn, however, retains a belief in shared scientific values such as accuracy and consistency, though these values are applied differently inside each paradigm. During a crisis, anomalies accumulate, yet because each paradigm defines what counts as an anomaly according to its own internal rules, there is never any genuine pressure to abandon the old framework. Consequently, paradigm choice is ultimately a matter of conversion or persuasion rather than rational argument, and incommensurability does indeed prevent rational comparison, even if Kuhn allows that scientists can still talk past one another using shared vocabulary.
