I run an advanced philosophy-of-rationality seminar: 18 students, one 75-minute session. Design a single-session classroom game—call it Broken Superrationality—that makes participants feel why superrational coordination collapses when Platonia-style and Luring-Lottery-style incentives are layered with hidden asymmetry.

Inject this structural twist: before play, randomly deal each student a hidden frame card—half Platonia (exactly one message-card sent yields 20 prestige points for the sender; zero or multiple yields none for anyone; cooperation forbidden), half Luring (each entry-card submitted increases the sender’s share of a fixed pool but dilutes the pool; if entries exceed three the prize collapses to near-zero). Students do not know their own frame until after committing to a shared reasoning protocol, and they must not reveal it during play. Payoffs use only seminar prestige points, never real money.

Specify rules, physical materials (cards, envelopes, a public scoreboard), the protocol-commitment round, and the debrief. The debrief should map: (i) the commitment round to common-knowledge failure; (ii) the hidden-frame reveal to the divergence between superrational expectation and strategic dominance; (iii) the observed runaway or total-abstention outcome to the structural instability of the Luring Lottery. Predict what will go wrong on the first run and how to recover. Be explicit about which formal feature—e.g., the infinite regress of reasoning about reasoning—this physical setup cannot reproduce, and what you as facilitator must supply verbally.

Lead with the bottom line in one paragraph: the core mechanism and the precise intuition it proves. Put the Platonia-condition vs. Luring-condition comparison—rational prescription, payoff structure, and failure mode—in a table. Stay under 350 words. No bibliography, no citations, no reading list. Avoid generic opening phrases.
