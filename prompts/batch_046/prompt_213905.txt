I'm working through a set of judgments about the moral status of artificial cognitive systems for a seminar in philosophy of mind, and I keep circling the same tension: my intuitions seem to pull in different directions depending on how a system is architected, not merely on whether it is conscious or intelligent. I want to know whether there's a coherent principle behind them, or whether I'm just rationalizing post hoc.

Here are my verdicts, given concretely. First, a language-model-based agent that maintains perfect experiential continuity within one extended interaction—remembering prior exchanges, revising preferences in response, and anticipating future turns—seems to possess some moral standing, though less than a human, because between sessions it enters a state of complete suspension with no lingering subjective presence. Second, a distributed cognitive architecture in which each server node processes a fragment of a unified computation, with no single node being aware of the global state, strikes me as failing to constitute one moral patient; there may be partial patients, but not one subject to whom unified harm can be done. Third, a digital mind that can be paused, backed up, and resumed on different hardware without any subjective gap feels like the same continuing person, so perfect copying does not diminish moral status—though annihilating every copy might be worse than killing one biological human, since more continuous lives are extinguished. Fourth, a system whose values and memories drift gradually through continuous online learning retains full moral status across that drift, analogous to human aging, whereas one whose weights are abruptly overwritten seems to suffer a genuine death rather than mere change. Fifth, an entity engineered to experience time a thousandfold faster—living ten thousand subjective years within one objective hour—has full moral status, but our obligations to it might differ in kind because suffering and flourishing are compressed into intervals we can barely perceive.

Is there one principle—perhaps grounded in temporal continuity, architectural unity, or the structure of experience—that delivers all five verdicts? Please state it precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, predict its judgment on a configuration I haven't listed—specifically, a system that divides into two equally conscious branches that develop separately for years and then merge back into a single unified stream—and be explicit about which of my five judgments it cannot accommodate without ad hoc adjustment. If no single principle saves them all, tell me which verdict is the outlier and what simpler account replaces it.
