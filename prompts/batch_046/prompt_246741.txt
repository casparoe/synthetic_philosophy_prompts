Many democratic states grant conscientious-objector exemptions from military service, but only to those who oppose all war in principle; citizens who refuse a particular war they judge unjust are denied exemption and may be imprisoned. The state typically requires alternative civilian service, treating absolute pacifism not as a political contribution but as a private disability of conscience that must be compensated. What philosophical view of moral knowledge, civic equality, and the authority of democratic decision-making does this institutional design presuppose? Reconstruct its implicit theory of why non-inferential, deeply held conviction excuses from duty while reasoned selective judgment counts as criminal breach. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, assess whether its parts are coherent, and say what would have to change if we replaced its underlying view with one that treated refusal to participate in an unjust war as a form of civic responsibility rather than a failure of citizenship. Separate what is settled about these norms from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of war and political obligation, and do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus.
