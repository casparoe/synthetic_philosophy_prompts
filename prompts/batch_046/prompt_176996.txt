I'm a high school student working through an independent study in philosophy of social science, and I have become stuck on a question about why the debate over gender as a socially constructed kind never seems to advance. On one side, philosophers treat gender as an institutional kind—essentially a hierarchical social position constituted by collective norms and practices—while on the other side, critics argue that this view mistakes the causal effects of gendered structures for the ontological status of gender itself, proposing instead that gender functions as a homeostatic property cluster sustained by both biological and social factors. Both camps agree on the empirical data regarding gender disparity, both accept that social practices exert enormous causal influence, and both reject naive biological essentialism; nevertheless, they remain deadlocked over whether describing gender as "constructed" means it is constituted by collective acceptance, merely caused by social structures, or something in between that neither vocabulary captures.

My own hypothesis is that the stall is not about gender specifically but about competing standards of ontological commitment: one side demands that a real kind be ultimately reducible to non-social base properties, whereas the other accepts that a kind can be fully real precisely in virtue of being sustained by what I have been calling "feedback-constituted" practices—recursive loops in which individual self-ascription continuously revises the institutional norms that define membership in the kind. I want to know whether this diagnosis is correct, or whether I am missing a hidden empirical disagreement about the causal architecture of gendered behavior, a purely verbal dispute over the word "construction," or a divergence in what counts as explanatory success. If the disagreement is indeed structural in the way I suggest, could a formal model of recursive institutional change actually move the debate, or does the impasse rest on bedrock intuitions about human nature and social reality that no conceptual distinction could settle? I am looking for an honest diagnosis of the disagreement's form, supported by what each side actually says, together with an assessment of what—if anything—would count as genuine progress.
