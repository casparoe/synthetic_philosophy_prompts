As a retired biologist who has only recently begun studying political philosophy in earnest, I should like a rigorous consistency audit of my present commitments on democratic membership. Before you respond, please adopt the role of an expert political philosopher specialising in democratic theory and the boundary problem. Take a moment to brainstorm the logical architecture of the claims below, then analyse them step by step with care.

Here is the package I currently hold:

(1) The correct criterion for membership in a democratic demos is the all-subjected principle: anyone who lives under a state’s coercive legal authority ought to have an equal say in how that authority is exercised.

(2) I am a committed moral cosmopolitan. State boundaries carry no intrinsic normative weight; they are administratively convenient but not morally fundamental.

(3) Democratic legitimacy requires what I call institutional reciprocity: members must be embedded in a closed feedback loop in which collective decisions materially alter the conditions of life for every member, and in which every member retains the capacity to reshape the governing institutions.

(4) I reject the all-affected-interests principle as a boundary criterion for present populations, on the grounds that it renders democratic jurisdiction indeterminate and tends to collapse national decision-making into an unworkable global referendum.

(5) I support extending the full franchise to long-term resident non-citizens who are permanently subject to the state’s laws.

(6) For decisions with long-term environmental consequences—climate policy, nuclear waste storage, biodiversity loss—I believe future generations ought to be represented by formal proxy advocates within the democratic deliberative process.

(7) Any stable democratic unit must satisfy a territorial contiguity condition: members should be geographically concentrated enough that no permanent coalition can systematically dominate a dispersed minority.

My question is whether these seven commitments form a jointly tenable position, or whether I am helping myself to incompatible motivations. Please proceed as follows.

First, examine the pairs in tension. For each pair that is genuinely incompatible, rank the tensions by severity and identify the least costly revision that would restore consistency without destroying the overall shape of the view. Second, distinguish these from pairs that are only apparently in tension: explain precisely why the conflict dissolves on closer inspection. In particular, address whether my cosmopolitanism in (2) undermines my reliance on state-based coercion in (1); whether my endorsement of institutional reciprocity in (3) conflicts with my support for proxy representation of future generations in (6); whether my rejection of the all-affected principle in (4) can be squared with my willingness to invoke affected-interests reasoning for future generations in (6); and whether extending the franchise to residents in (5) threatens the territorial contiguity condition in (7).

Finally, tell me which single revision—if I am forced to make only one—would clean up the greatest number of tensions. Please be precise, do not provide bibliographies or reading lists, and avoid citing specific texts or authors unless a direct quotation is genuinely necessary. I am interested in the internal logic of the position, not in scholarly attribution.
