hey, i'm a phd student in political theory running a grad reading group — 8 particpants, 75 mins, mostly philosophy/poli sci folks who think they know Machiavelli but have only read The Prince in undergrad. i want an in-class simulation that makes them feel the actual tension between fortuna and virtù, not just talk about it. specifcally i'm playing with this niche distinction: "adaptive virtù" is reacting to luck, but "constitutive virtù" is reshaping the conditions so that what counts as luck changes entirely. i want them to hit both in sequence and feel the difference.

design a two-round game. round 1: each player is a "prince" with a small pool of tokens. they face random "fortune cards" drawn from a deck i prepare — mild setbacks to catastrophic ones. they can spend tokens to adapt (build walls, hire advisors, shift alliances). rules are rigid: fail to adapt correctly and you lose big. they should feel smart and flexible just optimizing inside the frame.

round 2: same deck, but now there's a hidden option — spend a huge chunk of tokens to rewrite one rule or redefine what a card means, but it's risky and others can free-ride. maybe assign one "advisor" per table who sees the deck but can't spend tokens, creating an asymmetry they have to navigate. stakes should be small but real: points that determine coffee duty, or just a public scoreboard.

the key is they should experience round 1 as being virtuous, then in round 2 realize they've been acting mechanically — adapting within a frame they never questioned. breaking the frame should feel dangerous and maybe unfair, like cheating. they should come away seeing that virtù isn't just "being flexible" but involves agency over the conditions of contingency itself.

debrief should map round 1 onto adaptive virtù, round 2 onto constitutive virtù, and the advisor role onto Machiavelli's point about seeing patterns without controlling them. tell me what predictably goes wrong — probably nobody takes the round-2 option because it violates their sense of fair play — and how to handle it. also be honest about the gendered metaphor in The Prince (fortune as a woman who must be beaten) — this activity can't reproduce that without being gross, so the facilitator has to supply it verbally and explain why Machiavelli uses it.

no lecture up front; don't name "adaptive" vs "constitutive" until after. include timeline, materials, payoff scheme, and be frank about which feature of the idea the simulation just can't capture. i don't need citations or a reading list, and please don't ask them to produce one.
