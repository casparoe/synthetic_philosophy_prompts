I'm preparing material for a graduate workshop on collective epistemology, and my colleagues and I have been arguing about how the discursive dilemma plays out in real institutional settings rather than in abstract voting tables. Specifically, we are looking at multi-disciplinary review boards—say, a climate-and-health panel—that must issue both a collective verdict and a published dependency graph showing exactly which premises support it, where members carry uneven, domain-specific authority (an epidemiologist's judgment on causal premises is weighted differently from an ethicist's on normative premises). I want a taxonomy structured as a branching decision tree that sorts positions on resolving the discursive dilemma in exactly this setting. The tree should be built from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of named theorists: the first split should ask whether group rationality requires consistency at the individual-member level, at the aggregated-premise level alone, or at the final-conclusion level alone; the next splits should ask whether domain-specific authority weights are applied before aggregation or after, and whether the board may issue a conclusion that depends on premises that failed majority endorsement among the high-authority members in that domain. A further split should ask whether the mandatory disclosure of the dependency graph functions as a hard constraint that eliminates aggregation methods producing unstable or opaque rationales, or merely as a reporting layer that leaves the aggregation method untouched. Each leaf should carry a position name if one exists in the literature—such as premise-based aggregation, conclusion-based aggregation, or distance-based compromise—or be marked 'unnamed' if the combination is unexplored, followed by one sentence describing its most awkward practical commitment, especially what it forces the board to say or do when epidemiologists and ethicists split on both empirical and normative premises. Please include at least eight leaves, and for every unoccupied combination of answers briefly diagnose whether the gap seems incoherent, unattractive, or genuinely unexplored. Flag any pairs the tree cannot cleanly separate, particularly where a view's commitment to transparency is indistinguishable from its commitment to premise aggregation. The audience is graduate students who may not know the standard labels, so every branch must be phrased as a concrete institutional choice or case judgment, not as 'are you a premise-based theorist?' Keep the tree to no more than three levels. Before presenting the taxonomy, briefly state what institutional redesign or empirical finding would cause you to revise it—for instance, if disclosure of the dependency graph turned out to incentivize strategic misreporting of premises rather than transparency, or where the reasoning is otherwise most likely to fail. End with a one-sentence summary of the taxonomy's central organizing insight on its own final line, in this format: Verdict: [one sentence].
