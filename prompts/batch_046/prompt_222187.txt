I serve on my university’s Institutional Algorithmic Oversight Committee, and for next Tuesday’s session we must render a decision on a joint proposal from the Department of Criminology and the municipal police bureau to deploy a predictive-resource-allocation model across campus-adjacent neighborhoods. The model was engineered solely to minimize property-crime reports—the developers’ stated and documented purpose—but its validated outputs foreseeably concentrate patrol recommendations in a single, historically over-policed district with a majority-minority population. The proposal characterizes that concentration as an unfortunate but unintended side effect, and asks for approval with standard data-use safeguards. Half the committee, however, wants to invoke the intentionality asymmetry studied in experimental philosophy—the pattern in which observers attribute intentionality to harmful side effects far more readily than to beneficial ones—to argue that the development team “intentionally” accepted the disproportionate burden because it was foreseen, tolerated, and instrumental to securing the grant and achieving statistical efficacy. They contend this asymmetry should trigger stricter liability conditions, if not outright rejection, regardless of the absence of direct purpose. The other half insists that institutional intent should track explicit design goals, not predicted collateral patterns, and warns that importing folk-psychological attribution norms into governance will paralyze legitimate research.

Please draft the committee’s decision memo—no more than two pages in substance—that recommends a concrete course of action with an explicit time frame: for example, conditional approval tied to a mandatory redesign milestone within ninety days, rejection with a structured appeal pathway, or approval subject to an independent audit clause with a sunset date. The memo should be written as a document the researchers and the municipal partners could actually respond to, not as an abstract philosophical essay. It must engage the Knobe-effect argument on its own terms—treating it as a serious normative consideration about how institutions should be held accountable for foreseen but non-purposeful harms—while also giving an honest, substantive account of the strongest reason on the opposing side, namely that conflating predicted side effects with institutional intent may erode the distinction between negligence and deliberate discrimination in ways that make policy unworkable. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the reasoning should stand on the concepts themselves and on the concrete facts of the proposal.
