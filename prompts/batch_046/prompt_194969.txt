Here's an argument I sketched for my study group and now it feels like it proves way too much, but I can't find the exact leak.

(1) The St. Petersburg paradox shows expected value can diverge to infinity even with fair payouts, since each round doubles the pot while the probablity halves.

(2) The standard fix is concave utility: the tenth million dollars is worth less than the first, so you shouldn't pay infinite money to play.

(3) So rational agents maximize expected utility, not expected cash.

(4) Now apply that concavity to lives saved. Saving one life might have moral value U, but saving 2^N lives can't be worth 2^N times U—there's no physical way for one agent to experience or realize 2^N separate lives.

(5) So moral value also flattens out eventually.

(6) But if that's true, then once you've saved a few thousand lives, extra donations barely move the needel, so extreme altruisim is just a hobby.

(7) That sounds wrong, but (2) looks like basic economics and (4) looks like common sense.

Where does it slip? I'd rather keep (2) and reject (6), but I'm not sure I can without giving up (4).
