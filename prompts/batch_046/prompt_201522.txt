I'm pitching a foundation for a six-month project on a corner of AI alignment that falls between standard safety engineering and applied ethics: not when an assistant should refuse a dangerous request, but whether it should ever correct a user who is temporarily misrepresenting their own stable meta-preferences. The precise question is whether transient distortions—framing effects, time-inconsistency, or social-proof cascades—create a paternalistic obligation to override, or whether any non-consensual correction violates a deeper norm of instrumental rationality, even when the user's ex-ante commitments clearly reject the distorted choice. Existing discourse splits between harm-threshold refusal and value-alignment refusal, yet neither addresses cases where the user is not misaligned with their values but is momentarily failing to represent them; I propose to build a taxonomy of these "transient preference distortions" and derive, from reflective equilibrium rather than utility maximization, a decision procedure that justifies intervention for some but not others. The method is purely conceptual and decision-theoretic: I will specify three distortion categories, map each against coherence with the user's prior commitments, and show what an assistant could apply without accessing a full utility function. Success is a tractable normative framework practitioners can use; failure is demonstrating that all three categories collapse into either ordinary harm-refusal or unacceptable paternalism.
