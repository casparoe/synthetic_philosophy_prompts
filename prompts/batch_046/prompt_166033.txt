I have been relying on an analytical service that forecasts interpersonal dynamics—not astrology, but a system that models decision trees for professional relationships. Over the past year it issued three specific warnings. First, it predicted that accepting a speaking invitation in Boston would cause an irreparable rupture with a senior mentor; I declined, and no rupture occurred. Second, it warned that criticizing a particular proposal during a team meeting would permanently alienate a junior colleague; I withheld my criticism, yet the colleague was alienated anyway by another party's intervention. Third, and most troubling, it told me that traveling to Chicago for a March conference would result in my closest collaborator forming a decisive alliance elsewhere and abandoning our joint project. I canceled the trip. I have since learned that my collaborator went alone, met precisely the contact the service described, and has withdrawn—while noting that my absence made the conversation possible, since I normally redirect such interactions. I am trying to understand the logic at work here. The first case suggests avoidance succeeds; the second suggests the harm arrives regardless; the third suggests avoidance itself relocates and perhaps enables the harm. Is there a consistent framework for treating predictions that seem to map not fixed destinations but paths that shift when one steps away? Specifically, if altering my course in response to a warning changes the conditions that make the warning true, should I treat the forecast as an unavoidable appointment—something that will find me in whatever form I allow—or should I conclude that consulting the forecast and acting on it corrupts the prediction, rendering the system unreliable? I am less interested in whether to keep using the service than in what kind of reasoning applies when a warning about a specific future appears to become true precisely because one heeded it and changed direction.
