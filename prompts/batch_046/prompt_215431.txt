In my graduate seminar on comparative metaphysics this term, a sustained disagreement emerged between two participants—call them A and B—over how to interpret Jain anekāntavāda and its logical apparatus of syādvāda and the saptabhaṅgī. The exchange went through several rounds, touching on the ontology of substance and mode, the status of contradictory properties, and a modern analogy they devised to test the doctrine. I am writing as the seminar leader to ask for a precise, point-by-point adjudication; I do not wish for a bibliography or reading list, but rather for a direct assessment of the arguments as they actually unfolded.

A opened by arguing that anekāntavāda is fundamentally an epistemic thesis about the partiality of human cognition. On this reading, the seven-fold formula expresses that any predication holds only conditionally—syāt—relative to a specified naya, or standpoint: substance, region, time, state, and so forth. To say that a jar both exists and does not exist is therefore not to assert a contradiction in reality, but to record that, from the standpoint of substance (dravya), it exists, whereas from the standpoint of spatial displacement (paryāya), it does not. The doctrine, A insisted, is metaphysically neutral: it is a framework for avoiding ekānta, or one-sided dogmatism, by reminding us that our descriptions are necessarily partial.

B countered that this reduces anekāntavāda to a banal form of perspectivalism. In classical Jain metaphysics, B noted, objects possess ananta dharma—infinite properties—simultaneously. The jar is genuinely permanent qua dravya and genuinely impermanent qua paryāya. The syādvāda prefix is not merely a mental reservation or a speaker’s hedge; it is an ontological indicator that reality itself is structured so that contradictory predicates apply in different respects, and these respects are not subjective filters but objective dimensions of being. If the multiplicity were only in our articulation, B argued, then anekāntavāda would collapse into the very epistemic modesty that A describes, and it would lose its distinctive claim that contradictory properties inhere together in the same object.

A replied that B has failed to preserve the law of non-contradictio. If “in respect R” is built into the content of the proposition—“the jar is permanent qua substance”—then each claim is singly consistent. The multiplicity lies in our need for several such qualified propositions, not in the world’s being literally both ways at once. B responded by denying that “respect” must function as a conceptual qualifier added by the mind. In Jain ontology, dravya and paryāya are co-constitutive modes of being, not mental frames. The jar’s existence is literally composed of both permanence and change, comparable to a chord containing both C and E. To privilege one mode as the “real” underlying fact—say, the substance—while treating the other as merely apparent, is precisely to commit ekānta.

To test the disagreement, they introduced a modern case: a blockchain-verified digital record that is copied across distributed nodes. From the standpoint of informational content, the copies are identical; from the standpoint of physical instantiation—server location, energy state, timestamp—they are numerically distinct. A granted that both descriptions are necessary for a full explanation, but insisted that this only illustrates descriptive pluralism driven by our explanatory interests. It does not show that the record is literally both one and many in the same breath; rather, it shows that “identity” is ambiguous until we specify the parameter of evaluation. B rejected this as question-begging: the ambiguity thesis already assumes there must be a single parameter-independent fact of the matter that resolves the multiplicity. Anekāntavāda, B claimed, denies precisely that there is a “view from nowhere.” The record’s being-one and being-many are co-equal truths, not artifacts of linguistic ambiguity. To insist on resolving them into a single consistent picture is to fall back into one-sidedness.

A then raised a normative worry. If there is no view from nowhere, and if contradictory descriptions are all literally true in their respective respects, then anekāntavāda seems to collapse into an unprincipled relativism that undermines its own prescriptive force. The doctrine is meant to encourage intellectual humility—the recognition that one might be wrong. But if all contradictory descriptions are genuinely true, there is no error about which to be humble. The epistemic reading preserves normativity: we hold our beliefs conditionally because we might be missing standpoints. B answered that not all descriptions are true—only those grounded in valid naya. The doctrine is not “anything goes,” but “no single thing goes exclusively.” Intellectual humility is not skepticism about error; it is the recognition that any true claim is necessarily partial, coupled with the obligation to acknowledge the other sides.

Please adjudicate this exchange with precision. First, clarify whether syādvāda functions primarily as an epistemic hedge or as an ontological predicate-marker, and say which reading better accommodates the classical saptabhaṅgī structure without reducing it to trivial ambiguity. Second, assess whether the blockchain-copy example genuinely isolates the disagreement: does it favor B’s ontological reading, or does it remain compatible with A’s claim that we are merely selecting different explanatory parameters? Third, determine whether B’s position avoids collapsing either into a straightforward contradiction or into an unprincipled relativism, and whether A’s epistemic reading can adequately account for the Jain insistence that contradictory properties inhere in the same object rather than in our descriptions of it. Fourth, identify what both disputants appear to be missing—perhaps the distinction between naya as subjective standpoint and naya as objective mode of analysis, or the regulative role of kevalajñāna (omniscience) as an ideal that transcends multiplicity without negating it. Finally, state who is more right overall, whether a synthesis is available, and what feature of the case is doing the philosophical work.
