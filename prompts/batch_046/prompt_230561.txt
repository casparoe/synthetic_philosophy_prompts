I'm a high-school student organizing our philosophy club's next meeting—about fourteen members, ninety minutes, in the classroom after school. We've been discussing how thought experiments work as "intuition pumps" and whether they persuade us because they reveal genuine moral principles or just because they frame a situation in a particular emotional and temporal order. I want us to experience this distinction physically, not just talk about it, so I'm asking you to design a structured activity or game for the session.

The activity should split the group into pairs or small teams and have them construct two scenarios that share the exact same logical structure—say, one agent, one victim, one avoidable harm, and one unavoidable cost—but differ only in framing variables such as physical proximity, direct bodily contact versus mechanical mediation, and the sequence in which information is revealed. Each team should present both versions, have the club vote on moral obligation in each case, and then only afterward learn that the logical skeleton was identical. Include a clear timeline for the ninety minutes, specific rules or payoffs that prevent people from noticing the symmetry too early, and a debrief that maps each stage onto the idea that pumps mislead through selective framing rather than logical force. Be frank about which feature of intuition-pump persuasion—perhaps the long-term entrenchment of a frame as "common sense"—this activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Please write your answer in British English, and take a committed position: defend one specific framing mechanism—whether spatial proximity, agent causation salience, or temporal ordering—as the primary driver of the misleading intuition, with explicit reasons, rather than presenting a balanced list of options.
