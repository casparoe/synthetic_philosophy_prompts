I've been working through Wittgenstein's remarks on religious belief for a seminar in philosophy of religion, and I have constructed an argument that seems to push his grammatical distinction far past its intended boundary—perhaps collapsing the very line he wants to defend between religious and empirical belief. Here are the steps as I currently have them.

(1) For Wittgenstein, religious belief is not a hypothesis about empirical states of affairs but expresses a commitment that regulates a form of life; its grammar is displayed in practice rather than justified by observational evidence.
(2) A belief counts as genuinely empirical if and only if it is in principle susceptible to rational revision by shared experience, and disagreement over it can in principle be settled by appeal to common observational criteria.
(3) Religious beliefs—say, belief in a Last Judgment—are not revisable in this way: they determine how any possible evidence is interpreted rather than being interpreted by evidence.
(4) Therefore religious beliefs are non-empirical; they belong to a different grammatical category from ordinary perceptual or scientific beliefs, such as the belief that water is boiling in a kettle.
(5) Now apply the same test to the political conviction that certain welfare recipients are undeserving poor who have violated conditions of reciprocity or workfare obligations. This conviction regulates social practices of exclusion and sanction; when confronted with structural barriers to employment, proponents tend to interpret that very evidence as further proof of moral failure rather than allowing it to overturn the framework.
(6) By direct parity with (3), the belief in undesert is also not revisable by shared experience and therefore not empirical.
(7) But the reasoning does not stop there. If grammatical fixity—being a framework that interprets evidence rather than being interpreted by it—disqualifies a belief from empirical status, then scientific paradigms, beliefs about other minds, and even ordinary perceptual judgments must suffer the same fate, since each involves background commitments that regulate how anomalies are handled.
(8) So by the criterion in (2), there are no genuinely empirical beliefs at all; every belief is a form of life.
(9) This conclusion is absurd: Wittgenstein clearly wants to preserve a sharp distinction between religious belief and empirical belief, and we ordinarily take scientific and perceptual beliefs to be genuinely empirical.

Where does the argument fail? I would prefer to keep (1) and (4) intact, since they capture the standard Wittgensteinian picture. But I also want to know whether the move from (3) to (5) involves a hidden equivocation—perhaps between being resistant to a particular kind of counter-evidence and being grammatically non-empirical in Wittgenstein's specific sense—or whether there is a scope or quantifier shift between the claim that a belief functions as an individual framework and the claim that it belongs to a grammatical category that excludes empirical status altogether. Which premise—(2), (5), (6), or (7)—is least costly to reject, and what exactly do we pay for giving it up?
