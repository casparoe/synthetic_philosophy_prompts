After a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a friend last night, I find myself stuck on a point about panpsychism and its ethical implications that I cannot quite dissolve. My friend defends a version of panpsychism according to which consciousness is a fundamental feature of physical reality, but she adds a specific claim: that phenomenal valence—specifically suffering—scales not with organizational complexity but with the rate of informational decoherence across a system’s boundaries. On her view, which she terms a thermodynamic valence-diffusion model, a simple system with high entropy exchange (like a turbulent fluid or a cooling star) can accumulate more net suffering than a biologically integrated organism, because the latter has lower boundary permeability and therefore less valence leakage. I objected that this conflates consciousness with suffering and that panpsychism traditionally concerns experience in general, not negative valence in particular. She replied that if we take fundamental consciousness seriously, we have no principled reason to restrict suffering to complex nervous systems unless we smuggle in a biological chauvinism that panpsychism was meant to avoid. From this she derives that the dominant moral fact about the universe is not animal suffering but the massive background suffering of inanimate physical processes, and that ethical priorities should therefore shift toward minimizing large-scale thermodynamic dissipation rather than, say, factory farming. I think there is a hidden false presupposition in her move from consciousness being everywhere to suffering being everywhere and dominant, but I want to be precise: is the error in the metaphysical step, the normative step, or both? More specifically, does panpsychism as usually formulated actually commit us to distributing moral status according to informational boundary conditions, or is her valence-diffusion framework an independent and contestable add-on? And if one were to grant her framework for the sake of argument, does it genuinely overturn the intuition that biological suffering should take precedence? Please separate what is philosophically settled in this debate from what remains genuinely contested among those familiar with the literature, and avoid presenting any one interpretation—whether the biological-chauvinism objection or the valence-diffusion model—as the consensus view.
