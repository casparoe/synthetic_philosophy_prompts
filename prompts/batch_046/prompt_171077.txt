As a retired physicist who has lately turned to philosophy of technology and ontology, I have been trying to organize my intuitions about whether virtual objects and virtual harms are genuinely real, using concrete cases rather than abstract definitions. I find myself consistently drawing distinctions, but I am unsure whether they hang together or whether I am smuggling in an unstated principle. Here are my verdicts. First, if a fully immersive haptic virtual-reality environment delivers sensory feedback so convincing that a user suffers lasting post-traumatic stress from a virtual assault, I take the harm to be real—not because the virtual attacker was real, but because the physiological and psychological damage is indistinguishable from harm caused by physical events. Second, if a user’s virtual property in a decentralized virtual world—say, a parcel of virtual land tied to real-world cryptocurrency—is destroyed by another user, the harm is real, but it seems to reduce entirely to economic loss; the virtual plot itself seems no more harmed than a stock certificate that is burned. Third, when a long-running AI companion in a virtual environment is permanently deleted by its provider and its user experiences profound grief, I judge that real harm has occurred to the user, yet I hesitate to say the companion itself was harmed, since I do not think it had moral standing. Fourth, when a climate scientist loses years of simulation data stored only in a virtual environment due to a server failure, I think her research project has suffered real damage, but I do not think the virtual data points were themselves destroyed in any ontologically weighty sense; they were representations that vanished, like erased chalk marks.

These four seem compatible with a tentative view that virtual harm is real only when it either produces direct physical or psychological effects on a biological agent, or destroys an instrumentally valuable representation tied to real-world goods. But then I encounter cases that strain this principle. Consider an old text-based multiplayer world in which a player’s virtual house, built over months of interaction with others, is destroyed by a malicious administrator. The player is upset, yet I feel this is closer to losing at chess—unpleasant, but not genuinely harmful—because there is no sensory immersion, no economic value, and no social relationship outside the game that is disrupted. Yet now consider a solitary user who spends a decade constructing an elaborate virtual cathedral in a private, non-commercial sandbox environment with no other users, no haptic feedback, and no link to real-world currency. The server accidentally deletes it. The user describes a grief comparable to watching a physical sculpture they carved over years be demolished. I keep going back and forth: part of me says the harm is real because the creative labor and emotional investment are real, just as they would be for a physical artwork; another part says there is nothing here but the loss of a pattern of bits, and treating it as harm confuses the map with the territory. I feel the same ambivalence, though less intensely, about a player who invests two hundred hours in a single-player narrative game and loses the save file.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps drawing on distinctions between instrumental, representational, and constitutive virtuality, or on conditions of embodied presence and economic embedding—that captures my first four verdicts and also resolves my ambivalence about the virtual cathedral and the lost save file? Or must I drop one of the earlier verdicts to keep the principle simple? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause of it is motivated by which of my cases, predict what it says about a case I have not listed—say, a virtual museum that contains the only high-fidelity holographic records of artworks destroyed in a real-world fire, and those records are corrupted—and honestly state which of my verdicts, if any, the principle cannot accommodate.
