I'm working through my commitments on consciousness for a graduate seminar and need a consistency audit before I present. Here is the package: (1) I am an illusionist about phenomenal consciousness—there are no intrinsic qualitative properties that escape physical or functional description, and reports of "what it is like" systematically misrepresent ordinary representational or attentional states. (2) I treat the hard problem as a pseudo-problem generated by a category mistake rather than by an ontological gap. (3) I maintain that first-person authority is generally reliable for beliefs, intentions, and representational contents, but I make a selective exception: phenomenal reports are confabulatory products of predictive processing in which low-level dynamics are misattributed to a fictional "inner feel," and authority does not extend to these misrepresentations. (4) I also hold that confabulation—of the sort seen in anosognosia or split-brain narrative construction—is typically a marker of cognitive impairment, not a feature of healthy minds. (5) Nevertheless, I think the universal phenomenal illusion is not a malfunction but an adaptive social fiction: it enables shared first-person narratives that coordinate group behavior, which explains why it is preserved rather than corrected. (6) On moral status, I deny that phenomenal consciousness is necessary; functional capacities such as reasoning and preference-formation are what matter. Yet I also think that because rich phenomenal reports reflect deeper confabulatory misattribution, beings with more elaborate "inner lives" may actually exhibit a specific deficit in self-monitoring.

Please audit this set for joint tenability. Which pairs pull against each other in a serious way, which tensions are only apparent and dissolve once the commitments are properly parsed, and what is the least costly revision for each genuine conflict? Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them uniformly. I am especially unsure whether my characterization of phenomenal reports as both pathological confabulation (4) and healthy adaptive fiction (5) can be sustained, and whether my selective suspension of first-person authority (3) undermines my broader endorsement of it. In your response, please clearly separate what is broadly settled in philosophy of mind and cognitive science from what remains genuinely contested among specialists—particularly regarding whether predictive-processing models can genuinely support illusionism—without presenting any contested interpretation as the consensus.
