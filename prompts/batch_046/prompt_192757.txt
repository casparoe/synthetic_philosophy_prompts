As a doctoral student in collective epistemology, I am preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for first-year philosophy PhD students who have basic training in propositional logic and voting theory but have not encountered judgment aggregation. I want to teach the discursive dilemma not as a technical variant of Condorcet-style jury paradoxes, but as a structural problem about whether a group can be said to hold a reasoned view when its reasoning path is itself the object of aggregation. To make this land without collapsing into familiar social-choice machinery, I want to frame the session around a niche distinction drawn from recent work on epistemic delegation: the idea that the dilemma becomes acute precisely when we treat the group not as an instrument that delivers a verdict, but as a subject that must be able to justify its conclusion by citing premises it collectively accepts.

Plan the explanatory arc for me. First, specify the dependency order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced: which distinction—say, between outcome consistency and procedural consistency, or between collective belief and collective reasoning—must be in place before the formal structure of the dilemma can be presented, and why reversing that order causes students to misidentify the problem as a glitch in majority rule rather than as a conflict in the logic of group justification. Second, design the one carrying example or scenario: it should involve a concrete decision context other than the standard courtroom jury, in which premises and a conclusion are logically interdependent propositions—state exactly what features the example must possess (e.g., transparent reasoning steps, logical independence of premises, public accessibility of each vote) and what it must lack (e.g., strategic voting, private information asymmetries, hierarchical authority) so that the dilemma emerges purely from aggregation logic rather than from hidden incentives or power dynamics. Third, map the predictable misconceptions in the order they will arise: for instance, the belief that switching to a different aggregation rule dissolves the paradox; the belief that procedural consistency is normatively trivial because we only care about correct outcomes; and the belief that this is merely Arrow’s impossibility theorem applied to premises—indicate at which point in the sequence each should be defused and with what counter-distinction or miniature example. Fourth, propose a concrete check for understanding that reveals whether the students have grasped that the dilemma concerns the coherence of collective reasoning rather than the empirical unreliability of majority voting—what task or question would expose that they still think the problem is solved by gathering more voters or better information? Finally, specify what formal apparatus should be openly left out at this level—such as the canonical impossibility result for premise-based aggregation, full axiomatic characterizations of aggregation rules, or connections to belief merging in computer science—and explain how I should signal that omission without implying that the problem remains unsolved or that I am hiding technical difficulties from them.
