I've been chewing on Strawson's Basic Argument in my philosophy of action course and I think I pushed it somewhere it shouldn't go. Here's the extension I came up with, and now it feels like it proves too much.

(1) Strawson says true moral responsability requires being the ultimate cause of your own mental nature — your character, values, the way you are — and since nothing can be causa sui, true moral responsibility is impossible. (2) Now take epistemic justification. If I'm genuinely justified in believing P, then I'm genuinely responsible for having formed that belief well rather than badly. But being genuinely responsible for forming beliefs well requires being genuinely responsible for the reasoning capacities I used. And being responsible for those capacities requires being their ultimate cause — having intentionally designed or selected them — which launches the same regress. So true epistemic justification also requires being causa sui about my cognitive nature. (3) Obviously no one designed their own brain from scratch, so by Strawson's reasoning no one is ever genuinely justified in anything. (4) But that collapses the distinction between rational and irrational belief-formation entirely. We couldn't say that someone who checks evidence is more justified than someone who flips a coin, because neither is the ultimate cause of their reasoning equipment. (5) So either we reject the claim that justification requires being the ultimate cause of one's cognitive capacities, or we accept global skeptical nihilism about justification. (6) If we reject (2) — the analogy between moral and epistemic responsibility — we need to explain why moral responsibility is special. But if we keep the analogy, Strawson's argument doesn't just kill moral blame; it kills the very idea that anyone can reason well.

Where does this slip? Which step should I give up, and what's the price? I kind of want to keep (1) because the regress seems real, but I'm not sure the epistemic parallel holds water. What's the standard move here — denying the analogy, or saying Strawson's original principle was too strong all along?
