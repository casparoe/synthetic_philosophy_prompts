Design a 75-minute seminar activity for 14 upper-division philosophy-of-mind students on epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment. Frame it as a fictional clinical simulation called the Silent-Partner Reporting Protocol. Divide students into groups of three with rotating roles: Experiencer, Neural Reporter, and Auditor. The Experiencer draws a hidden phenomenal-state card (e.g., sharp metallic taste, low droning) and is instructed that this state is causally inert: it cannot influence speech, gesture, or report. The Neural Reporter independently draws a physically correlated brain-state card from a deck with 70% match-rate; this card alone determines the written judgment on a report form. The Auditor sees only the form and must guess the phenomenal state and rate confidence. Run Round 1 with a perfectly correlated deck so judgments track experience; run Round 2 with 30% mismatches, so the Reporter writes false judgments while the Experiencer privately sees the error but is rule-bound silent. Use a non-monetary payoff: groups earn points for Auditor accuracy, but Experiencers and Reporters earn hidden bonuses only when correlation holds, creating tension between tracking and causation. After both rounds, hold a 20-minute debrief mapping Round 1 to the appearance of phenomenal knowledge, Round 2 to the causal-exclusion problem, and the Experiencer’s helpless awareness to the paradox of phenomenal judgment—specifically, that judgments are physically caused yet seem about experiences they do not cause. State what will predictably fail: students will try to let the Experiencer signal, or will argue that awareness implies the power to correct the report; counter this by enforcing strict silence and adding a “correction round” in which any amended report is also dictated by a fresh brain-state card. Finally, be explicit about the simulation’s limit: it artificially splits one physical system into separate “experience” and “report” modules, whereas real epiphenomenalism assigns both to the same brain, so the activity cannot reproduce the actual metaphysical claim that a single causal chain produces both the phenomenal state and the judgment without interaction between them.
