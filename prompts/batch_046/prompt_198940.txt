ok so i was at this writers retreat in vermont and got into it with another novelist, lets call her M. we were arguing about that norwegian prison Halden, the one everyone writes about, with the kitchens and the guards in jeans. the arguement went like this: (1) M said the whole point is that treating people like humans actually works — recidivism is lower, the prison is quiet, and the architecture is designed so everyone cooks together and there's no spectacle of suffering. she said american mass incarceration fails precisely because it makes punishment into a theater of cruelty, which just produces more cruelty. (2) i pushed back that she's confusing comfort with justice. i introduced this idea i made up called 'penal transparency' — the claim that a democratic society needs its punishment to be visably distinct from ordinary life so citizens can understand what the state is doing in their name. if prison looks like a dorm, the public loses the ability to consent to it meaningfully, because the boundary between freedom and unfreedom gets blurred. (3) she said that's just aesthetic politics, and that the real danger is 'rehabilitative net-widening' — when prisons get nicer, the moral cost of locking people up drops, so the state starts putting more people in them. she said Halden might actually be worse for democracy because it hides the carceral state behind good design and soft furniture. (4) i told her she's made prison reform logically impossible: if making prisons better is bad because it expands them, and making them worse is bad because it's cruel, then there's no coherent position except abolition, which she doesn't actually support. please adjudicate move by move — rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and tell me what both of us are missing. and please, no reading lists, i'm writing fiction here not a dissertation.
