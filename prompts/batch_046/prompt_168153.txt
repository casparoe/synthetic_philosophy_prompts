Write a dialogue between Dr. Althea Vance, who holds that meritocratic desert survives only if it can be traced to effort-adjusted contributions to a cooperative surplus, and Dr. Marcus Chen, who maintains that every desert claim is a retrospective rationalisation of an already-fixed distribution of institutional power. The setting is not a seminar room but a concrete institutional dilemma: a research council has begun allocating grants through a proprietary predictive model that scores "potential impact" without revealing its training features or weightings to applicants. Vance should defend the view that, provided the algorithm genuinely tracks intellectual contribution rather than demographic proxy, its opacity does not vitiate desert; the reward still flows to merit, merely mediated by an epistemically opaque instrument. Chen should reply that opacity severs the agential link required for desert to be intelligible—if a researcher cannot know which of her capacities were causally efficacious, she cannot claim authorship of her success, and the institution has simply substituted one form of structural luck for another. Their exchange should be rigorously back-and-forth, with each speaker addressing the other’s actual premises rather than retreating to general slogans about equality or excellence.

In particular, the dialogue should probe whether algorithmic allocation introduces a novel species of "institutional luck" that renders pre-institutional talent morally irrelevant, or whether it merely renders visible the structural mediation that was always present in peer review and patronage. Both interlocutors should concede points where warranted—Vance might acknowledge that opacity risks corruption by hidden proxies, while Chen might grant that complete transparency is neither possible nor necessarily just—without either collapsing into the other’s position. The tone should be formal and analytical, written in British English, with no request for bibliographies, citations, or external sources.
