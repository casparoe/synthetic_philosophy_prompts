I'm prepping a grad seminar on philosophy of biology and we've been circling the old DNA-is-code analogy. You know the one—Crick and the central dogma crowd, then everyone extends it to say gene editing is basically debugging and synthetic biology is just rewriting source code. In my circles people use it to argue we should be relaxed about editing, like we're just fixing syntax errors in a program that was never designed anyway. But I'm trying to figure out how far the mapping actually holds before I let my students run with it.

So can you lay out the correspondance table? What in the genome plays the role of the compiler, the runtime enviroment, the programmer, syntax errors, deprecated functions? Then where does it break? Is there anything like a syntax error that selection doesn't just edit out? Does the genome have dead code that actually does hidden work we haven't mapped? And here's the part I'm stuck on: if there's no external programmer, isn't the genome more like a self-modifying script running in a distributed evolutionary system? My colleague made that move and said it kills the debugging framing for gene therapy—if there's no intended design, then fixing a mutation is just imposing a human preference on an open-ended process. Does that rescue the analogy or destroy it? I want the disanalogies that actually matter for ethics—does the relaxed it's-just-software conclusion survive once you see what's missing?
