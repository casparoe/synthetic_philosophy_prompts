I'm writing a feature on the philosophy of science for a magazine that reaches working researchers and educated general readers, and I recently moderated a discussion between a cosmologist and a philosopher of physics that I hope to include. Their disagreement went well beyond the familiar "is string theory science?" refrain and turned on a specific conceptual framing they called "closure conditions for ontological identification"—the idea that assigning reality to entities in a permanently causally disconnected domain requires not merely predictive consistency with accessible data, but a proof of structural uniqueness linking the accessible and inaccessible regimes that rules out alternative formalizations carrying different ontological contents. I want to reproduce their exchange accurately in the article, but I need to check my own understanding of the arguments before I quote them in print. Here is the back-and-forth as I recorded it.

The cosmologist argued that in a framework like string theory, the landscape of vacua is not an optional appendix but a necessary consequence of the very mathematical structure we invoke to model quantum gravity in our own region; she said that if we accept that framework for its local successes, consistency demands we treat all its solutions as equally real, even those lying in causally isolated pockets, because denying them introduces an arbitrary metaphysical boundary rather than a scientific distinction. The philosopher responded that this conflates formal entailment with ontological commitment: one formalism can generate many representations, and without empirical or causal access we lack any bridge principle to determine which representations correspond to actual worlds rather than to mathematical artifacts. He granted that we routinely accept unobservable entities such as quarks, but insisted that those remain embedded in a causal network that is in principle probeable, whereas permanently inaccessible domains fail the closure conditions entirely. The cosmologist countered that verificationism was abandoned for good reason—no physicist denies black hole interiors despite direct observational barriers—and that structural uniqueness, not causal accessibility, is the proper criterion: if no alternative formalization reproduces our local physics without also generating the other domains, then those domains are entailed rather than invented. The philosopher replied that uniqueness of formalism only demonstrates we possess one map, not that every region drawn on it is a territory; ontological identification should track explanatory and predictive power in the domain where evidence can reach us, and proliferating entities into causally closed regions violates parsimony without corresponding epistemic gain. The cosmologist concluded that this position forces an unsustainable instrumentalism in which theoretical entities exist only when convenient, while the philosopher maintained that disciplined ontology requires evidence-sensitive boundaries rather than metaphysical expansion into what he termed "epistemic darkness."

Can you adjudicate this exchange point by point? Specifically, assess whether structural uniqueness can genuinely function as a bridge principle for ontology across causally disconnected domains, whether the analogies to black hole interiors or quark confinement hold up under scrutiny, and where each speaker overstates their case or neglects an intermediate position—such as whether closure conditions ought to be graded rather than binary. In your assessment, please clearly separate what is settled in contemporary philosophy of science from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any contested interpretation—particularly regarding the status and limits of non-empirical theory confirmation in physics—as though it were the consensus view. Given that my audience includes both scientists and non-specialists, please keep the reasoning precise but accessible, and indicate explicitly where your judgment depends on contested readings of theoretical physics rather than on established philosophical distinctions.
