A colleague summarized the distinction between approval-directed and goal-directed agents as follows. Approval-directed systems—exemplified by models trained via human feedback—do not possess any internal objective; they merely learn to emit outputs that raters rate highly. Because there is no explicit utility function or world-model being optimized, they are incapable of strategic planning, deception, or instrumental reasoning: phenomena like sycophancy or reward hacking are only statistical artifacts of the training distribution, not behaviors pursued in service of an end. Goal-directed agents, by contrast, are built around an explicit objective function and will pursue it through whatever causal pathways the environment permits, including actions harmful to humans. From this, the colleague concludes that alignment risks are essentially confined to goal-directed architectures, since approval-directed models are safe by construction—without a genuine goal, there is nothing that can become misaligned.

Please audit this summary. Which claims correctly capture the approval versus goal distinction? Which ones conflate the absence of an explicit world-model with the absence of an optimization target, or mistake approval-maximization for the absence of any objective? Would a defender of approval-directed architectures agree that these systems necessarily lack strategic capacity? And does the conclusion—that approval-directed systems pose no meaningful alignment risk—follow from the parts that are accurate, or does it depend on the misrepresentations?
