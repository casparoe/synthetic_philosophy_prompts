The exam question was: Benacerraf's epistemological challenge to mathematical Platonism turns on the tension between a causal theory of knowledge and our apparent knowledge of causally inert abstract objects. In recent seminar discussion we considered whether this challenge can be met by combining ante rem structuralism with a cognitive-scientific claim that human numerical cognition tracks structural relations via approximate magnitude representations in the intraparietal sulcus, rather than through direct causal interaction with objects. In no more than 600 words, (a) set out Benacerraf's dilemma with precision; (b) explain whether this structuralist-cum-cognitive proposal genuinely dissolves it; and (c) identify one serious difficulty that remains for the account.

The student's answer (c. 520 words):

Benacerraf's dilemma arises because mathematical objects, if they exist as abstract entities, lie outside the causal order. Since knowledge requires a causal connection between believer and object—at least on a standard causal theory—we cannot know mathematical truths if Platonism is true.

The structuralist-cum-cognitive proposal offers a different picture. Rather than treating numbers as independent objects, ante rem structuralism holds that mathematics describes structures that exist in their own right. The cognitive component adds that humans do not need causal contact with these structures; instead, our intraparietal sulcus generates approximate magnitude representations that allow us to grasp structural relations indirectly. Because these representations are physically instantiated in neural tissue, the causal theory is satisfied at the level of cognition, and the ontological problem disappears.

This position is essentially the same as Hartry Field's fictionalism, which also replaces real mathematical objects with useful representations constructed for empirical science. Both views avoid ontological commitment and rely on physical cognitive processes.

A remaining problem is that the structuralist must still account for the necessity of mathematical truths. If structures exist independently and are grasped only via approximate neural representations, it is unclear how we achieve knowledge of exact modal properties—such as the fact that 7 is necessarily prime—rather than merely approximate correlates. Thus Benacerraf's challenge returns in a different form: we may have causal access to representations, but not to the precise structural properties themselves.

Overall, the proposal is interesting but conflates epistemology with cognitive psychology in a way that does not fully address the original dilemma.
