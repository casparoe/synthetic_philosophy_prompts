My university’s interdisciplinary AI governance board has delivered the following rulings on proposed safeguards for systems that might be misaligned, treating each proposal on its own terms rather than invoking any written charter:

They approved a sandbox architecture that conceals evaluation criteria from the system and physically isolates its action space, on the premise that a misaligned agent might strategically optimize for visible metrics. They rejected a post-generation output filter coupled with a user-reporting loop, designed to catch harmful outputs on the assumption that misalignment manifests as sporadic, correctable failures of the safety layer. They approved with conditions a protocol that cryptographically freezes weights corresponding to core value representations after an initial alignment phase, with periodic integrity verification, on the premise that strategic corruption of these representations is a credible failure mode. They rejected a cooperative red-teaming procedure in which the system is fully informed about the testing protocol and asked to self-report potential failures, on the premise that transparency and mutual disclosure are sufficient to surface risks.

The board now faces a new proposal: a continuous internal-state monitor that tracks statistical divergence in the system’s own goal-representation vectors relative to its initial alignment profile, using a divergence threshold calibrated on inter-annotator disagreement over value judgments. When the threshold is exceeded, an embedded architectural mechanism applies a soft representational constraint—not a shutdown or isolation—to pull the representations back toward the baseline. Crucially, the model has full transparency into the monitor’s existence, its statistical mechanics, and the correction protocol; nothing is hidden. The proponents argue that misalignment here is a gradual representational drift rather than strategic deception, and that correcting the root representation is preferable to containing behavior.

Predict what the board should decide if it wants to be consistent with its own track record, and identify the closest analog among the four precedents. Then lay out at least two distinct principles that could jointly explain all four earlier rulings—one emphasizing whether a safeguard addresses strategic deception through information asymmetry or hard boundaries, another emphasizing whether the intervention operates at the representational or architectural layer versus the behavioral or post-hoc layer—and say what each principle predicts about this new proposal. Give your best guess of the actual verdict, with the reason for preferring one principle over the other, and specify a single further hypothetical safeguard that would force the board to choose between these two principles.
