The heterogeneity problem for hedonism—whether every pleasure shares some common phenomenal feature that distinguishes it from pain or neutrality—has, in recent philosophy of value and mind, hardened into a standoff that neither introspective report nor neuroimaging seems able to break. On one side, phenomenal uniformitarians maintain that tasting wine, solving a proof, and feeling sunlight on skin all contain a single, isolable “hedonic tone,” an affective atom that makes them pleasures regardless of their intentional content or causal role; on the other side, heterogeneity pluralists deny any such shared qualitative core and treat “pleasure” as a functional family-resemblance concept united only by dispositions to seek repetition, reinforce behavior, and carry positive valence, allowing the subjective textures to differ radically. The debate looks empirical—perhaps a shared nucleus accumbens or insular signature could settle it—but neither camp accepts neural correlation as decisive, and both sides endlessly reinterpret the same introspective data. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not factual but structural: the uniformitarians are asking a metaphysical question about the decomposability of conscious affect, while the pluralists are asking a conceptual question about the conditions for falling under one evaluative kind, and they share neither a theory of phenomenal transparency nor an account of how introspection relates to classification. Diagnose this disagreement using what each side actually says about introspection, functional role, and value comparison. Is it verbal—are they using “pleasure” for different targets? Substantive but masked—does one side depend on an undefended commitment to phenomenal atomism or to the transparency of affect? Are they answering different questions—one about the ontology of feeling, one about the logic of evaluation—under a single heading? Or is the impasse bedrock, driven by incompatible intuitions about whether feeling can be decomposed? Then specify what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal model of affective vector space that replaces atomism with dimensional structure; a distinction between “hedonic vector” and “phenomenal tone”; an argument that the question is ill-posed because “common feature” ambiguates between qualitative identity and functional equivalence; or a frank admission that no empirical result or conceptual distinction could move it, and why. Be concrete about the form that progress would take and how it addresses the stall rather than merely restarting the cycle.
