Next week I present to a philosophy of decision-theory seminar my view that the Pasadena paradox—structured so that payoffs depend on the parity of an infinite fair-coin sequence, with positive partial expectations diverging to positive infinity and negative partial expectations diverging to negative infinity, leaving the overall expectation undefined in both directions—should not be treated as a failure of expected-utility theory to be patched by bounded utility, risk aversion, or non-Archimedean valuation. Instead, I argue that undefined expectation is itself a normative category: when no linear summation order of outcomes converges, there is no fact of the matter about the gamble's value, and rational choice requires either an explicit contractual specification of evaluation order or refusal to enter. Central to my argument is what I call summation-order neutrality—the principle that if every admissible reordering of outcomes yields a different expectation, then no ordering may be treated as normatively privileged without explicit justification.

The audience will include two formal decision theorists who defend continuity-based resolutions of St. Petersburg variants and regard undefined expectations as artifacts of improper limit construction; one philosopher of mathematics who works with nonstandard analysis and believes Pasadena-type divergences are always resolvable by appropriate hyperreal utility scales; one cognitive scientist who studies actual human evaluation of long-shot risks and will ask whether my claim has empirical purchase; and a senior philosopher specializing in normative uncertainty who suspects that refusing to choose under undefined expectation collapses into an overly conservative form of maximin.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as each of these audience members would actually phrase them. For each objection: what I should concede without weakening my core claim, what I should hold firm on, the weakest point in my reply, and how to defend that weak point in the limited time available for seminar responses. Rank them by how likely they are to arise and how damaging they are to my central thesis that undefined expectation marks a genuine normative boundary rather than a modeling failure. Finally, include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—specifically, the charge that if summation-order neutrality prohibits choice whenever reorderings produce divergent expectations, then it also prohibits ordinary portfolio decisions under continuous heavy-tailed distributions, rendering my principle paralyzing rather than illuminating—and tell me whether that charge sticks and where my reply is most vulnerable. I want this as a prep sheet, not a polished defense of my view.
