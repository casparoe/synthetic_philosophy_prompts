Construct a taxonomy of philosophical positions on the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism, presented entirely in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Structure it as a branching tree that begins with the root question: may a moral theory ever justify reducing one person's welfare in order to increase the aggregate welfare of others? From that root, divide positions first by whether the core problem is the mathematical aggregation of utility across distinct lives, the instrumental treatment of one individual for the benefit of others, or some interaction of both. Then branch by whether the objection applies only to direct actions or also to omissions and to institutional designs that predictably produce sacrifice without assigning it to any agent. Add a further split on whether aggregation becomes permissible behind a veil of ignorance or behind impersonal rules that no one directly enforces. At each leaf, provide either the recognized name of the position or mark it unnamed, followed by one sentence describing its most awkward commitment—for instance, having to reject all life-saving redistribution of organs, or being unable to explain why letting five drown is worse than actively harming one. Identify any combinations in the logical space that remain unoccupied and briefly diagnose whether they are incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Specifically, ensure the tree separates the view that rejects all interpersonal summation from the view that permits weighted summation but forbids direct sacrifice, and flag any pair of positions—such as certain contractualist and Kantian variants—that your branches cannot genuinely distinguish.
