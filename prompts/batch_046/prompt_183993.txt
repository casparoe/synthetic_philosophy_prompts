okay so I was trying to formalize the demandingness objection and I think I built a reductio that actually proves consequentialism is not just hard but incoherent, which cant be right. here's the chain:

(1) consequentialism says we should maximize welfare with no agent-relative cutoff.
(2) if there's no cutoff, then every resource I hold back — money, time, emotional energy, even cognitive bandwidth — is a failure to help.
(3) but "resource" here has to cover mental states too: if I spend an hour grieving my dead friend instead of counseling strangers, that's an hour of welfare-promotion lost.
(4) so consequentialism requires that I never prioritize my own grief, my own relationships, or my own mental health unless doing so produces more welfare elsewhere.
(5) now here's the part that feels wrong: a psychology that treats all suffering as equally urgent and all personal attachments as oppertunity costs isn't just exhausting — it's unstable. people who actually try this (effective altruists working on global health) report burnout, detachment, and eventually reduced capacity to help at all.
(6) so consequentialism requires a motivational structure that undermines itself: the closer you get to complying, the less able you are to comply.
(7) therefore consequentialism is self-defeating in the same way a car that requires going 200mph to reach its destination is self-defeating — it prescribes a state that makes the prescription impossible to follow.
(8) but if (8) is true then every demanding morality collapses, since any serious moral demand can be pushed to this kind of psychologcal breaking point.

where does this go wrong? I'd really like to keep (1) and (3) — the idea that emotional resources count — but I dont want to end up saying consequentialism is literally incoherent. is the slip in (5) treating empirical burnout as conceptual impossibility? or is there an equivocation in (6) between "hard to sustain" and "self-undermining"? I want to know exactly which step to reject and what it costs me.
