I'm teaching a seminar on political philosophy this term, and over coffee two of my grad students — Jordan and Sam — got into it about the paradox of tolerence applied to this campus group that pushes "intellectual segregation." Not violence, just actively discouraging people from engaging certain viewpoints in seminars and putting out lists of "unacceptable" readings they want professors to drop from syllabi. I stayed out of it, but I'd like your take on the actual exchange.

(1) Jordan said this is exactly the paradox: if the university tolerates a group whose practice is restructuring discourse norms so some views can't be heard, open inquiry eventually collapses. He called this "structural intolerance" — not just expressing dislike, but reorganizing institutions to exclude — and said the university should be intolerant of them for that reason.

(2) Sam replied Jordan is conflating tolerating people with tolerating every effect of their beliefs. The group isn't using coercion, just social pressure and organizing their own spaces. By that logic any strong advocacy that makes people uncomfortable should be banned, which is itself intolerant. The paradox only applies to groups that actively suppress rights, not ones that organize differently.

(3) Jordan pushed back: it's not mere self-segregation. They publish lists and pressure profs to drop readings — that's reshaping the curriculum, not opting out. He insisted there's a real distinction between "performative intolerance" (saying you hate a view) and "structural intolerance" (changing rules so the view can't be presented). Only the latter triggers the paradox, and this group is clearly structural.

(4) Sam said Jordan invented that split to justify silencing views he dislikes. There's no principled line — it's just rhetoric. Suppressing them uses institutional power against democratic, non-violent dissent, which is exactly the kind of intolerance Popper warned about. Jordan's reasoning is circular: "I should be intolerant of whatever I think will eventually make me intolerant."

Can you go through their exchange move by move and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification? Also say who's missing what — does Jordan's structural/performative split actually hold water, or is Sam right that it's a smokescreen? Don't just declare an overall winner; adjudicate the specific points. And please, don't give me a bibliography or cite specific papers — I want you to work through the argument yourself, not pretend you've read some obscure article I haven't assigned.
