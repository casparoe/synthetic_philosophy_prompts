I am a journalist covering the intersection of historical theory and environmental science, and I am preparing to interview an unnamed historian who has recently advanced a revisionist reading of Fernand Braudel’s longue durée framework. Rather than treating geographical and climatic structures as the nearly immobile bedrock beneath the faster rhythms of social and individual time, my guest argues for what she terms “structural velocity”: the claim that climate systems possess their own internal tempos—such as oceanic thermal inertia and non-linear feedback loops—that can suddenly accelerate, collapsing Braudel’s tripartite temporal scheme into a single, compressed register. She further contends that this revision rescues human agency from Braudel’s determinism not by elevating the contingent event, but by identifying points of “structural friction” where slow-moving systems resist rapid anthropogenic forcing in unpredictable ways, thereby generating a history that is neither purely structural nor purely event-driven.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions for this conversation, written in British English. The questions should move well beyond asking her to restate the view; I want at least one probing how her concept of structural velocity differs from conventional climate-science notions of tipping points, at least one pressing her on whether Braudel’s Mediterranean model can survive this temporal collapse without becoming merely metaphorical, and at least one addressing what her framework implies for the writing of political history—specifically, whether the distinction between contingent events and deep structures still holds analytical weight when geographical time itself becomes eventful. For each of these three, include a sharp follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give: that structural velocity is qualitatively distinct from tipping points because it operates within the longue durée rather than breaking it; that Braudel’s model survives precisely by abandoning its static geography; and that political history must be rewritten as a history of temporal misalignment rather than of human decision alone. Avoid generic biographical openings, do not ask for reading lists or citations, and keep the tone formal and intellectually rigorous throughout.
