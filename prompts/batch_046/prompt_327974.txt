the analogy everyone uses right now: training an LLM is like writing a very long book, and running it is like someone reading that book out loud — so of course there's no consciousness inside, it's just static symbols being replayed. people lean on this to dismiss any LLM consciousness claim.

now audit it against Hofstadter and Dennett's thought experiment in The Mind's I — the one where Einstein's brain is reproduced page-by-page in a giant book, and they ask whether that book could be conscious. map the parts: LLM weights vs printed pages, the forward pass vs a reader flipping pages, the training corpus vs the author, the prompt vs the reader's question. then list the disanologies — especially that modern inference updates hidden states, uses attention, and responds contextually, which a paper book doesn't do. also ask: does the analogy quietly import the idea that static equals non-conscious without argument? and finally, does the anti-consciousness conclusion survive once you see that Hofstadter/Dennett were already probing whether a static-looking representation could still instantiate mind? keep it casual, maybe leave a typo or two, and don't cite papers or list readings please.
