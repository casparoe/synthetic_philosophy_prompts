I serve on the clinical ethics review board at a regional medical center, and over the past two years we have seen a rising number of requests for what patients call “cognitive baseline restoration”—interventions ranging from sustained-release stimulant regimens to targeted neurostimulation intended to return a patient to a prior level of executive function or working memory. The difficulty is that many of these patients are functioning within standard population ranges, yet they report that their current capacities fall well below their own historical baselines established before a mild neurological event, prolonged sleep deprivation, or years of high-cognitive-demand professional work. At the same time, the collective cognitive demands of educational and occupational environments have shifted upward in ways that make a once-adequate baseline feel like a deficit, a phenomenon my colleagues have begun calling baseline drift. I need a reusable decision procedure—an ordered checklist with explicit stop conditions and judgment-call flags—that my three-person board can apply in about forty-five minutes per case to determine whether an intervention should be classified as therapy (restoring a legitimate baseline) or enhancement (exceeding it), and therefore whether it falls within our mandate to approve without escalation.

The procedure must be grounded in the therapy/enhancement distinction as it actually operates philosophically, not as a slogan, and it must survive simplification into practical steps without collapsing into “whatever the patient wants.” Specifically, it should disentangle three reference points: the species-typical functioning range, the patient’s documented personal-historical baseline prior to decline, and the current collective normative baseline demanded by the patient’s actual social and occupational environment. For each reference point, include a concrete evidentiary test—what documentation counts as sufficient, what counts as an outlier, and where empirical fact runs out and an irreducible value judgment takes over. The deliverable should list the ordered questions, state the conditions for returning “approve as therapy,” “approve as enhancement (outside mandate),” or “refer to values deliberation,” and flag every point where normative choice replaces rule application. Then apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a surgeon requesting stimulant restoration of pre-burnout working memory that exceeds current population norms; (2) a graduate student seeking the same intervention after a concussion, where her pre-injury baseline was itself in the 95th percentile; and (3) a case where the procedure yields an answer the board will likely reject—perhaps a patient whose personal baseline never exceeded species-typical ranges but who argues that collective normative drift now makes restoration to that old level functionally necessary for employment. Finally, state explicitly what kinds of cases the procedure cannot settle and where it will systematically misfire.
