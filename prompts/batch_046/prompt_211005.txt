I am working through a generalized reading of van Fraassen's Reflection principle that extends it from first-order credences to second-order confidence in one's own disciplinary competence. The idea is this: if I currently assign high credence to a claim in, say, philosophy of science, but I can identify a class of structurally analogous claims about which I have previously been highly confident and later significantly downgraded, then diachronic coherence does not merely permit me to anticipate future revision—it requires me to apply a present discount factor to my current confidence, scaled by the observed base rate of such revisions in my epistemic history. This is meant to be a calibrated form of expert humility rather than blanket skepticism, but I worry it collapses into the latter if every specialist must always hedge by their personal error rate.

What would genuinely count against this position? I want the arguments, formal results, or empirical observations that should move me, arranged from decisive to merely awkward: for instance, proofs that Reflection applies only to object-level beliefs and breaks down at the meta-level; studies of expert revision patterns that show they are domain-specific rather than structurally analogous; cases where rational agents should maintain confidence despite knowing they have erred in similar domains; and whether the similarity metric can be gerrymandered to protect the view from counterexample. I also want to know what defenders of the standard principle would themselves concede as damaging, whether the view can be insulated by qualification without becoming unfalsifiable, and if such insulation is a defect for a norm of rational belief rather than a virtue.
