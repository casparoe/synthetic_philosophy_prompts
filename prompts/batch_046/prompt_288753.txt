I'm revising a handout for an advanced philosophy-of-mind seminar that treats self-deception not as a historical problem but as a live question about the architecture of belief, intention, and first-person authority. During a recent discussion with colleagues, we kept returning to the same obstacle: most existing quizzes sort people by the philosopher they sound like rather than by what they actually commit to when pressed on concrete cases. So I want you to build a decision tree—structured as a taxonomy with branch nodes phrased as answerable questions and descriptive leaf positions—that maps the logical space of whether self-deception is genuinely possible, independent of named theories.

Start from the root scenario: a subject sincerely avows belief P, behaves consistently with belief not-P, and when confronted with the contradiction offers explanations that sound rational but systematically avoid the damaging evidence—imagine an archivist who curates her own files so thoroughly that she never encounters the documents that would undermine her avowed view. From this root, construct branches on three successive commitments. First, ask whether doxastic transparency—roughly, that one cannot simultaneously hold a belief and genuinely take oneself not to hold it—is absolute or can be locally suspended without global mental fragmentation. Second, ask whether intentional self-manipulation of belief requires occurrent awareness of the manipulation at the time it occurs, using the archivist case as the concrete test. Third, ask whether sustaining the contradiction demands literally false beliefs or can operate through "epistemic insulation," a strategically arranged ignorance that never becomes an explicit contradictory judgment but still functions to protect the avowed belief. Each leaf should carry a descriptive name (e.g., "hard impossibilism," "partitioned-mind possibility," "motivated irrationality without false belief," "performative self-fiction," "unnamed/unoccupied") plus one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. Build the tree from the logical combinations first, then place actual positions in the space; where a combination is unoccupied, briefly diagnose whether it looks incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag any pair of leaves—particularly the partitioned-mind view and a refined strategic-ignorance view—that the tree cannot cleanly separate using only behavioral or first-person evidence, and do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or references to specific texts.
