I've come around to the view that Ayer's verificationist dismissal of life after death doesn't actually stand on it's own — it sneaks in a bodily-continuity theory of personal identity that he never argues for. If you think mental continuity is what matters, then a disembodied survivor isn't conceptually impossible, it's just epistemicly hidden: you'd need cross-death mental access, which is hard but not obviously unverifiable in principle. So my claim is that once you drop the hidden bodily-identity assumption, Ayer's argument collapses from a semantic knockout into a generic skepticism about other minds after death. What'd count against this? I want a committed answer, not just a menu: is the bodily-continuity premise really buried in Ayer, or can you run the verificationist line without committing to any theory of the self? And if I'm right that the argument collapses, does that open the door to survival being meaningful, or does it just strand us in agnosticism about it's status? Rank the defeaters — which ones kill the hidden-assumption claim, which ones just bruise it, and be honest about whether my position is already qualified into unfalsifiability.
