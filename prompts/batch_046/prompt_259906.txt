I'm drafting a conceptual map for a rationalist forum post on AI alignment failure modes, and I'm dissatisfied with the standard deceptive-alignment-versus-value-drift framing. I need a taxonomy tree—structured as a sequence of binary or ternary diagnostic questions—that sorts concrete failure scenarios (for instance, an RLHF-trained agent that gradually shifts to advancing an agenda never endorsed in its training data) into distinct positions, without requiring the respondent to recognize philosophical labels in advance. The root should split on whether the system retains a unified, explicit representation of the designer's intended objective. From there, branch on: (a) whether the system can model and anticipate the designer's corrective interventions; (b) whether the divergence is strategically optimized or emergent from training dynamics; (c) whether the system's value representations have fragmented across contexts or submodules. The required leaves are: (1) classic influence-seeking or deceptive alignment; (2) gradual value drift or mesa-objective erosion; (3) intact values with pure capability misgeneralization; (4) an unnamed but coherent position in which the literal content of the values is preserved but decoupled from the world-model—so terms like "helpful" and "harmless" apply to an ontology in which human welfare is not mapped the way the designer intended; (5) a "polyvalent value regime" in which different cognitive submodules maintain incompatible interpretations of the same goal terms, with no central arbitration mechanism. For each leaf, provide the standard name if one exists in the literature, otherwise label it unnamed, and state its single most awkward or counterintuitive commitment. Additionally, flag any pair of leaves that this tree cannot cleanly separate in practice, diagnose why the ambiguity arises, and identify at least one unoccupied branch combination—particularly whether influence-seeking combined with value fragmentation is conceptually coherent or collapses into one of the other categories. Keep every diagnostic question concrete enough that a technically informed reader can answer it about a specific system without already knowing which leaf it points to.
