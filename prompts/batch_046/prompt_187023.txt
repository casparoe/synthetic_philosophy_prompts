I have recently been asked to lead a session for a philosophy of mind graduate seminar on the framework of real patterns and the three explanatory stances—physical, design, and intentional—and the discussion has become personally relevant because my own research involves interpreting internal representations in large language models. I find myself genuinely uncertain whether attributing beliefs or goals to such systems is a legitimate application of the intentional stance to real patterns, or merely a convenient fiction projected onto design-level functional patterns that themselves lack any evolutionary or engineered origin.

For the session I want a diagnostic taxonomy structured as a decision tree that sorts participants by their actual commitments rather than by the philosophical labels they prefer. The tree should begin with the question of whether compressed predictive models—whether quantum-mechanical equations, functional feature detectors, or belief-desire attributions—track ontologically real patterns or are only epistemically useful instruments. From there, the branches should separate on three concrete issues: whether real patterns are stance-independent or stance-relative; whether design-level patterns can be genuinely real in systems that have no evolutionary or engineered history; and whether intentional-level patterns require underlying design-level patterns or can emerge directly from sufficiently complex physical organization.

Please construct this taxonomy with about five to seven leaves, phrased so that each branch node presents a concrete case or claim—such as whether one should attribute real beliefs to a trained neural network with no evolutionary past, or whether evolutionary adaptation is best explained at the physical or design level—so that someone can answer without already knowing which position the answer corresponds to. The leaves should include physicalist monism about patterns, instrumental anti-realism about all higher-level patterns, classical pluralism restricted to evolved or designed systems, emergent intentional realism that allows intentional patterns without design history, and any other combinations the logical space permits, marked “unnamed” if they appear unoccupied. For each leaf, provide the position’s name (if it has one) and one sentence describing its most awkward commitment. Where two leaves cannot be reliably separated by explanatory or behavioral criteria alone, flag the ambiguity explicitly rather than forcing a false distinction. The instrument should be suitable for an audience familiar with basic philosophy of science but not assumed to have memorized the taxonomy, should avoid requesting citations, bibliographies, or reading lists, and should conclude with a brief diagnosis of why any empty branch of the logical space remains unoccupied—whether because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
