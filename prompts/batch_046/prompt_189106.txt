I'm preparing a one-semester dissertation prospectus on collective moral responsibility, and I want to turn a half-formed idea into a formal research proposal. The idea comes from discussing with colleagues whether the Fischer controversy does more than revise the historical record: if Fischer's archival work shows that German decision-making in 1914 was structurally fragmented—military, diplomatic, and bureaucratic nodes pursuing overlapping but non-identical aims—then the familiar moral question "was Germany to blame for the war?" may rest on a false presupposition, namely that there was a unified national agent with a coherent intention to be blamed. I want the proposal to ask a precise conceptual question: can there be a coherent form of national-level responsibility that does not require a shared collective intention or a single author of the decision, and if so, what is its normative structure—structural culpability, distributed complicity, or something else? Existing philosophical accounts of collective responsibility tend to split between corporate-agency models (which require unified intentions that the Fischer picture undermines) and downward-distributing models (which reduce blame to individuals and seem to erase the specifically national dimension of war guilt). The proposal should explain exactly why this leaves the question open, lay out a method that makes progress through conceptual analysis applied to the historical case rather than through more archival work, specify what a positive result would look like and what would count as failure (for instance, if fragmentation forces pure individualism with no residual national responsibility), and assess honestly whether this is tractable in one semester. I will supply all citations and historical sources myself, so do not include a bibliography, reading list, or specific citation requests. To keep the framing from being a standard rehash of collective-responsibility debates, inject a somewhat niche conceptual move—perhaps treating the absence of a unified national will not merely as an obstacle to blame but as revealing a distinct, under-theorized mode of collective wrongdoing that applies specifically to state-level actions with distributed causal architectures.
