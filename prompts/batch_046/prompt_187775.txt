I've recently become absorbed in the debate over whether cross-cultural divergence in folk morality—particularly the patterns documented in WEIRD psychology, where Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic populations systematically weight care and fairness over loyalty, authority, and sanctity—actually threatens moral objectivity, or whether it merely reveals that different groups apply shared normative structures to different social ecologies. This question has become personally urgent for me because I am helping design a graduate seminar on moral epistemology, and I need a diagnostic instrument that sorts philosophical responses not by the labels philosophers prefer but by the specific commitments they hold about concept-sharing, mind-independence, and the explanatory power of evolutionary psychology. I want you to construct a decision tree—or, if the logical space is better captured by a branching taxonomy—whose root is the question of whether observed cross-cultural moral disagreement is best interpreted as variation in application, variation in framework, or variation in non-moral descriptive beliefs that only appear normative. From there, each node should pose a concrete, answerable question: for instance, whether moral disagreement requires shared conceptual content; whether evolutionary debunking arguments apply uniformly to all moral domains or selectively to authority-based norms; whether local moral objectivity is compatible with global incommensurability; and whether a culture's own reflective equilibrium can serve as an internal standard of correction without collapsing into relativism.

The tree must terminate in named positions, but the branches themselves should be labeled with the diagnostic question rather than with a philosopher's name, and every path should be traversable by someone who does not already know which interpretation it represents. The leaves I require are: a robust moral realist view that treats WEIRD divergence as statistically noisy but normatively irrelevant; a culturally indexed constructivism that permits local objectivity within frameworks but denies cross-framework adjudication; a full deflationary error-theory that reads all folk moral variation as the output of evolved heuristics with no normative authority; and at least one hybrid position that distinguishes first-order moral norms from second-order meta-ethical authority—perhaps holding that norms are objectively binding within a culture's practical reasoning but that no culture holds authority over another's meta-ethical self-assessment. You must also identify any combinations of answers that appear logically available but are actually unoccupied—whether because they are incoherent (for example, combining strict concept-sharing requirements with the claim that no cross-cultural disagreement is real), unattractive (a view that accepts evolutionary debunking for care-based norms but not for loyalty-based ones without principled distinction), or merely unexplored—and briefly diagnose why the space is empty there. Where two named positions cannot be separated by your tree, flag the overlap explicitly rather than forcing a distinction.

Please deliver this as a committed classification: take a definite stand on which branches represent genuine philosophical options and which collapse into confusion, explain why the root question I have specified structures the space correctly, and do not present the taxonomy as a neutral menu. If you believe, for instance, that one branch mischaracterizes the relationship between evolutionary explanation and normative justification, or that the hybrid view I requested is unstable once its commitments are made explicit, argue for that conclusion directly rather than leaving it to me to infer.
