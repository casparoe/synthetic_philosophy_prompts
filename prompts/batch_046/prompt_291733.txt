You are a professional philosopher specializing in value theory, practical reason, and the structure of hard choices. Please think carefully and begin with brief brainstorming of the values at stake in each scenario below. Then work toward a precise, formally stated principle.

Here are four concrete cases in which I face a choice between values that appear genuinely incommensurable — not reducible to a single metric like welfare, preference-satisfaction, or utility. In each case I state my intuitive verdict. I want to know whether these verdicts can be captured by one account, and if not, which one I should abandon.

Case 1 — The reef and the desalination plant. A small democratic island state suffers chronic freshwater scarcity. A corporation proposes a desalination plant that would guarantee water security, but construction requires destroying the only coral reef that serves as the physical site of a centuries-old religious ritual and hosts a unique, non-replicable ecosystem. Alternative solutions exist — strict rationing, rainwater harvesting, and conservation — but they would require permanent, burdensome lifestyle changes. My verdict: reject the plant. The reef's value is not fungible with water security; the community should adapt its consumption rather than annihilate its constitutive environment.

Case 2 — The museum acquisition. A municipal museum must decide whether to acquire a world-famous painting that would generate major tourism revenue and international prestige. The acquisition requires deaccessioning and selling the museum's entire collection of local folk art produced by historically marginalized groups. That collection has negligible market value but constitutes the only material record of those communities' lived histories. My verdict: reject the painting. The museum's primary obligation is to preserve the non-substitutable historical record of its own community, not to maximize prestige or revenue.

Case 3 — The library and the flood barrier. A city must choose whether to demolish its sole brutalist civic library — an architecturally singular public commons designed by a deceased architect, with no comparable substitute — in order to build a climate-adaptive flood barrier that would protect thousands of homes from imminent, rising-sea damage. The barrier cannot be relocated. My verdict: demolish the library. When the competing value is direct, large-scale protection of lives and homes from imminent physical harm, even irreplaceable cultural goods must yield.

Case 4 — The language and the mental-health program. A democratic government with limited fiscal capacity must choose between fully funding the preservation of an endangered indigenous language spoken by fewer than two hundred elderly citizens — requiring sustained education, media, and documentation programs with no foreseeable economic return — and expanding mental-health services for youth, which have clear welfare benefits but can also be partially supported through federal partnerships and private funding. My verdict: sustain the language program. Linguistic continuity is a non-substitutable good that, once extinguished, cannot be reconstructed; mental-health provision, though urgent, admits of incremental and partial substitutes.

These four verdicts seem to cohere around some idea of non-fungibility for goods that constitute collective identity, but I see a sharp tension. In Case 1 I preserve an irreplaceable ecological and ritual good despite serious human need (water scarcity can be addressed through adaptation). In Case 3 I destroy an irreplaceable architectural good to prevent harm to lives and homes. Meanwhile, in Case 2 and Case 4 I preserve non-market cultural goods over practical benefits (revenue, welfare).

Please address the following:

1. Is there one precise principle — stated formally, with explicit conditions — that delivers all four verdicts? If you formulate one, identify the clause that motivates each case and show how it applies.

2. If no single principle can accommodate all four without ad hoc adjustments, which verdict should I overturn for the sake of theoretical simplicity? Give your reasoning.

3. Whatever principle you endorse (unitary or revised), apply it to a case I have not listed: a democratic legislature is considering replacing its traditional public deliberation forum — a face-to-face, consensus-oriented assembly with deep symbolic value — with an algorithmic decision-support system that demonstrably improves policy outcomes and reduces partisan bias, but eliminates the embodied practice of collective reasoning. What does your principle predict?

4. Be honest about any residual tension: state exactly which of my original verdicts your final principle cannot accommodate and why.

Please avoid appeals to authority or citation; reason from the structure of the values themselves.
