I am taking a seminar on hermeneutics and the philosophy of interpretation, and I have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern that I cannot find named in the literature we have read. In discussions of canonical philosophical texts, when a passage contains a claim that strikes the class as plainly false, anachronistic, or contradictory, we routinely invoke authorial intention, historical context, or a likely mistranslation to explain the error away—treating the author’s meaning as the authoritative court of appeal. But when the very same text offers a claim that strikes us as remarkably profound or prescient, the same interpreters reject appeals to what the author “really meant” as committing the intentional fallacy, insisting that the text’s meaning transcends or even contradicts its author’s design. The result is not a consistent method but a selective repair mechanism: intentionalism is deployed to protect the text’s authority from embarrassment, then suspended to allow that authority to exceed the author. I have considered whether this is simply charitable interpretation, or a variant of the hermeneutics of suspicion run in reverse, or mere confirmation bias in reading, but none of those capture the specific asymmetry—the framework-switching governed by whether an interpretation flatters or undermines the text’s perceived wisdom. Is there an established concept for this exact pattern? If not, how should one define its boundaries and distinguish it from ordinary contextual rescue, from anti-intentionalist autonomy claims, and from straightforward interpretive bias? And if it is widespread, does recognizing it require us to reformulate the debate between intentionalists and anti-intentionalists, or does it show that both sides are already practicing the same asymmetrical strategy under different names?
