In my political philosophy seminar we are working through Spinoza's Tractatus Politicus, and two classmates—Alex and Jordan—got into a sharp disagreement about whether Spinoza's democracy is fundamentally normative or descriptive. I want to understand exactly where each stands.

Alex started by arguing that for Spinoza democracy is the regime in which the multitude becomes a single rational agent. The democratic assembly does not just aggregate preferences; through collective deliberation it approximates the guidance of reason over passion. That is why Spinoza treats it as the best form of government. The exclusions of those who are not sui iuris—children, those under another's power, foreigners—are practical limits on who can participate in rational deliberation, not conceptual features of democracy itself. If everyone truly participated as equals, democracy would realize the rational state most fully.

Jordan replied that this reads Spinoza as a proto-liberal constitutionalist. Democracy is not valuable because it produces rational consensus; it is the ontologically basic form of sovereignty because the power of the multitude just is its right. The democratic assembly does not create sovereignty by reasoning together—it reveals that sovereignty was never located in a monarch or council but in the actual organized power of the people. The exclusions are not pragmatic side conditions; they are definitional. Spinoza excludes precisely those whose power is already determined by another, which shows that democratic citizenship tracks actual independence of power, not rational capacity. Calling democracy best is a claim about stability and proximity to the state of nature, not about moral superiority or deliberative quality.

Alex pushed back: if democracy is purely descriptive of power relations, then Spinoza's normative language collapses. Why call it the form closest to nature or link it to equality unless there is a standard—rational equality—that other regimes violate? A purely descriptive account cannot explain why monarchy is worse except by saying it is less stable, but Spinoza seems to think it violates something deeper about human nature.

Jordan answered that closer to nature means closer to the condition where no one commands another by right of nature alone. Democracy approximates that equality of power. It is better because aligning institutions with actual power relations makes the state more durable, not because it fulfills a rational ideal. The language about the multitude acting as if guided by one mind should be read metaphorically: it describes unified power, not literal collective reason.

Can you go through their exchange round by round and adjudicate each specific point? I need to know who is right about Spinoza's view of democratic rationality, about whether the exclusions are pragmatic or definitional, and about how to interpret the normative language. Also, what is the most important thing both are missing—perhaps about how Spinoza distinguishes between democracy in its absolute form and the institutional mechanisms he actually endorses?
