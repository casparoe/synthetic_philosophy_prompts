My friend and I just finished a long, somewhat heated argument about whether we can actually know why some nations are rich. I defended the institutionalist account—that inclusive political and economic institutions drive long-run prosperity—while my friend insisted that geographic endowments and cultural transmission are so deeply entangled with institutional development that causal separation is impossible from historical data alone. We want to see which of these two frameworks, institutional causation versus geographic-cultural entanglement, holds up better when pressed on a specific, under-discussed problem: the counterfactual of colonial selection, in which the same European powers imposed extractive institutions precisely on regions with favorable geography and existing resource wealth, making institutional quality and geographic advantage observationally inseparable in the post-colonial record. Compare the institutionalist and geographic-cultural entanglement views on this point. In particular, explain how each framework attempts to disentangle cause and correlation when the historical process that generated variation in institutions was not randomly assigned but strategically targeted by colonizers toward already-advantaged territories. Assess which view offers the more coherent response to the claim that historical causal inference here is fundamentally underidentified, and indicate where each position is weakest. Please answer in American English.
