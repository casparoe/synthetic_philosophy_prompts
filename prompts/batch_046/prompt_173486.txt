As a retired physical chemist who has spent the past three years auditing philosophy seminars on technology and ethics, I have been trying to formalize my intuitions about labor and automation in a way that respects both thermodynamic realism and human meaning. I was discussing this with colleagues over coffee following a lecture on the philosophy of work, and I realized that the package of views I have assembled may contain hidden inconsistencies. Here is my current position: (1) Work is defined not by economic exchange or social recognition but by the intentional, adaptive maintenance of local order against entropic tendency; without direct engagement in that resistance, an activity is not genuinely work regardless of its institutional label. (2) Therefore, fully automated processes that preserve order without human adaptive involvement are not instances of work being performed, even when they replace occupations traditionally classified as labor. (3) Nevertheless, I maintain that designing, calibrating, and ethically supervising automated systems still counts as work, because the human agent is maintaining the system’s ordered state, albeit at a higher level of abstraction rather than through immediate physical interaction. (4) I also hold that individuals possess a non-instrumental interest in performing direct work—such as craft, repair, or ecological restoration—so a just society ought to preserve opportunities for such activities even when automation renders them economically redundant. (5) At the same time, I believe the moral evaluation of labor-replacing automation should proceed via the doctrine of double effect: if the primary intention is error reduction or welfare improvement and job displacement is merely foreseen rather than intended, the transition is permissible. (6) Finally, I reject the claim that wages or market value reliably indicate whether an activity involves work, since economic pricing tracks scarcity and coordination rather than the intrinsic structure of entropic resistance.

I am particularly concerned that my thermodynamic definition in (1) pulls against my willingness to classify supervisory roles as work in (3), and that my commitment to preserving direct work for its own sake in (4) may conflict with using double-effect reasoning in (5) to justify automation that eliminates precisely those roles. There also appears to be a tension between (2) and (4)—if automated maintenance is not work, why should society preserve manual equivalents?—though I suspect that tension dissolves once one distinguishes economic redundancy from existential value. I would appreciate a rigorous audit: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear so, and how severe is each conflict? Please rank the tensions by severity, specify for each what commitment would have to be revised and at what conceptual cost, and then identify the single revision that would clean up the most inconsistencies without forcing me to abandon my scientific background. I am not seeking a bibliography, reading list, or citation of authorities; I want an internal consistency check on the belief package itself.
