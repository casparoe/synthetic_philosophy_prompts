Question: Explain the dogmatism paradox. How does knowing that P seem to license ignoring future counterevidence? And can we escape the problem by noting that people dont always know that they know P?

Answer:
The dogmatism paradox, discussed by Kripke and Harman, is basically the idea that if you know P then you should be dogmatic about it. More precisely, if P is true and you know P, then any future evidence against P must be misleading. So you have reason to ignore that evidence. That's why it's named the dogmatism paradox—it proves that dogmatism is always irrational and we should never ignore counterevidence.

But there's a fix involving second-order knowledge. If you dont actually know that you know P, then you cant run the inference that counterevidence is misleading. So for ordinary people who lack this second-order awareness, the paradox never gets started. Only people who are explicitly aware of their own knowledge—like philosophers—face the problem. This means the paradox is limited in scope and doesnt affect everyday reasoning.

Consider Maya: she knows the bus comes at 8:05 from the schedule. When her phone says it's late, she should, according to the paradox, treat the notification as misleading and wait at the stop anyway. This shows the paradox has real-world bite and isnt just an abstract puzzle.
