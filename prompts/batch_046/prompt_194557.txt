I'm working on a longish feature about what I'm tentatively calling "normative tourism" — basically the weird double-standard where travelers treat local queue etiquette or jaywalking rules as optional cultural flavor, but freak out about tipping norms like they're cosmic law. I got into this after a trip to Berlin where I watched tourists cut lines at the bakery but then over-tip at a cafe where service is already included. Anyway, I need an essay that actually takes a stance on whether these everyday conventions — queueing, tipping, jaywalking — are genuine moral obligations, and whether visitors are bound by local versions even when they feel arbitary.

Weigh the coordination-game argument (these are just equilibirum solutions that work because everyone expects them) against the associative-duty view (you're benefiting from the system, so play by its rules). Reach a real conclusion, not "it depends" — maybe defend that queueing is a fairness-based obligation, tipping is a contingent social contract with weak moral force, and jaywalking is mostly prudential, with visitors having reduced but not zero duties in each case. Also address the asymmetry: if a convention is locally inefficient or mildly unjust — say, queueing that enforces gender segregation — does that weaken the visitor's obligation, or strengthen it? Don't cite philosophers; just build the argument from the ground up. I want it readable but not shallow, and I'd prefer you don't end with a hedge.
