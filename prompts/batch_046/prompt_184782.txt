I'm working through a long-standing puzzle in philosophy of probability and want a structural diagnosis rather than another survey of positions. The reference-class problem—the fact that placing a particular case into different statistical populations yields different probabilities, with no obvious principle for selecting the correct class—has persisted for decades despite formal advances in causal modeling, Bayesian updating, and mechanistic reasoning. The standard responses divide roughly into those who think causal relevance or mechanistic structure should constrain class selection, those who treat reference-class choice as a pragmatic or epistemic decision guided by predictive utility, and those who deny that single-case probabilities are well-defined at all. Each camp seems to talk past the others, yet they share the same formal apparatus and often agree on the numerical outputs in concrete cases.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not mainly empirical or formal, but metaphysical in a specific, often unacknowledged way: one side treats reference classes as tracking real statistical kinds or causal structures that exist independently of our predictive aims, so that selecting the "wrong" class is a genuine error about the world; the other side treats them as conventional partitions constructed solely to organize data for inference, meaning the problem only arises if one illicitly assumes there must be a unique correct partition. I am not sure, however, whether this realist-versus-conventionalist framing actually captures the disagreement, or whether it masks a deeper divergence about the admissibility conditions for statistical inference—perhaps about whether probabilities must be grounded in frequencies, in causal mechanisms, or in rational degrees of belief.

Please diagnose the disagreement concretely: is it verbal, substantive, or a hybrid? Do the sides differ about discoverable facts, about standards of explanatory adequacy, about which examples count as decisive data, or about pre-theoretic intuitions neither side can defend? Is there a hidden formal question—say, about how causal graphs constrain admissible reference classes, or about the conditions under which single-case probabilities are meaningful—that is doing the real work beneath the metaphysical language? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a new conceptual distinction, a formal result, a reframing of the original question, or should we expect permanent irresolution, and on what grounds? Be explicit about where my metaphysical-kind hypothesis succeeds and where it fails, and identify the one objection or hidden premise I am most likely overlooking.
