I'm giving the retirement toast for Dr. Elena Vasquez, who directed our university's research-integrity office for thirty years. She is not religious and has long kept a private adaptation of Benjamin Franklin's virtue-tracking scheme—thirteen categories, a weekly rotation, a small notebook in which she records failures rather than successes—taped inside her desk drawer. She asked that the toast not claim she achieved perfect institutional integrity, because she regards her career as one of repeated, visible failures; but she also asked that it not treat those failures as comedy or proof that the effort was useless. The audience is mixed: her administrative staff, several academic ethicists, university officials, and her family. Please write the toast. Franklin's concept of moral perfection should do real structural work: perfection must be treated as an impossible craft-project, measured by the persistence of tracking rather than by any achieved state, so that praising her requires praising the discipline of the notebook, not the absence of fault. The toast must therefore avoid religious language of redemption and cynical language of futility, must be fully intelligible to people who have never read Franklin, and must not misrepresent his scheme as a religious duty, a psychological self-help program, or a Stoic exercise in self-sufficiency. At the close, include a brief note separating what is settled about Franklin's project—his specific list of thirteen virtues, his eventual abandonment of the weekly rotation, his deist framing of virtue as social utility rather than divine obedience—from what remains genuinely contested among scholars, particularly whether his admission of failure was a sincere assessment of human limits or a rhetorical strategy of humility, and present neither interpretation as the consensus.
