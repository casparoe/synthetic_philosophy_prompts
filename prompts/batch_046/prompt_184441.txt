I have been trying to formalize Taurek's resistance to aggregation in rescue cases through a distinction I call scalar rescue topology: a unitary rescue saves exactly one person through a discrete causal pathway, while a branching rescue saves several people through a single shared pathway—imagine one lever that diverts a train away from five passengers rather than away from one. In the branching case, saving the five requires precisely the same physical intervention as saving any subset of them, yet Taurek's recommendation to flip a coin between that lever and a separate rescue of one other person still seems to apply. This leads me to think that his real objection may not be that numbers should not count, but that he treats the internal scale of a causal pathway as morally opaque: he refuses to let the number of lives inside one pathway affect the comparison between pathways. If that is correct, then his view is less about the separateness of persons and more about the ontology of actions—about whether a rescue is individuated by its causal structure rather than by the independent claims of those affected. Am I misreading him? Does recognizing the branching structure actually undermine the coin-flip, or does it merely expose that his core concern is with how we individuate moral obligations rather than with aggregation itself?
