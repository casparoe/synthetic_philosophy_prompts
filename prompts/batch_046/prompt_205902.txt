As a journalist writing on the philosophy embedded in algorithmic governance, I want to understand a specific institutional practice: courts and parole boards are increasingly presented with risk-assessment reports that do not merely state population correlations, but assign a numerical counterfactual risk score to an individual — for instance, the estimated probability that this specific defendant would commit a violent act if released on bail, derived from a causal model that treats the counterfactual scenario as well-defined and determinately true or false. The practice treats these individual-level counterfactuals of free human choice as objects of probabilistic knowledge sufficient to justify detention or intervention. What philosophical commitments does that presuppose? In particular, does it implicitly depend on a Molinist framework — the idea that there are true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, a form of middle knowledge, that can be accessed without determining the outcome — or is it compatible with determinism, compatibilism, or a purely statistical view that denies any determinate fact about what this particular person would freely do? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the practice strictly requires it or is philosophically underdetermined, and describe what a policy built on a view that rejects such middle-knowledge-style counterfactuals would have to change about its use of predictive algorithms.
