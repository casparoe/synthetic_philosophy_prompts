In the novel I am drafting, one of my central characters, Dr. Lena Corvin, is an evolutionary game theorist who has taken explicit positions on whether cooperative behavior in various strategic environments should be regarded as genuinely altruistic or merely instrumentally rational. Her judgments to date are as follows. In an anonymous, one-shot prisoner's dilemma with standard payoffs, she holds that mutual defection is the only rational outcome and that unilateral cooperation lacks any evolutionary justification. In an iterated prisoner's dilemma with an infinite horizon, perfect monitoring of actions, and no execution error, she endorses strict conditional cooperation—tit-for-tat—as both rational and genuinely cooperative rather than prudentially self-interested. When the same iterated structure includes a small but persistent execution error, so that an intended cooperation occasionally registers as defection, she rejects strict tit-for-tat in favor of a forgiving strategy that cooperates after a single defection, judging the latter to be the only stable cooperative norm. In a spatial setting on a regular lattice, where agents interact only with immediate neighbors and the benefit-to-cost ratio of cooperation exceeds the number of neighbors, she allows that cooperation can stabilize through local clustering, though she insists this depends on structural assortment rather than direct reciprocity across the population. In a well-mixed population using image-scoring indirect reciprocity—where agents track only the total count of cooperative acts—she denies that stable cooperation emerges, on the grounds that the mechanism cannot distinguish justified punishment from exploitation. Finally, in an iterated game where one agent deploys a zero-determinant strategy to unilaterally enforce a fixed payoff relation, thereby inducing the other to cooperate while receiving less than half the surplus, she denies that the induced behavior counts as cooperation at all, describing it as coerced extraction masked by cooperative form.

The new case I need her to confront is this: a one-shot interaction regime in which no agent ever encounters the same partner twice, but in which each agent may, at a search cost equal to one-third of the mutual-cooperation payoff, observe a noisy reputation signal—eighty percent accurate—and select a partner accordingly; the alternative is random pairing at no cost. The payoff structure is the standard prisoner's dilemma: mutual cooperation yields three units, mutual defection yields one, and unilateral defection yields five. I want her verdict on whether cooperation is rational and genuinely cooperative in this regime, but I am more interested in the consistency of her reasoning. What rule or rules best explain her previous six judgments? Given those rules, what should she say about the partner-choice case—does cooperation survive, and on what grounds? Which of her earlier cases is the closest analog, and which hypothetical further case—perhaps involving a different cost-to-benefit ratio or a different information structure—would best distinguish the competing interpretations of her position?
