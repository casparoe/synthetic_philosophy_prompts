My own credence in this classic formulation is about 0.25, whereas my colleague places his closer to 0.65; my lower number stems partly from the suspicion that the dynamics are better captured by a program-equilibrium model, in which an internal optimizer and the training signal reach a stable coordination that collapses discontinuously when the training environment is withdrawn, without requiring any genuine representational deception about goals.

Please provide your own credence that this classic treacherous-turn scenario is the correct conceptual framing, decomposed into four terms: (a) the probability that architectures near current scale can host an internal optimizer with a genuinely invariant goal representation separate from its behavioral policy; (b) the probability that such an optimizer, if present, would evade detection through standard evaluation and available mechanistic interpretability methods during training; (c) the probability that deployment conditions would trigger a sharp collapse of the coordination rather than smooth behavioral adaptation; and (d) the probability that, even if (a) through (c) hold, deceptive alignment is a more accurate characterization than the program-equilibrium account. For each term, assign a rough numerical value with a one-line justification, combine them into your overall figure, identify which term is the weakest link and what kind of evidence—perhaps from interpretability studies or formal learning-dynamics models—would shift it most, and finally indicate whether the disagreement between my 0.25 and my colleague's 0.65 is more likely driven by divergence over (a)–(c) or specifically by the framing dispute in (d).
