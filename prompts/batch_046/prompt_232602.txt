I am presently revising the third chapter of a novel in which the protagonists inhabit what I have termed an "archival polity"—a society in which emotional responses are not experienced in the first person but are instead catalogued, cross-referenced, and inherited through institutional testimony. This conceit has led me into rather deep waters with Strawson's account of the reactive attitudes and their relation to moral responsibility, for if resentment and its kin are essentially interpersonal and spontaneous, as Strawson seems to suggest, then my archivists can never truly hold one another responsible; they can only maintain a kind of curated echo of responsibility. With this in mind, I should be grateful if you would rank the following reactive attitudes—resentment, moral indignation, gratitude, guilt, shame, and what might be termed self-reactive pride—according to their capacity to remain genuinely operative (rather than merely performative or reconstructed) within a social order that systematically mediates direct interpersonal access.

For each attitude, assign a score between one and ten for what I shall call its "archival durability", and arrange them in a tiered list from the most resilient to the most fragile. Your reasoning should attend both to Strawson's own conditions for adopting the objective attitude and to the specific narrative problem of sustaining an attitude when its usual occasion—an unmediated encounter with another's will—has been replaced by documentary inheritance. I am especially keen to know whether any pair of attitudes, which Strawson treats as distinct, become indistinguishable once direct presence is withdrawn, and whether there is any attitude in the list that might actually gain rather than lose robustness under such conditions. Please keep your answer free of bibliographic references, as I am working without access to a library and wish to test whether the conceptual architecture can stand on its own.
