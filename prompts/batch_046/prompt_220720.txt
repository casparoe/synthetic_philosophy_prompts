For a consistency audit before a metaethics seminar, here is the philosophical profile I plan to present. I accept that both personal affection and the appreciation of beauty are fundamental intrinsic goods, not reducible to pleasure or instrumental benefit, in roughly the Moorean sense. I hold that friendship is an organic unity: its value is not the sum of the values of its parts, and altering its parts can change or destroy the value of the whole. I extend this structural claim to aesthetic experience, maintaining that the beauty of a complex form is similarly an organic unity of its perceptual elements rather than an aggregate of local beauties. I also believe that a friendship can retain its full intrinsic value even when sustained by a deep but benign misapprehension of the other person's character, on the grounds that the false belief is not a constituent part of the unity in the way that affective warmth or mutual concern are. Finally, I maintain that intuitive judgments about the value of such organic unities are reliable in direct, dyadic relationships but become systematically unreliable when applied to diffuse, mediated, or large-scale social formations.

Please examine these commitments for tension. Identify which pairs are genuinely incompatible, which only appear to conflict, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, say what exactly pulls against what and what the least costly revision would be. Then tell me which single change, if I had to make only one, would restore the most coherence without collapsing the broader view. Do not treat all conflicts as equally serious; discriminate between structural contradictions and merely surprising combinations.
