I'm presenting a defense of endurantism specifically about collective institutional entities—universities, states, and corporate bodies—at a joint philosophy-of-law and metaphysics workshop next week. The core claim is that these entities are wholly present at each moment of their existence, and that this ontology is required to ground retrospective moral and legal accountability, for example in reparations claims, in a way that four-dimensionalism undermines, since later temporal segments of an institutional worm do not stand in the right normative relation to earlier wrongs.

The audience is precisely split. About two-thirds are legal philosophers who are comfortable with four-dimensionalism about persons—they invoke it to solve puzzles about criminal liability across psychological change—but who are conventionalists about institutions, treating membership and continuity as matters of legal recognition and charter obligation rather than metaphysical substrate. The remaining third are two metaphysicians committed to stage theory who regard institutions as the paradigmatic case of perdurant objects composed of temporal parts of people and artifacts, and who know the counterpart-theoretic machinery for cross-time relations inside out. They care more about ontological consistency than about jurisprudence.

My argument sketch runs as follows. I begin with the normative premise that if an institution is legitimately held responsible for past actions, there must be a subject of those actions that is numerically identical to the present institution. I then argue that on a perdurantist analysis, the present stage or segment of the institution is not numerically identical to the past stage that performed the wrong; at best, they are causally and conventionally related, which is insufficient for the kind of direct moral burden that reparations require. I defend institutional endurantism by appealing to a modified persistence condition applied to organizational roles: the institution persists through membership turnover not by having different temporal parts, but by continuously instantiating the same structural-functional kind, analogous to how an artifact might persist despite part replacement if we reject mereological essentialism for functional kinds.

The objections should target my specific argument, not generic endurantism versus perdurantism debates. Include at least:

1. The conventionalist legal-philosophy objection: legal responsibility does not need numerical identity because institutional roles and charter obligations are conventionally transferred, so my ontological premise is redundant.
2. The stage-theory composition objection: if persons are temporal stages, then institutions composed of them must also have temporal parts; my endurantism about institutions conflicts with the ontology I implicitly accept for their members.
3. The practical-destruction objection: if every membership change threatens numerical identity, then institutional reform—firing, restructuring, merging—becomes metaphysically destructive rather than transformative, which is legally and practically absurd.
4. The essentialism objection: my view seems to require that institutions have essential membership conditions, yet we clearly recognize universities after complete faculty turnover.
5. The hidden normative objection I most fear: that retrospective accountability does not actually require numerical identity at all, but only appropriate causal and narrative continuity, which perdurantism can easily accommodate through counterpart relations or stage-linking.
