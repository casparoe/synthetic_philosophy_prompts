Within the philosophy of pseudohistory and demarcation—taking as orienting cases the Atlantis tradition, ancient-astronaut narratives, and Fomenko's New Chronology—which conceptual priorities should dominate a five-year research program whose aim is a domain-general framework for distinguishing pseudohistory from legitimate revisionism, not merely debunking particular claims? Specifically, compare three lines of inquiry: first, developing a criterion of predictive archaeological fertility that requires pseudohistorical reconstructions to generate novel, empirically risky expectations about material culture rather than only accommodating anomalies post hoc; second, addressing the negative-space problem—whether demarcation should turn mainly on the false assertions a narrative makes or on the systematic exclusions it enforces (such as suppressing stratigraphic sequences or linguistic phylogenies), and whether those exclusions can be formalized without circular reliance on the very historiography being questioned; third, resolving whether chronological-compression theories like Fomenko's can be separated from legitimate historiographical revisionism without tacitly presupposing a Eurocentric civilizational timeline that the framework ought to be able to interrogate on its own terms. Rank these three in order of importance for a small conceptual-research group with minimal fieldwork access. Defend the ordering by identifying which problems are upstream of which, which are tractable with logical and philosophical tools alone, and what intermediate deliverable—such as a formal model of exclusion patterns or a comparative structural analysis—would cause you to reorder them.
