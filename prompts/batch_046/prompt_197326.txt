I've been running a graduate-level philosophy workshop—fourteen participants, one 90-minute session in a seminar room with movable chairs, a whiteboard, and a table of physical props—where I want to avoid the standard "where do you draw the line on sentience" conversation that every student has already encountered in introductory ethics. Instead, I want them to feel, from the inside, the instability of binary moral classification when the underlying properties are genuinely scalar and multi-dimensional. The framing should be somewhat in the weeds: the issue is not simply gradualism but what I think of as the clustering illusion—the tendency of our moral practices to treat continuously varying dimensions (phenomenal integration, teleological coherence, social recognizability, negative-valence capacity) as if they formed discrete natural kinds, solely because institutions need binary gates like "protected patient" versus "instrumental resource." To do this, I need a runnable activity—preferably with real but small stakes, such as each participant holding limited "intervention tokens" that determine which running devices or simulations on the table remain active, with group consensus binding—so that they construct a threshold rule themselves, apply it to ambiguous middle entities, and experience the consistency cost of maintaining it. Only after they've lived through the failure should the debrief name the idea, map each game moment onto the philosophical structure, predict the predictable failures (e.g., participants smuggling in hidden binary intuitions), and frankly admit that the one feature this cannot reproduce—actual phenomenal experience—must be supplied by conceptual argument. Please design the rules, roles, payoffs, and debrief structure; do not ask for citations or reading lists, and avoid naming "gradualism" or "scalar moral status" in the activity instructions themselves.
