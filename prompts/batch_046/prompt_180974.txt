I am putting together a taxonomy for a graduate seminar on comparative ethics that has been struggling with a specific confusion: students keep mapping Buddhist moral frameworks onto virtue ethics without noticing that certain Abhidharma and Mahayana conceptual resources—particularly the distinction between cetana (intention) and phala (karmic result), the scope conditions of karma, and the logic of upaya (skillful means)—actually generate a logical space that overlaps with, but is not reducible to, consequentialist reasoning. To correct this, I want a decision-tree instrument that classifies positions not by the labels people prefer, but by the answers they give to four concrete branching questions.

First: in a case where a well-intentioned action produces widespread harmful results, does the moral evaluation attach primarily to the mental state or to the external fruit? Second: is the scope of moral concern agent-relative—so that karmic consequences rebound only on the actor—or agent-neutral, allowing welfare across beings to be summed? Third: does the doctrine of skillful means ever license violating a sila (precept) when the aggregate suffering prevented exceeds the harm caused? Fourth: does merit-transfer (parinamana) function as a quantifiable, inter-agent welfare mechanism comparable to consequentialist aggregation, or does it resist such calculus? Please construct the tree so that each branch is labeled by the claim that splits it, each leaf carries either a recognized position name—such as Theravada mentalism, Mahayana bodhisattva consequentialism, or Abhidharmic result-neutralism—or is marked unnamed, with a one-sentence description of its most awkward commitment and, for unnamed leaves, a brief diagnosis of why the space is unoccupied (incoherence, unattractiveness, or neglect). Every node should be phrased as a scenario or claim that a student could answer without knowing the corresponding label, and please flag any pair of leaves that the tree genuinely cannot distinguish.
