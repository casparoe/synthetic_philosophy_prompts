I'm a PhD student in metaphysics of biology, and at our department's recent workshop on taxonomy and ontology, two colleagues—one with a background in philosophy of science and modal logic, the other in process metaphysics—got into an extended disagreement about the species problem that went well beyond the standard biological species concept versus phylogenetic systematics framing. Rather than treating pluralism as an embarrassment to be solved by picking one concept, they argued over whether the problem itself stems from a hidden commitment to substance ontology that can be dissolved either by modal essentialism about reproductive isolation or by abandoning kind-boundaries altogether. I am trying to work out who has the stronger case at each step, and I need an adjudication that does not just declare a winner but rates the specific argumentative moves.

Colleague A began by proposing that the species problem only arises if we assume there is a single mind-independent kind uniting all organisms we call Homo sapiens, and that once we distinguish metaphysical reality from epistemic practice, pluralism becomes a feature rather than a failure. She defended a modal essentialist view according to which species membership is fixed not by shared intrinsic properties but by belonging to a modal profile centered on actual interbreeding potential, with different contexts legitimately invoking different modal profiles without contradiction. She accused biological species concept advocates of conflating classification practices with reproductive communities, and argued that without this distinction, essentialism about one closure-condition arbitrarily privileges one biological interaction network over others. Colleague B responded that modal essentialism does not dissolve the problem but buries it in modal logic, because fixing the relevant modal space still requires answering whether counterfactual interbreeding is evaluated relative to actual genotypes, ecological niches, or evolutionary trajectories, each answer presupposing a prior kind-distinction that the view claims to avoid. He then advanced a process-ontology alternative: organisms are not substances belonging to kinds but spatiotemporally extended processes of metabolic and reproductive interaction, so asking what kind an organism belongs to is a category error analogous to asking what shape a river is, since the question illegitimately presupposes a substance ontology that contemporary biology has outgrown. He pressed further that A's pluralism is unstable, because if every reproductive context generates its own modal profile, then species fragments into an infinite family of ad hoc relational sets and taxonomy collapses into pure conventionalism with no non-arbitrary stopping point.

A pushed back with a move I found particularly striking: she claimed that B's river analogy fails because rivers are individuated by continuous spatiotemporal boundaries, whereas reproductive processes exhibit discrete topological closures—genetic exchange networks form natural joints that process ontology must recognize if it wants to avoid reducing all biology to undifferentiated flux. She concluded that B had not shown pluralism collapses into conventionalism; rather, he had demonstrated that any non-pluralist view must arbitrarily privilege one closure-condition, which is precisely the essentialist mistake that generates the species problem in the first place. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move, rating each of the seven moves outlined above from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. In particular, address whether B's charge of a hidden kind-presupposition within modal essentialism succeeds, and whether A's appeal to topological closure genuinely avoids the conventionalism worry or merely relocates it. Also indicate who is more right overall, and identify any important consideration both parties appear to be missing—perhaps regarding how phylogenetic systematics individuates lineages independently of either modal essentialist profiles or pure process ontology, and whether that offers a third path they have both overlooked.
