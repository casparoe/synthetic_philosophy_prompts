I've been thinking about consequentialist cluelessness lately, particularly in the context of longtermist cause prioritization, and I want to push a somewhat contrarian line that goes beyond the usual heuristic responses. The standard framing in longtermist ethics treats cluelessness as a symmetric epistemic fog: we don't know whether our actions will produce flourishing or catastrophe, so we fall back on expected value with broad confidence intervals, robustness reasoning, or near-termist retreat. But I think this misses a structural asymmetry in how cluelessness distributes across different kinds of moral goods, one that has direct implications for how we should prioritize suffering-risk mitigation relative to ambitious future-building projects.

Specifically, I want you to argue that consequentialist cluelessness is far more debilitating for projects aimed at creating large-scale future flourishing—what we might call upside cluelessness—than it is for projects aimed at preventing extreme suffering, or downside cluelessness. The reason is not merely that suffering is easier to identify, but that the representational requirements for reliably predicting and engineering complex flourishing states scale exponentially with temporal distance and model complexity, whereas the conditions for extreme suffering—especially those involving misaligned optimization, coercive control, or lock-in of bad values—have lower dimensional complexity and remain structurally stable across wide perturbations in our predictive models. Therefore, a rational agent facing deep cluelessness shouldn't treat all longtermist interventions symmetrically: they should adopt a form of epistemic pessimism that privileges suffering-risk mitigation not as a motivational prior or a heuristic shortcut, but as the only domain where consequentialist reasoning retains sufficient representational traction to justify ambitious action. Please make this argument rigorously, addressing the obvious objection that this just smuggles in a preference for suffering reduction disguised as epistemology, and show why the asymmetry in representational complexity is genuinely structural. I'd also like you to consider whether this position collapses into negative utilitarianism, and if not, precisely what distinguishes it from that view—perhaps something about the scope of the epistemic claim or the conditions under which upside reasoning might regain traction. I'm writing this up for a discussion group that meets regularly, and I want something sharper than the familiar refrain that we're clueless so we should just do near-term good.
