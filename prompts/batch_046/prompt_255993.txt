As a retired physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science, I have been attending a seminar on historical methodology led by a historian of science whose judgments I am trying to understand. She has ruled on whether various historical narratives are guilty of Whiggish history—writing the past as an inevitable progress toward present science—according to the following track record. First, she judged an account framing Lavoisier's overthrow of phlogiston as the triumph of empirical measurement over superstition, finally delivering chemistry to its modern form, to be Whiggish. Second, she judged an account describing medieval Islamic hospitals as repositories that preserved Greek medical texts until Europe developed the institutional capacity to revive them not to be Whiggish. Third, she judged an account portraying Newton's alchemical research as an unfortunate detour caused by metaphysical confusion, delaying the mechanistic physics he should have pursued exclusively, to be Whiggish. Fourth, she judged an account presenting the 17th-century development of probability theory from gambling problems to statistical inference as a contingent adaptation of mathematical tools to practical needs, with no inherent trajectory toward modern physics, not to be Whiggish. Fifth, she judged an account describing Pasteur's defeat of spontaneous generation as the inevitable victory of experimental rigor over mystical vitalism, clearing the path for modern biology, to be Whiggish.

The new case is a recent monograph arguing that the adoption of the heliocentric framework between 1543 and 1687 should be understood not as the correction of an obvious error but as a reconceptualization fully intelligible within its own mathematical and metaphysical context; the author explicitly rejects labeling Ptolemaic astronomy wrong and instead treats it as a coherent system with different priorities. At the same time, she structures her entire narrative as an irreversible progression—geocentric stasis, then Copernican breakthrough, then Keplerian precision, then Newtonian closure—presenting each phase as a required stage in the emergence of our current cosmological framework, with little consideration of alternative trajectories.

What should she decide, if she wishes to be consistent with her own precedents? Which of her earlier rulings is the closest analog, and if her precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, what principle would she be adopting with each possible choice? More generally, please identify at least two plausible rules that jointly explain her five rulings, state what each rule predicts about this new monograph, and give your best guess at her actual verdict with the reasoning that supports it. If there is a further, clearly different case that would distinguish these two rules, please describe it. In your answer, please use American English, and please clearly separate what is settled among specialists in the philosophy of history from what remains genuinely contested, without presenting any contested interpretation as if it were the consensus view.
