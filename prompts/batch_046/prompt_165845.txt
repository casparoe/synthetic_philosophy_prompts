I'm currently writing my dissertation in philosophy of mind on the causal status of phenomenal properties, and I've been working through a version of the phenomenal judgment paradox that seems to me more resilient than the literature usually admits. The standard challenge to epiphenomenalism holds that if phenomenal qualities are causally inert, then they cannot figure in the causal etiology of our judgments about them; yet when I judge that I am in pain, or that my visual field contains redness, the judgment appears to be about—and apparently caused by—the very phenomenal property in question. If the property does no causal work, the judgment should be mysterious, or so the objection runs.

What I need is a rigorous, original argument defending epiphenomenalism against this paradox by rejecting what I will call the transparency thesis of phenomenal attribution—the tacit assumption that a first-person phenomenal judgment is caused or justified by direct acquaintance with the qualitative character of the state itself. I want you to argue that phenomenal judgments are instead produced by a subpersonal, non-phenomenal representational mechanism—something like an indexical self-frame or a syntactic tracking tag—that registers the occurrence of the mental state without being identical to, or causally influenced by, its phenomenal feel. Your argument should show precisely how this allows the epiphenomenalist to maintain that consciousness plays no causal role in generating self-knowledge, while still explaining why our attributions are systematically reliable rather than arbitrarily generated. You should also address the two obvious objections: first, that such a mechanism renders phenomenal knowledge illusory or disconnected from its object, and second, that the mechanism itself must be sensitive to the phenomenal property in some causally relevant way, which would reintroduce efficacy through the back door. Please do not offer bibliographies, citation lists, or appeals to specific textual authorities; I am testing whether the position can stand on its internal conceptual architecture alone.
