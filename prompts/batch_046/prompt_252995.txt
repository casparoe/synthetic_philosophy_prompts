I've been trying to map my actual judgments about normative self-reference—game rules, constitutional provisions, formal codes—onto some coherent principle, and I'm not convinced they fit together without strain.

Here are my verdicts on concrete structures. First, a Nomic-style sequence: a player enacts Rule 101 permitting amendment by majority vote, then immediately uses that rule to replace itself with Rule 101', which requires unanimous consent for any further change. I judge this fully legitimate—there's strategic lock-in, but no logical paradox. Second, a constitutional clause that reads simply "This clause is unamendable." I think this is also coherent: it establishes hard entrenchment, not contradiction. Third, a statute stating "This statute repeals itself, and this repeal is effective if and only if this statute remains in force." Here my intuition flips: the enactment is malformed, void ab initio, because the self-reference produces direct oscillation rather than a stable fixed point. Fourth, a three-tier hierarchy: Level-1 rules govern conduct, Level-2 rules govern Level-1 amendments, and a Level-3 rule governs Level-2 amendments but explicitly includes itself in its own amendment procedure ("This Level-3 rule may be altered only by the procedure it specifies"). I feel this is conceptually unstable in a way case one is not, though both involve a meta-rule altering its own conditions; I can't articulate why the reflexive inclusion here feels like a regress, while the Nomic replacement feels like a discrete move. Fifth, a democratic-entrenchment clause ("No amendment may restrict voting rights") followed later by an amendment that restricts voting rights but carries a supersession clause ("This amendment overrides all prior limits on amendment power, including the entrenchment clause"). I think the second amendment succeeds and the entrenchment was always illusory—yet then I wonder why the Nomic lock-in in case one feels genuinely binding, while this hierarchical supersession feels like it was never truly constrained.

So my pattern is: discrete procedural replacement is valid, flat self-entrenchment is valid, direct logical self-contradiction is void, reflexive self-inclusion at the meta-level feels unstable, and hierarchical entrenchment with explicit supersession collapses. Is there one account—perhaps distinguishing procedural self-reference from substantive self-reference, or fixed-point stability from oscillation—that delivers all five? Or do I have to overturn one? Specifically, what does your best principle predict for a case I haven't considered: a single-article legal code stating "This article may be repealed only by a procedure that does not currently exist and must be established by this article itself"? And please say honestly which of my judgments—especially the instability I assign to case four versus the legitimacy I grant to case one—must be dropped if we want the simplest coherent principle rather than an ad hoc patchwork.
