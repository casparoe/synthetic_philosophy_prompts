After a long argument with a friend last night, I am trying to assess whether her summary of the hyperbolic-discounting account of akrasia—specifically what she called the intertemporal-bargaining or picoeconomic model—is fair, or whether it smuggles in conclusions that do not follow. She put it this way: because people discount future rewards hyperbolically rather than exponentially, the value of a distant benefit—say, the health gains from quitting smoking—plummets as the moment of temptation approaches, producing a preference reversal in which the agent genuinely prefers the immediate reward at the time of action. From this she drew two further claims. First, that weakness of will is not a failure of practical reason but merely the local temporal self rationally maximizing on its steep present discount curve, and that talk of a single true self being betrayed is therefore a folk-psychological fiction rather than a philosophical datum; she added that the intertemporal-bargaining framework shows there is no stable unitary agent, only competing temporal interests that negotiate precommitment contracts. Second, that since the akratic agent is always maximizing expected utility relative to her current discount rate, moral blame for yielding is inappropriate—precommitment strategies are rational, but criticizing someone for giving in is like criticizing an object for falling when gravity acts on it. I am inclined to think she overstated the normative conclusion, but I am unsure about the descriptive part as well. Is this a fair rendering of how hyperbolic discounting relates to philosophical discussions of akrasia? Which parts accurately describe the model, which parts conflate a mechanistic explanation with a normative dismissal of blame or with the denial of a unified self, and does her conclusion—that there is no stable agent to betray and that moral criticism is never warranted—actually follow from the premises she cited?
