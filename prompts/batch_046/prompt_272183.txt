I am preparing a seventy-five-minute session for an upper-level political-philosophy seminar of about fourteen students, focused on Burkean conservatism and Chesterton's fence. Rather than another textual discussion, I want a self-contained, in-class structured deliberation that makes the participants feel—before anyone names it—the epistemic asymmetry between the ease of dismantling an inherited institution and the near-impossibility of knowing what coordination problem it was silently solving. Please design a simulation divided into two roles: Reform Commissioners, who have access only to present-day efficiency metrics and must propose the abolition of at least two out of three practices within the first twenty minutes; and Institutional Custodians, who hold only fragmentary, mutually contradictory oral histories about why each practice was originally erected, with no documentary proof, and who may speak only after a proposal has been made. The three practices should be concrete but fictional—perhaps an archaic land-tenure ritual, a non-transparent apprenticeship gate, and a redundant ceremonial audit—each imposing visible costs now but with recoverable purpose only partially obscured. Construct a non-monetary payoff scheme, such as collective reputation tokens that determine access to hidden information in later rounds, so that Reformers are rewarded for speed and clarity of removal while Custodians are rewarded for preservation; both sides should suffer a shared penalty if they later discover, in a final consequence round, that an abolished practice had been preventing an unseen coordination failure that only becomes visible once the practice is gone. Include explicit rules, a timeline, required materials like role cards and hidden-information envelopes, and instructions for how the facilitator should stage the final revelation. The debrief should map each round onto Chesterton's fence and Burke's notion of prescription, identify what will predictably fail—likely that Custodians invent post-hoc rationalizations and Reformers treat the absence of proof as proof of absence—and be explicit about which structural feature of real institutional loss, perhaps its irreversibility across generations, this single-session exercise cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied conceptually. Do not include requests for citations, bibliographies, or references to specific editions; keep the design fully runnable without external sources.
