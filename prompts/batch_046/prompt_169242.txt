Next Thursday I present to our philosophy of mind and cognitive science reading group. My thesis is that the persuasive force of canonical intuition pumps—specifically the Chinese Room scenario and the Trolley Problem—derives not from revealing hidden conceptual truths, but from a mechanism I call frame-locked modal projection: the narrative fixes a baseline modal expectation drawn from ordinary social interaction, then evaluates formal or structural properties as deviations from that baseline, rendering them cognitively invisible. I apply this to argue that the Chinese Room fails as an argument against computational theories of mind not because syntax is sufficient for semantics, but because it illicitly transfers interactional heuristics into a domain where they have no purchase.

The audience comprises two defenders of biological naturalism who treat the Chinese Room as decisive; two functionalists who use it as a negative example but are suspicious of meta-level critiques; one cognitive scientist who regards armchair intuition-pumping as empirically empty; and one philosopher of science who defends thought experiments in physics but is wary of their deployment in philosophy of mind.
