I am drafting an essay for my seminar on moral status and political philosophy that examines a tension in Robert Nozick’s treatment of animals. In Anarchy, State, and Utopia, he assigns humans inviolable rights grounded in moral agency and long-term life-planning, while allowing that animals may be weighed in a utilitarian calculus because they lack these capacities. My notes propose that this sharp division is unstable once combined with Nozick’s rejection of the experience machine: if the core objection to simulated existence is that genuine contact with reality and causal efficacy in the world matter, then animals that maintain complex social bonds and navigate intricate ecological environments—such as cetaceans or corvids—possess a non-derivative form of reality-contact. This suggests they should be protected by side-constraints rather than merely aggregated in utility calculations, without granting them full human rights. I also wonder whether this line conflicts with Nozick’s later notion of symbolic utility in The Examined Life. Could you comment on whether this extension misreads his human-animal boundary and whether it illegitimately imports a criterion meant for persons?
