As a retired physicist who has only recently turned to philosophy of rationality, I have been struck by a pattern I encounter repeatedly when discussing strategic reasoning with friends—whether we are analyzing competitive market behavior or informal decision-making under uncertainty. The pattern runs as follows. One begins by identifying a particular course of action that, no matter what others do, yields a worse result than some alternative; consequently, that course is set aside. Once it is removed, the remaining options are re-examined, and frequently another option now appears inferior in every scenario within this reduced set, so it too is discarded. This step-by-step pruning seems intuitive, yet I have noticed a peculiar complication: if one permits not merely selecting among the survivors but blending or randomizing across them, an option previously eliminated can become part of an optimal mixture. I have observed this in casual debates about auction bidding and in philosophical arguments about collective choice, where interlocutors treat the blended strategy as if it were a new, distinct option subject to the same pruning rule, sometimes generating an apparently endless cycle of elimination and resurrection. I am unsure whether this iterative discarding, especially in its form that accommodates such blending, carries an established technical name, or whether the cyclical version I have glimpsed is recognized as a distinct failure mode. Could you clarify what concept, if any, captures this precise structure—distinguishing the single-step comparison of options from the sequential process, and marking the boundary where allowing mixtures either stabilizes or destabilizes the pruning?
