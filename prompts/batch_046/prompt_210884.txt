I am preparing the final exam for an upper-level moral philosophy seminar focused on agent-centered ethics. Draft one essay question on Bernard Williams's integrity objection and the phenomenon he describes as having "one thought too many." The question must not be answerable by summarizing Williams's critique of utilitarianism or by defining ground projects in the abstract. Instead, embed the issue in the following constructed scenario: a senior physician sits on a hospital allocation board for a scarce, life-saving therapy; her ground project is defined by her role as caregiver to a child with the same condition being treated, yet board rules demand strict impartial weighting of clinical criteria. Ask students to determine whether Williams's objection applies coherently here—specifically, whether the physician has "one thought too many" if she inserts her parental commitment into the board's reasoning, or whether institutional role obligations restructure the moral psychology so that the objection misfires. Require them to distinguish, with precision, between (i) the normative claim that agent-relative reasons may override impartial demands, (ii) the psychological claim about which motivations count as alienating rather than merely difficult, and (iii) the meta-level claim about whether professional roles can themselves constitute ground projects without collapsing into impersonality. Then ask them to defend one of these three readings as the correct interpretation of Williams, using the scenario to expose any internal tension.

Provide a model answer of approximately 650 words that does not merely apply the distinction but argues for a specific resolution—e.g., that role-structured deliberation transforms the motivational landscape in a way that either preserves or undermines the integrity objection, depending on how ground projects are individuated. Follow this with a marking scheme built around four must-hit moves, each assigned marks (total 20), and include a one-line note for each on the characteristic failure mode—such as conflating the psychological and normative claims, treating role obligations as automatically trumping personal commitments without argument, or failing to address whether the board context introduces a collective agent for whom "integrity" has no parallel. The question should be designed for a 75-minute response and should separate students who understand the architecture of the objection from those who have only memorized its standard formulation. Do not ask for bibliographies, citation lists, or references to secondary literature; the assessment should depend solely on conceptual analysis of the position as articulated.
