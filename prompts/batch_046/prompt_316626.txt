I'm preparing a single seventy-five-minute session for a mixed audience of second-year philosophy undergraduates and psychology graduate students who have just completed a module on affective forecasting and well-being measurement. I want them to understand the heterogeneity problem for hedonism not as a museum piece but as a live structural obstacle: if well-being is defined in terms of pleasure, then every pleasure must share some common feature that makes it a pleasure, yet the phenomenology of enjoying a vindicated long-held belief, the bodily ease after sustained exertion, and the quiet absorption in a mathematical proof seem to share no common "felt quality" beyond the tautological "feeling good." Before the problem can even appear, the students must first hold two conceptual frames in tension—pleasure as an intrinsic phenomenal tone versus pleasure as a pro-attitude or favorable stance toward an experience—because without that tension they will simply collapse heterogeneity into differences of intensity or duration.

Plan the explanatory arc: which frame must be introduced first and why the other depends on it; the paired carrying example that forces comparison between two pleasures that lack any shared sensory profile (for instance, the gratification of seeing a rival theory publicly fail versus the post-run bodily ease), specifying exactly what this example must possess and what it must lack so that students cannot resolve the tension by smuggling in a hidden physiological marker like dopamine release; the misconceptions they will predictably form—probably that intellectual pleasures are just "weaker" bodily ones, that heterogeneity is solved by pointing to reward-system activation, or that "feeling good" is an adequate descriptive common feature—and the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be intercepted; a single check-for-understanding question that reveals whether they have grasped why heterogeneity threatens hedonism rather than merely cataloging diverse pleasures; and an explicit statement of what should be left out at this level, such as the historical Cyrenaic-Epicurean distinction or the modern amendment that replaces pleasure with desire-satisfaction, named clearly as omissions so the session does not overreach. The goal is that they leave able to articulate why saying "all pleasures feel good" either begs the question or covertly imports a feature that not all pleasures actually share.
