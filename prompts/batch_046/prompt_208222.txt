I'm taking a course on ethics and representation, and a recent seminar discussion about biopics has left me genuinely unsure where the line falls between artistic license and moral wrong. Two fellow students, Mara and Leo, argued over a biographical film depicting a real, now-deceased researcher—someone whose private letters and public writings consistently rejected romantic or tragic framing, emphasizing instead an ascetic, intellectually driven life—by inventing a secret romantic affair and altering her documented death from prolonged illness into an implied suicide, all to fit a conventional romance-tragedy arc. Mara argued that this is not merely factual error but what she called "genre coercion": imposing a dramatic form that violates the subject's own "existential framing," and she claimed we owe the dead a duty of "narrative fidelity" that respects not just discrete facts but the overall genre of a life as the subject herself understood and lived it. Leo responded that dead people hold no interests and therefore cannot be wronged; that biography is always selective interpretation, so Mara's standard is either impossible or arbitrary; and that translating a life into accessible dramatic forms is a way of honoring legacy rather than colonizing it. Mara then distinguished accessibility from appropriation, insisting that if the subject explicitly rejected the emotional register being used to market her story, then deploying that register is not respectful translation but exploitation. Leo countered that this reduces to an unverifiable metaphysical claim about posthumous "self-definition rights," and that if taken seriously it would forbid nearly all historical drama, since we can rarely be certain of any dead person's private self-conception.

Can you adjudicate this exchange move by move: assess Mara's concepts of genre coercion and narrative fidelity, Leo's interest-based and interpretation-based objections, Mara's accessibility-appropriation distinction, and Leo's reductio and metaphysical critique? For each step, say who is making the stronger point and why; identify any shared assumptions or overlooked distinctions (for instance, whether harm requires a subject who can experience it, or whether artistic form itself can carry moral weight); and conclude with who is more right overall and what each side still owes the other. Please keep your response to at most 400 words, using clear headings for each move.
