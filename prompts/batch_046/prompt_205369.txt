I was talking with my roommate about my aesthetics class and she kept asking me what rule the professor is actually using. So far in seminar she's ruled that Wagner's Ring is still a masterpiece despite his antisemitism, that Pound's Cantos are great poetry even though he was a fascist making broadcasts for Mussolini, that a documentary by a director later convicted of fraud counts as artistically valid as long as the footage itself is authentic, that a painter who abused models still made beautiful portraits but museums should label the abuse, that a novelist who plagiarized big chunks from some obscure source didn't produce good art since the plagiarism breaks the artistic achievement itself, and that a performance piece making the audience sit through mild discomfort is good art only if the discomfort actually serves its theme.

Next week's case is this: a well-known photographer takes intimate portraits of refugees in a camp, technically masterful and emotionally resonant, but she paid the subjects almost nothing, used a few images without full consent forms, and donated all proceeds back to the camp. The subjects themselves said they felt exploited, though the portraits don't show any visible suffering and actually present the subjects with dignaty. Do you think the professor will call this good art? I'm not asking for your own opinion - I want to know what her track record predicts. There seem to be at least a couple rules that could fit her past verdicts: maybe she seperates the artist's moral behavior from the work's value except when the achievement itself is fake, or maybe she thinks the immorality is only relevant when it's baked into the artistic purpose or material. Lay out the competing principles, say what each predicts for this photographer case, tell me which one you think she's running and why, and name a hypothetical case that would actually tell the rules apart.
