Focus specifcally on how each handles second-order vagueness — whether there's a sharp cutoff between clear cases and borderline cases. To keep it concrete, apply both views to the predicate "schmold", which applies to buildings between roughly 50 and 150 meters, but where the community has no convention about exactly 100-meter buildings and explicitly denies there's any hidden fact. Does the epistemicist have to posit an unknown sharp boundary at some precise height? Can the supervaluationist avoid that?

Student answer:
Epistemicism, like Williamson argues, holds that vague predicates actually have sharp boundaries, we just dont know where they lie. So for schmold there's a precise height — maybe 97.3 meters — where buildings switch from schmold to not-schmold. Our ignorance explains why 100m buildings seem borderline. For second-order vagueness, the epistemicist can say there's also a sharp line between definitely-schmold and borderline-schmold, and we're ignorant of that cutoff too. So they don't deny sharp lines exist; they just say we lack epistemic access to them.

Supervaluationism says there's no unique correct precisification of schmold. Instead there are many admissible ways to draw the line — at 80m, 100m, 120m, etc — and a sentence is true only if it's true on all precisifications. So "this 60m building is schmold" is true, "this 140m building is schmold" is false, and "this 100m building is schmold" lacks a truth value because it's true on some precisifications and false on others. This avoids hidden sharp boundaries because vagueness is semantic indeterminacy, not ignorance.

For higher-order vagueness, supervaluationists apply the scheme recursively: "definitely schmold" is true only if the building counts as schmold on every admissible precisification of every admissible precisification. So there's no sharp cutoff for the borderline region either — different precisifications just classify the borderline differently. This lets them fully accommodate the intuition that there's no hidden line, unlike the epistemicist who ends up multiplying unknown boundaries.
