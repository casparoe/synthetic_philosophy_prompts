I am preparing a research agenda for a small interdisciplinary seminar—half philosophers, half legal theorists—focused on the diachronic dimension of Karl Jaspers’ framework for collective guilt. Most existing discussion treats his four categories (political, criminal, moral, metaphysical) as static snapshots: can a group be guilty now? I want to push past that and ask whether guilt-claims can be sustained across generations once every original agent is dead, but I need to decide which conceptual problems deserve the limited time we have. To avoid rehearsing standard objections, I am injecting a distinction that has not been central in the literature: the difference between institutional successor liability, where a persisting state or organization inherits obligations because it continues to benefit from and administer structures built on past wrongs, and residual moral taint, where citizens are said to bear a non-transferable stain regardless of consent or benefit. The seminar’s deliverable is not a literature survey but a prioritized map of open lines of work.

Given that framing, which three clusters of open questions should dominate the agenda, and in what order? Specifically: first, is the coherence of diachronic institutional guilt conceptually upstream—does resolving whether guilt can attach to an institution without attaching to any current individual determine whether reparative obligations must be framed in the language of guilt at all? Second, how should we weigh the normative work done by ongoing unjust enrichment versus broken institutional legitimacy as independent grounds for repair—are these rivals to guilt-based reasoning or supplements to it? Third, does insisting on granular subgroup analysis (dissenting minorities, immigrants who arrived later, descendants of victims within the same borders) dissolve the very category of national guilt, or does it merely refine its application? For each, defend its priority relative to the others: which is most tractable for philosophers without empirical archives, which is most upstream for constitutional design and transitional justice policy, and which—if settled—would most change how practitioners actually think about memorialization and reparations? If you think one cluster is overrated or collapses into another, say so explicitly, and suggest whether there is a missing fourth problem—perhaps about the phenomenology of inherited shame versus assigned blame—that should displace one of these.
