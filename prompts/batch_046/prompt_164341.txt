I am writing the final exam for an upper-level seminar in epistemology and metaphysics, aimed at advanced undergraduates and beginning graduate students, and I need one essay prompt that students can answer in about seventy-five minutes with roughly eight hundred to one thousand words. The topic is G. E. Moore’s proof of an external world, but I want to avoid the standard textbook recitation in which Moore holds up his hands, declares that hands exist, and is then accused of begging the question against the skeptic. Instead, please build the exam question around a conceptual framing that forces students to excavate the unstated architecture of the argument: distinguish three layers in Moore’s move—first, the phenomenological datum of experiencing a hand-like appearance; second, the demonstrative act of ostensively identifying an object as present; and third, the metaphysical conclusion that there exists an object genuinely external to the mind. Ask students to reconstruct Moore’s proof through these three layers and then to evaluate whether the transition from the second to the third requires a suppressed premise about direct demonstrative reference—specifically, the assumption that successful ostension is incompatible with being a brain-in-a-vat, which is precisely the point the skeptic contests. Have them consider whether Moore could defend that suppressed premise without circularity, and whether, if that premise is removed, his argument collapses into a mere report of phenomenological certainty rather than a proof of ontological externality.

Please provide the following: first, the exam question itself, phrased so that a student must engage with the three-layer reconstruction and the demonstrative-reference problem rather than simply summarizing the standard circularity objection; second, a model answer of approximately seven hundred words that executes the layered reconstruction, makes the suppressed premise explicit, addresses the circularity worry at the logical level rather than the psychological level, and takes a clear, defended position on whether Moore’s proof succeeds or fails once the hidden premise is exposed; and third, a marking scheme totaling twenty-five marks, organized around five specific moves or distinctions that a top answer must contain. For each criterion, state the mark allocation, the precise content required for full marks, and a one-line note describing the characteristic error that should cost points—such as conflating the demonstrative layer with the phenomenological layer, treating the circularity objection as a mere failure of persuasion rather than a logical gap, or neglecting to assess what happens to the proof if the direct-reference premise is suspended. The question should not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, citations to specific editions, or references to obscure secondary literature; it must be answerable through conceptual analysis alone, using only ideas and distinctions that are well within the reach of a student who has studied Moore’s argument and basic philosophy of reference but has no access to external sources.
