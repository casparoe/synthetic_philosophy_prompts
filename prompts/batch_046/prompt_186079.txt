I'm a PhD student in political philosophy working on collective action and democratic theory, and I constructed the following argument for a workshop on climate ethics that now seems to spill over into democratic legitimacy in ways I can't contain. Here is the chain exactly as I presented it to my study group. (1) For an individual action to ground either a positive or negative moral obligation, there must hold a counterfactual dependency between that action and the relevant outcome: had the agent acted differently, holding all other conditions constant, the outcome would have differed in a morally significant respect. (2) In large-scale collective harms—climate change, industrial animal agriculture—no single emission, purchase, or boycott satisfies this dependency, because the harm is structurally overdetermined by millions of parallel contributions operating below any discontinuity threshold. (3) Therefore, on causal-efficacy grounds, individuals have no direct obligation to reduce carbon footprints or to abstain from factory-farmed products. (4) Democratic practices are also collective phenomena governed by aggregation rules: in any large electorate, no single vote satisfies the counterfactual dependency with respect to the electoral outcome. (5) So if the condition in (1) applies generally, individuals have no obligation to vote, and—more severely—no individual consent can ground democratic authority, since authority requires that citizens' choices actually produce the binding outcomes they are subject to. (6) Yet democratic legitimacy plainly survives the statistical irrelevance of single votes; we treat outcomes as binding even when we know our vote was not pivotal. (7) Hence either the counterfactual dependency condition fails for obligations within collective structures, or democratic legitimacy lacks the causal foundation it appears to require. (8) But if (1) is rejected, the classic inefficacy objection to climate obligations collapses with it, since that objection precisely denies obligations wherever counterfactual dependency is absent. I am especially reluctant to abandon the intuition in (1) that obligations need some kind of causal traction, and I do not want to undermine democratic legitimacy either. Which step is the weak link—perhaps a hidden shift between the dependency required for negative duties versus participatory rights, or perhaps an equivocation in how "holding other conditions constant" operates across linear versus threshold-based aggregation? Tell me precisely where to cut, why that premise initially looked defensible, and what I am committed to paying if I reject it.
