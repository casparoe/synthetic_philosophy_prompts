I've more or less bought into the idea that "collapse" is mostly a historiographical mirage—what we label collapse in the Bronze Age, the Classic Maya, and Easter Island is really just the end of one kind of record-keeping and monument-bulding, not a societal failure. The people are still there, just reorginized into smaller-scale networks that don't leave the kind of traces archeologists like. So societies don't fail; they just become invisible to us.

What would actually count against this? I want the honest defeaters—not just "but there was violence," but what specific patterns in the evidence, or what arguments about persistence and continuity, would force me to concede that something genuinely broke rather than just changed shape? Which of these are live given what we actually know about those three cases, and which ones do defenders of the "reorganization not collapse" view just wave away? And be real with me: has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability—if every bad outcome gets redescribed as "just a different kind of continuity," is that a problem for the position, or is it actually its strength?

Also, if there's a result or comparison from outside these three—say, about how we detect continuity vs discontinuity in other contexts—that should change my mind, I want that too. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a committed defender of this view would themselves admit are damaging, versus which they'd dismiss as missing the point.
