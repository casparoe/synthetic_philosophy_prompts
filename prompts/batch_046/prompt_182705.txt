In my seminar on ancient biography and moral formation, I have been developing the view that hagiographic and Plutarchan biography does not merely distort a recoverable historical self but actively generates what I call a moral hyperfigure: a unified practical identity that the subject never fully instantiated, yet which functions as the necessary object of emulation. On my reading, Plutarch's pairing of Greek and Roman lives in the Parallel Lives does not invite direct imitation of either figure, but constructs an interstitial gap that forces the reader to synthesize virtues distributed across both lives, so that moral learning occurs through navigating tension rather than copying conduct. I hold that this makes idealization not an epistemic defect but a functional condition of biography's pedagogical power. What would genuinely count against this position? I want the observations, arguments, and conceptual distinctions that should move me: for example, whether ancient reception of these texts supports a synthetic rather than imitative reading; whether empirical work on moral exemplars shows that coherence undermines rather than enables practical identification; whether my view collapses into a fictionalism that severs biography from actual lives and thus eliminates the specific authority of lived example; and whether I have insulated the claim by allowing any historical discrepancy to be reinterpreted as part of the hyperfigure's construction, rendering it unfalsifiable. Please distinguish what its defenders would concede as damaging from what critics alone allege, rank the potential defeaters by decisiveness, and include an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into invulnerability — and whether that is itself a problem for a theory of moral learning.
