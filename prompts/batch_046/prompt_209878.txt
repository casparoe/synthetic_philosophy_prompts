I have been working through my own judgments about when post-war tribunals become instances of victors' justice or impermissible retroactive law, and I want to test whether they hang together under a single principle. Here is the pattern. I think the Nuremberg prosecution of major German leaders for crimes against humanity was legitimate justice, not retroactive lawmaking, because the conduct was already condemnable by norms accessible within the defendants' own legal and moral framework. I think the Tokyo Tribunal's charge of conspiracy to wage aggressive war was closer to impermissible retroactivity, since in 1941 international treaty law had not clearly established that planning a war of aggression was an individual criminal offense. I think a hypothetical 1948 Soviet tribunal that tried American officials for the bombing of Dresden and Tokyo under a newly drafted statute on systematic destruction of civilian populations would be victors' justice in reverse, even though the underlying acts were grave. But I think that if a genuinely multilateral court in 1950—composed of judges from neutral, defeated, and victorious nations—had applied that same 1948 statute to Soviet officials responsible for the Katyn massacre, the proceeding would have been legitimate, despite the retroactive statute. Finally, I think the 1921 Leipzig Trials failed not because of retroactivity—the German penal code already contained applicable provisions—but because the tribunal lacked institutional independence: the defendants' own state retained effective prosecutorial and judicial control. These five verdicts seem to pull in different directions: one emphasizes accessibility of norms, another the clarity of prior law, a third institutional composition, and a fourth structural independence from the victor. Is there one precise principle—perhaps combining conditions on normative accessibility and on the tribunal's institutional relationship to the accused's prior legal system—that delivers all five? If no single principle can save them all, which verdict should I overturn, and why? Please state your principle with the specific case that motivates each clause, give its verdict on an unlisted case—namely, a hypothetical 1962 court with equal representation from former Axis and Allied powers applying a 1961 treaty defining ecological warfare retroactively to a 1945 strategic decision involving large-scale river-diversion bombing in the Pacific theater—and honestly say which of my five judgments it cannot accommodate.
