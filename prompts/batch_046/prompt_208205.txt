For my graduate seminar in comparative epistemology—twelve students, seventy-five minutes—I want a structured deliberation that makes participants feel, through rules and payoffs rather than through any preliminary lecture, the practical pressure against Jain anekāntavāda, and specifically the difficulty of maintaining syādvāda (conditional, standpoint-relative predication) when a group must reach a collective decision.

Design a simulation called the Council of Partial Views. Invent a contested case in which physical, historical, functional, and geographic evidence all point in different directions—for instance, whether to decommission an aging water infrastructure project that sustains local agriculture, overlays an unexcavated archaeological stratum, and displaces a long-standing settlement. Do not use the parable of the blind men and the elephant.

Divide the students into seven roles, each assigned one naya (standpoint) translated into modern evidentiary categories: substantial (material and ecological data), modal (functional and relational impacts), temporal (historical trajectory and future projection), spatial (geographic and demographic distribution), and three synthetic standpoints that combine pairs. Give each role access only to the dataset corresponding to its naya. Every spoken intervention must be formulated using the formal structure of saptabhaṅgī—the sevenfold conditional predication—so that no student may issue an unconditional assertion; each contribution must take the form of one of the conditional modes (e.g., “in this respect it is X,” “in this respect it is not-X,” “in this respect it is indescribable,” etc.). However, the council’s procedural rules must reward only binary resolutions (approve/reject/defer with cost) and must penalize sustained ambiguity with a collective-cost mechanism: every round in which the council fails to render a binding decision depletes a shared resource representing institutional legitimacy or community trust.

Arrange the incentives so that students experience the structural pull toward single-sided, unconditional judgment organically—specifically, design the point system so that one student who abandons the conditional syntax to secure a decisive vote triggers a cascade in which others follow suit to protect their interests, and the group thereby “solves” the problem only by collapsing the seven standpoints into one. The facilitator should say almost nothing before the activity begins; the concept should be named only in the debrief.

Then provide a debrief of about fifteen minutes that does the following. First, map specific moments in the deliberation onto parts of anekāntavāda: the division of roles onto the naya framework; the mandatory syntax onto syādvāda; the cascade of unconditional assertions onto the failure to resist ekānta (one-sidedness); and the collective-cost mechanism onto the social impossibility of sustaining omniscient (kevala-jñāna) judgment in a finite body. Second, predict what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run—such as one dominant speaker collapsing all standpoints into their own evidentiary mode, or the group inventing a false compromise that retains binary voting but strips away the conditional form—and explain exactly how the facilitator should intervene without resolving the tension for them. Third, be frank about which feature of Jain doctrine the simulation cannot reproduce: specifically, the soteriological motivation for holding multiple viewpoints as a spiritual discipline oriented toward non-violence and epistemic humility, rather than as a procedural inconvenience in bureaucratic decision-making. Explain precisely what the facilitator must supply verbally to supply that missing dimension.

Write in a formal, polished register suitable for describing a seminar exercise to a colleague. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not lecture about Jainism before the activity begins; the framework should emerge only in the debrief, after the students have constructed the failure themselves.
