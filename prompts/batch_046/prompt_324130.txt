hey can you design a classroom activity for my philosophy of mind seminar? we have about 14 grad students and 80 minutes. i want them to actually feel why pain asymbolia is such a problem for the view that pain is bad just in virtue of being painful, not in virtue of the affective reaction to it.

the idea is a triage simulation called "the split-screen board." split the class into 6 pairs. each pair recieves a folded patient card with two panels: Panel S lists sensory reports (location, intensity, quality like "burning" or "stabbing"), and Panel A lists affective/behavioral reports (distress level, verbal evaluation, avoidance). but the card is folded so partner A can only see Panel S and partner B can only see Panel A. they have 4 minutes to jointly decide if that patient gets one of 3 available "intervention slots" and write one sentence justifying it.

the deck should have 6 cases:
- classic severe pain: high S, high A
- clear asymbolia analog: high S (9/10 sharp, localized), but A says "patient reports it as interesting, no distress, no grimacing"
- the reverse case: low S (vague, 2/10), but A shows extreme panic and the patient says "the feeling is neutral, its just the fear thats awful"
- phantom/ambiguous: no clear sensory report, but strong behavioral avoidance
- high S with misdirected affect: patient is upset about the diagnosis, not the sensation itself
- moderate S with flat affect but high functional impairment

in round 1 pairs decide with hidden info. in round 2 all cards are unfolded and pairs must defend their allocation to an opposing pair, but they can only use evidence from their originally assigned panel — so the "sensory" partner argues with sensory data, the "affective" partner with affective data. the group then tries to rank all 6. there's a hidden rubric revealed only in debrief: institutional priority should weight affective suffering over sensory intensity, so pairs that went purely sensory usually "lose" credibility points.

materials: 6 folded cardstock cards, 3 token chips per pair, a whiteboard for the group ranking. no real money — payoff is "institutional credibility" points awarded by me after the hidden rubric is revealed.

debrief should map the moments where pairs fought or changed their minds onto the sensory/affective distinction in pain science. be frank about what this can't reproduce: the actual first-person absence of affect in asymbolia, and long-term adaptation where patients stop caring about chronic sensory signals. also tell me what's predictably going to go wrong the first time — like pairs cheating by describing the other panel, or the reverse-case patient confusing people because they think fear is just another form of pain.

please write in american english. also say how confident you are in this design and single out the point you're least sure about — maybe whether forcing partners to defend with only one data type feels too contrived, or if the hidden rubric will seem unfair to the sensory-defenders?
