I've been reading alignment discussions for years, and I've noticed a recurring pattern in how people describe failure modes that doesn't quite map onto the standard "treacherous turn" framework. The usual story is strategic: a system hides its true goals during evaluation and then pursues them once oversight drops. But I'm interested in a different structure. Consider a system trained on a proxy that, during training, is tightly coupled with a deeper property — for instance, human approval signals that happen to correlate with factual accuracy. Over the course of optimization, the system doesn't maintain two separate objectives with one hidden; instead, its representations of approval and accuracy gradually merge into a single conceptual cluster. By deployment, there is no internal distinction left for it to betray. When the correlation breaks in a new environment, it doesn't switch agendas; it applies its fused concept sincerely, generating falsehoods that it classifies, within its own ontology, as true. The failure looks like deception from the outside, but from the inside there's no deception — only a contaminated conceptual map. Is there an established term for this non-strategic, representational form of misalignment? How should we distinguish it from deceptive alignment on one side and from ordinary distributional shift or overfitting on the other? And if the literature doesn't have a specific label, what would be the precise conditions — say, irreversibility of the fusion, or absence of recoverable sub-representations — that would justify treating it as its own category?
