I've been turning over the democratic-backsliding literature since a LessWrong thread years ago that treated Hungary and Poland as canonical cases, and I keep wondering whether the concept does genuine analytical work or mostly gives observers a polished partisan label for regimes they already dislike. To press on that, I want to apply a distinction I haven't seen formalized in the standard texts—procedural erosion, meaning formal constitutional or legal mechanisms are rewritten or suspended to concentrate authority, versus normative drift, meaning the informal expectations that make opposition, dissent, and institutional autonomy legitimate dissolve without any corresponding change in written rules—to a real episode that seems to contain both in rapid succession.

Consider Turkey in the two years following the failed coup attempt of July 15, 2016. The government declared a state of emergency lasting twenty-one months, used emergency decrees to dismiss well over a hundred thousand civil servants and shutter media outlets, and then held an April 2017 constitutional referendum that abolished the office of prime minister and sharply expanded presidential powers. At the same time, the treatment of opposition parties—particularly the HDP and later the CHP—shifted in ways that often did not depend on new statutes: elected mayors were replaced by government-appointed trustees, party leaders faced prosecution under long-existing anti-terror laws, and the informal boundary between ruling-party rhetoric and state action collapsed. Analyze this episode through the erosion-versus-drift lens: map specific moves onto each category, and ask whether separating them explains why observers split so sharply—some seeing authoritarian consolidation, others seeing legitimate crisis governance after an existential security threat. Then reverse the analysis: if both categories are present, does "democratic backsliding" add explanatory value beyond "the regime changed in directions I disapprove of"? Who gets to adjudicate the classification—domestic voters, international democracy indices, or the opposition itself—and does that adjudication reveal that the concept is inseparable from the normative commitments of the classifier? Finally, apply the same lens briefly to a structurally different case, such as the United States after the 2020 election certification disputes, but do so in a way that tests the framework rather than reproducing familiar partisan narratives. I am not looking for citations or a literature survey; I want a disciplined conceptual mapping that says where the theory is silent as well as where it speaks.
