Read Sidney Lumet's 1957 film Twelve Angry Men through the lens of the Condorcet jury theorem and its modern critiques in collective epistemology. The theorem holds that if each member of a group is independently correct with probability greater than one-half, majority aggregation yields accuracy that increases with group size; yet the film depicts precisely the dynamics the theorem excludes—sequential deliberation, emotional contagion, social pressure, and a single initially dissenting juror who gradually converts the others through narrative persuasion rather than parallel evaluation. Analyze where the film respects or violates the theorem's conditions: are the jurors' initial errors uncorrelated, or do they share biases about class, age, and evidence that make their mistakes systematically aligned? Does Juror 8 operate as a high-competence outlier whose presence rescues an otherwise biased group, or does he function as a catalyst that destroys independence and replaces it with informational dependence? Consider specifically whether the resolution vindicates collective judgment under heterogeneous competence—where the group is accurate not because every member exceeds the 0.5 threshold but because one expert elevates the others—or whether it instead shows that deliberation improves outcomes precisely by abandoning the independence assumption that makes the formal result work. What does the dramatic structure, in which conviction collapses through rhetorical and emotional shifts rather than through simultaneous independent voting, add to philosophical debates about whether juries should deliberate at all?
