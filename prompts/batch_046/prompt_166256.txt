I am composing a philosophical dialogue for a novel in which Immanuel Kant, imagined as a living interlocutor in our present century, is asked to address the knowability paradox associated with Fitch. I do not want a general summary of that paradox, nor bibliographic suggestions, nor appeals to secondary literature. Instead, I want you to reconstruct, strictly from within Kant's critical system, how he might diagnose the tension between the principle that every truth is in principle knowable and the derivation that every truth is therefore actually known. Specifically, would he treat the paradox as arising from a category mistake—applying modal categories of possibility and actuality to the totality of truths as though they formed a single object of cognition? Would his distinction between phenomenal knowledge, governed by the conditions of possible experience, and noumenal ignorance, in which no cognition is warranted, lead him to reject the premise that the set of all truths constitutes a legitimate domain for epistemic modalization? Furthermore, how might he respond to the contemporary resolution that distinguishes actual human knowers from idealized epistemic agents? Please construct your answer using only conceptual resources internal to Kant's philosophy—such as the transcendental unity of apperception, the regulative use of ideas, and the principle that epistemic possibility must be grounded in the conditions of experience—without naming later philosophical movements. I am particularly interested in whether he would regard the paradox as a reductio of unrestricted epistemic optimism, or rather as confirmation that the notion of omniscience exceeds the boundaries of a coherent regulative idea for human reason. Conclude by indicating which modern dissolution of the paradox, if any, he would find least objectionable, and why.
