I'm running a small aesthetics seminar with about 11 grad students, 90 minutes, pretty low-key. Last month I learned that the "original" sketch hanging in my parents' place — the one my dad always called the family Picasso — was actually done by his college roommate in 1987, same paper, same charcoal, literally indistinguishable from the real thing he was copying. I kept looking at it and felt this wierd negative pull, like the lines hadn't changed but the value had evaporated. I want an in-class activity that makes them hit that same wall themselves, without me lecturing about Benjamin or whatever.

I'm thinking of a game called The Vault Problem but I need you to actually design the rules, materials, and debrief. There should be six object cards — each card shows an image and a description, but there's a hidden provenance card. Round one: students get 100 budget points each and bid blind, just on the visual/description. Round two: flip the provenance — some originals, some perfect forgeries, one "authorized workshop copy" made with leftover materials from the actual studio. They rebid knowing the history. Then the twist: reveal that one "original" had 65% of its visible surface repainted by a conservator in the 90s, and the workshop copy was actually signed off by the artist's estate after death. They have exactly three "vault slots" and must defend which go in.

Can you flesh out the full mechanics — payoff scheme without real money, what predictably breaks the first time (I think people will just hoard points in round one?), and a debrief that maps each round onto the philosophical tension? I especially want them to feel genetic essentialism — that causal history matters — but then get destabilized by the restoration case. Also please inject one slightly wierd object into the set: maybe a forgery that was made with malicious intent but accidentally fixed a compositional flaw in the original, or a case where the original was destroyed and only the malicious copy survives. Don't make them cite anyone; the idea should come out in the bidding and the arguments. And tell me honestly which part — probably the actual phenomenological "aura" thing? — this game just can't reproduce, so I know what I have to supply verbally at the end.
