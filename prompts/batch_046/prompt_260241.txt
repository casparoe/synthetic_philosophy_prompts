I work as a distributed-systems engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I have been reading about sortition and democratic design, and I have constructed an argument that seems to prove far more than defenders of lot-based selection usually intend. I would like you to tell me where it fails, if it does, and what I must give up to fix it.

The argument runs as follows:

(1) A democratic mechanism for selecting holders of public authority is legitimate only if it is Sybil-resistant—that is, no actor or coalition can deterministically engineer the outcome in their own favor.

(2) Voting, credential-based appointment, and patronage are all vulnerable to strategic coordination—campaigning, coalition-building, gatekeeping—that deterministically biases results; in distributed-systems terms, they allow Byzantine nodes to influence leader election.

(3) Cryptographically verifiable random selection eliminates deterministic influence, because the selection seed is computationally unpredictable even to the authority administering the draw.

(4) Therefore, democratic legitimacy requires sortition for every position that exercises delegated public authority.

(5) The same reasoning applies to expert appointment: selecting an epidemiologist or an engineer deterministically selects for a particular skill profile, which is itself a form of engineered outcome.

(6) Hence, if (4) holds, then appointing specialists to technical boards, regulatory agencies, or emergency bodies is also illegitimate.

(7) But we do not regard expert appointment as illegitimate.

(8) So we must either reject (1)—and accept that democratic legitimacy tolerates some strategic influence—or accept that all governance roles, including highly technical ones, must be filled by lot.

I am inclined to keep (1) and (3), because the intuition that randomness is uniquely capture-resistant feels technically sound, but I want to understand exactly what I pay for that commitment. Is there a hidden equivocation between "strategic influence" and "deterministic engineering," a scope error in applying leader-election logic to role assignment, or is the argument actually valid and the real choice simply which plausible premise to sacrifice? Please examine the numbered steps, identify the problematic transition if one exists, explain why it looked plausible, and price the alternatives—especially what rejecting (1) costs versus what accepting (6) costs.
