I am a novelist who has been asked to deliver the commencement address at a small liberal-arts college whose graduating class includes naturalized citizens, military families, and refugees recently resettled. The occasion demands celebration, yet I want the speech to be structured around a philosophical problem rather than merely decorated with one: is patriotism a virtue or a vice? Rather than rehearse the familiar dialectic, I propose an unconventional framing that should govern the entire address—patriotism understood not as loyalty to a state or culture, but as a narrative virtue: the disciplined willingness to remain an obligated character in a collective story one did not author and cannot fully control. This framing must do genuine structural work. It should license praise for the graduates' persistence and participation, but it should also render certain statements impossible: I must not call dissent a form of betrayal, nor treat national identity as a finished achievement that demands uncritical endorsement. The room will contain philosophers and historians attuned to the conceptual architecture, alongside family members who need to feel addressed rather than instructed. Write a ten-minute address in a literary, first-person voice that moves between these audiences without simplifying the idea. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to specific philosophical texts; build the argument solely from the framing itself. Avoid both triumphalist nationalism and reflexive condemnation; let the narrative conception of patriotism determine precisely what can be affirmed and what must remain open to revision.
