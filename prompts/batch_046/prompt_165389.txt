I am writing an independent study on bounded rationality and have become interested in exactly how different theories explain the moment a decision-maker stops searching for better options. Could you rank the following four frameworks—Herbert Simon’s aspiration-level satisficing (stopping once an option clears an internal, adjustable threshold), Gerd Gigerenzer’s ecological rationality of fast-and-frugal heuristics (stopping via simple rules matched to environmental structure), the resource-rational analysis of cognition (treating mental computation itself as a costly resource to be traded against decision accuracy), and Mullainathan and Shafir’s scarcity-capture model (tunneling of attention under severe resource deprivation)—according to how well each explains stopping rules that are driven by internal thresholds rather than by purely external constraints such as time or information limits? Please assign each framework a score from 1 to 10 and justify that score in a single sentence.

Separately, I want to test these frameworks against a more obscure case that my teacher proposed: a decision-maker stops searching not because any available option is good enough, but because continuing requires first resolving an ontological ambiguity—for instance, whether a particular outcome even counts as “success” at all—and the cognitive cost of resolving that ambiguity exceeds any probable gain from further search. Let us call this “ontological satisficing.” For each of the four frameworks above, indicate whether it can accommodate this case by extending its existing mechanism, or whether accommodating it requires importing an entirely different kind of rationality altogether.
