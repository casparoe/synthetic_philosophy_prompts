Many museum cataloging systems record an artwork's properties—medium, dimensions, creator—and its relations to other entities—“depicts a saint,” “was restored by X,” “belongs to collection Y”—in one of two ways. Some standards treat relations as bare links or foreign keys with no independent entry; others treat them as first-class records that carry their own attributes, such as certainty level, date of attribution, or evidentiary source. The first design seems to treat a relational claim as nothing over and above its relata, while the second seems to require that every relational fact itself be bound to its terms by a further relation, threatening an infinite hierarchy.

What philosophical commitments about the unity of propositions and the reality of relations does each cataloging standard presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit ontology: does the bare-link view depend on a hidden non-relational binder—perhaps the database schema itself or the cataloguer’s judgment—and does the entity-relation view genuinely escape Bradley’s regress or merely displace it onto metadata relations? Distinguish what each practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, say whether the choice between them is philosophically forced or underdetermined, and specify what a cataloging practice built on an explicit theory of propositional unity would have to change, for instance in how it records attribution, depicts relations, or handles conflicting sources.
