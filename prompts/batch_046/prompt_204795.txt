Analyze the 1965–1968 U.S. escalation in Vietnam—the incremental commitment of ground forces from the initial Marine landing at Da Nang in March 1965 to more than half a million troops by 1968, coupled with the refusal to reverse course despite rising internal assessments that military victory was improbable—through the lens of the dollar auction model and the escalation-of-commitment framework. Begin by mapping the episode onto the auction mechanics with discipline: who counts as the bidders (the Johnson administration collectively, the U.S. as a strategic actor, or the interaction with North Vietnamese decision-makers?), what the prize is (the credibility of American commitments, the political survival of South Vietnam, or domestic prestige?), what the bids are measured in (troop levels, bombing tonnage, political capital, and foregone domestic priorities?), and what the payoff structure implies once cumulative costs clearly exceed the expected value of winning. Then apply the model's prediction: rational actors should cut losses before the bids surpass the prize, yet the administration kept bidding. Show precisely where that prediction aligns with the record—particularly the 1965–1966 increments after Pentagon and intelligence assessments had already questioned feasibility—and where it clashes with it. Next, identify at least two structural features of the Vietnam case that the dollar-auction framing either misdescribes or leaves silent: for example, whether the adversary faced a symmetric payoff structure; whether organizational dynamics such as bureaucratic self-justification or group-level escalation operate analogously to individual rational bidding; and whether strategic signaling to allies and adversaries transforms the interaction into a war of attrition or a commitment game rather than a pure auction. Conclude by assessing what the episode reveals about the theory itself—whether it serves as a clean confirmation that psychological or strategic escalation overrides cost-benefit rationality, as an anomaly that requires distinguishing sunk-cost psychology from rational commitment strategies, or as an unanticipated case because its stakes were geopolitical and multi-actor rather than monetary and dyadic. Throughout, separate what is broadly settled about the decision dynamics of this period from interpretations that remain genuinely contested among historians and political scientists, and do not present any disputed claim—such as the precise weight of credibility concerns relative to domestic political motives, or LBJ's private beliefs about the likelihood of victory—as if it were the consensus view.
