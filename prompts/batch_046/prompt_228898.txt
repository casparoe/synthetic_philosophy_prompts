You are an expert in applied philosophy and technology ethics. Before you construct the deliverable, spend a brief moment brainstorming the precise tension between Chesterton's fence—the principle that one should not remove a barrier without knowing why it was erected—and Burkean conservatism, which treats long-surviving practices as repositories of distributed knowledge that should be changed only incrementally. Keep that tension in view as you work.

I am a senior software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. I need a reusable decision procedure that my team can apply repeatedly—roughly twice a week—when we propose deprecating, removing, or radically refactoring a legacy code module, internal feature flag, workflow, or organizational process. The stakes are moderate-to-high: incorrect removals cause outages or compliance failures; correct removals reduce maintenance burden. I typically have thirty minutes to review a proposal, incomplete documentation is the norm, institutional memory is fragmentary, and I must sign off with a brief written justification to engineering leadership within a two-hour window.

Translate Burkean conservatism and Chesterton's fence into an operational protocol for this role. Specifically:

1. Define an ordered sequence of practical tests—no more than six—that a tech lead can execute in fifteen to thirty minutes using only internal documentation, dependency graphs, traffic logs, and short interviews with available staff. Present them as a flowchart in words.

2. Within the procedure, distinguish explicitly between a Chesterton fence (a component or rule whose purpose is currently unknown) and a Burkean prescription (a practice that has survived long enough to embed solutions to distributed problems the team no longer fully perceives). Specify how the procedure treats the two categories differently, and identify the decision node where the distinction must be made despite missing records.

3. Flag every point where judgment cannot be eliminated and where the lead must make a call without full information. Also flag the organizational conditions—such as total loss of institutional memory or absence of dependency telemetry—under which the procedure should not be used and the proposal must be escalated.

4. Apply the procedure step-by-step to three worked cases:
   a. A deprecated internal API endpoint with zero documented consumers but persistent non-zero traffic, proposed for removal to clean up architecture. The procedure should recommend retention; explain clearly why this answer will feel wrong to me as an engineer who values system cleanliness.
   b. A three-day manual approval workflow added after a 2019 security incident; there is no written post-mortem, only two senior staff members recall the event, and the workflow now delays every release.
   c. A configuration-flag system that has grown to approximately four hundred interdependent flags, where removing a single flag risks cascading behavioral changes across services with no centralized map of interactions.

5. For each case, state the procedure's output, the specific justification text I should write for leadership, and any required follow-up actions.

6. Conclude by stating what the procedure explicitly cannot do: which classes of legacy artifacts fall outside its scope, where it will systematically misfire, and what kind of organizational decay renders it unreliable.

Write formally and precisely. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote philosophers or other authors verbatim; paraphrase their ideas without quotation marks. Keep the language accessible to an engineer without philosophical training, but preserve the conceptual distinctions that make the procedure honest rather than a simplistic checklist.
