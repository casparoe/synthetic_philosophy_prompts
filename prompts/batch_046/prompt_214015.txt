As a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of mind, I have been studying Douglas Hofstadter's proposal that the self is not a substance but a strange loop — a self-referential symbol, the first-person symbol I, that emerges from feedback within a representational system. I want to move beyond familiar summaries and calibrate my own view precisely.

Please provide your credence, expressed as a specific number between 0 and 1, that the strange-loop account is the best available explanation of phenomenal selfhood — not merely of narrative self-modeling or functional self-reference. Decompose this into at least four parts, each with a rough probability and a one-line justification:

1. The probability that a non-substantialist, pattern-based ontology of mind is correct in principle.
2. The probability that a strange-loop structure specifically generates first-person phenomenology, rather than only a functional self-model, including the possibility that the loop might be distributed across a biological brain and an integrated external symbolic prosthesis (a sustained cognitive extension that becomes part of the feedback architecture) and still instantiate the same self.
3. The probability that neuroscience could, in principle, empirically distinguish a strange-loop mechanism from bundle-theory or global-workspace alternatives.
4. The probability that framing the issue in terms of the hard problem does not distort the evaluation — that is, that we are not demanding the loop perform explanatory work that actually requires a primitive subject.

Then combine these terms into your overall number, with a brief explanation of how you weighted them. Identify which term is the weakest link — the one where new conceptual or empirical evidence would shift your overall credence the most.

Please present the answer in American English, using standard American spelling and punctuation conventions.
