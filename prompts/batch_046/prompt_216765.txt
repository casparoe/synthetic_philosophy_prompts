Two colleagues, Jordan and Mira, argued over lunch about whether the “dirty hands” problem in politics is a genuine moral category or just consequentialism with rhetorical dressing. (1) Jordan said it is real: when a leader authorizes torture to avert catastrophe, she acquires a distinctive representational stain—she acts not merely as a private agent but as the embodied will of those she governs, so the wrong is structurally different from ordinary personal failure. (2) Mira replied that this only relabels agent-relative deontology; either torture is impermissible and should be refused, or it is permitted under extreme conditions with no moral residue, and “stain” is just emotional coloring added to an ordinary harm-benefit calculus. (3) Jordan countered that dirty hands also involves a second-order epistemic rupture—the leader must come to see herself as someone capable of ordering such acts, altering her practical identity in a way that is reducible neither to guilt nor to the balance sheet of outcomes. (4) Mira answered that framing this as an “epistemic rupture” romanticizes ordinary moral trauma; if the act was genuinely necessary, the agent should integrate it without mystifying psychological cost, and treating self-knowledge as an independent moral category risks turning political judgment into therapy. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four steps, say who is more right overall, and note what both are missing. Respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
