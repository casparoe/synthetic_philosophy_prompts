For a graduate seminar on normative decision theory, specify the full set of desiderata that an adequate framework for choice under uncertainty must satisfy if it is designed to reject expected-value fanaticism—the willingness to accept arbitrarily large risks of catastrophic failure in exchange for arbitrarily large but uncertain rewards—without collapsing into arbitrary utility caps or ad hoc domain restrictions. The framework should cover both individual prudential choice and moral aggregation across populations, since fanaticism appears in both registers.

List each requirement with its motivating case or argument: which phenomena must be accommodated (e.g., the St. Petersburg paradox, Allais-style preference reversals, longtermist aggregation over future generations, and the intuition that near-certain extinction is not compensated by sufficiently large expected welfare); which distinctions must be preserved (e.g., risk aversion versus bounded-utility risk neutrality, epistemic versus practical uncertainty, scale-sensitive versus scale-invariant reasoning in moral aggregation, and the difference between rejecting a specific gamble and rejecting all unbounded maximization); which formal conditions must be respected (e.g., consistency with Savage-style axioms where defensible, compatibility with causal decision theory, immunity to Dutch-book exploitation in finite problems, and preservation of transitivity and completeness in well-behaved domains); and which verdicts must be determinate versus permissible to leave open.

For each desideratum, flag whether it is non-negotiable or whether it represents a camp-specific preference—such as the demand for axiomatic elegance versus the demand for robustness against scale-driven absurdities. Then address the central structural question: whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable given standard representation theorems, particularly whether blocking unbounded expected-utility maximization while retaining continuity and independence forces a breakdown in completeness or generates a new family of paradoxes.

Keep the conceptual weight on the criteria, their interactions, and their justifications rather than on grading existing theories, though a brief comparative note on how standard expected-utility theory, risk-weighted expected-utility frameworks, and a two-tiered model with domain-specific scaling rules fare against the list is appropriate.
