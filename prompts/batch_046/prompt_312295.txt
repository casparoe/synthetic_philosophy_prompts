Write a deadpan internal corporate memorandum, approximately 600 words, issued by the fictional Office of Interhemispheric Integration at a post-surgical neuroscience clinic. The memo should announce a new "Unified Consciousness Governance Policy" for patients following total corpus callosotomy, formatted with numbered sections, bullet-point compliance directives, and a sidebar on liability. The satire should derive from treating established split-brain phenomena—left-hemisphere interpreter confabulation, right-hemisphere-initiated action without verbal report, alien-hand conflict, and the absence of a central executive after callosal section—as routine human-resources and legal-compliance problems. Address, without resolving, the following: which hemisphere holds signing authority for informed-consent renewals; whether the left hemisphere's post-hoc rationalizations count as binding institutional statements when the right hemisphere has executed a contradictory motor plan; how to adjudicate preference conflicts expressed simultaneously by each hand; and whether the clinic should recognize one or two subjects of moral responsibility for a single post-surgical body. The voice must remain bureaucratically neutral and deadpan; the irony should emerge from the mismatch between administrative formality and the philosophical gravity of the underlying issues. The piece should exaggerate real clinical features—the interpreter module's compulsive narrative construction, the right hemisphere's independent intentional capacity, the literal disunity of volition after section—rather than invent false ones, and it should stay internally consistent: do not allow the memo's author to collapse into either an unexamined unitary-self assumption or an explicit dual-subject conclusion. By the end, a reader familiar with debates on the unity of consciousness should be able to detect which implicit metaphysical position—perhaps a bundle or no-self framework applied intra-cranially—structures the liability framework, without that position being named. Do not include citations, named philosophers, bibliographies, or references to specific studies by author and date.
