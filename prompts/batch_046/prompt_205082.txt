As a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy, I have been struck by the contrast between explanation in the natural sciences and in historiography. In physics, we often appeal to covering-law explanations, subsuming particular events under general regularities. In history, by contrast, narrative explanation appears to organize particular events into a coherent temporal sequence without necessarily invoking universal laws. I would like you to compare these two explanatory forms—not merely as rival methodologies, but with respect to a more specific conceptual question. Can narrative explanation be reconstructed as a kind of non-law-based mechanistic explanation, one that traces causal dependencies step-by-step through a complex system, much as one might follow the evolution of a statistical mechanical ensemble without deriving each intermediate state from a deterministic law? Or does the narrative form introduce an irreducible mode of understanding, comparable perhaps to the emergent macro-level descriptions in statistical mechanics that resist full reduction to micro-level dynamics? In your comparison, please address whether adopting a narrative structure in history necessarily abandons the explanatory rigor characteristic of natural science, or whether it establishes a distinct but equally legitimate form of comprehension—one that might even illuminate how we understand law-governed systems when complete reduction is impractical.
