I'm presenting to a cross-disciplinary seminar — economists, philosophers of value, and several longtermist-oriented rationalists — my claim that Keynes's 1930 essay Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren advances a normative vision of the good life centered on non-instrumental leisure, aesthetic cultivation, and the deliberate social suppression of accumulation motives that is structurally incompatible with both modern growth economics and with the instrumentalized leisure norms common in effective-altruist and rationalist circles. I argue Keynes was not predicting a utopia of consumption but prescribing a post-economic culture in which the drive to accumulate wealth is treated as a semi-pathological motive to be engineered out of social life, not optimized around, and in which the art of living well becomes the central human project once material scarcity is solved. The audience includes two economists who will treat his forecast failure as a reason to dismiss the normative argument, a philosopher committed to objective-list theories of well-being who will ask why leisure and aesthetics should trump achievement or knowledge, and several longtermists who will read my rejection of value accumulation as a dangerous form of value-lock-in or anti-growth bias that threatens long-run welfare maximization. Give me the objections in the order they will come, phrased as each camp would phrase them: the empirical dismissal, the objective-list challenge, the longtermist recasting, and the hidden concern about authoritarian preference engineering. For each, note what I should concede up front — especially Keynes's poor forecasting record — what I should hold, where my reply is weakest, and the reply itself. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks: whether my reading requires attributing to Keynes an explicit theory of intergenerational value drift that the text does not support. Flag my weakest point: I have not specified the transition mechanism to Keynes's post-economic culture without either centralized planning or the kind of preference-manipulation that rationalists typically reject.
