I am preparing an 80-minute session for an upper-level philosophy-of-mathematics seminar of fourteen students who have encountered the Hex and Chomp proofs but have not worked through the formal logic of finite perfect-information games. I want a structured in-class game — with explicit rules, assigned roles, physical materials, and symbolic stakes — that makes the strategy-stealing assumption feel genuinely puzzling rather than merely clever. The point is for students to experience, before I name it, the non-constructive punch: that assuming Player Two possesses a winning strategy allows Player One to appropriate it after an arbitrary opening move, yielding a contradiction that proves Player One must win, yet leaving the actual strategy permanently unidentified.

Please design the activity with the following specifications.

Setting and group structure: Fourteen students, seated in pairs for Rounds 1–2 and in a full circle for Round 3; one 80-minute block with tables arranged for pair play and then for collective observation.

The core mechanism: A simplified symmetric board game — a 5×5 token grid with one poison token fixed at the origin — in which pairs play rapid rounds under standard placement rules. Inject the following novel framing device, which I will call the Epistemic Theft Protocol. Each student assigned the role of Second Player receives a sealed envelope containing a blank index card printed only with the line "Winning Strategy: exists [ ]" and nothing else. They are told they may not write on the card, but they must behave as though the box is ticked. The First Player, after making any legal opening move, may invoke the Stealing Rule: they physically take the envelope without opening it and declare that they are now executing the strategy inside. If that strategy ever directs them to an already-occupied square, they make another arbitrary legal move and continue. The Second Player must then either concede defeat (on the ground that the First Player now holds their strategy) or open the envelope and reveal its emptiness — at which point the contradiction is visible to the group.

Roles and rules: Assign rotating roles of Mover (Player One or Two), Thief (the First Player who invokes the rule), Observer (who records conflicts on a shared sheet), and Adjudicator (who rules on legality without interpreting strategy). Provide explicit rules for token placement, the poison-square loss condition, the precise conditions under which the Stealing Rule may be invoked, and the strict prohibition on writing inside the envelope. Provide materials: the grid boards, colored tokens, the sealed envelopes with blank cards, conflict-record sheets, and symbolic stake tokens. Break the 80 minutes into segments: 10 minutes for rules and role assignment, 25 minutes for Round 1 (pair play without the Protocol), 20 minutes for Round 2 (play with the Protocol active), 15 minutes for the circle debrief, and 10 minutes for my formal mapping.

Symbolic stakes: Rather than money, use course-credit tokens. Each pair begins with five tokens; losing a round costs one token; successfully defending an empty strategy against a Thief costs two tokens from the Thief; reaching zero requires writing a one-paragraph reflection on non-constructive existence proofs.

The experience must precede the explanation: arrange the rounds so that students feel the frustration of holding an "existing" strategy they cannot display, and feel the hollowness of the First Player's victory — they win by holding an empty envelope — before I identify the logical structure.

Debrief: Map the specific moments of the game onto the formal steps of the strategy-stealing proof: the arbitrary first move that can only help; the conflict-resolution clause that permits another arbitrary move; the contradiction with the assumption that the Second Player had a unique winning strategy; and the conclusion that determinacy forces the win onto Player One. Predict exactly what will go wrong the first time I run it: specifically, that some students will try to secretly write strategies inside the envelopes despite the prohibition, that others will reject the empty-envelope victory as "not a real win" and demand to see a constructed strategy, and that the Adjudicators will be drawn into metaphysical arguments about whether an unwritten strategy "exists." Say how I should handle each failure — for instance, by treating the secret writing as a rule violation that voids the round, and by insisting that the emptiness is precisely the point — without breaking the frame of the game. Finally, be explicit about which feature of the mathematical argument this activity cannot reproduce: namely, the prior proof that every finite perfect-information game of this type is strictly determined (Zermelo's theorem). State clearly that I will have to supply that determinacy premise in lecture, because the game alone cannot establish that one player or the other must have a winning strategy; it can only show what happens if we assume one does.

Keep all descriptions answerable from standard philosophical knowledge of the argument, with no reliance on obscure sources.
