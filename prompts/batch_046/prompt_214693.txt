I was arguing with my roommate about whether you'd still be you if your brain got slowly replaced part by part, like the ship of theseus but for neurons. We got into this weird idea: what if they didnt replace random parts, but replaced neurons in order of how "quiet" they were -- like, any neuron that hadnt fired in the last day gets swapped for a synthetic analog that behaves identically but runs on photonics instead of chemistry. The idea is that if consciousness is just the active processing, then replacing the silent hardware shouldnt change your experience at all, since those parts werent contributing to your current mental state anyway.

Write a short story about someone going through this procedure to treat early-onset neurodegeneration. The replacements happen in waves over six months, always targeting the least active tissue first. At first everything seems normal, but gradually the protagonist notices that certain memories -- specifically ones tied to emotional states that corresponded to low activity in those regions -- feel different. Not gone, but flattened, like hearing a song you used to love through a wall. Meanwhile thier partner insists theyre exactly the same person, same jokes, same habits. By the end, the protagonist has to decide whether to finish the last wave, which will replace the remaining quiet substrate in the hippocampas and parts of the prefrontal cortex tied to long-term narrative identity. Include a moment where they encounter a recording of themselves from before the procedure and have to confront whether the gap they feel is real or just anxiety about change. Dont make it obvious whether theyre right about the loss or just paranoid; let the ambiguity sit there.
