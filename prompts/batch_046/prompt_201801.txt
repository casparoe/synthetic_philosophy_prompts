I have been testing my intuitions about meaningful lives by holding concrete scenarios up against each other, and I want to know whether a coherent principle governs the pattern or whether I must abandon one of my verdicts. Here are the cases.

A man spends forty years rebuilding an elaborate mechanical garden that is destroyed by storms every autumn; he knows it will always fall, takes no steps to preserve it, and reports deep satisfaction in the craft alone. I judge his life meaningful, despite the complete absence of lasting achievement.

A mathematician proves a profound theorem in her final months, dies feeling accomplished, and then her only notes are accidentally burned; no one ever learns the result. I still judge her life meaningful, because the achievement was real even though recognition was permanently lost.

A nurse spends her career producing enormous objective good for strangers, but feels only emptiness and never once experiences purpose or satisfaction. I judge her life not meaningful, even though the welfare she creates is vast.

A cult member lives in total subjective ecstasy, produces local harmony, and spends decades in devoted projects, but every belief driving him is false and the deity does not exist. I judge his life not meaningful, despite the absence of suffering and the presence of intense engagement.

A person chooses to live the same perfect Tuesday in an eternal loop, fully aware of the repetition, experiencing identical joy each cycle with no accumulated memory. I judge this not meaningful; it strikes me as a parody of meaning rather than an instance of it.

Finally, a writer produces a genuinely profound philosophical work by randomly pressing keys; she understands none of it, feels no connection to it, but readers are genuinely transformed. I am genuinely torn: one part of me says her life gained meaning retroactively through the work, while another part insists that meaning requires some agential or cognitive relation between the person and the value created.

My pattern seems to demand both some objective value and some subjective appropriation of it, but not in any simple conjunction: the sandcastle builder lacks lasting objective value yet qualifies; the nurse has massive objective value but lacks subjective appropriation and fails; the mathematician has objective value and past subjective engagement but no enduring recognition, yet qualifies; the loop-liver has subjective appropriation but lacks objective growth or irreversibility, and fails; the cultist has subjective appropriation but false objective grounding, and fails; the accidental writer has objective value but apparently no subjective relation, leaving me divided.

Is there a precise principle—perhaps specifying the kind of relationship an agent must bear to an objectively worthy activity, together with constraints on replaceability, repetition, or falsehood—that accommodates all six verdicts without collapsing into pure subjectivism or pure objectivism? Or must I drop one? In particular, can any principle distinguish the sandcastle builder from the loop-liver without smuggling in a hidden commitment to permanence, and distinguish the lost mathematician from the accidental writer without making recognition or understanding unnecessary? And what is the correct verdict on the accidental writer?
