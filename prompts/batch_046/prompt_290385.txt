I'm delivering the opening remarks for a three-day interdisciplinary workshop titled "Boundaries and Wholes," co-hosted by the university's metaphysics research group and the museum's conservation science department. The audience is deliberately split: on one side are analytic metaphysicians who have spent years on the special composition question — whether parts ever genuinely compose a single object beyond themselves, and if so, exactly which conditions license that union; on the other are art conservators, curators, and materials scientists for whom the same issue appears in practice every time they must decide whether a heavily restored sculpture remains the original work or has become something closer to a replica sustained by original fragments. There are also graduate students from both tracks who are still mapping the vocabulary across fields. I need a polished, welcoming address of roughly five minutes that avoids collapsing into either pure philosophy or pure shop talk, and that respects both groups as experts in a shared problem they do not yet share a language for.

The special composition question must do real structural work in the speech, not serve as decorative framing. I must set up the workshop around the problem of when parts genuinely make up a whole, and I should use that framing to explain why the two disciplines need each other without resolving the metaphysical dispute in either direction. Crucially, I must not imply to the conservators that the distinction between restoration and replication is merely conventional or easily dissolved — that boundary is professionally and ethically central to their work — but I also cannot pretend to the metaphysicians that the problem is settled, or that practical judgment can substitute for ontological argument. To keep the remarks from feeling like a generic plea for interdisciplinarity, I want to inject a self-referential, somewhat niche conceptual framing: I should ask, in a genuinely open way, whether this very gathering — these particular participants, in this particular room, across these three days — composes a single unified "workshop" in any metaphysically robust sense, or whether that is only a useful fiction we adopt for organizational purposes. This should motivate the stakes: if composition is restricted, context-sensitive, or otherwise non-universal, then cross-disciplinary collaboration itself might be an assembled object whose boundaries are genuinely unclear, and our shared task over the next days is to determine whether such assembled wholes are real or merely convenient.

Please write the remarks. They should be formal, inviting, and conceptually precise; they should address both halves of the room directly without lecturing either; and they should close by setting up the workshop's central tension — that we will spend three days examining cases where the answer to "do these parts make one thing?" determines not just abstract ontology but institutional, ethical, and artistic practice — without taking a definitive stand on whether unrestricted composition, restricted composition, or some intermediate view is correct. Keep the tone appropriate for an occasion that is serious but not somber, and let the philosophical problem shape what can and cannot be said about our being here together.
