I have followed the illusionism versus phenomenal realism debate since the 2000s — eliminativist accounts that treat qualitative properties as representational errors, against realist defenses that take first-person qualitative character as primitive data — and it strikes me as a classic stall: both sides accept the same cognitive architecture, the same neuroscience, the same patterns of introspective report, and yet neither has conceded an inch. The illusionists claim that what we call phenomenal consciousness is a misrepresented feature of access-conscious representational content, lacking any metaphysically independent qualitative residue; the realists reply that representational content alone cannot account for the qualitative dimension revealed in experience, and that treating it as error simply denies the phenomenon rather than explaining it. My working hypothesis is that this is not an empirical deadlock but a disagreement about evidential standards — specifically, whether introspective transparency counts as revealing structure or as generating illusion — but I am unsure whether a hidden formal question about the determinacy of representational content is actually driving the disagreement. Please diagnose it: is this a verbal dispute about the term phenomenal, a substantive disagreement masked as a debate about consciousness, or two distinct projects — one metaphysical, one epistemological — conducted under one label? Ground your diagnosis in what each camp actually asserts about introspective reliability, about the relationship between access and phenomenal character, and about whether illusionism targets properties, existence, or both. Then say what progress would look like: a distinction that separates property-illusionism from existence-illusionism and shows part of the dispute to be ill-posed; an empirical finding about the reliability of introspective judgments; a formal result on whether representational content can be fully determinate without qualitative grounding; or an argument that no resolution should be expected because the disagreement rests on bedrock intuitions that neither side can rationally compel. I want an honest assessment of whether the stall is structural.
