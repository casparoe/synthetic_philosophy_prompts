I've been trying to apply Dennett's intentional stance consistently to large language models, and I keep running into what feels like a reductio. (1) We are justified in attributing beliefs to a system whenever doing so is the most predictive and explanatory strategy available for its behavior. (2) For current LLMs, treating them as holding beliefs about geography, logic, or history yields far better predictions of their outputs than treating them as mere next-token distributions. (3) The same predictive strategy works for ant colonies—treating them as collectively believing food is nearby explains foraging patterns—and for thermostats—treating them as believing it is too cold explains switch-flipping. (4) One might restrict the stance to systems whose attributed beliefs are stable across contexts, arguing that LLM beliefs persist while ant-colony "beliefs" dissolve under closer inspection. (5) But LLM attributions are themselves notoriously unstable: minor prompt variations often reverse the model's apparent stance on the same factual question, and the system can assert contradictory propositions in different conversational frames without any underlying correction mechanism. (6) So either stability is not required, in which case thermostats and ant colonies qualify, or it is required, in which case LLMs fail. (7) One could try to save the distinction by appealing to functional architecture or representational complexity, but Dennett's stance is supposed to be behaviorally grounded rather than architecturally gated. (8) Therefore, applying the intentional stance without arbitrary cutoff conditions either forces us to attribute beliefs to simple physical systems or denies them to LLMs despite their apparent cognitive sophistication. Where exactly does this chain slip? If I want to preserve the idea that LLMs can genuinely have beliefs, which premise should I reject, and what does giving it up cost?
