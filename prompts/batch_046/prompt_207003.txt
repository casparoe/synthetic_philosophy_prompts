I'm working out my actual view of metaphysical grounding through concrete verdicts, and I'd like to know whether they cohere. The existence of this wooden chair strikes me as fully grounded in the microphysical arrangement of its parts and their relations. The non-existence of a married bachelor in this room strikes me as grounded in the incompatible natures of being married and being a bachelor, not in local physical conditions. That this statue weighs more than that one strikes me as partly grounded in their respective parts, but also in the conventional metric system being applied—without which the comparative fact wouldn't hold. That the United Nations exists strikes me as grounded in documents, collective intentions, and institutional rules, yet it is not reducible to the particles composing UN buildings, though it is less fundamental than they are. That this shadow is present strikes me as grounded in the object and light source, but also in the absence of other blockers; still, I resist saying that absences generally ground positive facts. That my assigned government identification number belongs to me strikes me as fully grounded in bureaucratic records and practices, such that if every record and human memory disappeared the fact would disappear—unlike my having mass. Can these six verdicts be unified by a precise principle—say, one that distinguishes full grounding, partial grounding with conventional contribution, and grounding by essential incompatibility—without forcing me to treat the UN as mere particles or to allow absences to ground positives in general? If the best principle must overturn one verdict, which should go? And apply it to a case I haven't listed: is the fact that this is the only crimson object in the room grounded partly in the absence of other crimson things?
