I'm writing a feature on whether philosophical models of collective action can survive contact with institutional crisis management. Take the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear emergency in March 2011—specifically the delayed venting of Reactor 1 and the contested seawater-injection decisions—as a case study in collective intentionality and joint action. Map the decision structure across TEPCO plant operators, Japanese government regulators, and external support units onto the conditions for shared we-intentions and joint commitment: where is there a genuine collective agent, and where is there only parallel individual action masked by hierarchy? Identify precisely how information asymmetry about radiation levels, the absence of mutual deliberation, and command-driven authority break or strain standard conditions for joint action. Then reverse the analysis: do the coordination failures—the venting delay, conflicting orders about seawater—show that shared intentionality was absent and therefore explain the breakdown, or do they reveal that crisis response relies on distributed role-obligations and institutional authority that collective-intentionality theory was never designed to cover? Be disciplined about what the theory illuminates, what it misdescribes, and where it is silent. Answer in American English.
