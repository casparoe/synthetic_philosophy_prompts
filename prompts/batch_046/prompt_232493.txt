Analyze the UK National Health Service's 18-week referral-to-treatment target, introduced in the early 2000s and widely gamed by 2008, as a case study in Goodhart's law. Do not treat the law as a single mechanism; instead, apply a distinction between first-order gaming (manipulating the measurement procedure while leaving clinical practice unchanged — for instance, delaying the official start of the clock or using temporary waiting-list initiatives to clear backlogs) and second-order structural displacement (where the metric becomes constitutive of the good it tracks, so that "successful treatment" is redefined as whatever can be delivered within 18 weeks, altering which patients and procedures are prioritized). Map the institutional behaviors that actually occurred — clock manipulation, reclassification of follow-up care, and the documented shift toward easier-to-treat cases — onto this two-level framework. Then run the analysis in both directions: does Goodhart's law, in its standard formulation, explain both levels, or does it presuppose that the underlying good (effective care) remains stable and separable from its proxy? Conversely, what does the NHS episode reveal about the law itself — is specification gaming fundamentally an incentive failure correctable by better metrics, or does it show that metrics in complex institutions can redefine the practices they govern, making the gaming-versus-measurement distinction collapse?
