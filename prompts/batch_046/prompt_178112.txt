hey, i've been puzzling over this split in campaign finance and lobbying rules. on one hand, lobbying is tightly policed: you have to disclose every contact, there are gift caps, and the assumption is that direct access to officials is inherently risky. on the other hand, "independant" campaign spending — ads bought by outside groups that don't "coordinate" with a candidate — is treated as protected speech with no caps, even when it's clearly meant to influence the same election.

officials recieve all kinds of one-sided info through lobbying, but if an outside group buys ads without coordinating, we call it free expression. the legal line hinges on "coordination," but it's defined in this procedural, box-checking way: separate consultants, no shared strategy. that seems to smuggle in a heavy philosophical claim about when influence becomes corruption. does the practice presuppose that harm or corruption only occurs when there's a shared intention — a kind of joint agency — between the spender and the official? and if so, is that consistent with lobbying rules, where we treat one-sided information as influence regardless of whether the official agreed to anything?

i'm also struck that negligence law punishes the risky act, not the bad outcome, but campaign law cares obsessively about whether a "meeting of minds" happened, not just whether the money actually moved votes. what implicit theory of responsibility, or of speech versus action, holds those two parts together?

reconstruct the philosophical commitments here. distinguish what's strictly required from what's just a good fit, point out where lobbying rules and campaign rules might presuppose different things, and say what would have to change in practice if we dropped the idea that shared intent is the key marker of corruption.
