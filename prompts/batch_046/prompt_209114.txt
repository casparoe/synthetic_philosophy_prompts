Consider the long-running impasse in the philosophy of archaeology over whether the formal properties of ritual deposits—standardized orientations, repeated symbolic motifs, or restricted-access spaces—can license inferences about the collective beliefs and institutional intentions of the people who made them. One camp, drawing on cognitive archaeology, holds that material patterning is a form of externalized representation: if burial orientations are consistently aligned without practical reason, that consistency expresses a shared propositional commitment about cosmology or the afterlife. The opposing camp, rooted in practice theory, holds that the same patterns are better explained as sedimented habitus—embodied dispositions that reproduce social order without ever being articulated as belief—so that attributing specific theological content to the builders is an anachronistic projection of modern representational psychology onto societies that did not separate cognition from action. The dispute has persisted for decades despite broad agreement on the stratigraphic and comparative ethnographic record, with neither side conceding that the other has misdescribed the material evidence.

My working hypothesis is that this stall is not an empirical deadlock that a new excavation could break, nor a mere verbal quarrel about the word “belief,” but a deeper disagreement about the ontology of institutions: whether an institution must involve shared mental representations in order to exist, or whether it can be fully constituted by distributed practices that never pass through explicit individual cognition. Yet I am uncertain whether that diagnosis captures the real division, or whether there is instead a hidden disagreement about standards of explanation—for instance, whether an adequate archaeological account must be recoverable in intentional terms, or whether functional and causal explanation is autonomous and sufficient.

Please diagnose the structure of this disagreement: is it substantive or verbal; do the sides differ about facts, about what counts as evidence for a belief attribution, about the criteria of explanatory adequacy, or about bedrock intuitions regarding human agency that neither side can independently justify? Is the original question ill-posed because it presupposes a separation between belief and practice that the societies in question may never have recognized?
