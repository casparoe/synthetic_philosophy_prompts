As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of science and technology ethics, I have been struck by the longevity of a particular impasse in algorithmic fairness. The impossibility results—showing that group-wise calibration and equality of false-positive and false-negative rates cannot be satisfied simultaneously except in degenerate cases—are treated by one camp as binding normative constraints that force explicit value choices, and by another camp as formal artifacts that dissolve once one allows context-dependent feature weights or continuous-score adjustments. For nearly a decade, the exchange has followed the same pattern: formal proofs are met with domain-specific counterexamples, and neither side seems to have altered its premises. I suspect the stall is not merely about evidence but about the structure of the disagreement itself. Is this a substantive dispute about what fairness requires, or a verbal disagreement in which one side uses 'fairness' to denote a statistical property of predictive distributions and the other to denote a relational property of social treatment? Could there be a hidden empirical premise—perhaps an assumption about the exogeneity of predictive features analogous to the distinction between state variables and control parameters in physical theory—that one camp accepts and the other implicitly rejects? Or are the two sides answering different questions altogether: one normative question about what an algorithm ought to satisfy, and one institutional question about what regulators can audit? Given your diagnosis—supported by what each side actually claims—what would constitute genuine progress? Would it take a formal bridge between statistical and relational notions of fairness, an empirical demonstration that practitioners implicitly prioritize one criterion, or a conceptual distinction that shows the conflict is ill-posed?
