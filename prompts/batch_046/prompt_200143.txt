Here is the argument, which I worry proves too much because it seems to undermine Socrates’ own answer to Meno’s paradox. The premises each look defensible to me, yet the conclusion is that knowledge is not genuinely more valuable than true belief.

(2) If being tied down in this way is what confers extra value, then the value-adding feature is stability: knowledge is valuable because it persists in the face of counterevidence and revision.
(3) A true belief maintained by rigid indoctrination, or by a cognitive architecture hard-coded to resist any revision regardless of evidence, can be just as stable—perhaps even more persistently stable—than a justified belief.
(4) Therefore, on the stability account, indoctrinated true beliefs and hard-coded true beliefs should be just as valuable as knowledge.
(5) But this is clearly wrong: we do not value dogmatic or mechanically fixed true beliefs as highly as knowledge, and in many cases we judge them to be epistemically worse.
(6) So stability, understood as persistence or resistance to change, cannot be the feature that explains knowledge’s superior value.
(7) Every alternative proposal—virtue epistemology, practical-reliability theories, or accounts that appeal to understanding—either collapses back into stability or fails to distinguish knowledge from lucky true belief in every case.
(8) Consequently, there is no feature that distinguishes knowledge from true belief in a way that explains its greater value; the Meno problem has no satisfactory solution.

Please diagnose this chain precisely. Which step contains the hidden flaw—an equivocation, a scope shift, or an ambiguity in what stability means?
