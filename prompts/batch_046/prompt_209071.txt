At a philosophy of mind reading group, two members — Alina and Marcus — fell into a disagreement about whether phenomenal consciousness is anything over and above access consciousness, particularly in relation to global workspace theory. Alina argued that the framework already dissolves Block’s familiar distinction: once information is broadcast widely enough to guide behaviour, reasoning and report, there is no further ‘phenomenal’ residue left unexplained; any alleged experience that plays no functional role is, she said, indistinguishable from absence and risks being epiphenomenal. Marcus replied that broadcasting can be conceived too narrowly. He proposed a hypothetical ‘modular island’ in which a sensory representation is richly structured, supports fine-grained discrimination within its own module, and is monitored by a local meta-cognitive mechanism that registers its presence, yet the representation is permanently blocked from entering the cross-modal integration layer that makes content available for verbal report and rational deliberation across the system. On Marcus’s view, this is phenomenal consciousness without global access: there is something-it-is-like to be in that state, but it never enters the workspace in the sense required for access consciousness. Alina countered that Marcus has smuggled broadcasting back in under a different name: if a local meta-cognitive system tracks the representation, and if the representation guides behaviour within its domain, then it is already available to some cognitive consumers, which is all global workspace theory requires; the ‘integration layer’ is merely one implementation of broadcasting, not its definition. Marcus answered that Alina conflates horizontal broadcasting across specialised modules with vertical integration into a unified, narrative self-model. He insisted that local meta-cognition — a module monitoring its own states — can generate a genuine phenomenal character without ever making that content available to the system as a whole for reasoning or report. He added that if phenomenal consciousness genuinely exists, its presence should not depend on its being detectable from outside the module. They turned to me and asked me to referee, since I had been listening without taking sides. Please go through their exchange step by step and say who is right about each specific point — not merely who wins overall. In particular, address whether Marcus’s ‘modular island’ genuinely describes phenomenal consciousness without access, whether Alina is correct that local monitoring collapses into a form of broadcasting, and whether Marcus’s distinction between horizontal and vertical integration holds up. Say who is more right overall, and identify what, if anything, both parties are missing — for instance, whether they have adequately considered how global workspace theory treats the boundary between conscious and unconscious processing, or whether either has addressed the possibility that phenomenal and access consciousness might come apart in both directions.
