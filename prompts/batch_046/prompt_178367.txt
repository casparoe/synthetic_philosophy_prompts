I've come around to thinking the gender-equality paradox — where more egalitarian countries like Sweden show more gendered career splits than less egalitarian ones — is mostly a measurment artifact of how we bin occupations. Cluster jobs by actual task content rather than official title, and the cross-country correlation between gender-equality indices and occupational segregation either flattens or reverses. Whatever segregation remains tracks adolescent peer-netowrk homophily much closer than adult economic incentives, so I think the nature-vs-culture framing misses the point: it's about which life-stage filter dominates career formation. What'd actually count against this view? Not just generic "socialization matters" pushback, but specific methodological flaws, countervailing data on occupational mobility, or peer-effect studies that'd break the argument. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones defenders of this view would concede and which they'd dismiss, and tell me if the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and whether that's a problem for an empirical claim of this kind.
