The familiar thought experiment involves a hypothetical machine that can permanently stimulate a person's brain so that they inhabit a flawless virtual existence, experiencing nothing but intense pleasure and satisfaction, whilst their physical body lies dormant. I wish, however, to push the scenario in a more intricate direction. Imagine a translucent experience machine in which the subject retains a diffuse background awareness that the experiences are simulated, yet possesses the ability to suppress that awareness selectively within discrete experiential modules; in addition, the apparatus runs a parallel shadow-track of the life the subject would have led outside the machine, generating counterfactual regrets that the subject may access at will. Given this modified apparatus, I should like you to predict how Jeremy Bentham—who died in 1832, decades before such neuro-technological speculations could have arisen—would have evaluated its bearing upon his hedonistic calculus. Specifically, would he have insisted that the quantitative intensity and duration of simulated pleasures are strictly identical in value to those obtained through physical interaction with the world, or might the presence of partial transparency and accessible counterfactual shadow-tracks have compelled him to introduce qualitative distinctions that his usual framework rejects?
