I am drafting a novel in which a logician and a fiction writer argue over whether a thought can be more than the sum of its parts, and I need the conceptual architecture made precise before I write their dialogue. Write the desiderata that any theory resolving Bradley's regress—any account of how a proposition achieves genuine unity beyond its constituent terms and relations—must satisfy to count as adequate. For each requirement, state the motivating failure mode or case it guards against, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of a particular camp (realists about relations, deflationists about truth, compositional semanticists, and so on). Include the standard constraints: blocking an infinite regress of relating relations, preserving the distinction between a relation as relating and a relation as related, maintaining compositional transparency, and avoiding an unexplained primitive. Then incorporate this further condition: the theory must accommodate what I call fictive unity—the binding of terms like Sherlock Holmes and Baker Street into a proposition that is true-in-the-story despite the absence of corresponding real-world entities, without reducing such propositions to mere lists or eliminating them via a Russellian analysis. Is this fictive constraint genuinely independent of the others, or does it collapse into one of them? Finally, identify whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable—especially whether the demand for a non-mysterious, non-regressive unifier can coexist with full compositional transparency—and indicate which single requirement, if relaxed, would open the field of candidates most widely. Keep the weight on the criteria, their interrelations, and their tensions rather than on scoring existing theories.
