For my senior seminar on moral and political philosophy, I am building a diagnostic taxonomy—structured as a decision tree with roughly six leaves—to help classmates locate their actual view on whether famines are natural disasters or political crimes. I want to use Amartya Sen's entitlement framework as the starting point and apply it to three cases: the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the famine during China's Great Leap Forward (1958–62). Each branch should split on a concrete claim respondents can evaluate without knowing philosophical labels: first, whether aggregate food availability was the binding constraint in all three regions; second, whether entitlement collapse is best explained by market dynamics, direct state extraction, or a distinct mechanism of institutional data suppression and forced procurement (particularly in the Chinese case); and third, whether state culpability amounts to negligent omission or active commission. Every leaf should carry a name (or 'unnamed') and one sentence on its most awkward commitment—specifically, what it forces one to say about at least one of the three famines. Please flag any pair of leaves that this tree cannot genuinely separate, and note which combinations of answers appear unoccupied and why (incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored). Finally, please distinguish what is broadly settled in the scholarly literature—such as that physical food scarcity was not the decisive factor in Bengal—from what remains genuinely contested, such as whether the Irish and Chinese cases share a single entitlement structure or require separate causal explanations. The handout is for students without access to specialized sources, so do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
