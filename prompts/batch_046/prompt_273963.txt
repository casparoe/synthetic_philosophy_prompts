Consider the institutional practice, widespread in large technology and consulting firms, of replacing fixed vacation allowances with "unlimited paid time off." Formally, the employee is told she possesses total autonomy over when, how often, and for how long she rests; there is no accrual ceiling, no tracking mechanism that guarantees a floor, and no explicit prohibition on extended absence. At the same time, the institution removes the structural markers that would make rest a protected entitlement, ties team performance metrics partly to continuous availability, and rewards managers whose reports show minimal absence. The empirical result is that employees typically take less leave than under a mandatory-minimum regime, and those who take substantial leave report implicit social and career penalties despite the formal freedom. What philosophical commitments about freedom, responsibility, and self-deception does this design presuppose? Does it presuppose a Sartrean view that freedom is radical and unconditional, so that any guaranteed minimum would be an inauthentic flight from the burden of choice? Or does it presuppose a liberal-negative-liberty conception—freedom as the mere absence of formal barriers—while treating exhaustion and under-rest as purely internal failures of will for which the employee alone is responsible? Might the practice itself constitute an institutional form of bad faith, in which the organization denies its own facticity—its active role in shaping which choices are genuinely available—by framing every outcome, including overwork, as the employee's own authentic transcendence? Reconstruct the implicit theory, carefully distinguishing what the practice strictly requires from what merely accommodates it, and assess whether its parts are philosophically consistent with one another. Then compare it with a counterfactual policy that imposes a non-waivable minimum leave, penalizes employers for underutilization, and prohibits managerial evaluation of absence rates: what conception of freedom and institutional responsibility does that alternative presuppose, and does it resolve the bad faith or merely relocate it into the structure itself? Finally, describe what a policy that genuinely honored both situated freedom and organizational facticity would have to include. End with a single line beginning "Reconstruction: " that states the implicit theory of freedom and responsibility in one sentence.
