hey, i'm a phd student in moral psych/philosophy of mind and my advisor dropped this on me. i keep coming back to the problem of telling preferences from biases in morality, and i think i've walked myself into a paradox. here's the chain:

(1) A moral judgment counts as a preference only if it survives coherence filtering: internal consistency, stablity under reflection, and insensitivity to framing.
(2) A judgment counts as a bias if it fails that filter but is correctable by external debiasing techniques.
(3) If two people disagree and both survive the filter, we have a preference clash, not a bias problem.
(4) But empirical literature on moral cognition (the generic kind, not any one study) suggests framing effects penetrate almost all substantive moral disagreements—distributive justice, punishment, etc.
(5) So either nearly every moral disagreement involves at least one biased party, which makes the preference/bias distinction useless for normative work, or we relax the filter.
(6) If we relax it to let framing-sensitive judgments count as preferences, then implicit biases (like racial bias in hiring scenarios) also survive relaxation, since they're internally consistent and stable.
(7) Therefore no relaxation saves genuine preference while excluding bias.

this feels like it proves too much—like coherence-based filtering is dead on arrival. i can't tell if the slip is in (4) treating framing sensitivity as universal, or in (6) assuming bias and preference respond identically to relaxation. maybe there's a hidden move involving third-order monitoring, where we check not just judgments but our methods for correcting them? which step do i reject, and what's the cost? i don't want to just say "empirical work is messy" because that feels like giving up.
