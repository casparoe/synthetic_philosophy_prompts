I am a doctoral candidate in moral philosophy drafting a framework I call institutional tranquilism, which extends the view that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value. In my notes I distinguish first-order tranquility—the direct cessation of individual suffering—from second-order tranquility, which requires the dissolution of the collective meta-desire that social conditions be improved, including progressive ambition itself, and I argue that a society achieves tranquilist value only when both orders are institutionally secured. I am concerned that this distinction collapses into mere conservatism, that attributing tranquil states to institutions commits a category error, and that suppressing the craving for progress violates the very autonomy that makes suffering morally salient. Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, commenting on the conceptual coherence of the two-order distinction, the risk of category error in institutional attribution, whether eliminating progressive ambition is compatible with tranquilist premises, whether the framework offers anything beyond standard negative-utilitarian accounts, and whether the argument is interesting enough to pursue in a full chapter.
