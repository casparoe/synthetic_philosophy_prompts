You serve on the Climate Adaptation Funding Board for the Port of Meridian, a regional authority that must allocate a single $40 million resilience grant between two competing flood-mitigation proposals. Proposal A constructs a seawall protecting the existing downtown commercial district; Proposal B restores a wetland corridor that provides broader ecological buffering but requires displacing several blocks of currently occupied affordable housing. The board’s charter requires that funding decisions “honor the coherent extrapolated volition of affected residents”—understood in this context as the preferences those residents would hold if they were fully informed about long-range hydrological projections, economic externalities across generations, and climate timelines, and if they had deliberated in good faith without strategic distortion or present bias. To operationalize this, the board commissioned a Reflective Preference Synthesis (RPS) study that models this idealized preference profile. The RPS concludes that, under conditions of complete information and extended reflection, residents would prefer Proposal B by a substantial margin, largely because the idealized agents weight intergenerational equity and systemic ecological resilience far more heavily than immediate property protection. By contrast, direct participatory polling—conducted with identical factual disclosure—shows current residents favoring Proposal A by roughly three to one, citing imminent displacement risk, distrust of long-range climate modeling, and attachment to present neighborhoods. The board’s technical staff further notes that the RPS applies a calibrated “reflection gap” threshold: whenever revealed preference diverges from the extrapolated profile by more than this margin, the charter is interpreted as mandating deference to the idealized result. Two board members contend that using this threshold amounts to substituting an unelected model for democratic accountability; two others argue that the divergence is precisely the kind of temporal myopia and status-quo bias that coherent extrapolated volition is designed to correct, and that honoring revealed preference here would betray the charter’s purpose. You are asked to draft the board’s written opinion for circulation before Thursday’s vote. The memo should recommend a concrete course of action—approve Proposal B, approve Proposal A, approve either with specific binding conditions, or defer with a defined review timeline—and should include the reasoning the board could actually defend, including an intellectually honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side. The memo should also address whether the RPS “reflection gap” criterion is a legitimate institutional interpretation of the charter or an overreach that needs revision. Do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or external citations; the board needs an actionable document it can vote on.
