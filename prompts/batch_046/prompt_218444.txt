Construct a thought experiment that isolates a non-computational failure of epistemic closure: a rational agent who explicitly believes both that her colleague submitted the report before the deadline and that if the report was submitted before the deadline then the funding will be released, yet she does not believe that the funding will be released. The failure must not arise from forgetfulness, inattention, or processing limits. Instead, it should stem from a structural partition in her belief system—specifically, that beliefs formed through direct perceptual testimony are stored in one representational register, while beliefs formed through explicit logical inference are stored in another, and the agent applies a norm of acceptance only within each register, not across them. Describe the scenario in one paragraph, keeping the propositions ordinary and the setting a normal workplace. Then state the belief, the method of formation for each premise and for the missing conclusion, the structural feature, and why standard closure principles (knowledge or belief closure under known entailment) yield the wrong verdict. Next, explain how a defender of logical omniscience—say, by appealing to implicit belief or to a competence-performance distinction—would respond. Finally, identify exactly which feature of the case does the work, show what happens if that partition is removed (does the case collapse into a standard cognitive-overload example?), and say whether the collapse shows that logical omniscience is best understood as a normative ideal rather than a descriptive constraint.
