Please explain the conceptual tension between William James's defense of the will to believe and W. K. Clifford's evidentialist ethics, concentrating on beliefs that are pragmatically self-constituting—that is, beliefs whose adoption partially determines the state of affairs that would serve as evidence for or against them. Begin by outlining James's criteria for a legitimate belief-option—live, forced, and momentous—and by reconstructing Clifford's shipowner analogy and his universal prohibition on belief without sufficient evidence. Then address the following: when a belief is live, forced, and momentous, and when holding it genuinely improves an agent's prospects in a domain where success depends partly on confidence, does Clifford's prohibition still apply with full force? Analyze this separately for beliefs about external states of affairs that happen to be self-fulfilling and for beliefs that concern the agent's own reasoning capacities or practical agency. Conclude by assessing whether James and Clifford are genuinely disputing the norms of belief formation, or whether they are instead operating with different implicit conceptions of what counts as sufficient evidence once belief-dependent outcomes and practical stakes are included in the calculus.
