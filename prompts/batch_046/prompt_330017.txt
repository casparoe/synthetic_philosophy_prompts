In a seminar on metaphysics of personal identity, I have been trying to articulate where I stand on origin essentialism, but my intuitions across counterfactual scenarios do not align neatly. I hold that if my parents had conceived a child from a different sperm and egg—say, because of a laboratory error in IVF that swapped the gametes—then that child, even if raised in my family with my name and eventually acquiring my psychological profile, would not be me; the particular zygote from which I actually developed seems essential to my identity. Yet I am less certain when the genetic material is the same but development diverges: if the original zygote underwent an early mutation that yielded a genetically distinct embryo, yet that embryo developed into an adult with my memories and character, my inclination is to say that might still be me, since the causal origin in that specific fusion event is preserved. I also think that delaying implantation of the same embryo by a decade, or gestating it on another planet, would not affect my identity, which suggests that spatiotemporal contiguity of development is not required. But when I consider monozygotic twinning where the split produces two psychologically identical adults—imagine a futuristic procedure that copies my neural architecture into both—I feel neither can be me, or perhaps both can, and I have no stable intuition. By contrast, a clone produced from my adult somatic cells, raised in isolation with my psychology duplicated, strikes me as clearly not me, despite sharing more of my genome than the swapped-gamete child.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps centered on the uniqueness of the originating causal process, or on a specific kind of genetic-psychological continuity—that can accommodate all of these verdicts? If the answer requires abandoning one, I want to know which judgment is the outlier and why. I am especially interested in whether the principle should treat psychological continuity as necessary but not sufficient, and whether it needs to invoke something like the non-branching of the causal chain.

Please also tell me what your reconstructed principle predicts for a case I have not mentioned: tetragametic chimerism, in which two distinct embryos fuse in utero and develop into one person, with one embryo contributing the brain and central nervous system and the other contributing the body and peripheral tissues. Would the resulting individual be identical to one of the original embryos, to neither, or to both in some divided way, and what does that imply for how strictly we should understand origin essentialism?
