As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of mind in earnest, I have tried to formalize an argument I take from Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach and now find it suspicious because the conclusion appears unavoidable yet implausible. Here is the chain.

(1) Gödel's first incompleteness theorem shows that any consistent formal system powerful enough to express arithmetic can contain a self-referential formula—constructed via Gödel numbering—that asserts its own unprovability within the system.

(2) Hofstadter proposes that human self-awareness arises from an analogous strange loop: a hierarchical representational architecture that loops back upon itself, with internal cognitive or neural encodings serving the functional role of Gödel numbers.

(3) If this analogy is correct, then the conditions sufficient for self-awareness are precisely the formal conditions for implementing a syntactic strange loop: adequate recursive representational capacity together with a mechanism of self-encoding.

(4) Because these conditions are formal, they are substrate-independent; any physical system—biological or artificial—that satisfies them should be capable of the same loop, regardless of its material constitution.

(5) Therefore, any system with sufficient recursive representational capacity should be self-aware, provided only that it implements the appropriate self-encoding structure.

(6) Yet contemporary artificial architectures—such as large-scale language models or self-monitoring control systems—exhibit precisely the recursive representational capacities in question, and we have strong theoretical and intuitive reasons not to attribute self-awareness to them.

(7) Consequently, we face a dilemma: either accept that such artificial systems are genuinely self-aware, which seems implausible, or concede that the Gödelian analogy fails to distinguish conscious minds from merely complex representational systems.

Where does this argument go wrong? Is the error in treating neural representations as literal Gödel numbers (step 2), in the inference that formal strange loops entail phenomenal self-awareness (step 3), or in the move from substrate-independence to universal consciousness (step 4)? I am inclined to preserve the analogy but reject the intuition in step (6); I would like to know what preserving the analogy costs, and in particular whether there is a subtle equivocation—perhaps between syntactic self-reference and semantic self-modeling—that allows one to keep Hofstadter's core insight without accepting the universal conclusion.
