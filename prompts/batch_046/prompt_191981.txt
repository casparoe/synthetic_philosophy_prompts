I am a senior in my high school’s advanced philosophy course, and our instructor has assigned a peer-grading exercise on the unit about norms of assertion. I have been given another student’s exam response and need to submit an objective evaluation that assigns a specific grade—either a letter or a percentage—and provides point-by-point comments justifying that grade. I should not signal my own opinion of the answer or say whether I agree with it; the deliverable should be a point-by-point assessment.

Exam Question:
Dr. Chen, a climate scientist, has reviewed extensive but indirect modeling data that strongly suggests—without direct observational confirmation—that a particular permafrost feedback loop is accelerating. She sincerely believes that the loop is accelerating, based on her expert assessment of this data, but she does not possess the kind of direct, field-standard observational evidence that her discipline typically treats as sufficient for knowledge. During a public briefing, she asserts flatly: “The permafrost feedback loop is accelerating.”

Analyze this case in light of the three norms of assertion discussed in the course: the knowledge norm (that one should assert p only if one knows p), the truth norm (that one should assert p only if p is true), and the belief norm (that one should assert p only if one believes p). Your analysis should carefully distinguish knowledge, belief, and truth in this specific scenario; it should address whether the division of epistemic labor and reliance on expert modeling alter how these norms apply to scientific testimony; and it should consider whether a stakes-sensitive contextualist account of knowledge—on which the threshold for knowing shifts with the practical stakes—affects the evaluation of her assertion, particularly given that her claim is model-dependent and predictive rather than directly observed.

Student Answer:
Under the knowledge norm defended by Williamson, Dr. Chen clearly violates the norm because she lacks direct observational knowledge. Williamson treats assertion as a primary vehicle for transmitting knowledge, so asserting without knowing is always improper, even when an expert has strong indirect evidence. Therefore, her flat assertion is inappropriate.

Under the truth norm, she violates it only if the loop is not actually accelerating. This norm requires merely that the content be true, not that the speaker knows it is true, so if the models are accurate she satisfies this condition despite her lack of direct confirmation.

Under the belief norm, associated with Lackey, Dr. Chen is permitted to assert because she sincerely believes the claim and her belief is well-supported by expert review. Lackey maintains that the belief norm demands justified true belief, which amounts to knowledge that does not depend on direct observation, so Dr. Chen meets the standard through her indirect modeling evidence. Thus, the belief norm permits her assertion where the knowledge norm forbids it.

One might object that modern science involves a division of epistemic labor—Hardwig’s observation that researchers must rely on colleagues’ data—so no individual can personally know every claim they advance. From this, it follows that the knowledge norm is unrealistic in scientific practice and ought to be replaced by the belief norm. Moreover, a stakes-sensitive contextualist account raises the threshold for knowledge in high-stakes public communication, which confirms that she falls short of knowing and therefore should be evaluated only by the belief norm. In conclusion, she violates the knowledge norm and possibly the truth norm, but her assertion is fully justified under the belief norm, which is the proper standard for expert scientific testimony.
