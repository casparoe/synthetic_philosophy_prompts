I have recently been confronting a carpenter-ant infestation in an old house I just purchased, and the practical decision between targeted bait and broad-spectrum spray has led me to draft notes for an essay on the ethics of insecticides and pest control. My central claim is that the standard sentience-based framework in animal ethics misses a distinct wrong in broad-spectrum pesticides: they do not merely harm individual organisms but collapse what I term “ecological reference classes”—functionally integrated groups whose boundaries are defined relative to a stable ecological baseline—while targeted interventions respect the indexical nature of the category “pest.” I argue that labeling an organism a pest involves a self-locating judgment structurally analogous to the epistemic reference-class problem: the classification depends on whose interests define the baseline, and ethical reasoning should therefore include a “reference-class check” that distinguishes harm relative to an arbitrary human preference from harm relative to an ecologically stable system. Applying this, I contrast neonicotinoids, which damage pollinators that fall outside any defensible ecological reference class for harm, with pheromone-based disruption of ant mating trails, which targets only the specific interaction causing damage without dissolving the broader functional group.

I would welcome your comments on these notes. Specifically, I am unsure whether the analogy to the reference-class problem is genuinely illuminating or merely ornamental; whether targeted methods actually preserve ecological reference classes in practice rather than simply delaying collapse; and whether I am understating the weight of direct suffering-based objections even for organisms with minimal sentience. Please also say what would change your mind—whether an empirical finding about insect nociception or about long-term ecosystem resilience after targeted intervention would cause you to reject the central argument—and identify the point at which my reasoning is most likely to fail.
