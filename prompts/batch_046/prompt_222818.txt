As a journalist who covers philosophy of science and ethics, I was recently pressed in a post-lecture discussion by a philosopher friend who had reconstructed what she called the institutional extension of Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument against moral responsibility. She claimed that the reasoning, if taken seriously, does not merely erase personal blame and praise but also dissolves the normative foundation for holding collective bodies—democratic states, corporations, international institutions—morally accountable for harm. The chain she set out was as follows. (1) For an agent to be genuinely morally responsible for an action, that agent must be the ultimate originator—causa sui—of the causal chain that produces it. (2) No human being is causa sui, since every person’s character, values, and deliberative capacities are the product of heredity, environment, and prior causal conditions for which that person bears no responsibility. (3) Therefore no individual is genuinely responsible for any particular action. (4) Collective moral responsibility must supervene on the responsibility of the constituent individuals; if none of the parts carry genuine responsibility, the whole cannot possess it either. (5) Yet institutional accountability is indispensable to legal and political order, and denying it wholesale is practically and morally untenable. (6) So the Basic Argument, if sound, eliminates not only individual moral responsibility but also the basis for holding institutions answerable, which is absurd. (7) Therefore one of the premises or inferences must fail. I feel that something slips between (3) and (4), or perhaps between (1) and (2), but I cannot precisely name the fault. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost the hard incompatibilist? I would prefer to preserve the core intuition behind (2), yet I want to understand whether that preference is unstable once the collective-implication is taken seriously.
