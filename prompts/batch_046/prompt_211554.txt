You are advising a software engineer who has built a personal investment algorithm with a built-in "trust audit." Before trading, the algorithm inspects its own inference rules and training data for bias. The engineer has programmed a self-endorsement rule: if the audit derives that the system is unbiased, the system treats that derivation itself as direct evidence for its own reliability, amplifying confidence in the audit's conclusion. Before deploying this on her retirement savings, she asks whether she should accept a positive audit result. She points out that the audit's judgment about its own trustworthiness is among the premises it uses to reach that very judgment. Is this circularity damaging, or is this kind of self-trust rational?

Please respond in the following sections, with approximate lengths:
- The philosophical structure (~150 words): identify the underlying theoretical problem instantiated in her situation; map exactly which parts of her algorithm correspond to which parts of the problem.
- The case for deploying (~150 words): defend why accepting the audit might be rational, including arguments that some form of self-trust is unavoidable for any agent that acts on its own reasoning.
- The reflective obstacle (~150 words): explain the specific epistemic and formal difficulty that arises when an agent applies a self-endorsement rule to its own reliability—why deriving one's own trustworthiness fails to provide independent assurance, and what this implies about the limits of self-certification.
- Verdict (~100 words): advise her concretely—should she deploy the system, disable the rule, or adopt some intermediate safeguard?
