Archaeological field reports frequently treat the spatial clustering of standardized ritual objects—small limestone figurines placed exclusively in child burials across several Neolithic sites—as adequate evidence to infer a regional cult of ancestor veneration or shared institutional religion, even in the absence of textual confirmation. At the same time, the same reports typically reject attempts to specify the propositional beliefs behind the practice—whether the figurines represent actual ancestors, protective spirits, or symbolic substitutes for living kin—as speculative psychologizing that exceeds what material culture can legitimately reveal. This produces a notable asymmetry: practitioners claim institutional knowledge, asserting that a shared religious institution existed, while denying doctrinal knowledge, insisting that we cannot recover what people actually believed.

What philosophical commitments about the relation between material culture, social institutions, and mental states does this double standard presuppose? Does it assume that institutions are behaviorally transparent in ways that individual beliefs are not? Can it be reconciled with a view that treats religious practice as primarily social coordination rather than propositional commitment? Or does it depend on a tacit behaviorist theory of religion—one that a cognitive archaeologist, who argues ritual artifacts are engineered to trigger specific mental representations, would reject?

Please distinguish what this practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, examine whether archaeologists apply the same asymmetry to technological versus ritual analogies, and explain what would have to change in archaeological method if we replaced its implicit epistemology with a framework that granted beliefs and institutions equal inferential accessibility from physical remains. Also, state clearly what kind of evidence or argument would change your view and lead you to judge this asymmetry philosophically justified rather than a methodological artifact, and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
