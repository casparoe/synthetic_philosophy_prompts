In preparing this term’s advanced seminar on existentialism and political philosophy, I have noticed that students tend to treat the Grand Inquisitor episode in Dostoevsky’s The Brothers Karamazov as a settled parable—either a triumphant defense of human dignity or a cynical exposure of it—rather than as an unresolved argument. I want you to write an essay that treats the Inquisitor’s indictment of freedom as genuinely live. The Inquisitor maintains that human beings are constitutionally incapable of bearing the burden of free will: given the choice between happiness secured through miracle, mystery, and authority, and the anxious liberty of conscience, they will invariably choose the former. Dostoevsky responds not with a counter-argument but with a silent Christ who kisses the Inquisitor and departs, leaving the old man shaken but unconverted. Your central task is to determine whether this silent gesture actually answers the Inquisitor’s psychological and moral challenge, or whether it merely offers an aesthetic consolation that avoids the hard problem.

To prevent a simple summary of the episode, frame your evaluation through a distinction I have introduced in our discussions: freedom as optionality versus freedom as narrative authorship. Freedom as optionality is the bare structural presence of open choices—whether one may technically select among goods. Freedom as narrative authorship is the more demanding condition that one’s choices can be woven into a coherent life-story that one recognizes as genuinely one’s own, irrespective of how expansive the menu of options might be. Apply this framework precisely: does the Inquisitor’s proposed social order eliminate both forms of freedom, or does it preserve a hollow optionality (permitting choice among permitted satisfactions) while systematically destroying narrative authorship? Furthermore, does Christ’s kiss, read not as a refusal to debate but as an affirmation of suffering liberty, succeed in restoring narrative authorship without recourse to the three temptations? Weigh the strongest objections: some interpreters hold that Dostoevsky intends the kiss to show that love transcends rational argument, making conceptual rebuttal inappropriate; others contend that if freedom genuinely requires the possibility of catastrophic failure, then the Inquisitor’s paternalism is not a betrayal of dignity but its only sustainable protection. Reach and defend a clear verdict—either that Dostoevsky’s silent Christ provides a philosophically sufficient response when freedom is understood as narrative authorship, or that the Inquisitor’s realism remains unrefuted and the kiss is symbolic rather than argumentative. Do not conclude with a non-committal synthesis; justify your position with explicit reasoning drawn from the text and from the conceptual framework above. The essay should run to approximately 1,200 words.
