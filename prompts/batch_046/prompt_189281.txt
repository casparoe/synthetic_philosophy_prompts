I've recently been appointed to a working group redesigning our city's climate framework, and I'm struck by a tension in the proposal we're drafting. The plan imposes a carbon tax, but the rate is tied not to a social-cost calculation but to an explicit "degrowth trajectory" for high-emission sectors — an institutional target for reducing aggregate economic throughput. Revenue is not rebated to taxpayers; it funds "restorative commons" — shared infrastructure governed by deliberative assemblies weighted by ecological vulnerability rather than by income or property. And non-compliance is treated not as a punishable offense but as a signal that the rate was set below true ecological cost, triggering automatic adjustment rather than individual liability.

What philosophical commitments does this design presuppose? Does tying the tax to degrowth commit us to the view that growth is not merely inefficient but actively harmful, and can that be reconciled with welfare-economic justifications for pricing carbon? Does funding unconditional-access commons governed by vulnerability-weighted deliberation presuppose a relational or capability-based conception of welfare that conflicts with the consumption logic of the tax base? And does treating failure to comply as a calibration error rather than a moral failure deny direct individual obligations, substituting a systemic, cybernetic model of responsibility? Where is the framework philosophically underdetermined — which rival theories of climate justice could license these same institutional choices — and which parts are genuinely inconsistent if one rejects the underlying commitments?
