I have been reading informally in aesthetics and philosophy of art, particularly around the longstanding question of whether judgments of beauty can be genuinely correct or mistaken rather than merely expressions of personal preference. In discussions—both in books and in conversations with friends who study design—I keep encountering a particular argumentative pattern that I do not have a precise name for, and I would like to understand exactly what it is and where its edges lie. A discussant will begin by appealing to near-universal agreement on very simple cases, such as the shared preference for a clear mountain vista over a polluted industrial ditch, in order to establish that human sensibilities are sufficiently aligned for some kind of aesthetic calibration to be possible. But then, when the conversation shifts to genuinely contested works—for instance, whether a specific abstract expressionist painting possesses real beauty—the same person treats widespread disagreement as decisive evidence that no objective standard could exist, without ever explaining why the calibration that seemed plausible at the simple level should be expected to vanish rather than to become more difficult, more nuanced, or more refined at the complex level. The baseline agreement is invoked to prove that taste is shared, and disagreement at higher complexity is invoked to prove that taste is irreducibly private, with little argument for why complexity should undermine rather than challenge the shared framework. Does this configuration have an established name in the literature on Hume's standard of taste, or perhaps in philosophy of science regarding how calibration cases are selectively protected? Is it a recognized failure mode—related, say, to the improper expansion of a domain of disagreement, or to a misunderstanding of how refined critics are supposed to operate? I am not looking for a bare label, but for an account of what exactly the move consists in, how it differs from ordinary appeals to complexity, and whether Hume's own appeal to refined judgment was intended precisely to address this asymmetry rather than to be defeated by it.
