Two friends of mine—I'll call them Maya and Jonah—were arguing about Derek Parfit's hitchhiker thought experiment after hearing it summarized on a podcast. They kept circling back to whether rational irrationality still makes sense once you can use external commitment devices.

(1) Maya said the point of the case is that you must be the kind of person who pays the driver after you're safe, because if the driver expects you to act in your immediate self-interest upon arrival, he will never stop for you. She insisted this requires being genuinely irrational in that later moment—choosing against your own welfare—to make the earlier promise credible.

(2) Jonah replied that this mistakes acting irrationally for being irrational. He argued that using a binding contract, an escrow, or any automatic payment mechanism achieves the same credible commitment without psychological strangeness: you remain fully rational, because you've simply removed the future option to defect.

(3) Maya countered that drivers care about your character, not your paperwork. She said if payment depends only on a mechanical lock or smart contract, the driver may still fear you'll disable it, and in any case you've turned a paradox about practical reason into an engineering problem about trust. The irrationality, she claimed, has to live in your preferences, not in your tools.

(4) Jonah then accused Maya of smuggling morality into rationality. Parfit framed the problem as pure practical reasoning, he said, but if the only reason to pay is that you are the kind of person who keeps promises, that is a virtue-ethical answer, not proof that rational agents should sometimes harm themselves.

(5) Maya answered that Jonah missed the possibility of self-constructed preference change. Even without morality, she argued, your present self can cultivate future gratitude or loyalty, so the irrational payment is not forced by an external gadget but is a feature of your redesigned agency—meaning commitment can be internal without being merely mechanical.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, identify who is closer to the mark on the distinction between character-based and mechanical commitment, and say what both miss about whether the paradox survives if agents can reshape their own preference structures. Give your answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
