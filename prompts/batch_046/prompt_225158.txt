My university's disability-services office is revising its intake form. A student-advocacy group wants to add "chronic cognitive depletion without mood disturbance" — a phrase circulating in online peer forums but absent from the DSM and ICD. The office director worries that if they list it, students will begin organizing their self-understanding around it, seek accommodations for it, and thereby actually produce the stable pattern of impairment the form describes. The advocates reply that people are already suffering; the form merely gives them a name and access to reduced course loads.

Treat this concretely: map what plays the role of the classifier, the classified population, and the institutional feedback mechanism. Then examine whether this case behaves like an interactive kind in the relevant philosophical sense, or whether an implicit comparison to physical-disease taxonomy — where labeling does not alter the pathology — is carrying hidden weight. Does the loop here seem benign, harmful, or unavoidable? And does the fact that this particular label unlocks a specific administrative benefit — eligibility for reduced workload — change the moral calculus in a way that a purely descriptive diagnosis would not?

Give me the conceptual audit, not generic administrative advice.
