I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm trying to work through a disagreement I witnessed at a recent workshop on moral aggregation. Two researchers—I'll call them Dr. Alina Voss and Dr. Marcus Chen—got into a sustained exchange about what they were calling "threshold-commensurability lexical negative utilitarianism," or TCLNU for short. The idea, as they presented it, is that pleasure and pain are evaluated on a single cardinal scale below a lexical boundary T, but once suffering crosses T it acquires lexical priority and becomes incommensurable with any quantity of well-being. Their debate wasn't about whether negative utilitarianism is correct in general, but about whether this particular hybrid—adding a sharp or fuzzy commensurability threshold—holds together conceptually.

Here is how their exchange went, reconstructed from my notes.

Move 1 (Voss): TCLNU solves the classic aggregation problem cleanly. If suffering is below T, we can weigh it against pleasure in the usual additive way; if it crosses T, no amount of happiness can justify or offset it. That preserves mathematical tractability for ordinary cases while installing an absolute floor against atrocity.

Move 2 (Chen): A sharp boundary creates a "boundary explosion." A world with one person at T + ε has lexically worse suffering than a world with billions at T − ε, no matter how much pleasure those billions enjoy. The experiential gap between T − ε and T + ε is negligible, but the normative gap is infinite. That discontinuity makes the view unusable for any real policy that operates near the boundary, since tiny measurement errors or minor interventions near T would carry unlimited moral weight.

Move 3 (Voss): That problem only arises if you insist on a sharp line. She proposed instead an "epsilon-band" [T − ε, T + ε]—a zone of normative indeterminacy where comparison between suffering and pleasure is not merely epistemically difficult but category-mistaken, analogous to asking whether a symphony weighs more than a promise. Outside the band, standard aggregation applies; inside it, the theory remains deliberately silent rather than exploding.

Move 4 (Chen): A fuzzy band just relocates the incoherence. If comparison is suspended inside the band, then we cannot rank worlds where some people fall inside it and others fall outside. You end up with decision paralysis across exactly the configurations—mass trauma with scattered survivors—that a moral theory ought to guide. Worse, if pleasure and pain are commensurable everywhere except inside a narrow window, then TCLNU collapses into ordinary negative utilitarianism with an ad hoc exception clause that does no independent work.

Move 5 (Voss): Chen is treating the band as an epistemic gap, but it is normative. The silence inside the epsilon-zone is not ignorance; it is a recognition that certain configurations of suffering break the scale on which trade-offs make sense. She then added that both of them had been assuming static world-comparisons and missing the temporal dimension: if suffering is dynamic and agents can move in and out of the band over a life, then lexical priority applies not to static snapshots but to trajectories, which fundamentally changes how long-term policies should be evaluated.
