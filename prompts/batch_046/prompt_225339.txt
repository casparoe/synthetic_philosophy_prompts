I've been working through epiphenomenalism—the idea that conscious experience is like a shadow cast by brain activity, with no causal power of its own. The well-known difficulty is the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if my experience of redness doesn't cause my belief that I'm seeing red, then my belief seems groundless, yet I clearly make judgments about my own consciousness. Most responses say the brain causes both the experience and the belief in parallel. But I want you to argue for a stricter position: that this parallel-causation answer fails because it treats phenomenal judgments as if they must track something, when in fact they function as coordinative placeholders—speech acts that organize behavior and social interaction without representing inner states. Build the argument that once we see phenomenal reports as tools for aligning action rather than descriptions of private events, the paradox disappears, because there is no missing causal link that needs explaining. Address the obvious objection: if these reports don't track consciousness, how do we explain cases where people seem to correct each other about their own feelings? Resolve this by arguing that such corrections are adjustments to behavioral coherence, not corrections of representational error. Keep the reasoning internal; don't appeal to named theories or authors.
