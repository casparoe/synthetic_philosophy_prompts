Here are my considered verdicts about knowledge attribution across a range of cases that mix practical stakes with conversational setting. I want to know whether a single principle can capture them all, or whether consistency requires me to abandon at least one.

Case 1 — Low-stakes deposit. Sarah checks her bank's posted hours on a Tuesday and forms the belief that the branch opens on Saturdays. She needs to deposit £50. When asked whether she knows the bank is open on Saturday, she answers yes without hesitation. Verdict: she genuinely knows.

Case 2 — High-stakes deposit. The evidence and belief-formation are identical to Case 1, but Sarah must deposit £50,000 that will cover her firm's payroll; failure means insolvency. Asked the same question, she replies that she does not know and ought to telephone to confirm. Verdict: despite identical evidence, she does not know.

Case 3 — Conversational shift. In an ordinary street conversation, attributing knowledge to Sarah (with the evidence and stakes of Case 1) sounds correct. In a philosophy seminar devoted to scepticism, attributing knowledge to her on the same basis sounds false or at least highly strained, even though nothing about her mental state or practical situation has changed. Verdict: the truth of 'Sarah knows...' varies with the conversational context, not with her stakes.

Case 4 — Insulated expertise in a collective-risk setting. Dr. Patel has reviewed exhaustive clinical-trial data and writes in an academic article, 'We know that Treatment Y reduces mortality by thirty per cent.' Several years later, she testifies before a parliamentary inquiry where an erroneous claim could trigger mass withdrawal of the treatment and widespread harm. The data are unchanged. Asked whether she knows, she says only that the evidence strongly supports the claim, and declines to assert knowledge. Verdict: in the article she knows; in the inquiry she does not, because the collective practical burden of error transforms her epistemic standing rather than merely her willingness to speak.

Case 5 — Epistemically insulated basic belief. A student correctly computes that 7 + 5 = 12. In a classroom quiz she knows this. Imagine now that the same computation, if wrong, would cause a catastrophic engineering failure, though she performs it only for herself and is unaware of any connection to the system. Verdict: she still knows that 7 + 5 = 12; practical stakes cannot erode knowledge of this kind.

These five judgments are in tension. Cases 1, 2 and 4 point toward pragmatic encroachment: high practical costs can defeat knowledge. Case 3 points toward epistemic contextualism: the standards for correct knowledge-attribution shift with conversational context, regardless of stakes. Case 5 points toward a domain of beliefs that are wholly insulated from pragmatic pressure.

Please do the following, and present your response in British English:

1. Formulate one precise principle, or a small family of competing principles, designed to accommodate all five verdicts. For each clause or parameter, identify the specific case that motivates it.

2. Apply your principle(s) to two further cases that I have not mentioned:
   (a) A juror who has heard standard courtroom evidence and is convinced of the defendant's guilt; she is then informed that if her judgment is wrong, an innocent person will be executed. Does she still know?
   (b) A mathematician who proves a theorem in isolation, then presents the identical proof in a high-stakes security audit where a single error would collapse a financial system. Does her knowledge of the theorem's validity change?

3. State explicitly which of my five verdicts each principle must reject, and argue that the rejection is the least damaging revision, or explain why the apparent tension dissolves upon closer inspection.
