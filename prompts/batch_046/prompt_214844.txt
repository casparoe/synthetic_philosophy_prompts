I'm drafting a short position paper for a graduate seminar on social epistemology and would like critical feedback on its coherence, its novelty, and any hidden empirical dependencies. The core claim is that Miranda Fricker's distinction between testimonial and hermeneutical injustice captures two symptoms of a deeper mechanism I want to call interpretive debt. On this view, hermeneutical injustice is not merely a gap in the collective hermeneutical resource pool—say, the absence of a concept like workplace gender coercion from dominant discourse—but a structured failure to acknowledge and integrate interpretive labor performed by marginalized groups under conditions of exclusion. When consciousness-raising groups in the 1970s developed vocabulary for describing coercive workplace dynamics, the dominant culture did not simply lack the term; it had actively foreclosed the channels through which that labor could enter shared discourse, while benefiting from the social stability that the concept's absence maintained. The debt is therefore compound: it includes the original exclusion, the unacknowledged extraction of labor once the concept is developed, and the ongoing resistance to restructuring shared interpretive practices. I argue that recognizing this debt changes the moral geometry of the case. Standard hermeneutical injustice focuses on the harm of not being intelligible; the debt framing adds that the dominant group owes not just recognition but a kind of epistemic reparative labor—revising its own conceptual schemes in ways that may feel disruptive. This seems to generate a tension with testimonial injustice: if a speaker's testimony is initially discounted because the concept is missing, but later accepted once the concept enters dominant discourse, has the original testimonial wrong been corrected, or has it been transformed into a failure to pay interpretive debt?

Could you comment on whether the interpretive debt concept genuinely adds explanatory power beyond Fricker's framework, or whether it collapses back into either testimonial or hermeneutical injustice? I'm particularly interested in whether the idea of unacknowledged extraction of interpretive labor is coherent without smuggling in property-rights metaphors that do not apply to concepts, and whether the moral obligation I sketch—revising one's conceptual scheme—is too demanding or implausibly specific. Please also flag any points where my argument might depend on an empirical claim about how the vocabulary for workplace coercion actually developed in feminist consciousness-raising, since I want the argument to stand or fall on conceptual grounds rather than historical ones. If the framing holds, does it imply that dominant groups have a positive obligation to adopt every interpretive framework developed in marginalized spaces, or can we distinguish legitimate integration from illegitimate appropriation without appealing to the very hermeneutical resources that are in dispute?
