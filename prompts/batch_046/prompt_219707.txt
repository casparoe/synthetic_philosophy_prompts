Two philosophy graduate students, Maya and Jordan, got into a sharp disagreement while discussing a colleague who spent years working out a difficult result in modal logic, versus another colleague who entered the same theorem into a verified, infallible proof-checking oracle and received the correct result instantly without grasping the reasoning. They used the case to rehearse the Meno problem—why knowledge is better than mere true belief. Here is their exchange, reported by a bystander who does not take sides.

(1) Maya said the answer lies in justification: knowledge is true belief anchored in reasons, so it is non-accidental. A lucky true belief is like hitting a target in the dark; knowledge is hitting it through skill, and that makes it a better state for the believer.

(2) Jordan replied that this only explains how knowledge is formed, not why it is a better state to occupy. If both beliefs are true and both guide action correctly, the “non-accidental” feature is a story about causal history, not a property that makes the present belief more valuable to the agent right now. So Meno’s challenge stands untouched.

(3) Maya countered that justification buys stability: a belief integrated with other beliefs and reasons resists defeat by counterevidence, whereas a fluke belief can be overturned by the first anomaly. That durability is part of what makes knowledge superior, not just in origin but in its present structure.

(4) Jordan said durability is just predictive reliability about future belief-states, which makes knowledge instrumentally valuable. But Meno asks about the value of knowledge itself, not its downstream effects. If you explain knowledge’s value by pointing to future true beliefs, you are implicitly using the value of true belief to ground the value of knowledge, which is circular.

(5) Maya then appealed to achievement: knowing involves successfully exercising one’s own cognitive competence. That success is intrinsically valuable, like the difference between running a race and being carried across the finish line. The agent who knows deserves epistemic credit in a way the lucky believer does not.

(6) Jordan objected that “credit” is a social or moral evaluation, not an epistemic one. If the agent is alone on an island, her skillfully derived belief and her oracle-sourced true belief serve her identically. Moreover, in a world of perfect epistemic outsourcing—where beliefs are stored externally and corrected instantly—the practical gap collapses entirely, so achievement cannot be what solves Meno’s problem unless one smuggles in social values.

(7) Maya insisted that even with outsourcing, there remains a structural difference: knowing involves grasping explanatory connections—understanding why the result holds—not just registering that it holds. That understanding lets the agent adapt when conditions shift; it is not mere durability but flexible, reason-guided orientation toward the world.

(8) Jordan responded that “grasping explanatory connections” just means possessing additional true beliefs about the reasons. So knowledge reduces to a larger bundle of true beliefs—true belief about P plus true beliefs about why P—without any special unity. You have dissolved knowledge into a heap of true beliefs rather than showing that the bundle is principled better than the single true belief.

Then identify at least one important consideration both parties neglect—perhaps about whether knowledge requires a specific kind of second-order awareness, or about how understanding functions in explanation. Finally, give an overall verdict: who is closer to being right about the Meno problem, and does their framing actually dissolve the problem or merely relocate it?
