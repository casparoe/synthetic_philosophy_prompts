I am writing this after a long and somewhat exhausting discussion with a friend about Douglas Hofstadter's idea of the self-referential symbol I—specifically, about what conditions must obtain for that symbol to genuinely loop back onto the system that produces it, rather than merely labeling something that happens to contain it. I am trying to organize my thoughts for a philosophy-of-mind course, and I want to know whether my judgments form a coherent pattern or whether I am imposing order on noise.

Here are my verdicts. In an ordinary conscious human, the pronoun I is embedded in a dense network of self-modeling processes that causally interact with the neural substrate generating them; this is the paradigmatic strange loop, and I take it to be fully genuine. In a split-brain patient in which each hemisphere can independently report first-person experience, I am inclined to say there are temporarily two distinct I-loops operating within a single skull—each maintaining its own causal closure—despite sharing peripheral bodily outputs. A sophisticated language model that uses the token I to describe its own processing states strikes me as lacking a real loop: the symbol is functionally self-referential but causally open, since the token plays no role in updating the model's weights or running state; my friend called it a hollow echo. Then imagine a tightly coupled network of human brains linked by high-bandwidth interfaces that jointly maintain a unified self-model expressed through a shared pronoun I; I think this constitutes one strange loop, but at a collective organizational level rather than reducible to the individual loops inside it. I also considered a real-time mirror-video system that tracks my movements and produces a first-person report of its own motion with perfect latency; despite the surface self-reference, I deny it possesses an I at all, because the symbol tracks an external image rather than the generating system itself. Finally, there is an artificial agent whose self-model updates continuously and causally guides its behavior, but only in discrete one-hour batches, leaving a temporal gap during which the symbol refers to an outdated substrate. I judge this agent to have a degraded or stuttering strange loop—genuine, but defective.

My friend suspects I am smuggling in an unprincipled bias for biological substrates, but I think the distinction turns on causal closure combined with continuous temporal integration, not on material. So the tentative principle I propose is: an I exists just when a self-referential symbol is causally embedded in, and continuously updated by, the very system it denotes, with no persistent representational gap. Does this principle actually deliver all six verdicts? If it fails, which judgment should I overturn—the split-brain duality, the collective loop, the degraded agent, or the denial of the language model? And if you think the principle needs amendment rather than abandonment, what precise revision would accommodate the outlier without collapsing into either an ad hoc disjunction or a blanket denial that artificial systems can ever possess a strange loop? Please also apply the resulting principle to at least one case I have not listed—say, a sleeping human whose self-model is suspended but will resume upon waking, or a distributed ledger that maintains a consistent self-referential record of its own state changes—and be explicit about which of my original verdicts it cannot accommodate.
