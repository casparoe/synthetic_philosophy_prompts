Act as a professional philosopher of science with actual workshop facilitation experience. Before you draft the activity, take a moment to brainstorm three different game mechanics that could make people feel the pull of heuristic independence arguments — then pick the best one and run with it.

I'm running an advanced philosophy of science seminar, about 13 people (mostly grad students plus a couple sharp seniors), one 80-minute session. I want them to physically feel why heuristic arguments for treating evidence pieces as independant are so seductive, and then feel the formal collapse when that independence is violated by hidden common structure.

Design a fully runnable classroom game centered on a fake disputed measurement — say, an obscure decay constant in some niche subfield. Give participants evidence cards that carry provenance tags: "Lab A direct human observation", "Lab B automated readout", "independant replication by third party", "algorithmic cross-check". Build in rules and a simple collective betting/scoring mechanic so they have to update confidence step-by-step and lock in a group verdict. The hidden setup should include a shared calibration source or software dependency that ties two apparently seperate channels together, but don't reveal it until after they've committed.

Make sure they construct the update steps themselves — no lecture up front, and don't name the "presumption of independence" or "provenance tracking failure" until the debrief. The debrief should map each stage onto the heuristic argument versus the formal graphical condition (mention d-separation or common-cause structure, but don't require them to memorize formalism). Be explicit about what feature of formal independence — probably the exact criterion for separation in the graph — the activity cannot reproduce and you have to supply verbally. Also tell me what's predictably gonna go wrong the first run (someone spots the hidden link too early? the betting pool collapses? people refuse to update?).

Keep it somewhat in the weeds: inject the idea that provenance tracking failure is its own distinct epistemic error mode, not just generic "bad reasoning". No bibliographies, no reading lists, no citation requests — the students shouldn't need to look anything up, and neither should you invent fake references. Write casually, a typo or two is totally fine.
