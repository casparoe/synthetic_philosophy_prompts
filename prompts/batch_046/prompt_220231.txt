I have been reflecting independently, without formal training in philosophy or economics, on what fairness requires when people start from different positions. I keep circling a specific puzzle: if equality means identical resources, it ignores that people need different inputs to reach the same outcomes; if it means equal satisfaction, it risks endorsing people who have simply adjusted their desires downward; and if it means equal real freedom to achieve valuable activities, the idea becomes hard to measure and easy to confuse with mere opportunity. I want to present this puzzle—whether equality should target welfare, resources, or capabilities—to a small discussion group of curious, educated adults who lack any background in these subjects. Rather than a finished lecture, I need a structured teaching plan: the exact sequence in which these three conceptions must be introduced, with explicit attention to which one depends on the prior one being fully clear; the single paired example or scenario that should carry the entire explanation, together with precise instructions about what properties that example must possess and what it must omit so that the distinctions remain sharp rather than collapsing into one another; the misconceptions this particular audience is likely to import—such as the belief that equality demands sameness of treatment, or that happiness is the only legitimate metric of well-being—and the precise moment in the sequence where each misconception should be named and neutralized; a concise check for understanding that would demonstrate the dependency structure has genuinely taken root; and an honest statement of what should be left out at this level and acknowledged as too complex. I am especially uncertain about where, if at all, to place the phenomenon in which people adapt their preferences to constrained circumstances: should this be woven into the sequence, and if so, at which stage does it illuminate rather than obscure the main argument? Please answer in American English.
