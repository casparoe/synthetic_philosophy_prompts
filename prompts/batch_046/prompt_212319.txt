You are an expert in archaeological epistemology and heritage-compliance methodology. Before you begin, take a moment to brainstorm the core distinction between indexical traces (direct physical residues of an action) and structural inferences (claims about institutions or beliefs that depend on pattern, absence, and analogical reasoning), because your task turns on keeping those two modes separate under practical pressure.

Your audience is a tribal heritage compliance officer who must review accidental-discovery reports from construction and development sites. She has no authority to excavate further, rarely has access to living cultural consultants, and must sign off within 72 hours on whether the assemblage supports a protected-institution finding (e.g., ceremonial or governance-related use) versus a domestic-functional finding. She needs a reusable decision procedure she can run without a PhD in archaeology—ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a clear negative, where it hands off to a values judgment rather than a factual determination, and where it explicitly misfires.

Write a formal, polished procedure in American English. Structure it as a flowchart in words: numbered stages, each with (a) the precise question to ask of the report and physical evidence, (b) the threshold that must be met to proceed, (c) the failure condition that stops the process with a “no protected-institution inference,” and (d) the judgment-node flag where the officer must invoke community values rather than material logic. The conceptual work should survive simplification: preserve the difference between formation-process noise and intentional arrangement, between analogical reasoning and direct identification, and between negative evidence (what is missing) and absence of evidence.

Then apply the procedure to three cases, describing each assemblage in enough detail to run the steps:
1. A cluster of deliberately modified bison-bone tools arranged in a radial pattern around a central fire-cracked rock feature near a perennial spring, with no domestic debris.
2. A scattered mix of burned maize kernels, fragmented cooking ceramics, and deer-bone fragments recovered from a mechanically disturbed plow zone with no intact stratigraphy.
3. A geometric circle of unmodified river cobbles set in a shallow depression with no artifacts, no burning, and no faunal remains inside or adjacent—an assemblage that produces a false-positive institutional signal if the procedure is followed mechanically.

For each case, walk through the stages, state the procedure’s output, and for case 3 explicitly identify the misfire and say what the procedure cannot do to prevent it.

Finally, conclude with a concise statement of the procedure’s hard limits: what kinds of belief-reconstruction it can never license from objects alone, what role ethnographic or community knowledge must play that this procedure cannot replace, and the specific risk of over-reading pattern in low-information contexts. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for further sources.
