hey, i've been going in circles trying to pin down what diachronic rationality actually demands when money pumps are involved. here are my gut verdicts on concrete cases -- tell me if there's one principle behind them, or if i'm just being inconsistent and need to drop one.

1. the classic dutch book loop: you have A > B > C > A, and a bookie offers trades with $0.01 fees each step. you loop around and end up with A again minus three cents. verdict: irrational, full stop. you've been pumped.

2. belief revision over time: you prefer bet X to Y with prior 0.5, then observe data and update to 0.8 so you flip to Y, then more data sends you back to X. each switch costs $5 in transaction fees. verdict: totally rational, just bayes being bayes. not a pump.

3. life-plan drift: at 20 you commit to art school, at 30 you piviot to finance, at 40 you're back to art. each piviot costs real tuition and lost earnings. verdict: i keep going back and forth. part of me says your values genuinely evolved, so it's fine; part says you're failing at being a consistent planner across your life.

4. precommitment: knowing you have loop #1, you pay $100 for a "preference lock" that blocks any trades for a year. verdict: smart move, not irrational.

5. the social-media framing loop: you spend about $1 in attention and cognative load to adopt framing S1 after argument A, then the same cost to revert to S2 after counter-argument B, repeating with no net information gain. verdict: this feels like being pumped, but i can't articulate why it's different from case 2.

6. the reverse-pump fear: you refuse to switch from X to Y even after a genuine belief update, just because you're terrified an adversery might be setting you up for a loop. you stay stuck in a worse choice. verdict: also irrational, but in the opposite direction.

is there a coherent principle that saves all six? maybe something like "local rationality is required, plus you must avoid predictable net-loss cycles that you could foresee and prevent"? but then case 3 is still murky, and case 5 vs 2 is genuinely hard for me. which verdict should i drop? and what does the best account predict for a case i haven't listed: you have stable preferences and symmetric evidence, but an adversery can selectively order the presentation so you pay switch-costs back and forth forever, with zero net belief change?
