I've been working through the literature on well-being for an independent study, and I keep returning to the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: the claim that eating a ripe peach, completing a difficult proof, and feeling relief after pain share nothing in common beyond being labeled pleasant. In my reading group, discussion always reaches the same impasse. The hedonists maintain that there is a uniform phenomenal quality — a distinctive pleasantness — present in every case, which justifies treating all pleasures as instances of a single basic good. The critics respond with family-resemblance arguments, noting that introspection reveals no shared feature across sensory, cognitive, and affective enjoyment, and that comparing these states seems as arbitrary as comparing chess with soccer because both are called games.

Yet I suspect the stall is not really about phenomenology. It strikes me that one camp is asking a normative-aggregative question — what property must be present for an experience to contribute positively to welfare in a manner that permits comparison and aggregation — while the other is asking a psychological-taxonomic question about what feature, if any, unifies the mental-state class we call pleasure across domains. If so, then disputing introspective reports is like debating chess rules by inspecting chess pieces. There may also be a hidden empirical disagreement about whether neural reward circuits provide a unifying substrate, though both sides tend to dismiss that as conceptually irrelevant. Or the dispute may be partly verbal: in welfare discourse, pleasant might function indexically, picking out different evaluative properties in different contexts, so demanding a single qualitative essence could be a category mistake.

Please diagnose this impasse. Is it substantive, verbal, or a case of two disciplines — normative ethics and philosophy of mind — answering different questions under one heading? Identify any hidden empirical or formal issue doing the work. Tell me what each side actually says about the phenomenology, about welfare comparison, and about the relevance of neuroscience, and ground your diagnosis in those commitments. Then specify what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, an experimental result, a formal model of welfare aggregation, or an argument that the question is ill-posed. I am particularly interested in whether the hedonist's demand for commensurability is performing hidden normative labor that the phenomenological critics overlook. If my framing rests on a confused premise — for example, if the heterogeneity problem is not genuinely stalled, or if the normative-taxonomic split I propose collapses on inspection, or if I have mischaracterized either side's actual commitments — say so plainly rather than accommodating the frame politely. I want direct pushback if the task as posed cannot be done well.
