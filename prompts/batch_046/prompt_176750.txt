At a recent philosophy of decision-theory reading group, my friends Elena and Marcus spent nearly an hour arguing about superrationality, focusing on the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery. Elena opened by defending cooperation in Platonia: the two agents are perfect reasoning duplicates isolated in separate rooms, so each can model the other’s choice as identical to her own; the correlation is logical, not causal, which makes leaving the smaller guaranteed payoff untouched the rational move, since both will reach the same conclusion independently. Marcus replied that this confuses logical identity with predictive dependence—without a causal channel between the rooms, treating the other agent’s choice as linked to mine is just magical thinking dressed in formal language, and standard game theory correctly predicts defection because each agent should maximize regardless of what a copy in another room does. Elena insisted that superrationality never requires causation, only shared algorithmic structure: if the code is identical, the outputs match regardless of physical separation.

Marcus then shifted to the Luring Lottery, in which entrants submit unique integers and the lowest unique number wins a large prize. He argued that superrational reasoning does not stabilize at the top but produces an unstable downward cascade—every agent realizes others will avoid 1, so they should claim 1; then they realize everyone thinks that, so they claim 1 anyway—meaning true superrational agents should either randomize uniformly or abstain entirely rather than cluster in a high range. Elena countered that Marcus was applying level-k reasoning and stopping arbitrarily at a shallow depth; a genuinely superrational agent runs the recursion to its fixed point, which in a competitive entry game should be a symmetric randomization over a high interval, not a race to the bottom. Marcus answered that there is no stable fixed point in the Luring Lottery, and that introducing even slight algorithmic noise—where agents run nearly but not exactly identical reasoning code—breaks the correlation Elena needs, leaving only conventional strategic reasoning and undermining her Platonia argument as well.

They turned to me and asked for a referee’s judgment. Please adjudicate the dispute point by point: is superrational cooperation in Platonia justified without causal linkage between the agents; does the Luring Lottery have a stable superrational equilibrium or an inevitable downward cascade; and does algorithmic noise defeat the correlation argument entirely? Then tell me who is more right overall, and say what both parties are missing—perhaps about the difference between logical correlation and common-cause reasoning, or about how superrationality applies differently in symmetric one-shot cooperation games versus competitive entry games with unique winners.
