There's this norm in some parliamentary coalitions that I keep thinking about. The junior party signs on to a deal where they have to vote for bills they hate, and there's an unwritten rule that if they break ranks and vote no, they don't just loose influence — they get publically labeled as unfit for government by the senior partners, even when the bill is exactly the kind of thing they campaigned against. Meanwhile, if they stay and vote yes, they're expected to defend it in public, and nobody treats that as hypocrisy, just as doing the job.

What theory of political morality does that practice presuppose? Is it basically the dirty-hands idea — that you have to get your hands dirty to do any good — or is it something more specific about loyalty and role morality? And is it consistent with the same system's idea that politicians should be personally accountable for thier choices? I mean, if the junior minister says I only did it because the coalition required it, that sounds like an excuse, but the practice seems to treat it as a justification. So what's actually being assumed about responsibility here, and where does the practice fall apart if you reject the idea that institutional roles can absorb moral blame?
