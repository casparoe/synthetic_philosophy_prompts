I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science working on formal approaches to biological organization. In a recent seminar on the logic of life-definitions, a colleague presented an argument that applies Gödel's incompleteness results directly to autopoietic theory. I find the conclusion—that a complete scientific definition of life is formally impossible—too strong, but I cannot locate the exact misstep. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Any consistent formal system S that is recursively axiomatizable and can represent elementary arithmetic contains a Gödel sentence G_S: a statement that is true in the standard interpretation of S but unprovable within S itself.

(2) Autopoietic theory—the framework that defines living systems in terms of organizational closure, self-production, and structural coupling—can be formalized as just such a system A. Its state-space is countable, its boundary conditions are computable, and its rules of structural change are recursively specifiable.

(3) Therefore, there exists a Gödel sentence G_A describing an organizational configuration that actually satisfies all conditions for being an autopoietic system, yet is not provably living within A.

(4) If the theory cannot prove that this configuration belongs to its own class of living systems, then the theory is necessarily incomplete with respect to its domain: there are genuinely living organizational states that science, using this theory, can never classify as alive.

(5) By Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, if the consistency of A can be expressed in A, then A cannot prove its own consistency. Hence we can never establish, from within the theory, that our criteria for life are not self-contradictory.

(6) Since every formalizable theory of life will share these properties, no scientific framework can ever provide a complete and self-certifying definition of life. There will always be borderline organizational states that are alive but permanently unrecognizable as such, and we can never be sure our criteria are coherent.

(7) Consequently, the long-standing philosophical project of defining life—whether via metabolism, reproduction, or organizational closure—is not merely empirically difficult; it is structurally doomed in the same way that a complete formalization of arithmetic is doomed.

The conclusion seems to collapse a logical result about formal derivability into an empirical claim about biological classification. Still, I am unsure whether the error is in (2) (that autopoiesis is arithmetically expressible in the required sense), in (3) (that the Gödel sentence maps onto an empirical organizational state), in (4) (the slide from unprovable in A to unrecognizable in practice), or in (5)-(6) (the application of the second theorem to empirical consistency). I would prefer not to abandon (1), which is standard, and I am inclined to defend the idea that formal theories of biology have real limits. Which premise should I reject, and what does that rejection commit me to? If the flaw is indeed in (2), does that mean biological theories are essentially non-formalizable, or only that this particular formalization fails? If it is in (4), how do I avoid the charge that I am simply ignoring the genuine epistemic gap the Gödel sentence reveals?

Please diagnose the argument without appealing to specific bibliographic references I could not verify without database access.
