I have recently inherited management of my family’s experimental orchard, which has forced me to think seriously about whether honoring sunk investments can ever be rational—a question that kept surfacing in Dr. Elena Voss’s graduate seminar on practical reason that I have been attending this semester. Over the past months, Dr. Voss has delivered consistent verdicts across five cases: she held that a novelist who has drafted four hundred pages of a conceptually flawed manuscript should abandon it unless the remaining chapters possess independent merit, not because of the labor already expended; that a city with a half-completed tunnel should finish it only after comparing the costs of completion against demolition and abandonment, treating the sunk construction as altering the present option space rather than as a psychological weight; that researchers working within a largely discredited theoretical framework should continue only long enough to transfer its local problem-solving capacities, never because the decade of refinement itself demands loyalty; that a caregiver for a relative now in permanent unconsciousness should maintain care because the commitment’s internal logic extends to this phase, not as repayment for years already served; and that a community preserving an ancient irrigation system should do so because centuries of continuous maintenance have created an irreplaceable ecological state, not out of reverence for past labor. These judgments appear jointly consistent with more than one underlying principle—perhaps a strict future-value rule modified only by present structural dependencies, or perhaps a broader view that past investments can generate present commitments or ecological baselines carrying independent rational weight.

The decision I now face concerns the orchard itself. For three generations, my family has maintained a heritage grafting protocol—now abandoned by commercial agriculture—that binds antique rootstock to modern scions through a labor-intensive manual technique no longer taught outside our region. The yields are substantially lower than monoculture alternatives, climate projections suggest the method will become unsustainable within fifteen years, and conversion would free considerable resources. At the same time, the chronic physiological stress induced by this specific grafting process has produced an unusual root architecture that sustains a rare mycorrhizal network currently under study by soil ecologists; if we switch to standard high-yield planting, that network will likely collapse within a decade. Moreover, the orchard has gradually come to be understood—not only by my family but by local agricultural historians—as a living archive of practice rather than a mere production site. Given Dr. Voss’s track record, should she advise continuing the protocol, converting entirely, or adopting some intermediate course? Which of her five precedents is the closest analog, and if her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, what principle would she be endorsing with each possible recommendation?

Please lay out the competing rules that could generate her earlier verdicts with equal consistency, predict her most likely ruling on the orchard with explicit reasoning, name the further case that would best discriminate between those rules, and clearly separate what is broadly settled in discussions of practical reason and sunk costs from what remains genuinely contested—without presenting any disputed interpretation as though it were the consensus view.
