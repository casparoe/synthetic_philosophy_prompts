I spent thirty years as an experimental physicist before turning to philosophy in retirement, and I still tend to frame institutional problems in terms of equilibrium conditions and feedback loops rather than in purely normative language. I now serve on the advisory board of a small interdisciplinary foundation that distributes research grants according to a formal charter—call it the equity protocol—which requires all recipients to deposit raw data in an open repository within six months. The protocol was adopted democratically and is, by our internal lights, fair. Yet our largest industrial partner, which supplies roughly forty percent of our operating budget, has informed us that it will withdraw all support if the protocol is applied to its proprietary projects. Several early-career researchers depend on that funding stream for their salaries. The board is split: one faction argues that compromising the protocol to retain the donor would destroy the charter’s moral authority and reduce us to accomplices in a kind of institutional blackmail; the other faction holds that preserving the foundation and protecting those positions is the greater practical good, and that a rule which annihilates the institution it governs is self-defeating.

In trying to think this through, I have been experimenting with a distinction of my own—between normative gravity, by which I mean the pull of a principle regardless of whether anyone can enforce it, and institutional inertia, by which I mean the resistance of existing power structures to that pull—but I am unsure whether this distinction actually clarifies anything. I have also been reading Pascal’s reflections on the necessary relation between justice and force, and I wonder whether his framework applies here. Is there a coherent philosophical position that allows one to maintain that the protocol remains genuinely just even when we lack the power to enforce it against the donor, without reducing justice to a mere rhetorical ornament? Or does the recognition that justice without power is impotent entail that, in this specific case, we should treat the donor’s leverage as a brute fact that redefines what justice can demand of us? I do not want a bibliography or a survey of the literature; I want to understand whether the intuition that there is a real difference between recognizing a norm and being able to instantiate it can survive philosophical scrutiny, and what concrete guidance it should offer for our vote.
