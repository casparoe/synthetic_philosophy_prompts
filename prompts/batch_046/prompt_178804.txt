As a retired biologist who has recently begun studying philosophy of mind, I have become absorbed by the disagreement over whether dreams are genuine experiences. Norman Malcolm maintained that dreaming is not an experience occurring during sleep, because in sleep we lack the behavioral criteria that justify attributing an experience to someone; the report given upon waking is therefore a judgment or narrative construction rather than a recollection of something actually lived through. Daniel Dennett, by contrast, argued that dreams do have experiential status, though they may be assembled during REM and become accessible only upon awakening, so that rejecting them as experiences depends on an implausibly direct, Cartesian model of inner observation.

To test the resilience of these two positions, consider a hypothetical technology: a non-invasive neural decoder that, during REM sleep, continuously translates cortical replay dynamics into a coherent running narrative displayed on an external monitor, without waking the sleeper. Suppose that the sleeper's verbal report upon awakening matches this externally displayed narrative with high fidelity, and that the correlation persists even when the sleeper is briefly aroused into a confusional state that disrupts normal report formation, while the decoder still captures the narrative intact. We would then appear to possess third-person access to the dream's structure during the sleep episode itself, quite apart from the sleeper's retrospective judgment.

Compare Malcolm's and Dennett's views in light of this scenario. Would Malcolm regard the decoder display as supplying the missing criterion for an experience occurring during sleep, or would he insist that the narrative is genuinely constituted only at the moment of waking report, with the decoder tracking only a causal precursor? Would Dennett interpret the decoder as confirming that a fully formed experience takes place during REM, or might he maintain that the experience is still constituted at the point of retrieval upon awakening, with the decoder merely observing the loading process? Finally, which framework more naturally extends to cases where verbal report is impossible—such as a non-human mammal exhibiting analogous replay patterns, or an artificial neural network trained on sleep-state dynamics—so that we might speak of dreaming in the absence of any first-person account?
