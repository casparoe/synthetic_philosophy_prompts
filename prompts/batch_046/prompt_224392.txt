I am working through a graduate seminar on the historiography of genocide, and after a session comparing situational and intentionalist explanations of perpetrator behavior—where the instructor briefly mentioned the organizational-sociology literature on normalization of deviance—a fellow student sent me a summary they had pulled from an unnamed review essay. They wrote that Christopher Browning's Ordinary Men demonstrates that Reserve Police Battalion 101 consisted of ordinary middle-aged working-class men from Hamburg who had received no special ideological training, and that these men participated in mass shootings in Poland primarily because of peer pressure, careerist ambition, and the incremental normalization of violence rather than out of pre-existing hatred; since only a small minority refused the orders when explicitly given the option to step aside, Browning concludes that situational and organizational dynamics are powerful enough to turn ordinary people into killers. Daniel Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners contends, in opposition, that ordinary Germans were animated by a deep and distinctive culture of eliminationist anti-Semitism that made them not reluctant participants but eager executioners, so that the Battalion's violence should be read as a willing expression of widely shared beliefs rather than as the result of conformity or bureaucratic pressure; consequently, Goldhagen treats Browning's situational framework as an exculpatory distraction that obscures the perpetrators' genuine ideological commitment. The reviewer then claims that because both scholars draw on the same archival records yet arrive at diametrically opposed interpretations, the disagreement is ultimately irreducible to empirical evidence and amounts to a normative choice between blaming social psychology and blaming cultural ideology, with neither framework being genuinely falsifiable.

How fair is this characterization? Please audit it point by point: indicate which parts accurately capture Browning's and Goldhagen's respective positions, which parts subtly misrepresent or overstate one or both arguments, whether the reviewer is correct that the two frameworks are empirically indistinguishable, and whether the conclusion that the debate is merely normative follows from the accurate portions. I am especially unsure whether Goldhagen actually denies all situational influence, and whether Browning's refusal-rate data supports the broad generalization that situational forces can make almost anyone a perpetrator.
