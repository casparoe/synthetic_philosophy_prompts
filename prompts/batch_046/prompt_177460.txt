I was talking with a friend in enviromental history and I keep noticing this pattern in the literature she sends me that I can't find a name for. An author spends chapters mapping how slow geographic and climate rhythms—soil depletion cycles, the North Atlantic Oscillation, river sedimentation—shaped settlement and law over centuries, classic Braudel-style longue durée where the event-layer almost disappears. Then the narrative suddenly pivots: a famine strikes, and instead of treating it as the inevitable expression of those deep structures, the author blames a specific minister's grain-tax decision in 1742, framing the centuries of ecological constraint as mere 'background conditions.' Flip to another chapter about a different region, and the causality inverts: now a century-long drought gets full explanatory weight for a societal collapse, while the political crisis that coincided with it is just the 'trigger event.'

There's no consistent rule for when slow structures carry the causal load versus when an individual decision or political event does. It's not ordinary multicausality, because the deep factor is always present in the description but its explanatory status shifts depending on whether the story needs human agency to feel complete. I know Braudel critiqued event-history for overvaluing the short term, but this feels like the mirror problem—using long-duration structures descriptively while reserving actual explanation for events and actors whenever it suits the narrative arc. Does this have an established term in historiography or philosophy of history—maybe a specific bias or framing failure? If there's no recognized concept, what's the clearest way to define it so it doesn't just collapse into the standard structure-versus-agency debate or plain causal pluralism?
