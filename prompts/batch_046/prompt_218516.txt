I am a high school senior in an independent study on the philosophy of mathematics and visual art, and for the past month my mentor and I have been stuck on a disagreement that keeps resurfacing in our discussions of Douglas Hofstadter's idea of tangled hierarchies. Specifically, we keep returning to Escher's Drawing Hands and whether the image genuinely demonstrates that hierarchical levels—like the artist and the artwork, or the object-language and meta-language—can collapse into one another, or whether there is always what Hofstadter describes as an inviolate level that remains untouched by the loop. One position, which my mentor defends, holds that in a true strange loop the distinction between levels is not merely blurred but dissolved: if a system can represent and modify its own rules, then there is no stable outside—no sheet of paper, no hardware substrate, no physical body—that is not already inside the loop. The other position, which I have been arguing for, insists that the very intelligibility of a loop depends on an inviolate boundary: without the paper that holds the drawn hands, there is no drawing; without the silicon and electricity that execute code, there is no self-modifying program. We have applied this framework to everything from Gödel's incompleteness proof to recursive AI architectures that rewrite their own weights, yet neither of us moves.

Is our impasse substantive—do we disagree about whether formal self-reference can ever be fully closed—or is it verbal, turning on whether level and inviolate are being used with different criteria of identity? Could it be that one of us is asking a logical question about the structure of representation, while the other is asking a physical or causal question about implementation, and we have been treating them as one? I want to understand what kind of disagreement this actually is, what evidence or conceptual distinction could break the stalemate, and whether there is any reason to expect progress at all.
