Since being asked to help design a seminar on the Plutarchan tradition of parallel lives and the uses of biography for moral learning, I have become preoccupied with a problem that seems internal to the narrative form itself rather than to any particular historical figure. Give an example of a conceptual issue—one that does not turn on whether a given subject was genuinely virtuous, but on how the architecture of hagiographic biography actively reshapes the moral concepts it purports to teach—that arises when the contingent, chronological particularity of a lived life is translated into an atemporal, pedagogical exemplar. Your example should specify a precise tension between the temporal, context-bound unfolding of practical reasoning in a life and the categorical, generalizable structure required for moral instruction, and it should be articulated with enough rigor that one could see why adding further biographical detail would not dissolve the conceptual problem but might instead obscure it further.
