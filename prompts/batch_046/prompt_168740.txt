Analyze the design and legacy of South Africa’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC), established in the mid-1990s, as a case study in the tension between a collective duty to remember and a political case for forgetting, using the conceptual frameworks associated with Avishai Margalit and David Rieff. Do not treat the TRC as a generic transitional-justice mechanism; examine it specifically as a mnemonic institution that had to allocate visibility and silence—deciding which victim testimonies entered the official archive, which perpetrator disclosures were traded for amnesty, and whether the state should preserve every account or allow certain memories to recede so that a shared civic life could be reconstructed.

Structure your analysis as follows.

First, identify the relevant players—victims seeking acknowledgment, perpetrators seeking legal closure, the post-apartheid state, and future citizens who did not experience the violence directly—and map them onto Margalit’s idea that remembering wrongs can function as a form of moral solidarity with victims, and onto Rieff’s argument that institutionalized remembrance risks locking societies into perpetual victimhood and obstructing reconciliation.

Second, examine three specific institutional choices: (i) the public victim-hearing sessions versus the closed amnesty-committee proceedings; (ii) the decision to produce a comprehensive written report rather than erect a singular national monument; and (iii) the exchange of full disclosure for immunity from prosecution. For each, say whether it embodies the duty to remember or the case for forgetting, how Margalit’s framework evaluates it, and how Rieff’s framework assesses its risks—particularly what might be called mnemonic overload or the freezing of political identity around past injury.

Third, locate the points where the theory is silent or strained. For example, Margalit’s notion of shared memory may presuppose a moral community that apartheid deliberately destroyed; Rieff’s defense of strategic forgetting may apply differently to state-sanctioned violence than to private grief. Does the TRC demonstrate that remembering and forgetting are not binary opposites but structurally coupled—each requiring the other to function institutionally? Or does it reveal an asymmetry, for instance that the duty to remember attaches primarily to victims’ voices while the license to forget attaches primarily to perpetrators’ guilt?
