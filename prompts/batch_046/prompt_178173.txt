In a seminar on international law someone dismissed the International Criminal Court as conceptually self-defeating by invoking Pascal's line, "Justice without force is powerless; force without justice is tyrannical." His point was not merely that the ICC is weak in practice—everyone knows it lacks its own police—but that a tribunal with no independent coercive apparatus instantiates "justice without force" in a strong sense, and is therefore necessarily impotent rather than just inefficient; he added that giving it an enforcement arm would immediately turn it tyrannical, so the institution is caught in a conceptual bind. I think he is running together several different claims and I want to disentangle them. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that slogan could be expressing—distinguishing, for example, an empirical prediction about compliance rates, a definitional claim about what counts as justice, a normative condition on legitimate authority, and a modal claim about whether justice can exist unaccompanied—indicating for each whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, false, or ambiguous in a way that hides the real work? Then say which of those readings actually support his specific conclusion that an unenforced court is conceptually incoherent or not worth establishing, and which ones he probably meant but do not actually entail it. Please present the readings as a menu rather than selecting one, and note where Pascal's own concerns in the Pensées about custom, force, and equity might align with or diverge from the modern institutional application he was making.
