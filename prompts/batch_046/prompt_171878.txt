I've been writing about science and ideas for years, and lately I've been stuck on this metaphysical take I heard at a conference bar — probably half-baked, but I can't shake it. The position is that there just isn't a special composition question at all. Instead of asking 'when do parts make a whole?', we should think in terms of a continuous 'causal insulation gradient': an object exists to exactly the degree there internal dynamics are shielded from outside interference, full stop. A chair scores high, a random pile of leaves scores near zero, but there's no hidden binary switch. The whole debate, they said, only looks hard because we're still smuggling in Aristotelian ideas about substantial forms without admitting it.

I'm trying to figure out if this is actually defensible or if I'm missing the obvious killer objection. So if you take this gradient view seriously — not universalism, not nihilism, but this specific continuous-scale version — what counts against it? I don't mean the cheap 'so does the Eiffel Tower and my shoe make an object?' line; I mean the real defeaters. Which formal results in mereology, if any, are genuinely fatal versus just awkward? What empirical work on part-whole relations in biology or physics does it risk contradicting? And since the view borrows from ideas about persistence and causal integration, which results in those other areas does it stand or fall with?

I'd also like to know how a committed defender would respond. Which objections would they concede are damaging, and which would they dismiss as category errors? Rank the defeaters for me — fatal, serious-but-manageable, and merely embarassing — and tell me whether this position is actually well-insulated or just hasn't been stress-tested properly yet.
