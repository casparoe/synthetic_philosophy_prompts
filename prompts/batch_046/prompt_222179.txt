I am applying for a three-month independent study grant at the intersection of philosophy of history and decision theory, and I need a polished proposal for a 5,000-word paper that can actually be completed in that window. The half-formed idea is this: forecasting imperial decline through the “outside view”—grouping Rome, Qing, and Habsburg collapses into reference classes defined by structural conditions like fiscal-military overextension—claims to be agnostic about the mental lives of historical actors. But I suspect this agnosticism is unstable. The precise question I want funded is whether selecting and applying a reference class for imperial fall necessarily commits the forecaster to a tacit theory of other minds—specifically, to attributing shared patterns of collective miscalculation, institutional overconfidence, or strategic blindness—without which the class would not be projectable. In other words, is the outside view genuinely purified of mental-state attribution, or does it merely relocate those attributions from explicit narrative into implicit functional-role descriptions? Existing forecasting literature treats reference-class construction as an empirical clustering problem, while philosophers of history treat it as a problem of explanatory adequacy; neither asks whether the coherence of a base-rate inference about empires requires treating them as entities with beliefs, intentions, or rational failures. I propose to answer this not by gathering new archival data, but by conceptually reconstructing three standard comparative taxonomies—overextension, succession crisis, and peripheral revolt—to expose the minimal ontology each requires. For each taxonomy, I will ask whether its conditions can be satisfied by purely structural descriptions, or whether they necessarily include analogues of mental states that make the correlation intelligible. A successful deliverable would be a sharp distinction between “structurally pure” and “functionally mentalized” reference classes, with operational criteria that a forecasting practitioner could use to audit her own commitments. The project fails if the distinction collapses—if every structural description of an empire can be translated without loss into a mental-state description, or vice versa—because then the debate between outside-view forecasters and inside-view narrative historians is not methodological but merely terminological. Given the time constraint, I will not attempt a general theory of collective intentionality or statistical validation of the taxonomies; I will limit the analysis to conceptual reconstruction of three classes and one fully worked forecasting example. Write the proposal stating the question precisely, explaining why current positions leave it open, laying out the step-by-step method, specifying what counts as success and failure, and giving an honest assessment of scope and limitations.
