In my school's ethics club, a senior defended the idea of "earn to give" by saying that anyone who can secure a high-paying finance job and donate half the income is doing more good than most nonprofit workers. When I pushed back, he just repeated the phrase as if that settled everything. I want to understand exactly what commitment that slogan carries. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses "earn to give" might stand for—separating, for instance, a comparative-impact claim about career choice, a deontological claim about moral obligation regardless of personal fit, a claim about institutional legitimacy (that high-earning industries are acceptable funding sources for charity), and a claim about measurability bias (that donated dollars have more reliably quantifiable outcomes than direct labor)? For each reading, say whether it is a near-trivial truism, a substantive but defensible ethical position, or a claim that rests on contested empirical premises about charitable effectiveness and labor markets. Please present these as a menu rather than selecting one, and indicate which readings would actually support using the phrase to defend a finance career over direct work. Also, please distinguish what is broadly settled about career impact and charitable cost-effectiveness from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers and development economists who study effective altruism, and do not present any one contested interpretation as the consensus view.
