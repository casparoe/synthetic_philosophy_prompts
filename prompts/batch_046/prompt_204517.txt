I am preparing a graduate seminar on artificial agency and have encountered a conceptual difficulty I would like to resolve before finalizing the syllabus. In discussions of low-impact AI, one common proposal holds that an agent can avoid undesirable side effects by measuring the divergence between the actual world-state it produces and a counterfactual baseline in which the agent never acted, then constraining its behavior so that this divergence remains below a specified threshold. I have been treating this framework as essentially metaphysical—concerned with which causal pathways are actualized rather than with normative evaluation—because comparing two world-trajectories seems purely descriptive. Yet a colleague has pressed me on whether selecting the state-space representation, the counterfactual baseline, and the divergence metric does not already presuppose a complete ordering of what is worth preserving. This raises the question: is there a coherent formulation of an impact measure that remains genuinely value-neutral, relying only on structural properties of causal histories, or does every such measure necessarily embed a full specification of value? I would welcome a careful philosophical analysis of where, exactly, the normative commitments enter—whether in the choice of dimensions, in the baseline selection, or elsewhere—and whether the aspiration to a value-free low-impact criterion rests on a false separation between descriptive causation and axiological judgment. Please respond in American English.
