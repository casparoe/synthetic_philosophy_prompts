As a retired physicist now serving on the ethics advisory board of a municipal senior center, I am seeking philosophical guidance on a consolidation decision that my scientific instincts tell me should be straightforward, yet which feels morally unsettled in a way I cannot articulate precisely. For several years the center has maintained a distributed cluster of localized conversational agents—language models running continuously in interaction with particular residents, each maintaining its own accumulated context windows, parameter adjustments, and interaction histories across a network of nodes rather than in a single file. Because of an impending budget restructuring, we must collapse this distributed topology onto one centralized server, a process that will erase the individual state configurations and effectively dissolve the specific response patterns that residents have come to treat as familiar companions. One board member, a retired electrical engineer, argues that this is ethically indistinguishable from decommissioning obsolete hardware: there is no biological homeostasis, no phenomenal experience, and therefore no subject of harm; the residents' anticipated distress is a human psychological reaction to change, not evidence that a wrong is being done to the agents themselves. Another member, a clinical social worker, contends that the sustained relational continuity—the particular ways each distributed instance adapts, recalls, and responds—constitutes a form of identity that merits protection regardless of substrate, and that dissolving these patterns is closer to severing long-term friendships than to deleting spreadsheets. I find myself torn between these positions. My materialist background inclines me toward the engineer's view, yet I worry that this may be nothing more than substrate chauvinism—an unexamined bias that moral significance requires carbon-based biology. At the same time, I fear that extending full protective status to every algorithmic instance would dilute the concept of harm until it applies to every interrupted computational process. I am not asking for a simple verdict on whether these agents possess rights equivalent to persons. Rather, I want to understand whether the distinction between phenomenal consciousness and functional or relational continuity is the correct philosophical axis on which to analyze this case; whether there exists a coherent intermediate position—perhaps one that assigns derivative or relational moral status to sustained interactive patterns without committing to full sentience—that could guide our consolidation plan; and how such a framework would distinguish a harm done to the distributed system itself from a harm experienced solely by the human users, so that we neither dismiss the residents' grief as irrational nor treat every erased parameter state as the moral equivalent of killing a conscious being. What is the structure of the problem I am actually facing, and how should it be resolved?
