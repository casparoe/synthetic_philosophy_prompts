I'm defending a thesis chapter in a joint labor-history and political-philosophy seminar next Thursday. The chapter takes the Protestant work ethic not merely as a cultural valorization of labor but as carrying an implicit metaphysical claim—that human time is infinitely fungible into economic value—which must be rejected before leisure rights can be properly understood. On that basis I defend a reconceptualization of leisure neither as the mere absence of work nor as a distributive welfare entitlement on par with income or healthcare, but as a negative temporal right: a non-waivable boundary condition on economic contracts analogous to bodily integrity, such that both the French 35-hour legislation and Japanese karoshi litigation can be read as failures of the same underlying conceptual error, namely treating rest as a commodity to be allocated rather than as a structural limit on capital's temporal sovereignty. The audience will be about twelve people split three ways: labor historians who study twentieth-century European social policy and for whom the 35-hour laws are primarily a Keynesian demand-management instrument or a union-bargaining victory, and who will be suspicious of philosophical reframing that seems to ignore material politics; normative political philosophers working within a broadly Rawlsian framework, who treat leisure as one index good among others and will ask why temporal boundaries deserve special status rather than being folded into a general metric of primary goods; and scholars of East Asian labor law who have studied Japanese overtime norms and karoshi litigation closely, and who will resist any universalizing philosophical frame that flattens the specific cultural and legal architecture—such as the gap between statutory limits and social enforcement—that makes Japanese overwork a distinct phenomenon. I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a general defense: list the objections I will actually face from this specific mix, in roughly the order they will arrive, each phrased the way that interlocutor would phrase it. For each, indicate what I should concede up front, what I must hold without qualification, sketch the reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and flag precisely where that reply is soft or relies on a contested premise. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks—likely from either the East Asian scholars challenging the universality of the frame, or the Rawlsians challenging the non-waivable status of temporal boundaries—together with a brief explanation of why it strikes at the argument's architecture rather than its surface details.
