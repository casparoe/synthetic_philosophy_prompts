I teach an upper-level philosophy of language seminar with about ten students, for one 75-minute session. Design a classroom game that makes participants feel why two-dimensional semantics needs two dimensions — specifically, how the same sentence can come out a priori without being necessary, or necessary without being a priori. Invent a miniature artificial language with two predicates, 'vrek' and 'plorn', whose primary intensions (how speakers identify instances in their actual environment) and secondary intensions (what they rigidly designate across possible worlds) are deliberately split by the rules of the game. Divide students into roles — 'Evaluators' who apply the predicates to cards representing different possible worlds, and 'Observers' who track whether each decision was made on epistemic or metaphysical grounds — with a coordination mechanism that rewards agreement only when both dimensions align, which they rarely do. Provide rules, materials (cards, boards, or tokens), a timeline for the session, and a debrief that maps moments of confusion onto the formal distinction (primary intension, secondary intension, and centred-world evaluation). Be frank about which feature — for instance, the formal indexing of contexts to worlds — the physical setup cannot reproduce and you must supply verbally. Also say what will predictably go wrong on the first run and how to recover. There should be no introductory lecture; the divergence must emerge from the rules and incentives themselves. Lead with the bottom line — the essential design and the insight participants should come away feeling — in the first sentence, then give the reasoning and practical details with no preamble and no restating of the question.
