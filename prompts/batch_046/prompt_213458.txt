As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I have observed a tension in my industry's institutional norms that seems to depend on unstated assumptions about where thinking actually happens. Technology firms routinely conduct blameless postmortems after system failures: the investigation explicitly forbids attributing an outage to any individual engineer, instead describing the error as emerging from interactions among the engineer, automated testing pipelines, deployment scripts, monitoring dashboards, and documentation repositories. At the same time, hiring practices, code-ownership policies, and architecture reviews treat understanding as strictly internal to the individual—engineers are evaluated on their ability to reason about algorithms without relying on external documentation, and accountability for a module's design is assigned to a single person rather than distributed across the tools that produced it.

What implicit theory of cognitive agency or mental location does this pairing of practices presuppose? Reconstruct the view that renders both norms coherent, distinguish what the practices strictly require from what they merely accommodate, and assess whether they are genuinely consistent or whether they quietly import contradictory commitments about whether reasoning can occur partly outside an individual brain. Finally, describe what would have to change in engineering management—hiring criteria, accountability structures, or the design of development environments—if that implicit view were replaced by a rigorously internalist theory in which only skull-bound neural processes count as cognition and no external artifact can genuinely participate in understanding.
