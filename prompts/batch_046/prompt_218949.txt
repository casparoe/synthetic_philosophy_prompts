I have been trying to formalise Glaucon's challenge from Republic Book II using Bernard Williams's internal-reasons thesis, and I have constructed an argument that seems to prove morality is rational only to the extent that one expects to be observed. The steps are these:

(1) The ring of Gyges eliminates every external consequence of injustice: there can be no legal punishment, social disgrace, or reputational damage, because the bearer is both invisible and undetectable.

(2) Once all external sanctions are removed, the only considerations that could weigh against injustice are internal—damage to one's character, loss of self-respect, or disruption of one's psychological equilibrium.

(3) But an internal consideration functions as a genuine reason for action only if it connects to something the agent already cares about; if it does not, it is merely an abstract duty with no motivational grip (this is the heart of Williams's claim that reasons must be internal).

(4) Glaucon's unjust man, by hypothesis, does not care about justice; it plays no part in his subjective motivational set.

(5) Therefore, for the ring-bearer who is unjust by nature, there is genuinely no reason—neither external nor internal—to refrain from injustice.

(6) Now apply the same structure to ordinary moral life: in any given situation, the rational force of a moral consideration depends on whether it connects to the agent's actual concerns.

(7) But in ordinary life, people's concerns are partly constituted by social practices: we care about fairness partly because we inhabit societies in which fairness is recognised, reinforced, and mirrored back to us.

(8) Consequently, the internal value of justice for an ordinary person is itself sustained by a background of social transparency; remove that transparency completely, as the ring does, and the internal value dissolves rather than persisting independently.

(9) It follows that moral reasons are always conditional on social visibility. Where visibility is absent, moral reasons vanish—not only for the naturally unjust man, but for anyone whose moral concerns depend on social constitution.

(10) This proves too much: it implies that anonymous voting, secret charitable giving, and action in a crowd all occur in a space where moral reasons are weakened or nullified, reducing morality to a calibrated response to the probability of being observed.

If the argument fails, I want to know which step carries the fault. I am inclined to defend (1) and (5)—the ring really does strip away external sanctions, and for someone who genuinely lacks justice in his motivational set, internal reasons are indeed absent—but I cannot accept (10). Which premise or inference should I reject, and what am I committed to if I do?
