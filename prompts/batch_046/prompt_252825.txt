I am outlining an essay on evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics. The central claim is that the debunking challenge applies with different force to “thin” moral concepts (such as wrongness or harm) and “thick” ones (such as cruelty or betrayal): because thick concepts already combine descriptive and evaluative content, evolutionary explanations of our grasp of them might track genuine social-coordination needs rather than merely distorting independent moral facts, which could leave a limited moral realism intact for thick judgments. I ask whether this asymmetry genuinely protects thick concepts, or whether the debunker can reply that evolutionary pressure explains only the descriptive component, leaving the evaluative residue equally undermined. I also worry I may be conflating conceptual analysis with evolutionary psychology in treating the descriptive/evaluative split as clean. Could you comment on the coherence of the argument, point out mischaracterizations of standard debunking strategies (e.g., whether they already accommodate thick concepts), and suggest where the reasoning needs tightening? I am not looking for citations or reading lists—only an assessment of whether the distinction does the philosophical work assigned to it.
