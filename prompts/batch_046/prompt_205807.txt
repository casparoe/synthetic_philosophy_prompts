I am designing a 50-minute seminar for second-year philosophy undergraduates who have encountered utilitarianism but have not read G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica. I want to teach Moore's specific thesis that both beauty and friendship are intrinsic goods displaying organic unity, but that they differ structurally: in aesthetic appreciation the value is qualitatively inseparable yet ontologically independent of the object's awareness, whereas in friendship the value is partly constituted by reciprocal consciousness—each party's awareness of the other as aware—so that the friends are ontologically interdependent in a way that the parts of a beautiful object are not. To carry this, I intend to use a single comparative case study: a listener's engagement with a live performance of a string quartet versus a sustained conversation between two long-time friends, calibrated so that removing one musician damages the whole differently from removing one friend damaging the relationship. Please lay out the teaching plan: (1) the exact sequence in which the conceptual components—intrinsic value, organic unity, ontological interdependence versus qualitative inseparability, and reciprocal consciousness—must be introduced, with the dependency relations that dictate the order; (2) the precise properties this carrying example must possess and must lack, especially to prevent collapse into hedonism, subjective preference, or instrumental valuation; (3) the three misconceptions students are most likely to form or import (for example, that Moore is a hedonist about friendship, that organic unity means merely that the sum exceeds its parts, or that reciprocal awareness makes friendship instrumentally valuable), together with the specific point in the sequence where each should be defused; (4) a concise check-for-understanding that would reveal whether a student has genuinely grasped the distinction between ontological interdependence and qualitative inseparability rather than memorizing it; and (5) which elements of Moore's broader metaethics—such as the open-question argument, the natural/non-natural distinction, or his critique of evolutionary ethics—should be explicitly set aside, with a brief justification of why introducing them here would derail the specific comparison rather than illuminate it. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list.
