Draft the keynote address for the closing session of the symposium Formal Systems and First-Person Perspective, held in honor of the retirement of Dr. Elena Vasquez, a logician whose career moved from technical work on arithmetization and modal extensions of Gödel numbering to philosophical writing on self-reference in cognition. The audience is deliberately mixed: specialists in logic and philosophy of mind, graduate students from unrelated disciplines, university administrators, and family members. The speaker is Vasquez’s former doctoral student, now a philosopher of science.

The address must use the Gödelian analogy as developed in Douglas Hofstadter’s Gödel, Escher, Bach—specifically the move from self-referential formal sentences to strange loops in self-aware minds—but it must do real structural work rather than serve as ornament. The central constraint is this: the speaker wishes to celebrate Vasquez’s intellectual trajectory without committing either of the errors she herself has criticized. First, the speech must not claim that Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem demonstrates that human minds transcend all algorithmic description. Second, it must not reduce her philosophical contribution to vague metaphor.

Instead, build the address around a precise, somewhat niche feature of Gödel’s original construction: that the self-referential sentence refers to itself not through any direct reflexive mechanism internal to the formal language, but through the encoding of syntactic properties into arithmetic via Gödel numbering, combined with an interpretive assignment of semantic content that operates from outside the system. Label this the interpretational asymmetry—the sentence is an internal syntactic object, yet it is about itself only for an interpreter who maps numbers back onto formulas. Use this asymmetry to structure the narrative of Vasquez’s career: her early technical papers refined the encoding mechanisms; her later philosophical essays asked whether consciousness involves an analogous split between an internal syntactic or neural representation and an external interpretive frame that completes the loop, or whether in minds the distinction between object and interpreter collapses.

The philosophical framing should therefore determine exactly what the speaker can and cannot say. The speaker may praise Vasquez for showing that self-reference is always mediated by representation and interpretation, but may not assert that she solved the hard problem of consciousness or that formal logic proves mental exceptionalism. The speech should be accessible to non-specialists—briefly explaining Gödel numbering without condescension—yet precise enough that logicians in the room recognize the technical point. It should be celebratory and appropriate for a retirement occasion, not a lecture, but the philosophical idea should shape the emotional arc: the recognition that understanding another mind, like interpreting a Gödel sentence, requires both rigorous internal analysis and the humility of an outside perspective. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or references to sources that require verification. Write in a polished, formal register, approximately 600 to 800 words.
