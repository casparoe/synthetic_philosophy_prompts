I am giving the opening remarks at the dedication of our county’s new community archive next month, and I need the address to be more than ceremonial. I have spent three years volunteering to organize it with no formal training in history or library science, and the audience will be split: half are archivists and local historians who understand provenance and cataloguing, and half are residents and family members whose ancestors may or may not appear in the boxes. I want the speech to honor the work of preservation without pretending the archive is neutral, and to welcome the community without suggesting that opening the doors fixes everything.

Through the work I have come to think that archives do not merely have “gaps” or “silences”; they have what I call directional deafness. The institution is structured to record certain kinds of events—property transactions, court proceedings, official correspondence—and is structurally deaf to others: informal care networks, disputes resolved without authorities, the labor of people who never held title, and the solidarity that kept families housed when no official body was watching. The absence is not random; it clusters in predictable patterns that mirror power. Yet the archive looks complete because every shelf is full. In our own collection, every land sale is documented with names and dates, but the mutual-aid societies that sustained families through hard years appear only as occasional names in church donation logs—the archive hears property clearly, and hears solidarity only as faint echo.

I want the philosophical idea to do real structural work in the speech: it should determine what I can sincerely say and what I must avoid. Because of this framing, I should not claim that the archive “tells the whole story,” nor should I dismiss it as useless propaganda. I should be able to express genuine gratitude to the donors and volunteers, warn that completeness is not the same as justice, and invite residents to treat the archive as a starting point rather than a final authority—without losing either half of the room. Please write a ten-minute address that moves between experts and non-experts, that uses directional deafness to shape both the gratitude and the warning, and that lands as an honest invitation rather than a lecture. Do not include any bibliography, citation, or reading list; the argument must stand entirely on its own.
