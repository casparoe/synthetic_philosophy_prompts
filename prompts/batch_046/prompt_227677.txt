I am a retired biophysicist who, since leaving the laboratory, have been working through elementary moral philosophy with a small discussion circle of other retired scientists. At our most recent meeting, a colleague summarized an account she had heard in a public lecture on what the speaker called the paradox of deontology, and her rendition struck me as both philosophically sharp and suspiciously tidy. She presented it as follows: deontological ethics posits an absolute, agent-centered constraint against actively harming an innocent person, no matter the consequential calculus, which is why pushing one bystander onto railway tracks to halt a train that would otherwise kill five is strictly forbidden, whereas merely allowing the train to proceed and kill the five is permitted. Because the theory thereby assigns greater moral weight to the causal purity of the agent’s own hands than to the actual number of lives preserved, she said, it collapses into a kind of moral narcissism. She added that defenders typically invoke the doctrine of double effect to escape this result, but since in the trolley case the harm to the single victim is intended precisely as the means of stopping the train—rather than being a merely foreseen side effect—that defense fails, leaving the paradox intact. More recently, she noted, some philosophers have adopted threshold deontology, which preserves the constraint under ordinary conditions but permits rights-violations once the stakes cross a catastrophic boundary; yet she concluded that this merely smuggles consequentialist reasoning in through a side door, since the threshold itself is fixed entirely by the magnitude of good outcomes that might be achieved. Therefore, she argued, the paradox forces deontology to choose between accepting morally horrific outcomes and surrendering its theoretical identity.

I have the distinct impression that her summary is largely accurate but contains at least one or two subtle distortions—perhaps a conflation between distinct deontological responses, or an overstatement of what threshold deontology actually concedes to consequentialist logic. Could you audit this characterization with some care? Specifically, please distinguish what a careful defender of agent-centered restrictions would accept, what is a common popularization that oversimplifies the position, and what is genuinely misrepresented—particularly regarding the role of double effect in this particular case and the charge that threshold deontology is covert consequentialism. Finally, I would ask whether her conclusion—that the paradox leaves deontology with an unavoidable dilemma between moral horror and internal incoherence—actually follows from the premises once they are corrected, or whether it depends on the very distortions you identify. I am not seeking bibliographic guidance or a reading list; I only wish to return to my group with a precise sense of which parts of her account can be trusted and which should be treated with skepticism.
