In my reading group on Holocaust perpetrator studies we've been stuck for months on the Browning vs Goldhagen question—whether Reserve Police Battalion 101 killed mostly because of situational pressure and peer conformity (Browning's ordinary men) or because of a deep, eliminationist antisemitism that made them willing executioners (Goldhagen). Everyone keeps recycling the same testimony about peer pressure versus the same propaganda leaflets, and nobody has moved an inch in like a year. But lately I started wondering if the stall isn't really about the historical facts at all, but about a hidden conceptual mess in what we mean by 'character' when we explain perpetrators. Browning treats character as a kind of dispostion that should survive situational pressure, and since behavior changed he concludes character wasn't the driver. Goldhagen, meanwhile, treats cultural beliefs as character-forming, but he never really distinguishes between holding an antisemitic belief and having it be action-guiding in a specific moment—he just assumes culture writes the script and the script runs automatically.

So is the disagreement actually about Battalion 101, or is it a deeper dispute about whether moral character is a stable trait across contexts versus a culturally installed script that activates without reflection? And if it's the second, then what would even count as progress—more granular archival work on individual policemen's pre-war lives, some formal model from social psychology about belief-to-action pathways, or should we just admit that Browning is asking about causal mechanisms in one unit while Goldhagen is asking about the moral culture of Germany as a whole, and they're not actually answering the same question? I'm asking because I want to know whether there's any move that could unstick us, or if we're just talking past each other under the same label 'explanation of perpetrators.'
