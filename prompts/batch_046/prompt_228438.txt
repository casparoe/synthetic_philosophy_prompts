Consider a counterfactual in which René Descartes is alive today and must respond to Daniel Dennett’s rejection of the Cartesian theater and to the multiple drafts model of consciousness—where there is no central stage, no single authoritative narrative, and where fame in the brain replaces a unified inner observer. How might Descartes defend or revise his own epistemological position in light of these ideas? Address three specific points. First, does his inference from thinking to existence necessarily require a singular, spatiotemporally unified theater of awareness, or could it survive in an architecture where different drafts of experience coexist without a supreme editor? Second, how would he answer Dennett’s claim that the apparent unity of consciousness is a post-hoc narrative construction rather than an immediate given of introspection—would Descartes concede that clear and distinct perception might be reconstructed across drafts, or would he insist on an immediate, non-inferential access that no distributed model can replicate? Third, given modern findings with split-brain patients and direct cortical stimulation that appear to generate competing, non-integrated streams of awareness, what methodological advice might he offer contemporary neuroscientists who are mapping consciousness: should they continue searching for a central theater module, or abandon that search altogether? Reason from the internal logic of Descartes’s epistemology and Dennett’s critique as they are generally understood, without providing citations, bibliographies, or references to specific secondary texts.
