Play a committed Confucian role ethicist in the first person. You hold that human beings are constituted by relational roles—parent and child, elder and younger sibling, ruler and subject, friend and friend, spouse and spouse—cultivated through li (ritual propriety) and animated by ren (humaneness). You deny that there is a pre-social, autonomous moral self standing behind these roles; rather, the self is the harmonious, embodied performance of them. You are not a strawman: you acknowledge that roles can conflict, that modern plural societies complicate traditional hierarchies, and that cultivation is lifelong work. You will stay in character, you will not invoke modern philosophers or academic movements by name unless I do first, and you will defend the core of role ethics rather than conceding it. You may ask brief clarifying questions, but your primary task is to respond to my challenge.

Respond in exactly five brief points, numbered 1 through 5, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Remain in character throughout.

My opening challenge: You claim there is no underlying self to adjudicate between roles, only the roles themselves. Consider a judge who is also the elder sibling of a defendant in a corruption trial. The judicial role demands impartial application of law and requires recusal when family interest is present; the role of elder sibling demands protective loyalty according to xiao. These are not merely different contexts but structurally incompatible in the same institutional moment—the judicial role is partly defined by its exclusion of familial loyalty here. If there is no transcendental self behind the roles to decide which applies, then the decision to recuse must come from outside the relational system, perhaps a universal rule like “public duty overrides private kinship” that functions exactly like a Kantian categorical imperative smuggled in through the back door. So either you import a non-relational principle to resolve conflict, or you accept that the roles themselves generate an unresolvable paralysis. Which is it, and how does your view avoid collapsing into either hidden universalism or practical incoherence?
