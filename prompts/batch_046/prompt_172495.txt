Two members of a mechanism-design reading group, discussing a proposed voting rule for selecting among five public projects, got stuck in a four-round exchange about whether the rule escapes the Gibbard-Satterthwaite impossibility result.

(1) The first argued that the rule is genuinely strategy-proof because it restricts admissible ballots to linear extensions of a fixed, acyclic precedence graph with a unique maximal element. Within each chain of that graph, preferences are single-peaked, and the aggregation applies a median-voter mechanism along each chain. Because the universal-domain condition required by Gibbard-Satterthwaite is violated, the impossibility result does not apply.

(2) The second replied that restricting ballots to linear extensions of a sparse graph does not automatically guarantee strategy-proofness unless the full domain satisfies global value-restriction or single-peakedness conditions—not merely pairwise conditions along edges. With five alternatives arranged in a sparse partial order that leaves some branches unordered, the set of admissible linear extensions remains rich enough that a voter can profitably reorder middle-ranked alternatives across branches.

(3) The first countered that the rule is not a standard median mechanism over the full alternative set, but a path-selection rule: it computes pairwise majority comparisons only along edges present in the graph, then selects the alternative reachable by the longest directed path in the transitive closure. Because the graph is constitutionally fixed and acyclic, the rule is monotonic—improving an alternative’s support can never cause it to lose—and monotonicity combined with the fixed edge set eliminates profitable misrepresentation.

(4) The second responded that monotonicity is necessary but insufficient for strategy-proofness. A voter can manipulate the transitive-closure computation by misranking an intermediate alternative that is not adjacent to her true top choice: suppressing a strong competitor requires only elevating a weak intermediary, which changes which edges are activated in the closure without altering her reported top rank. Thus strategic behavior is relocated to the intermediate-comparison layer rather than eliminated.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the four moves, rate it on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and provide a brief justification. Then identify what, if anything, both parties are missing—specifically, whether the path-selection rule actually resolves the manipulation problem raised in move (4), and whether the appeal to monotonicity in move (3) addresses the correct form of strategic behavior. Do not provide bibliographies or citation lists; evaluate the arguments solely on their internal logic.
