I've been thinking about those actions people call "going above and beyond"—like working unpaid overtime, caring for a dying relative with no support, or donating a kidney to a stranger. Usually I understand supererogation to mean acts that are genuinely good yet not strictly required. But in real conversations I've noticed a specific pattern that doesn't sit right with that framework. People praise someone for enduring a hardship that shouldn't have existed in the first place, and then treat that praise as if it settles the moral account. During the pandemic, for instance, essential workers were widely called "heroes" for laboring in dangerous conditions, yet that admiration seemed to substitute for any serious demand to fix the conditions—almost as if labeling their endurance heroic made it acceptable that the risk was unavoidable. The workers received praise appropriate to a free gift, but had they refused and demanded safety, they likely would have been blamed for failing a basic duty. So the same act is sorted into the "gift" box when we want to praise it and into the "obligation" box when we imagine someone withholding it.

I'm wondering whether philosophers have a specific name for this configuration, where an act gets classified as supererogatory not because it exceeds a clear and legitimate baseline of duty, but because the baseline itself has been quietly lowered, corrupted, or ignored, and the praise for "going beyond" functions as a moral substitute for repairing that baseline. It's not quite hypocrisy, since the admirers may be sincere, and it's not exactly exploitation in the economic sense, because the harm is partly done through categorization—putting something in the realm of voluntary virtue when it actually belongs in the realm of owed justice. It also feels different from the general worry that praising supererogation can be oppressive, because here the problem isn't that people are being pressured to be saints; it's that a failure of justice is being recast as an opportunity for heroism. If there is an established concept for this, I'd like to know how it differs from plain moral luck or from cases where someone genuinely does something extra within a well-ordered system. If there isn't, could it be named and defined with precise boundary conditions—so that one could look at a case and determine whether we're seeing authentic supererogation or this other phenomenon, where praise is being used to cover a gap in what should have been required all along? I don't need references; I want to understand the carving itself—what it includes, what it excludes, and where its edges blur into nearby ideas.
