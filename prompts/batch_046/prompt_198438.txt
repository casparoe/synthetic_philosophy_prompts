Contemporary AI governance frameworks for high-risk language models often impose an interpretability audit: regulators require not only predictive accuracy but that the model produce coherent, step-by-step verbal justifications for its outputs, treating this justificatory capacity as a marker of genuine understanding rather than mere statistical correlation. These same frameworks typically deny that understanding requires consciousness or biological substrate, yet they treat audit failure as a regulatory defect even when accuracy is perfect. What philosophy of mind and meaning does this practice presuppose? Does it implicitly accept a functionalist account of understanding that Searle's Chinese Room argument was designed to undermine, or can it be reconstructed as locating semantics in the human-auditor interaction rather than in the model? Reconstruct the implicit theory, ask whether the practice is internally consistent—given that the audit itself operates through syntactic symbol-manipulation that the framework deems insufficient for understanding—and say what the framework would have to abandon or add if it took the Chinese Room conclusion seriously rather than treating it as a problem for the model alone.
