I'm designing a session for an upper-level moral philosophy seminar — about 14 advanced students, 80 minutes, in a room with movable chairs — and I want them to feel why particularism is not just "context matters" but involves a structural claim about practical reasoning: that moral reasons are holistically configured, so a feature that functions as a decisive reason in one case can reverse its valence entirely in another, and any attempt to rescue generalism with ceteris paribus clauses collapses into an unlearnable baroque taxonomy. We've been working through standard generalist frameworks, and I need a structured deliberation that makes participants build rules, watch them fracture under feature interaction, and then experience the epistemic instability of reasoning without them — all before anyone names the view.

Design a classroom activity with clear phases, roles, and a lightweight point scheme that needs no real money. Divide participants into groups of three or four, each acting as an advisory panel to a fictional institutional review board. Provide each group with a deck of eight brief case cards, each annotated with three to four color-coded feature tags — for instance, "deliberate deception," "vulnerability of the recipient," "prior explicit commitment," "autonomy of the affected party." In Phase One (15 minutes), groups must articulate one explicit moral principle that covers Cases 1–3 and predict the correct judgment for Case 4, writing it on a shared board; they earn consistency points for applying it without exception. In Phase Two (20 minutes), reveal Cases 4–6, which are constructed so that every decisive feature from Phase One reappears but in a configuration where its moral valence has inverted — deception combined with autonomy-protection becomes not merely excusable but required, while the same deception feature in isolation was previously condemned. Groups may amend their principle, but each amendment costs points and must be written as a formal exception clause. By Phase Three (15 minutes), present Cases 7–8 with dense feature interaction and impose the "particularist constraint": they must reach unanimous judgment without citing any general principle, justifying the decision solely by describing the specific gestalt of features in that exact scenario and how they weigh against each other here.

The debrief should map Phase One onto the generalist aspiration for invariant principles, Phase Two onto reasons holism and valence reversal, and Phase Three onto particularist practical reasoning. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong: groups will cling to baroque rulebooks rather than abandon them, one dominant student will likely seize the particularist role and suppress dissent, and the absence of real-world stakes means some participants will treat the scoring as a logic puzzle rather than a moral failure. Also be honest about the feature the activity cannot reproduce — it demonstrates the practical breakdown of articulation by finite reasoners but does not establish the metaphysical particularist claim that there literally are no moral principles in the world — and tell me exactly how I should supply that distinction in my closing remarks, including what language to use so I don't misrepresent the view as mere skepticism about rules.
