I am preparing a philosophy of mind seminar discussion on mental causation and want to read Michel Gondry’s film Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind (2004) through the lens of the causal exclusion argument. The plot is familiar: after a failed relationship, Joel and Clementine undergo a procedure to erase each other from memory; during Joel’s session, his subconscious appears to resist, hiding Clementine inside unrelated memories and eventually derailing the technical process. Read the film in terms of physical causal closure and exclusion: if every neural event in Joel’s brain during the erasure has a sufficient physical cause, then his mental states—his grief, his attachment, his desire to preserve Clementine—are either reducible to those brain states or causally inert.

Where does the film dramatize mental causation, in moments where Joel’s mental state seems to alter the physical trajectory of the deletion? Where does it instead suggest that what looks like mental resistance is just another physical mechanism—neural rewiring, procedural error, technician oversight, or the algorithmic mapping of associative networks? Give an honest critical reading: do not reduce the film to a textbook illustration of exclusion, and do not distort the argument (closure, the exclusion principle, the options of reduction or epiphenomenalism) to make the narrative fit. Does the film’s depiction of memory as spatial and navigable—Joel literally walking through his own recollections—implicitly commit it to a particular view about the causal structure of mental content? And what does the work add to the philosophical debate that formal argument alone does not capture—perhaps something about the phenomenology of trying to hold onto a mental state that one’s physical substrate is being forced to discard?

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
