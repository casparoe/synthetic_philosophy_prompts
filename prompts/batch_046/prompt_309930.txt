In philosophy of consciousness, the debate over whether perceptual experience is retrospectively revised—distinguished into Orwellian accounts that rewrite an earlier experience in memory and Stalinesque accounts that replace it with a fabricated narrative—has long been invoked to explain the color phi phenomenon, in which a dot that changes color and appears to move seems to undergo the color shift before reaching its second location. Proponents of revision argue that consciousness is a post-hoc construction and that color phi reveals the brain editing its own temporal history; critics reply that this confuses report mechanisms with phenomenology and that predictive processing can accommodate the illusion without any retrospective alteration of experience itself. For years the exchange has repeated the same structure: revisionists cite the temporal lag of neural processing, critics cite the subjective immediacy of the perceptual flow, and neither side moves. Is this stall a verbal disagreement about whether “experience” tracks phenomenal presence, cognitive accessibility, or reportability? Is there a hidden empirical dispute about whether early sensory activation is genuinely absent or merely inaccessible? Or are the sides answering different questions—one about information flow in the brain, one about the structure of phenomenology—under the single heading of “the timing of experience”? Diagnose the disagreement concretely, using what each side actually says about color phi and retrospective revision, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that dissolves the impasse, an experimental design that could adjudicate it, or an argument that the question is ill-posed and no resolution should be expected.
