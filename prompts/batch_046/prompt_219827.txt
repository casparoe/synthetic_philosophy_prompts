I'm facilitating a ninety-minute interdisciplinary seminar—about fourteen graduate students split between philosophy and computer science—on the ethics and mathematics of algorithmic fairness. Last week we walked through the standard proof that calibration, balance for positive and negative classes, and predictive parity cannot be jointly satisfied when base rates differ across groups, but the students treated it like a logic puzzle rather than a practical bind. I want the next session to make the impossibility feel visceral: participants should construct a scoring rule themselves, discover that every fix breaks something else, and have to decide which violation they will live with, using small but real stakes (tokens exchangeable for a shared snack budget at the end).

The specific tension I want embedded is not only the classic trio of calibration, equal false-positive and false-negative rates, and predictive parity, but also multicalibration across subgroups formed by intersecting protected attributes with unprotected features—so that satisfying calibration at every subgroup level exhausts the degrees of freedom and makes error-rate balance literally impossible to approximate. Design a runnable activity with rules, roles (committee members drafting scores, applicant representatives arguing for adjustments, auditors checking subgroup tables), materials (simplified data cards, token budgets), and a payoff scheme that doesn't use real money but makes trade-offs painful. The rules should be arranged so that participants first believe they have found a clever workaround—perhaps by conditioning on an additional feature—before the auditor reveals that this either violates multicalibration or shifts the base-rate problem into a hidden subgroup. Then provide a debrief that maps each moment of the game onto the formal constraints, predicts exactly what will go wrong the first time I run it (for instance, the philosophy students will try to reject the framing as "just math" while the CS students will overfit to the sample data), and be explicit about which feature of the impossibility—namely, the dependence on the underlying distribution of outcomes across intersecting subgroups—the activity can actually reproduce and which part I must supply in lecture afterwards. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the students will have no access to sources during the session, and I want the prompt to be entirely self-contained.
