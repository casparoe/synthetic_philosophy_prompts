I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of science working on mechanistic explanation across neuroscience and machine-learning interpretability, and I'm trying to determine what I actually think about when an anomaly should be attributed to the "same mechanism" rather than to a different mechanism or to an external interference, going by my gut reactions to concrete cases.

Here are my verdicts:

1. A biological pacemaker node in the heart is replaced by an artificial silicon implant that reproduces the identical electrical waveform. When arrhythmias later emerge, I judge that we are observing an anomaly in the same rhythm-generation mechanism, despite the complete substrate change.

2. A vision model misclassifies adversarial images because high-frequency noise corrupts early-layer edge detectors; the same model misclassifies out-of-distribution images because its object taxonomy simply lacks the relevant category. I feel strongly that these are anomalies of two different mechanisms—feature extraction versus categorical mapping—even though both yield the same surface failure, misclassification.

3. In a mechanical clock, the brass escapement gear is replaced by an identical titanium gear. When timekeeping drifts, I take this to be an anomaly within the same clock mechanism, specifically a parameter-range failure, not a change in mechanism identity.

4. A hippocampal neuron fires aberrantly because of a point mutation in its voltage-gated potassium channel that alters ion conductance. Another hippocampal neuron, rewired into the prefrontal cortex, receives aberrant synaptic inputs and also fires aberrantly. I think the first is an anomaly in the same neuronal firing mechanism; the second involves a different mechanism producing a similar output failure.

5. A steam engine misfires because the coal is wet (input contamination) versus because the piston ring is cracked (structural failure). Both disrupt the thermodynamic cycle, but I hesitate: one feels like external interference with an intact mechanism, the other like internal breakdown of the mechanism itself. I lean toward calling both anomalies in the same mechanism, yet I keep reversing myself.

6. In a large language model, a specific attention head is ablated, and the model initially compensates by redistributing computation across remaining heads, then eventually fails on long-context reasoning. I am genuinely uncertain whether this failure is an anomaly within the original attention mechanism or evidence that a different, distributed mechanism has replaced it.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving preservation of interaction topology, functional-role continuity, or substrate independence—that captures cases 1 through 4? And if so, what does it force us to say about the ambiguous cases 5 and 6? Must one of my verdicts be overturned for the principle to remain simple rather than ad hoc?

Please also apply the principle to a case I have not listed: a brain-computer interface gradually replaces chemical synaptic transmission with direct electrical stimulation in a cortical circuit, after which the circuit exhibits epileptiform activity. Does that count as an anomaly in the original neural mechanism, or as a failure of a new mechanism altogether?
