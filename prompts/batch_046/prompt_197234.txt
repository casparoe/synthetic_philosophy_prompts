I've mostly bought into the view that split-brain patients after commissurotomy literally contain two distinct conscious subjects, not just one mind with a broken internal chat line. Left hemisphere does its own thing, right hemisphere does its own thing, and neither has access to thier mental states in any robust sense. But I want an honest map of what actually counts against this. Skip the standard objection that this is just information access - I know that one. Instead tell me which specific empirical findings or conceptual moves would genuinely weaken the two-subjects claim. For example, if the anterior commissure or other subcortical pathways turn out to integrate emotional and perceptual content in a way that preserves a single ownership structure across both sides, is that fatal? What about split-brain patients who consistently report unified agency or a singular I despite the disconnection? Rank the defeaters for me: which ones would force a committed defender to concede the view is wrong, which are merely awkward adjustments, and which are ones defenders already dismiss? Also, has this position been qualified into unfalsifiability - two subjects except when emotional integration happens, except when behavior looks unified, and so on - and if so, is that a problem for a claim about consciousness specifically? I want the pressure points, not a reading list.
