I'm currently in a philosophy of science seminar focused on scientific realism and the limits of empirical confirmation, and a fellow student keeps repeating the maxim that "you can always save a failing theory by tweaking the auxiliary assumptions around it." At first he used it to deflect a specific objection to a climate-model projection—suggesting that critics were merely refusing to adjust background parameters—then escalated it to a general claim that no scientific theory is ever genuinely falsifiable, and finally to something close to global skepticism: since every dataset is compatible with infinitely many explanations, we can never be rationally justified in preferring one theory over another. I can sense these are not the same argument, but I'm struggling to map out exactly which precise philosophical theses the slogan is sliding between, and whether any of them actually license all three moves.

Could you separate the distinct readings this one-liner might carry—distinguishing, for instance, logical underdetermination of theory by evidence from the holistic thesis that predictions always depend on auxiliary hypotheses, and both of those from the practical claim that scientists can always invent ad hoc saves? For each reading, indicate whether it amounts to a trivial observation that working scientists routinely accept, a substantive but defensible claim about scientific methodology, or something that is either false or philosophically self-defeating. I especially want to know which version actually supports each of my classmate's three moves: the localized defense of a particular model, the blanket denial of falsifiability, and the skeptical conclusion that empirical data can never rationally constrain theory choice. Is there also a version of the claim that is technically true yet entirely harmless—something like the ordinary flexibility scientists show when refining measurement conditions—such that invoking it as a conversation-stopper is just a category mistake?
