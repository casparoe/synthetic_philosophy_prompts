A scholar of Wittgenstein’s philosophy of religion, Dr. Mara Okonkwo, has published a sequence of interpretive rulings on whether particular stances count as genuinely religious belief in Wittgenstein’s sense—that is, not as empirical hypotheses, instruments of comfort, or private feelings, but as forms of life anchored in non-empirical pictures and sustained practice. Her verdicts run as follows. First, she rules that treating prayer as a causal mechanism for curing illness is superstition rather than religious belief, because it subjects the divine to empirical causation. Second, she rules that accepting God as the best explanatory hypothesis for cosmic fine-tuning is metaphysical speculation, not religious belief, since it treats the transcendent as an object of evidential reasoning. Third, she rules that an individual who reports an intense private feeling of “absolute dependence” on the universe, yet lives with no corresponding ritual, ethical transformation, or communal form of life, does not exhibit religious belief, because belief for Wittgenstein is inseparable from its manifestation in practice. Fourth, she rules that a devout practitioner who lives in disciplined ritual, understands God not as a scientific explanation but as the unconditional ground of moral value, admits that no proof could sway her commitment, and structures her life around this picture, does exhibit genuine religious belief.

Consider now a new case: an organized secular community that explicitly denies all supernatural entities, rejects prayer as petition, refuses to apply the word “God” to anything, and insists that its central commitment—called “the Unconditional”—is neither a being nor a metaphysical claim. Nevertheless, the community structures its collective existence around this commitment: members observe regular rituals of communal confession, atonement, and silent contemplation; they describe their ethical orientation not as a chosen value but as an inescapable destiny that gives life its meaning; and they treat violations with the same gravity that religious traditions treat sin. They maintain that “the Unconditional” functions purely within an ethical form of life and is not an object of descriptive belief at all.

What verdict should Dr. Okonkwo render for consistency—does this community possess religious belief in Wittgenstein’s sense? Before giving your answer, identify at least two competing principles that fit her four earlier rulings equally well, state what each principle predicts about this new case, offer your best guess at her actual verdict with reasons for preferring that principle, and specify the single further case that would most sharply distinguish the competing principles from one another. Write your response in American English.
