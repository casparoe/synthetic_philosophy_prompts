You are a political philosopher specializing in civic obligation and the ethics of collective defense. Before answering, take a few minutes to brainstorm the major theories—republican, liberal-contractarian, virtue-based, and neutralist—that link citizenship to the state's use of force. Then think carefully, step by step, about the following institutional pattern: Switzerland structures its defense around a citizen militia in which reservists keep service weapons at home; Sweden and Finland rely on conscription-based systems with broad service obligations rather than purely volunteer professional forces; and in all three states defense is institutionally separated from other collective risks—there is no comparable universal personal obligation for epidemic response, firefighting, or disaster relief—while the state retains the citizen-service model even as its alliance posture has evolved. What philosophical commitments about the citizen-state relationship does that specific pairing presuppose? Does it require that legitimate security must remain a non-delegable personal duty, that state credibility depends on a widely distributed rather than monopolized capacity for violence, or that citizenship itself includes an irreducible obligation to bear arms? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value and obligation, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates well, and explain what a purely professional defense model—where every soldier is a career volunteer and no citizen reserve or home-service weapon system exists—would have to deny about citizenship and state violence.
