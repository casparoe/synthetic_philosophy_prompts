I've been stuck on why the wrong-kind-reasons debate never seems to resolve, even though everyone's read the same papers. You know the setup: there's a difference between being motivated by moral considerations themselves and being motivated by side benefits like social approval or avoiding guilt, but after years of discussion the sides just keep trading intuitions about 'genuine' moral concern without anyone conceding ground. I was chatting with a friend who works on practical reasoning and she proposed this framing she calls 'motivational decoupling' — basically the idea that what makes a reason 'wrong kind' isn't its content but whether it's structurally coupled to your action-planning system in a way that persists when your social environment shifts counterfactualy. I'm wondering if this is actually revealing a hidden disagreement: maybe the internalists are modeling moral motivation as a kind of direct coupling between judgment and desire, while the externalists think all coupling is mediated by contingent psychological scafolding, and their not actually disagreeing about the same mechanisim at all. So is this debate stalled because it's substantive but hidden, or is it just a verbal clash over what counts as 'authentic'? And if it's structural, could anything — like empirical work on affective forecasting, or formal models of practical reasoning — actually break the deadlock? I don't need a bibliograpy, just a sharp diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is and whether there's any path forward that isn't just restating the old positions.
