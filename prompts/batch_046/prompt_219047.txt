I'm writing a piece for my science-and-ideas column and I've been noticing this weird move in illusionist writing about consciousness—maybe you've seen it too. The speaker starts by saying phenomenal consciousness is like a magic show our brain puts on, that phrases like what it's like are basically empty pointers with no real target, comparible to phlogiston or witch. But then, a few paragraphs later, they explain why this illusion is so hard to shake: they say it has a special self-certifying structure, that when you introspect you don't just form a false belief, you form one that feels immediately given and therefore resists correction in a way false beliefs about external objects don't. Now, if the concept is genuinely empty—no referent at all—why does it have this distinctive causal profile? You can talk about why people believe in witches without attributing any special epistemic force to the concept of witchcraft itself; people just have bad reasoning. But illusionists keep attributing this unique stickyness specifically to phenomenal representation, almost as if the empty concept has a special grip because it mimics direct acquaintance. So there's this tension where phenomenal concepts are demoted to being entirely fictional or misrepresentational, yet simultaneously upgraded to being uniquely resistant-to-correction mechanisms. Is there an established name for this exact double-status? Not just the hard problem or the explanatory gap, but specifically this move where a concept is treated as both vacuous and as having a sui generis cognitive power? If there's no term, can you coin one and map its edges—how is it different from ordinary sticky false beliefs that persist for social reasons, and is there an analog in other domains like moral anti-realism? I'm not looking for citations or a bibliography, just whether the pattern has been named and where its boundaries sit. Oh, and please say how confident you are in your answer, and point out the specific part you're least sure about—probably the analog to other domains or the boundary conditions.
