As a retired biochemist who has recently taken up philosophy in earnest, I find myself drawn to conceptual tensions that arise when scientific possibility outpaces ethical framework. I have been studying the debate over procreative beneficence, the principle that prospective parents have a moral obligation to select, among the possible children they could have, the one expected to live the best life, alongside the liberal eugenics position that such selection should remain voluntary and state-neutral. To ground these abstractions, I wish to read Andrew Niccol's 1997 film Gattaca through this lens, but with an added conceptual twist: I want the analysis to examine whether the film treats genetic determinism as an institutional self-fulfilling prophecy rather than a biological fact, and whether this undermines the liberal claim that genetic selection merely expands reproductive freedom without coercing anyone.

How does the film dramatize the choice of Vincent's parents to conceive him naturally without genetic engineering, a decision the society regards as a faith birth, in relation to the argument that they had a duty to select against his predispositions? Does Vincent's eventual success in assuming the identity of Jerome, a genetically engineered athlete, serve as a counterexample to procreative beneficence, or does the narrative frame his triumph as exceptional and therefore reinforce the statistical rationality of selection? Furthermore, I want the reading to address whether the structural discrimination faced by those designated invalids reveals a hidden coercion that liberal eugenics overlooks, and whether the swimming rivalry between Vincent and his engineered brother Anton illustrates something about moral luck and genetic desert that formal arguments about beneficence tend to miss. Please take both the cinematic narrative and the philosophical positions seriously: point to specific scenes, such as Vincent's assumption of Jerome's identity, the swimming competition, and the parents' initial decision, without reducing them to mere illustrations, and indicate where the film resists the philosophical reading. I am particularly interested in what this treatment adds to the debate that argument alone does not capture, especially regarding the lived experience of being the product of a choice not made.
