I am facilitating a session for an upper-level political philosophy seminar—fifteen graduate students, ninety minutes—on the conceptual structure of the right to organize, and the design has become personally urgent: I recently began working in a logistics distribution center where an organizing drive is proceeding under conditions of severe information asymmetry, and I have watched management deploy legally permissible “micro-concessions” targeted at pivotal departments to erode collective resolve without ever infringing formal labor protections. I want the participants to come away seeing that the right to strike is not best understood as an individual liberty or a simple bargaining chip, but as a fragile coordination mechanism that must solve a two-level problem—workers need credible common knowledge of solidarity to make a withdrawal of labor effective, yet the very conditions that make solidarity credible, including protected confidentiality, departmental separation, and the risk of targeted retaliation, simultaneously prevent workers from achieving that common knowledge. Design a participatory simulation or structured deliberation—complete with assigned roles, decision rules, and small real stakes, such as pooled participation credit or a collective resource pool—so that the group runs into this tension themselves before it is named.

Structure the activity so that roles include not only “workers” distributed across vulnerability tiers (full-time, part-time, temporary) but also a management player operating with a constrained budget for selective incentives, and arrange the decision nodes so that participants must commit to or reject concerted action without full disclosure of others’ commitments. The rules should permit the management player to legally fracture the coalition by offering targeted improvements to pivotal roles, and the payoff structure should make it individually rational for some workers to defect even when universal defection is collectively catastrophic; include a timeline that moves through an initial information-gathering phase, a confidential commitment round, and a revelation phase where commitments are partially disclosed and micro-concessions are deployed. Specify exactly how the withdrawal of cooperation—work-to-rule, slowdowns, or selective non-participation—differs in its strategic dynamics from a full withdrawal of labor within the simulation’s rules, and ensure that the activity’s mechanics, not the facilitator’s commentary, generate the intuition that protected concerted activity can coexist with the dissolution of collective capacity.

Provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the simulation onto the conceptual distinction between withdrawal of labor and withdrawal of cooperation, anticipates predictable failures—likely an overestimation of solidarity in the first round and an underestimation of management’s legal latitude in the second—and explains how to handle them without collapsing the pedagogical point. Be explicit about which feature of actual labor organizing the simulation cannot reproduce and that you, as facilitator, must supply: for instance, the long temporal horizon over which credible strike capacity accumulates, or the emotional and economic weight of precarity that makes defection feel like survival rather than strategic calculation. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the deliverable should be a fully runnable classroom design with rules, roles, timeline, debrief, and an honest account of its limits.
