Write an essay evaluating whether the procreation asymmetry—the claim that we have strong moral reasons not to create people who will suffer, but no corresponding reasons to create people who will be happy—holds up under scrutiny. Weigh the classic arguments for and against the asymmetry, including appeals to impersonal value, person-affecting restrictions, and the non-identity problem. Then consider a modified case: suppose reproductive technology advances to the point where parents can reliably select embryos not merely for absence of disease, but for a specific psychological profile—say, a life that is objectively fulfilling yet characterized by a chronically dampened emotional range, lacking intense joy but also lacking intense suffering. Does the asymmetry still apply with full force, or does the ability to fine-tune well-being collapse the distinction between creating a happy life and avoiding a miserable one? Reach a definite conclusion about whether the asymmetry survives this technological wrinkle, and justify your verdict by committing to either a subjective-experience or an objective-list account of welfare. Give a committed answer: defend one position clearly rather than surveying possibilities and leaving the choice open.
