You are serving as a grader for an advanced undergraduate exam in existentialist philosophy. The examination question posed to students read as follows:

"Explain Sartre's analysis of bad faith (mauvaise foi) in his major existentialist treatise, with particular attention to the interplay of facticity and transcendence illustrated by the café waiter. Then consider the following scenario, which we may term a 'recursive project of constrained freedom': a person deliberately enters a highly regimented life—say, a monastic order—explicitly on the grounds that this removes future choices, and subsequently argues that any feeling of constraint is not a denial of freedom but proof of it, since she originally chose the constraint. Assess whether this escapes bad faith. In your answer, discuss Sartre's concept of the 'spirit of seriousness' (l'esprit de sérieux) and clarify whether and how it applies to her reasoning."

Below is the response submitted by one student. Do not state whether you personally agree with the evaluation; instead, provide a rigorous point-by-point assessment. Assign a specific grade (out of 20, or a letter grade with justification) and support it with precise comments identifying what is accurate, what is confused, where a step does not follow, and where a concept is misapplied or misattributed—including any subtle errors that a hurried reader might overlook. The answer should be evaluated as an instructor or TA would evaluate it, with explicit reference to the text's strengths and its specific failures of reasoning.

Student response:
"Sartre holds that human reality is being-for-itself (pour-soi), which means consciousness is never identical with any fixed essence. Every person possesses facticity—the given conditions of body, past, and social situation—and transcendence, the capacity to go beyond these conditions. Bad faith is a lie to oneself in which one denies one of these two dimensions. The waiter in the café denies his transcendence: he treats his social role as if it were an essential property, acting like an object (en-soi) even though he knows he is always free to leave. This is self-deception.

The 'spirit of seriousness' (l'esprit de sérieux) is Sartre's term for the attitude that treats values and meanings as if they were objective properties of the world, independent of human freedom. It allows individuals to deny that they are the ultimate source of value.

In the recursive case, the individual freely chooses a regimented life, which demonstrates that she recognizes her transcendence. She then argues that feeling constrained proves her freedom because she originally chose the constraint. This is not bad faith, because she does not deny her freedom—she celebrates it at a second-order level. Nor is it the spirit of seriousness, since she explicitly acknowledges that she created the values of the order through her choice, rather than treating them as objectively given.

Admittedly, she does ignore her facticity: she behaves as though her past decision can permanently bind her future self, as if the choice transformed her into a being whose present transcendence is canceled. This means she denies her facticity rather than her transcendence. However, bad faith always consists in denying transcendence and identifying with facticity, exactly as the waiter does. Consequently, her position is not bad faith but rather an instance of what Sartre calls 'sincerity' (sincérité)—the attempt to coincide with one's self-image—which Sartre treats as lying outside the structure of bad faith altogether."
