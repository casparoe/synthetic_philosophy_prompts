I am preparing a single ninety-minute session for an interdisciplinary graduate reading group composed mainly of sociology and literature students with little prior exposure to analytic moral philosophy. Our text is P.F. Strawson's account of reactive attitudes and moral responsibility, but every member has arrived with the same second-hand summary: that Strawson essentially argues determinism is irrelevant because people feel resentful, and therefore the free-will debate is a pseudo-problem solved by psychology. I need a teaching plan that dismantles this framing without first requiring them to master metaphysics of freedom. Start by telling me the dependency order: which conceptual distinction must be firmly in place before the others can even be heard—specifically, whether the reactive-objective attitude distinction should precede or follow the taxonomy of personal, vicarious, and self-reactive attitudes—and why that order protects against the misconception that reactive attitudes are merely emotional reactions. Choose one concrete carrying example that can travel through the entire arc, not the familiar foot-stepping vignette but a scenario involving institutional judgment—say, a university disciplinary panel responding to plagiarism—so that it can display personal resentment, vicarious indignation, and the objective attitude applied to the same agent. Specify what this example must contain and what it must deliberately omit. Then, in the sequence they will actually arise, list the predictable misconceptions: that Strawson offers a compatibilist theory of free will, that excusing conditions are identical to adopting an objective stance, and that social practices are merely descriptive background rather than normative conditions. For each, state the precise point in the arc where it should be intercepted and how. Design one final check for understanding that reveals whether the group sees that the freedom debate has been displaced rather than resolved, and explicitly identify which element of Strawson's view—perhaps the conditions for self-reactive attitudes or the claim that reactive attitudes are not rationalized—should be left out at this level with an honest admission of the omission. Finally, please indicate what would change your mind about this plan: which failure in the example's capacity to distinguish the attitudes, or which hidden dependency in the sequencing, if it proved real in the room, would cause you to reorder the arc or abandon the institutional scenario.
