Over the past six months I have become the primary surrogate decision-maker for my mother, who was diagnosed with early-stage frontotemporal dementia and had previously structured her affairs through detailed advance directives and a long-standing trustee arrangement. Watching her navigate choices within these pre-established frameworks—sometimes endorsing them, sometimes appearing alienated from their outcomes—has made the philosophical debate about free will feel urgently concrete rather than abstract. I want to understand whether the disagreement between compatibilists and hard incompatibilists genuinely concerns whether she remains a morally responsible agent, or whether part of it is merely verbal.

Please draft the joint statement that would result from an adversarial collaboration between a compatibilist philosopher (Party C) and a hard incompatibilist philosopher (Party I). Structure the document as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims signed by both parties: agreement on the formal conditions of action explanation; on what the major no-go results—specifically the consequence argument and manipulation arguments—actually establish and what they leave open; on the empirical and clinical facts about progressive cognitive impairment, structured dependency, and advance decision-making that are not in dispute; and on the precise framing case, namely an agent with diminishing present-moment deliberative capacity who continues to operate within pre-committed institutional and relational structures, which the collaboration will call diachronic agential scaffolding.

Second, a disagreements table with two columns—Party C’s position in wording Party C accepts, and Party I’s position in wording Party I accepts—parsed into cruxes such that settling any one crux would move at least one party. The table must include the central crux about diachronic agential scaffolding: whether operating within pre-established decision structures that one previously authorized but currently lacks the capacity to revise counts as an exercise of free agency or as a form of external determination.

Third, for each crux, specify the tests, arguments, or cases both parties agree are relevant, with each party’s prediction recorded in advance. For the scaffolding crux specifically, both should address whether retrospective endorsement versus retrospective alienation from outcomes produced within the scaffold is philosophically decisive, and whether empirical or phenomenological study of such responses in progressive dementia care could bear on the dispute.

Fourth, a brief concluding section identifying which parts of the disagreement are verbal—for instance, disputes about the word free that dissolve once replaced by the specific capacities each party cares about—and explaining why the residual disagreement is substantive rather than terminological.

The statement should avoid bibliographies, reading lists, appeals to named scholars’ proprietary theories, or citations. It should not reduce to generic textbook contrasts presented without adaptation; instead, the reasoning should be worked out through the concrete problem of sustained agency through structured dependency, so that the document could be followed by a reader without specialized outside lookup but could not be produced by mere memorization of standard compatibilist and incompatibilist slogans.
