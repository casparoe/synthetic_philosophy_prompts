I am a doctoral student in political philosophy, currently working on autonomy and choice architecture in behavioral public policy, and I am preparing a single 90-minute workshop for about twelve graduate students in philosophy and public administration. I want a structured in-class simulation—complete with assigned roles, explicit rules, and small but real stakes—that makes participants feel, before anyone names the concept, a particular tension I have been framing as epistemically transparent but normatively opaque nudging: a situation in which every chooser knows she is being nudged, knows the direction of the default, and retains full formal freedom to opt out, yet still lacks access to the weighting of values or the counterfactual welfare calculus that justifies the specific architecture for her particular case. The activity should move away from the familiar hidden-manipulation critique of libertarian paternalism and instead stage the discomfort of being guided by a mechanism one can see but cannot fully evaluate as appropriate for oneself.

Please design the simulation with concrete rules and a payoff scheme that requires no real money—perhaps points that determine a collective decision about a minor but actual workshop resource, such as the allocation of a shared reading budget or the selection of a case study for the following session. Divide the group into Choice Architects, who design a retirement-style savings default for a hypothetical population with heterogeneous risk preferences, and Choosers, who experience the default from within and may opt out at a defined cost. Arrange the information asymmetry so that Architects possess the full normative model justifying the framing, while Choosers have complete transparency about the existence and mechanics of the nudge yet only a stylized summary of the welfare calculus—enough to recognize that a judgment is being made on their behalf, but not enough to check whether that judgment applies correctly to them. The rules should be arranged so that Choosers run into the problem themselves: through the act of opting out or staying in, they should discover that they cannot rationally defend their choice without either deferring to expertise they do not understand or inventing ad hoc reasons that expose a gap in their epistemic agency.

Include a precise timeline, a debrief that maps specific moments in the simulation onto the conceptual distinction between visibility of mechanism and access to justification, and an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time—for example, Architects over-explaining to demonstrate good faith, or Choosers treating opt-out as pure rebellion rather than reasoned dissent—along with how to handle it. Be frank about which feature of this form of paternalism, likely the long-term habituation to deference in transparent architectures, the single-session activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied in discussion. Finally, distinguish what the practice of transparent nudging strictly presupposes about the chooser's rational capacities from what it merely accommodates, and flag where the simulation is philosophically underdetermined—specifically, where a defender of soft paternalism could reinterpret the Choosers' discomfort as simple information costs rather than as a failure of epistemic autonomy.
