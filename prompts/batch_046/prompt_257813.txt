I'm taking a course on early analytic ethics, and we've been working through selections from G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica (1903). After our last seminar, a study partner sent me a long message summarizing what she thinks Moore says about intrinsic value, with special attention to beauty and friendship. She writes that Moore identifies only three things as good in themselves: conscious pleasure, the contemplation of beautiful objects, and personal affection—what he calls friendship. She notes that he uses a kind of isolation thought-experiment to establish this, asking us to imagine each item existing alone in a universe and seeing that it still carries value. But then she adds a stronger interpretive claim: she says Moore actually treats friendship as a subspecies of aesthetic appreciation, arguing that when we value a friendship we are really just valuing the beautiful qualities we perceive in the other person's character, much as we value a painting. On her reading, this means friendship does not require reciprocity, mutual awareness, or shared experience to contribute to the intrinsic value of the world; even an unrecognized admiration of someone's virtues would count as an instance of this good. She also suggests that because Moore treats both goods as simple, indefinable properties detected by intuition, he has no resources to distinguish the interpersonal structure of friendship from the one-directional structure of aesthetic contemplation. I suspect she is importing ideas from elsewhere, but I am not sure which parts are faithful simplifications and which distort Moore's actual commitments—especially regarding his well-known doctrine of organic wholes. Could you audit this summary for me? Please separate what is accurate about Moore's view, what counts as a common but defensible simplification, and what he would likely reject; and please address specifically whether his account of organic unities and personal affection allows friendship to collapse into aesthetic appreciation in the way she describes, or whether that conclusion misreads the role of reciprocity and shared consciousness in his ethics.
