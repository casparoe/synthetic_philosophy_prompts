I'm taking a course on comparative political ideologies, and my advisor keeps reaching for the same analogy to explain why American and European deployments of "liberal" clash so persistently. She says the situation is exactly like the American-versus-British split over "football": the same word labels different local practices—gridiron versus association—so once you map each regional usage onto the actual rules being played, there is no substantive contradiction, only a dictionary problem that clears up with translation. She uses this to license the conclusion that transatlantic debates between, say, American progressive "liberals" and European classical "liberals" are largely verbal: substitute "social-democratic" for the American term and "market-oriented constitutionalist" for the Continental one, and the apparent disagreement dissolves, leaving both sides to stop talking past each other.

But I'm not convinced the mapping is that clean, and I want a proper audit of how far the analogy holds. Can you lay out the correspondence table—what plays the role of the word, the regional speaker, the two sports, the shared etymology, the referee who could adjudicate, and the player who might switch codes—then identify precisely where the analogy breaks? In particular, I'm struck by features it quietly imports: that each "game" is internally coherent, that there exists a neutral translation manual, and that no speaker is simultaneously playing both versions. These assumptions seem strained once you look at cases like the Scandinavian Venstre parties (historically agrarian-market liberal, now often socially progressive within a center-right frame) or the German ordoliberal tradition, which does not sit neatly on a left-right axis borrowed from American politics. There is also the American figure who calls herself a "classical liberal" yet supports universal healthcare—a hybrid that looks like a category error under the translation model but may instead signal a genuinely different conceptual architecture. Does the analogy's conclusion—that the disagreement is verbal and can be dissolved by substitution—survive once these disanalogies are on the table? Or does the "football" comparison actually obscure a structural divergence in how the political spectrum itself is constructed across the Atlantic?

If the analogy collapses or is severely weakened, is there a better one—perhaps involving family resemblances, contested inheritance of a shared institutional tradition, or a term whose meaning was fixed by different founding conflicts? And what should the resulting account say about the case I keep returning to: is the American "classical liberal" who endorses robust welfare provision making a conceptual mistake, operating with a hybrid category the manual cannot accommodate, or revealing that the European-American divergence is not just lexical but involves different answers to the question of what a liberal state owes its citizens?
