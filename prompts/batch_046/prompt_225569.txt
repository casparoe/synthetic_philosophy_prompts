My study group has reconstructed a version of Kim’s causal exclusion argument, then pushed it one step further, and now the result looks like a reductio rather than a defense of physicalism. Here is the chain, numbered precisely:

(1) Causal exclusion: if a physical event E has, at time t, a complete and sufficient physical cause C, and C is not identical to some other event M occurring at t, then M is not also a cause of E—otherwise E is systematically overdetermined.

(2) Every mental event (e.g., a decision to raise one’s arm) is realized at t by a specific neural state N, and N is physically sufficient for the subsequent bodily movement B.

(3) So the mental decision is excluded by its realizer: mental properties, being distinct from their neural bases, do no causal work in producing behavior.

(4) Apply the same structure to any macro-property realized by micro-properties: the biological property of being a heart is realized by cellular arrangements sufficient for pumping blood; macro-economic properties are realized by individual transactions sufficient for price movements.

(5) Hence all non-fundamental causation is excluded—not only mental causation but also biological, economic, and chemical causation at the macro-level.

(6) In particular, the mental event of grasping this very argument—call it G—is realized by some neural state R that is sufficient for the speaker’s utterance of the conclusion; by (3), G does not cause the utterance.

(7) Generalizing: no philosophical reasoning event ever causes belief-formation, assent, or speech; only the underlying neural states do.

(8) But rational justification is not merely correlation: for premises to justify a conclusion, the mental act of seeing their connection must play a causally-rationally efficacious role in leading the thinker to assent.

(9) Therefore, if (1)–(3) are true, the exclusion argument is rationally self-defeating: its premises can never cause anyone to rationally accept its conclusion, so it provides no justification for itself.

We are reluctant to abandon (1) and (2), since they seem like direct consequences of the causal completeness of physics together with a plausible non-identity relation between mental and neural properties. We are also reluctant to abandon (8), because without some efficacy of reasoning, philosophical argument collapses into neurology. So where does the argument go wrong? Is there a hidden equivocation in (6)–(7) between token mental events being locally excluded and mental types being globally causally inert? Or does (8) illegitimately conflate causal efficacy with normative rational force, so that rejecting it is the least costly option? If we must reject one premise, which one preserves both physicalism and the possibility of mental causation, and what exactly do we pay for that rejection?
