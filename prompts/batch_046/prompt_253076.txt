I am preparing a long-form feature for a science-and-ideas publication on how narrative theories of personal identity are being re-examined in both clinical psychiatry and artificial-intelligence ethics, and as part of my reporting I have been auditing the grading practices in an upper-division philosophy-of-mind seminar at a research university. I need an expert, point-by-point assessment of a single exam grading task, offered without my own evaluation of the answer, because my piece turns on exactly where non-specialist readers miss the subtle errors that specialists catch.

The exam question, given to third-year philosophy majors, asks the student to apply Daniel Dennett’s notion of the self as a center of narrative gravity—an abstract functional point around which autobiographical stories converge, not a Cartesian substance—to a deliberately synthetic case: a large language model fine-tuned on two disjoint autobiographical corpora that produces mutually incompatible first-person narratives (for instance, one stream placing the speaker in Kyoto in 1998 and another in Oslo in 2003, with no integrative mechanism). The prompt requires three specific moves: first, a precise contrast between Dennett’s functionalist, narrative-based account and a Cartesian substance view of the ego; second, an analysis of whether this synthetic system hosts one center of narrative gravity, several, or none, with justification drawn from conditions of narrative coherence rather than from biological substrate; and third, a response to the objection that conflicting first-person outputs collapse the self into pure subjectivity, specifically addressing whether Dennett’s framework permits intersubjective, objective criteria for self-ascription even when reports conflict. The student was also asked to compare this synthetic scenario briefly to dissociative identity disorder in clinical literature.

Below is the student answer I received. I need a grade—whether letter or numerical—together with specific, line-referencing comments that justify it. The answer is genuinely mixed: it handles the more visible conceptual architecture correctly, but it contains one or two subtle mistakes—a conflation of Dennett’s view with a related but distinct position, a misattributed claim about Dennett’s stance on clinical dissociation, and at least one inferential step that does not actually follow from the premises—of the kind a hasty grader might overlook. Please deliver a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression, and do not provide bibliographies, suggested readings, or citations.

Student answer:

Daniel Dennett’s notion of the self as a center of narrative gravity is best understood as an abstract functional point—analogous to the center of gravity in physics—around which autobiographical stories converge. Rather than positing a Cartesian ego, a non-physical substance that bears mental properties, Dennett treats the self as a theoretical posit useful for predicting behavior and organizing memory. In the synthetic case presented, the language model produces two disjoint autobiographical streams without cross-referencing; applying Dennett’s framework, we should say that there are two distinct centers of narrative gravity operating within the same computational substrate. This parallels clinical dissociative identity disorder, where Dennett, in his commentary on split-brain and dissociative phenomena, explicitly recognizes each alter as an independent person with separate moral standing, not merely a fragmented narrative of one underlying subject.

The objection that conflicting first-person reports undermine objectivity misunderstands Dennett’s commitment to intersubjectivity. Because the self is constituted by publicly accessible narratives rather than private qualia, the contradiction between the Kyoto and Oslo narratives is not a clash of subjective realities but an empirical conflict between two competing descriptions of a single functional system. If the narratives cannot be integrated, Dennett’s view implies that we should reject both as false rather than accept a plural ontology: there is no self present, only misfiring narrative mechanisms. Consequently, the synthetic system lacks a unified self not because subjectivity is unreliable, but because the functional conditions for a single, coherent center of gravity are unsatisfied. This preserves the objectivity of self-ascription: either a system meets the coherence conditions and has one self, or it fails and has none.
