I've been trying to formalize when we should treat two first-person reports as genuinely independant drafts versus just successive edits of the same Cartesian-theater replay. Here are my verdicts, and they're kind of a mess.

If someone reports sharp pain in their left hand, then thirty seconds later reports a dull ache in the same hand, I treat the second as a revision of the first draft — not an independant observation. Same deal if they first say "I picked the red card because I liked the color" and then, after seeing eye-tracking data, say "actually I picked it because it was on the left" — that's post-hoc narration editing the original script, not a seperate mental state being reported.

But if they report feeling anxious about a talk and simultaneously report feeling excited about it, those feel like parallel drafts running in different streams, and I want to call them independant. Same with split-brain cases where the left hemisphere reports seeing a chicken and the right hand points to a shovel — those are clearly different drafts with different content, and treating them as one unified "experience" is exactly the Cartesian theater mistake.

The hard case: a meditator in deep jhana reports "there is no subject here, just raw sensation" in real time via button-press, then ten minutes later reports "I was calm and focused the whole time." My gut says the first is a pre-narrative draft (no theater, just content being generated) and the second is the usual narrative reconstruction, so they shouldn't be pooled as independant data points about the same mental interval. But then I look at blindsight — the subject sincerely reports seeing nothing, yet their pointing behavor tracks the stimulus. Here I want to treat the verbal report and the behavioral report as independant streams, not as one correcting the other. So my rule can't just be "successive reports are revisions."

What principle, if any, captures when mental reports are draft-independant? Is there a coherent account of "inter-draft independence" that saves all my verdicts, or do I have to drop one? And if there is a principle, what does it say about cases where a single brain generates contradictory drafts with no clear temporal ordering — like simultaneous conflicting emotional reports? I keep wondering if the presumption of independence I'm borrowing from stats is even doing real work here, or if I'm just smuggling in Cartesian intuition about a "true" inner state hiding behind all the drafts.
