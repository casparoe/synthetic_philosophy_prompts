Several governments now pair generous R&D tax credits for industrial automation with lifelong-learning mandates that place the burden of adaptation on individual workers rather than on the firms that deploy the machinery. What implicit theory of technology and labor does this pairing presuppose? Does it treat machinery as neutral fixed capital whose social effects depend only on ownership rules, or does it assume that labor-power is a fungible skill-set that can be remapped without structural loss? Contrast that implicit view with Marx's analysis of automation—especially his claim that machinery tends to reduce living labor to a supervisory function and that collective knowledge becomes incorporated into capital as an external dominating force. Reconstruct the policy's hidden commitments, say whether the practice is genuinely compatible with Marx's framework or only cosmetically so, and specify what a policy genuinely shaped by that framework would have to change about risk-bearing, revenue allocation, and who controls the pace of mechanization.
