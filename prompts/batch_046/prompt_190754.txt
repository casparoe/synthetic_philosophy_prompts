Hey, so I'm in this philosophy of mind seminar and we've been going in circles about knowing-how versus knowing-that for like three sessions now. The intellectualist camp — you know, the ones following Stanley and Williamson — keeps saying that knowing how to do something is really just knowing a fact, specifically a proposition about a way of doing it, and they point to cases where you can know how without being able to do it (like knowing how to swim but being paralyzed). Then the anti-intellectualists, more in Ryle's line, say that's missing the point entirely: knowing-how is a kind of ability or dispostion, not a mental state with propositional content at all. But here's the thing — I keep feeling like they're not even talking about the same phenomenon. I was chatting with a friend who works in cognitive science and she mentioned something she calls "procedural transparancy", meaning how visible the sub-steps of a skill are to the agent themselves. I wonder if the intellectualists are really asking about high-transparancy skills (like following a recipe) where propositional knowledge dominates, while the anti-intellectualists are focused on low-transparancy embodied skills (like riding a bike). So is this a substantive disagreement about the nature of skill, or is it verbal — are they using "knowing how" for different things? Or maybe it's about standards of evidence: the intellectualists want linguistic reportability, the anti-intellectualists want behavioral success. Diagnose it for me using what each side actually argues, and tell me what kind of progress would break the stalemate — an experiment, a conceptual distinction, or maybe just admitting they're answering different questions? I don't want you to just declare a winner; I want to understand the structure of the impasse, and whether that hidden variable of procedural transparancy is actually doing all the work.
