I am a retired molecular biologist who has recently begun reading philosophy of science. In a popular science book I encountered a summary of Douglas Hofstadter's Copycat programme and his thesis of fluid concepts. The author writes that Copycat demonstrates analogical reasoning to be purely probabilistic symbol manipulation, with no genuine understanding involved. He then applies this to scientific taxonomy: just as Copycat's categories slip within their microdomain to accommodate new letter strings, so biological concepts such as enzyme or gene are entirely fluid. Once restricted to proteins, enzyme now includes ribozymes and synthetic deoxyribozymes, which proves, according to the author, that these categories have no stable core and are merely linguistic fashions. Hence, the author concludes, my four decades of classifying metabolic pathways amounted to little more than negotiating arbitrary conventions. He further claims that Hofstadter's notion of slippage—the bending of a concept to fit a novel case—shows that every scientific distinction is ultimately conventional rather than a discovery about nature.

This seems to me both intriguing and suspiciously quick. The inference from letter-string analogy to biochemical ontology appears to conflate descriptive flexibility with prescriptive anti-realism, and the author treats Hofstadter's microdomain analysis as licensing global scepticism about taxonomic reality. Yet I am not certain where precisely the summary becomes unfair. Could you audit it? Please distinguish what is faithful to Hofstadter's actual claims about Copycat and fluid concepts, what is a harmless popularisation, and what he would reject—particularly regarding whether slippage within a domain entails the dissolution of all conceptual boundaries. Does the argument about enzymes and taxonomic arbitrariness actually follow, or does it depend on an unstated premise linking conceptual fluidity to ontological commitment? I should be grateful for your assessment in British English, with no need for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
