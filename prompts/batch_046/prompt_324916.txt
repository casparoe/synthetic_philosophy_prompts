Read Le Guin's 'The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas' through Scanlon's contractualism, specifically the aggregation problem. The story sets up a classic utilitarian bargain: one child's suffering for the happiness of thousands. Most readings treat it as a rejection of that calculus. But I want you to take Scanlon seriously: contractualism says wrongness is about what we could justify to each other, and it rejects aggregating small harms across people to outweigh one big harm. Apply that to Omelas. Does the fact that thousands benifit from the child's suffering mean anything contractually? Or does the wrongness stay exactly the same whether it's one beneficiary or a million? Then there's a twist I want you to consider: in the story, some citizens walk away. Does contractualism have anything to say about the moral status of those who stay versus those who leave, especially since the stayers arn't individually harming the child but are collectively maintaining the system? And where does the aggregation problem bite if we ask whether the stayers' collective benifit can be broken into individual justifications? I want this for a philosophy seminar, not a lit-crit essay. Also please say what argument or consideration would change your mind on whether contractualism actually handles the story's moral structure better than a rule-utilitarian reading does — like if there's a specific feature of the social contract in Omelas that undermines the contractualist framing.
