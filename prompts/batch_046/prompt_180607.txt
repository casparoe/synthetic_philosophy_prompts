I was arguing with a friend about evolutionary debunking in metaethics and we tried to formalize the "it proves too much" worry but now I'm stuck and maybe overthinking it. Here's the argument we cooked up over coffee. (1) If a belief-forming mechanism in domain D is selected purely for reproductive fitness rather than tracking D-truths, and we have no independent access to D-truths, then beliefs in D are unjustified. (2) Our core moral beliefs—about harm, fairness, authority—are clearly shaped by kin selection and social coordination pressures, and we lack independent moral intuition that bypasses those mechanisms. (3) So evolutionary debunking undermines moral justification, at least for thick normative claims. (4) But our logical, modal, and probabilistic reasoning mechanisms are also fitness-shaped: predator avoidance and coalition-building selected for quick inference patterns, not for tracking necessary truths. Their's no clear evolutionary reason we'd have mechanisms that reliably track abstract modal facts. (5) So by (1), this should debunk logic and probabilistic reasoning too—including the very inference patterns used to derive (3). (6) A friend suggested logical beliefs are "counterfactually stable" across evolutionary histories, but if (1) is right, stability shouldn't matter unless we already have independent access, which we don't. So either the debunker is self-undermined, or they must arbitrarily exempt logic with no principled boundary, which feels like special pleading. The conclusion that evolutionary debunking is dead on arrival seems wrong to me, but I want to keep (1) and (2) if possible since they capture the heart of the Street/Joyce-style challenge. Where exactly does this argument go wrong, if it does? Which premise or step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost me?
