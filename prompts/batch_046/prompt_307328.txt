You are a professional philosopher of politics and an experienced curriculum designer for secondary education. Before constructing the plan, take a moment to brainstorm the conceptual dependencies and misconception pathways carefully, then think step by step through the logic of the explanation. I am a high school junior organizing a fifty-minute study session for peers—bright students with no formal background in political theory—who have just finished a news article about a high-profile bribery trial and are now convinced that corruption is simply “breaking the rules for money.” I need you to design the teaching plan, not deliver the lecture itself. The target idea is that corruption is not a culturally self-evident category: practices like clientelism and ritual gift-giving occupy a contested middle space, and applying Western legal standards universally can amount to conceptual imperialism unless we first understand the functional and social roles those practices serve.

Please organize your response around the following, in a clear, formal structure:

1. The dependency order: specify which distinctions must be established before others can land—for instance, whether “abuse of entrusted office” must precede “clientelism,” whether a functionalist account of reciprocity must come before any normative judgment about Western standards, and why that sequence is necessary rather than merely convenient. Justify the ordering.

2. The single carrying example: construct one concrete, somewhat niche scenario—specifically, a municipal water-infrastructure negotiation in an unspecified post-colonial setting, where a tribal elder offers ceremonial hospitality and long-term reciprocal obligations to an engineer, and where acceptance creates both local social legitimacy and contractual ambiguity. State exactly what this example must include (no explicit cash exchange, no clear statutory prohibition in local customary law, a tangible public good at stake) and what it must deliberately lack (an unambiguous villain, a simple legal resolution), so that it forces the boundary problem rather than resolving it.

3. The misconception map: identify four misconceptions these students will likely bring or form—anticipating at least “gift-giving is just bribery with better manners,” “Western anti-corruption rules are culturally neutral instruments,” “clientelism is merely corruption operating slowly,” and “if a practice is locally accepted, it cannot be corrupt.” For each, indicate the precise point in the dependency sequence where you would head it off and the brief rhetorical move that would do so.

4. The check for understanding: provide one precise end-of-session question or brief written task that would reveal whether the students have grasped the boundary problem—distinguishing functional reciprocity from abuse of office—rather than having memorized a definition.

5. The explicit omissions: state openly what should be left out at this level—particularly any demand for bibliographies, country-specific legal citations, or references to obscure theoretical frameworks that a student or a small model without web access could not verify or reproduce. Confirm that you will not include reading lists.

Work through this carefully, keep the explanation grounded in the conceptual architecture rather than in anecdote, and present only the plan.
