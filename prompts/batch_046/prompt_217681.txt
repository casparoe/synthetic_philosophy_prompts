Compare Bernard Williams's thesis that norms such as dueling and slavery end primarily through shifts in peer honor and social status—rather than through the victory of moral arguments—with Kwame Anthony Appiah's claim that moral revolutions occur when longstanding practices become incompatible with the very honor codes that once sustained them. Apply both frameworks to the following constructed scenario: in a tightly knit research discipline, senior scholars have for generations publicly corrected junior colleagues' errors in harsh, ritualized terms; the practice is defended as "toughening the field," yet it collapses within months after one eminent figure quietly refuses and is widely praised for "protecting scientific dignity." Would Williams interpret this as a straightforward recalibration of honor—where refusal became the status-enhancing move—while Appiah might argue the practice was already undermined by a latent contradiction between collegial honor and the ritual? Which account better explains the speed of the collapse, and does either framework leave room for genuine moral progress as opposed to a mere redistribution of prestige?
