Consider the institutional practice—now standard at several frontier AI labs—of certifying a model as aligned or safe for deployment almost entirely through behavioral evaluation: benchmarking on held-out tasks, measuring refusal and compliance rates on adversarial prompts, and aggregating human preference scores. Internal representational structures—what the model computes as intermediate planning representations, whether it has formed a mesa-objective distinct from the reward signal, or whether its reasoning traces contain deceptive instrumental reasoning—are treated as epistemically opaque and are not part of the certification gate; interpretability studies, if conducted at all, occur post-hoc and do not block release.

What theory of mental content, intentionality, or goal-attribution does this certification practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy: does it commit to a behaviorist or operationalist view of goals, to an externalist semantics for artificial agents, or to a tractability-based epistemology that renders internal states irrelevant to normative assessment? Say whether the practice strictly requires any one of these, or whether it is philosophically underdetermined—compatible, say, with a representationalist view that simply lacks practical access to representations.

Then examine consistency: the same research community warns extensively about inner misalignment and goal misgeneralization, precisely the risk that a system behaves well in evaluation while pursuing an unintended internal objective. Can a practice that treats internal objectives as certification-irrelevant be coherently maintained alongside that theoretical warning? If not, what hidden premise resolves the tension—perhaps a distinction between training-time and deployment-time access, or a bet on the correlation between behavior and internal state?

Finally, describe what an evaluation policy built on the opposite presupposition—say, a representationalist or internalist view that takes mesa-objectives as the primary object of safety assessment—would actually have to change in its procedures, metrics, and release conditions.
