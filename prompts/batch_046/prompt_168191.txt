I am currently enrolled in a seminar on early modern practical philosophy, where we have been examining Leibniz's moral metaphysics—specifically his characterization of justice as charity guided by wisdom, his account of evil as a privation rather than a positive reality, and the way moral obligation derives from the perfection of rational substances within a pre-established harmony. Most of our coursework has focused on texts available to Leibniz in his own century, but I want to test whether his system has internal resources to address a form of moral reasoning that emerged long after his death. In particular, I am interested in modern longtermist and effective-altruist frameworks, which treat moral duty as an expected-value calculation across vast, counterfactual future populations—including individuals who do not yet exist and whose preferences are entirely unknown—and which prioritize reducing existential risk over the immediate welfare of presently existing beings.

Given Leibniz's commitment to the principle of sufficient reason, his distinction between physical and moral necessity, and his view that each monad expresses the universe only from its own irreducibly particular perspective, how do you think he would have evaluated this kind of cross-temporal aggregation? Would he have seen it as a legitimate extension of rational charity, or rather as a metaphysical confusion—perhaps one that treats merely possible monads as if they possessed actual present claims on our perfection, or that attempts an illegitimate global optimization from a point of view that violates the harmony of perspectives? I am not asking for a reading list or bibliographic references; I am trying to determine whether his ethics can coherently accommodate reasoning that aggregates across possible worlds without collapsing into the kind of universalist abstraction his monadology seems designed to resist.
