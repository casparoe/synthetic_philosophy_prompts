Construct a branching decision-tree taxonomy of answers to the special composition question, intended as a seminar instrument that sorts participants by their judgments about concrete cases rather than by familiarity with canonical labels. Build the tree from the logical space of commitments, not from a list of named philosophers. The root node should pose the question of whether any composite objects exist beyond elementary entities. From there, branch first on whether composition is unrestricted, restricted by a single criterion, or restricted by a disjunctive set of criteria; then, for all restricted branches, on whether the operative criterion is spatiotemporal (contact, spatiotemporal continuity, or causal integration across spatial or temporal gaps), biological (life processes, evolutionary lineage, or metabolic closure), functional-artifactual (design intention, social or institutional role), or an explicit hybrid of these. At every internal node, phrase the split as a yes-or-no or either-or question that can be answered by considering one of the following diagnostic cases, with no reference to which philosophical position the answer produces: (i) a diffuse cloud of dust particles held together only by transient gravitational attraction and lacking any cohesive boundary; (ii) a human organism in which every original atomic constituent has been replaced over decades; (iii) a lichen composed of interdependent fungal and algal partners that can survive separation but function as a unit; (iv) a marble statue gradually replaced part-by-part over centuries, then digitally scanned and 3D-printed in a distant location using entirely new material; (v) a scattered causal process in which successive parts never coexist in space but are linked by continuous causal transmission; and (vi) an artificial heart or neural prosthesis fully integrated into an organism's homeostatic regulation, with no biological lineage shared with the host. The tree should yield exactly six to eight leaves. Each leaf should carry the name of the position it represents—such as unrestricted universalism, mereological nihilism, biological-organic restriction, contact-continuity restriction, functional-artifactual restriction, hybrid-pluralist restriction, or "unnamed" if the combination is logically available but undefended—followed by one sentence stating its most awkward or counterintuitive commitment. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate, particularly whether biological and functional restrictions collapse in cases involving synthetic organ replacement or bio-engineered symbiosis. In addition, identify at least one branch combination that remains unoccupied in the logical space, and briefly diagnose whether the vacancy stems from internal incoherence, severe unattractiveness, or mere neglect. The framing should be fully self-contained: a respondent without access to external sources should be able to navigate every node, understand each leaf, and locate their own view without encountering citation-based distinctions or philosopher-name prompts such as references to specific twentieth-century metaphysicians.
