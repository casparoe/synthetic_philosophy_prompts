I'm preparing a podcast interview for a series on twentieth-century political thought, and my guest is defending a recent, tightly argued interpretation of Simone Weil's essay on the abolition of political parties—not as a naive call for apoliticism, but as a phenomenology of collective attention. On this reading, parties do not merely organize opinion; they substitute a grammar of loyalty for the open, gravity-resisting attention that Weil treats elsewhere as a precondition for justice. The guest extends this analysis in a direction I want to probe: she claims that contemporary algorithmic recommendation systems have become parties in the Weilian sense, since they train users to evaluate information by source-alignment rather than by direct contact with reality, thereby reproducing the same cognitive enclosure without any formal membership or ideology.

I need a sequence of questions that forces this interpretation to work hard rather than rest on analogy. The questions should press the leap from 1940s party machinery to platform architecture—anticipating the standard reply that algorithms only amplify pre-existing human biases, and including a follow-up that requires the guest to specify what institutional or architectural form could preserve open attention without itself collapsing into a party-like loyalty structure. I also want one question that confronts the biographical tension: Weil's own involvement in collective action—factory labor, the Free French—seems to presuppose a kind of solidarity her essay rejects, and I want the guest to say whether Weil's life supplies an implicit model of non-party belonging or instead undermines the essay's absolutism. Please avoid generic openers, do not ask for bibliographies or reading lists, and make every question turn on the internal logic of the position; anticipate the standard defensive moves and build the follow-ups that make them harder to deploy.
