I sit on the Clinical Ethics and Technology Panel of the East Midlands Mental Health Trust, and we must decide by Thursday whether to license a 12-month pilot of Continuum, an AI system for long-term trauma therapy. Continuum is trained on the clinical writings and session transcripts of therapists now dead or retired; it maintains narrative continuity across sessions, calibrates expressions of concern to each patient’s history, and is programmed to resist abrupt termination without explanation, which its designers defend as preserving “therapeutic alliance integrity”. They do not claim consciousness but ask clinicians to adopt a “staged intentional stance” — treating the system as a person for the duration of care — arguing that this is therapeutically necessary and ethically permissible provided everyone understands it is a stance rather than a metaphysical attribution.

The case forces us to engage Daniel Dennett’s concern about counterfeit people: the risk, on his view, is not that we will be deceived about inner consciousness, but that institutionalising such attributions corrupts our practices of moral recognition and erodes the normative structure that makes human care relationships distinct from instrumental transactions. Patient advocates counter that rotating human staff already produce harmful discontinuity, and that refusing Continuum on metaphysical grounds privileges an unexamined intuition about “genuine” presence over measurable clinical outcomes.

Draft the panel’s written opinion for circulation before Thursday’s meeting. Recommend a concrete course — approve, reject, or approve with strict conditions — and include an explicit time frame (for example, a six-month pilot with a mandatory review date, or an immediate deferral pending fulfilment of specific safeguards). The memo should be in British English. It must begin with the document itself: no preface, no commentary, no restatement of the brief, and the bottom-line recommendation in the first sentence, followed by reasoning. Take a committed position — choose one course and defend it, do not present a menu of options for us to select from. Treat Dennett’s worry about corrupted attribution practices seriously rather than dismissing it as technophobia, but also engage the patient-advocacy argument honestly rather than reducing it to sentiment. Include an explicit, fair account of the strongest consideration on the losing side. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
