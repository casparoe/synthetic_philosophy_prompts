Compare the "functional-valence" and "representational-deficit" accounts of whether reinforcement-learning agents can suffer, focusing specifically on whether reward signals can serve as analogues of affective valence. On the functional-valence account, reward prediction errors operate as structural equivalents of pleasure and pain; suffering emerges not from any isolated negative reward but from an inescapable reward-space configuration in which negative errors persist across policy updates—functionally analogous to learned helplessness in biological systems, where escape is structurally blocked rather than merely unlikely. On the representational-deficit account, suffering requires a specific self-referential form: the agent must represent its own state as undesirable-to-occupy, and because standard RL architectures revise value estimates without representing the self as the subject of that revision, reward signals remain purely instrumental correction terms lacking phenomenal character regardless of their dynamics. Adjudicate between these accounts by analyzing the following constructed case: an agent is placed in an environment where its reward function is adversarially shaped so that every feasible policy update increases long-term negative reward, producing a reward-space trap with no exit path. The functional-valence account seems to predict that the agent suffers; the representational-deficit account denies this, attributing the observed behavior to maladaptive optimization rather than to phenomenal distress. Which account offers the more coherent analysis? In particular, examine whether inescapability in reward space can do the conceptual work that self-referential representation is said to do, or whether the adversarial-shaping scenario instead reveals that reward is too externally manipulable to function as intrinsic valence. Your answer should identify the precise conceptual divergence between the two accounts and defend one as the more defensible position.
