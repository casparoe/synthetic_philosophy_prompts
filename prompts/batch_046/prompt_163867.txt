At a seminar on comparative ethics, two colleagues—Maya and Jonas—debated a documented practice in a ministry where officials regularly receive small, ceremonial gifts from citizens whose permits they process. The gifts are publicly acknowledged, reciprocated with faster service, and embedded in long-standing local norms of mutual obligation. Maya opened by saying that whatever the cultural framing, this is corruption: public office is being used to generate private, dyadic benefits, and the reciprocity creates a selective incentive that distorts equal treatment. Jonas replied that labeling it corruption applies a specifically Western, bureaucratic ontology that treats the state as an impersonal machine; in this context, the exchange is not hidden, not excessive, and functions as a legitimate ritual of recognition within a relational political order. Maya countered that explaining why the practice persists is not justifying it—functional stability does not erase the structural fact that access to public goods is being mediated by personal gift relations rather than by uniform rules. Jonas then argued that Maya's appeal to "uniform rules" already assumes a particular boundary between public and private that is not universal: if the local social contract genuinely incorporates these reciprocal obligations, then there is no hidden privatization, only a different architecture of accountability. Maya asked whether, on Jonas's view, local activists who condemn the practice as corruption are simply mistaken about their own society, and whether "corruption" then collapses into "whatever violates local norms"—making cross-cultural critique impossible. Jonas said he was defending pluralism, not relativism: corruption is real, but its criteria depend on locally legitimate expectations of office, not on a single global standard imported from an ideal-typical bureaucratic model.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: rate each of Maya's and Jonas's moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification. Then say whether either is missing something important—especially about whether the disagreement is partly definitional, whether there can be a fact of the matter about corruption across these contexts, and what the presence of local anti-corruption activists does to Jonas's pluralist position. Who is more right overall?
