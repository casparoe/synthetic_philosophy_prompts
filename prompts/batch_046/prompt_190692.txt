Construct a decision-tree taxonomy for sorting philosophical positions on collective intentionality and joint action, designed for an advanced undergraduate seminar. Build the tree from concrete diagnostic scenarios rather than from author names. Begin with the case of two strangers silently coordinating to carry a table up stairs, then branch through four binary distinctions: (a) whether shared intention requires explicit mutual acknowledgment or can arise from mere behavioral coordination; (b) whether participants must represent the action in a we-mode (as subjects of a plural agent) or whether interlocking I-mode intentions are sufficient; (c) whether joint commitments generate obligations that persist after one party privately withdraws mental assent; and (d) whether a non-conscious artificial system could be a genuine co-agent in joint action with a human. For each internal node, present a brief concrete scenario—such as a choir continuing after one singer forgets the shared goal, or a human and an AI jointly navigating a maze—so that respondents can answer without knowing the literature. The leaves should map onto named or clearly described positions (reductionist shared-intention view, plural-subject view, normative joint-commitment view, minimalist interactionism, and eliminativism about collective minds), with each leaf stating its most awkward commitment in one sentence. Identify at least one unoccupied branch combination and diagnose whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, flag any two positions that your tree fails to separate and explain why they collapse onto the same path. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; describe any philosopher's view in one sentence of substance rather than attributing it by name.
