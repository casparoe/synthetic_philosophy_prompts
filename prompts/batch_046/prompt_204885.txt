I've been turning over an argument about whether historical periodization picks out real structures or only later inventions, and the conclusion is so destructive that I think a step must be wrong, but I can't locate it.

(1) For a macro-historical unit—say, the Renaissance or the Scientific Revolution—to be a real feature of the past rather than a retrospective invention, there must be a scheme-independent fact of the matter about which events belong to it.

(2) Take an event near any conventional boundary: the operation of a particular printing press in Mainz around 1455, or the publication of a particular cosmological work in 1543. Whether these belong to the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, or the Scientific Revolution varies with whether we sort by technological diffusion, by the institutionalization of empirical observation, or by the self-conscious recovery of classical texts.

(3) Because legitimate historiographical schemes can assign the same event to different periods, there is no counterfactual-stable answer to the membership question: if our disciplinary traditions had emphasized different markers, the boundary would fall elsewhere.

(4) So period labels do not track scheme-independent joints in history; they are classificatory artifacts.

(5) Now, if a category is a pure artifact with no scheme-independent reality, then using it in causal explanation is circular. To say that the Renaissance caused the secularization of art is just to say that the cluster of phenomena we have labeled Renaissance includes secularized art.

(6) A non-circular causal claim requires that the cause be identifiable independently of the effect; otherwise we have only redescription.

(7) Therefore, any macro-historical explanation that appeals to periods, epochs, or revolutions is spurious.

(8) By the same reasoning, not only named periods but any large-scale unit—civilizational cycles, economic regimes, global ages—collapses into invention, leaving only micro-narratives of individual action.

(9) But this eliminates the possibility of explaining large-scale change altogether, which is too high a price.

If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and (6) than accept (9), but I want to know exactly what I'm paying. Which step should I reject? Explain why it looked plausible—whether there is an equivocation between epistemic sorting and ontological reality, a hidden quantifier shift about boundary cases versus core cases, or a modal slide from "depends on criteria" to "lacks causal power"—and tell me what each of the main alternatives costs. In particular, if I reject the step that saves macro-causation, do I have to abandon the idea that historical categories need scheme-independent membership conditions, or can I weaken (1) without giving up on historical realism altogether?
