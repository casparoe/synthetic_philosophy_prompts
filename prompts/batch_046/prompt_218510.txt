I have been reflecting on what makes a life genuinely good ever since I encountered a popular account of the experience machine thought experiment, and I find that my intuitions across different scenarios hang together in a pattern I cannot quite formalise. I have no academic background in philosophy, but I have discussed these matters with friends and tried to remain consistent. Here are my verdicts so far: in the classic case where one is unknowingly plugged into a perfect simulation, I say it is not a good life despite the pleasure; in a variant where one knowingly enters a virtual environment, collaborates with other real people who have also chosen to enter, and produces work that affects the physical world, I think that can be genuinely good; in an ordinary real life of modest labour, real affection, and unavoidable difficulty, I say that is clearly a good life; in a case where a drug induces feelings of profound love and accomplishment while in reality one is alone and one's family is dead, with no prospect of discovery, I still judge it a bad life; and in a case where one accidentally achieves something important—say, curing an illness—while feeling like a failure throughout and never learning of the result, I say the world is better off but it was not a good life for that person.

Now consider this further case: a person lives permanently inside a fully enclosed virtual world. They are fully aware of their situation. They maintain deep, reciprocal relationships with other conscious minds who are also present in that environment. They labour hard, accomplish complex goals that are genuinely meaningful within that world, and experience sustained purpose and contentment. Yet nothing they do has any causal effect beyond the simulation; they possess no physical body except a maintained brain, and they have no intention of ever returning to physical existence.

Would I regard this as a genuinely good life? Predict my verdict, then identify the precise principle I seem to be implicitly running—whether it is a dual condition requiring both causal contact with non-simulated reality and accurate awareness of that contact, a narrative-authorship condition that cares only for genuine endorsement and mental integration, or perhaps a modified hedonism constrained by a transparency requirement. State which of my earlier verdicts most strongly supports your reading, and then specify the single additional case that would settle the matter between your preferred principle and its closest rival. Please reply in British English.
