I've been working through whether the classic Needham formulation—why Britain industrialized and not China—is genuinely well posed, and comparing it with other contrastive explanations that appear in the Great Divergence literature. I find myself with firm but apparently inconsistent intuitions about which formulations open up real causal work and which collapse into descriptive catalogues of civilizational difference. I want to see if there is a single principle that orders them, or whether I have to abandon some of my verdicts.

Consider these cases. First, "Why Britain and not France by 1800?" strikes me as legitimate: both were large, centralized European states with Atlantic access and colonial networks, so isolating the divergence demands explaining specific institutional or resource differences. Second, "Why Britain and not Qing China?" feels malformed unless severely restricted—the background conditions are so disparate that selecting one factor as decisive seems arbitrary—yet comparative economic historians clearly treat the pairing as explanatory once they hold constant population scale and market integration up to about 1750. Third, "Why did Britain shift to coal and steam rather than continuing with water and wood?" is well posed despite not comparing countries at all; the technological trajectories share the same economic and scientific context, so the contrast isolates a specific mechanism. Fourth, "Why factory production in Britain rather than in the Netherlands, given both were wealthy, urbanized commercial societies?" also feels sharp, because the shared background makes the divergence genuinely puzzling. Fifth, I am torn by a pair that looks structurally parallel. "Why did Britain develop a patent system encouraging mechanization rather than maintaining guild secrecy like parts of the German states?" feels answerable and focused. But "Why did Britain develop parliamentary constitutionalism rather than maintaining imperial bureaucratic examination like China?" feels like it explains nothing—merely describing two civilizations—despite both comparing Britain with an alternative institutional path and identifying a real difference.

My tentative pattern is that a contrastive historical question is well posed when the alternatives share enough causal background that isolating one difference is meaningful, and ill posed when the differences are so total that causal selection becomes arbitrary. But this cannot be quite right: if background disparity makes Britain-China the worst case, why does the literature treat it as fruitful once certain variables are held constant? Meanwhile, is my resistance to the constitutionalism case actually about the granularity of the contrast—bundling too many institutional variables—rather than about background disparity per se? Is there a more precise principle—perhaps about whether the explanandum is defined relationally within a homogeneous causal space, or about the number of independent dimensions on which the alternatives differ—that captures all these judgments? Which of my verdicts must I drop: that Britain-China is malformed without restriction, that Britain-France is well posed, or that there is a real explanatory distinction between the patent-system and constitutionalism comparisons? And what does the correct principle say about a formulation like "Why did Britain experience sustained per-capita growth after 1800 rather than stagnating at a Malthusian ceiling like pre-industrial economies generally did?"
