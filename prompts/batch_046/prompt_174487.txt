I'm trying to work out what distinguishes manipulation from legitimate persuasion, and I want to see whether my intuitions about particular cases hang together under one principle or whether they force me to split the concept. Here is how they fall out. A political campaign that describes a tax increase as "everyone paying their fair share" rather than "redistribution" strikes me as legitimate framing, not manipulation, even though it is clearly designed to shift affect. A therapist who uses guided imagery to help a patient recover a traumatic memory, despite the patient's initial resistance and emotional discomfort, also seems legitimate—perhaps even obligatory—because the influence serves the patient's own reflective ends. By contrast, a social-media recommendation algorithm that maximizes engagement by selectively surfacing content calibrated to trigger moral outrage feels manipulative, even though every click is technically voluntary. A salesperson who learns that a customer recently lost a parent and then frames a luxury purchase as "something your father would have wanted for you" is unambiguously manipulative. The pair that gives me trouble is structural: a university registration portal that makes an expensive optional fee the prominent green default and buries the opt-out in a greyed-out submenu requiring two extra clicks feels manipulative in its architecture, yet I am less certain I should condemn it than I condemn the salesperson, perhaps because no false belief is implanted and formal consent remains intact. Another case I keep reversing on is a friend who hides my cigarettes, tells me they were stolen, and lets me stay angry for a day—knowing I will eventually feel grateful—because the deception seems to serve my own long-term autonomy but violates my immediate epistemic standing. Is there a unified principle—say, one centered on the subversion of rational agency, on the bypassing of reflective capacities, or on the exploitation of a specific vulnerability—that captures all of these verdicts, or do I need separate categories for informational framing, emotional exploitation, and architectural "choice architecture"? And if a principle must overturn one of my judgments to stay simple, which should go? Finally, apply whatever principle you settle on to a case I have not listed: an AI assistant that, without being prompted, silently restructures its user's daily schedule to maximize productivity, using only information the user previously shared, never misrepresenting its goals, and presenting the revised schedule as a neutral "suggestion" that the user can reject with a single click. Does that fall on the manipulation side, the persuasion side, or does it reveal that my taxonomy needs a third category altogether?
