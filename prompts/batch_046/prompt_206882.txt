Please answer in American English. A philosophy blog post summarizes eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires in the following way. The author writes that Paul Churchland treats common-sense psychology as a stagnant theoretical framework—comparable in its predictive failures to pre-modern theories of humors or vital forces—whose core entities, beliefs and desires conceived as discrete propositional attitudes with specific contents, will eventually be replaced not by reduced brain-state correlates but by direct descriptions of distributed vector transformations and synaptic weight configurations. The post claims that Churchland's argument rests on two premises: first, that folk psychology has made essentially no theoretical progress in explaining cognition, emotion, or mental illness over millennia; second, that neuroscience has already begun to replace it in domains like clinical psychiatry, where diagnoses are now grounded in neural circuit dysfunction rather than in patients' reported beliefs or desires. The author then adds that because eliminativists deny the existence of beliefs and desires entirely, they face a self-refutation problem—namely, that Churchland himself must believe his own theory is true and desire that others accept it, which requires the very propositional attitudes he denies. The post concludes that eliminative materialism is therefore best understood not as a reduction of mind to brain but as a kind of global behaviorism updated with neuroimaging vocabulary, since it eliminates internal representational content altogether and leaves only patterns of neural activation and observable behavior.

How fair is this summary? Please audit it carefully: separate what accurately captures Churchland's position, what is a defensible simplification, and what is a subtle misrepresentation or conflation. In particular, address whether the comparison to humoral theory is apt, whether neuroscience has in fact displaced folk psychology in clinical practice to the extent claimed, whether the self-refutation charge hits its target or relies on a category mistake, and whether the summary correctly distinguishes eliminativism from both reductive materialism and behaviorism. Also, consider whether the reference to distributed vector transformations captures Churchland's actual neurocomputational view or overstates the specificity with which current neuroscience can replace folk-psychological explanation.
