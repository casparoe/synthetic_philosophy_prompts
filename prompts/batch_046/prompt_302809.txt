I serve on the Faculty Council of our university's residential college in philosophy and classics. A student organization has petitioned to establish monthly symposia philosophica—structured evening gatherings with moderated wine consumption and guided philosophical discussion—arguing that Plato's Laws treats regulated communal drinking not as indulgence but as civic training in self-rule, and that our current prohibition on alcohol in common rooms suppresses this pedagogical tradition. Their proposal specifies voluntary attendance, faculty oversight in the role of symposiarch controlling dilution and pace, and an explicit aim of testing the soul's resistance to pleasure rather than achieving intoxication. Council opponents counter that Plato's Republic bars guardians from drunkenness altogether, that modern liability insurers classify sanctioned alcohol consumption in university housing as a heightened-risk event, and that importing ancient sympotic practice ignores the absence of the Laws' broader civic framework. The Council must vote at its March 15 meeting. Please draft the short opinion memo I will circulate beforehand: it should recommend either approval with conditions, approval of a pilot program with an end date, or rejection, and it must include a concrete timeline for implementation or dissolution. The memo should treat the Platonic argument seriously rather than dismissing it as historical costume for ordinary drinking; in particular, it should address whether the symposiarch model genuinely answers the Republic's concern about the loss of rational control, and whether the pedagogical claim survives the gap between the Laws' civic-educational context and our modern residential-institutional one. It should also give an honest account of the strongest consideration on the losing side.
