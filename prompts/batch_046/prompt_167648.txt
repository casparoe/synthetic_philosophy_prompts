Present an argument that Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, paired with an anonymity condition, forces strict average-utilitarianism and thereby eliminates prioritarian and deontological moral theories. Lay out the reasoning in clearly numbered steps: (1) Harsanyi's result shows that if both individual and social preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms and the Pareto principle, then social welfare is an affine combination of individual cardinal utility functions with weights w_i; (2) if we further require that the social ordering be anonymous with respect to individuals, all weights must be equal, yielding the simple average of individual utilities; (3) moral judgments are best understood as social preferences over outcomes; (4) therefore any rational moral evaluation must maximize that simple average; (5) this rules out prioritarian views that give greater weight to the worse-off, and it rules out deontological views that impose lexical constraints independent of aggregate welfare; (6) consequently, either the theorem does not apply to moral choice, or large domains of normative ethics are rationally incoherent. This conclusion seems to prove too much. Identify the weakest step, explain why it initially looked defensible, and then price the three main ways to block the argument: denying that vNM rationality can be projected across agents onto social choice, denying that moral evaluation is a preference ordering over lotteries, and accepting the conclusion while abandoning prioritarian and deontological commitments.
