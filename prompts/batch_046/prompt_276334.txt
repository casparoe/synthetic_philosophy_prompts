I am a philosophy professor preparing to host a colloquium podcast series for advanced graduate students and specialists, and next week’s guest is an unnamed contemporary defender of a strengthened version of Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument against moral responsibility. Rather than presenting the infinite regress of self-creation as a bare logical trap, she treats it as revealing a structural feature of agency itself: every attempt to ground moral responsibility in a prior state of character or will merely relocates the problem one level backward, and she deploys this to critique not only libertarian but also hierarchical compatibilist accounts that rely on “mesh” between first-order desires and second-order volitions. She further responds to the familiar social-practice objection—that reactive attitudes and institutional blame might be pragmatically justified even if desert is metaphysically impossible—by sharply distinguishing pragmatic warrant from metaphysical entitlement, arguing that the former cannot survive explicit acknowledgment that no agent is ever truly causa sui. I want a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that move from the internal architecture of her argument to its institutional implications, with each major question paired with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to offer. Specifically, include at least one question probing how her reading of the regress differs from standard interpretations that treat it as merely epistemic or practical; at least one on how she addresses Frankfurt-style cases without collapsing into a generic determinism objection; and at least one on what her view implies for practices of punishment if desert is impossible yet reactive attitudes remain psychologically entrenched. For the follow-ups, anticipate her likely defenses: if she claims that hierarchical mesh resolves the regress, press whether that mesh is itself the product of conditions she did not author; if she invokes social practice, ask whether pragmatic justification can survive the explicit admission that no one genuinely deserves suffering. The tone should be rigorous and conversational, suitable for an audience familiar with the free-will literature but not reliant on citation or bibliographic reference, and please avoid any generic biographical or introductory questions.
