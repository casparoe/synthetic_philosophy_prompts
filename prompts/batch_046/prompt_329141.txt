Read Ursula K. Le Guin’s short story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas through the lens of Mahayana Buddhist ethics, specifically the interplay between upāya (skillful means), prajñā (wisdom as emptiness of inherent existence), and the bodhisattva ideal of non-abiding (apratiṣṭhita). The story presents a utopia sustained by a single child’s abject suffering; some citizens, upon learning this, walk away and live in hardship rather than accept the bargain. I want a reading that takes both the story and the Buddhist framework seriously: does walking away express a correct refusal to instrumentalize sentient suffering for collective welfare, aligning with the bodhisattva’s refusal to enter final nirvana while beings suffer? Or does it betray upāya by withdrawing from the social conditions that make the child’s suffering possible, substituting personal purity for engaged transformation? Further, apply the Madhyamaka critique of svabhāva (inherent nature): if neither the utopia nor the walkers possess an independent essence, does the moral choice collapse into conventional compassion rather than absolute refusal? I am not asking for a simple allegory. Where does the story resist the Buddhist reading—for instance, in the absence of any path that actually frees the child—and what does that resistance reveal about the limits of non-abiding ethics when structural evil has no visible agent? End by saying whether the story adds something to the Buddhist-consequentialist debate that philosophical argument alone misses.
