In a seminar on the ethics of aesthetic evaluation, a colleague dismissed my concern that a painter's documented history of fraud should affect our assessment of her late canvases by saying, "Separate the art from the artist" — as though that phrase alone dissolved the problem. I want to understand what work that slogan is actually capable of doing. Can you lay out, as a menu of distinct precise theses rather than a single interpretation, the different claims "separate the art from the artist" might be expressing in this context? For each reading, indicate whether it amounts to a platitude, a substantive and defensible principle of aesthetic appraisal, or a claim that is actually false; and, crucially, specify whether that particular reading genuinely supports the conclusion that knowledge of the fraud ought not to diminish our judgement of the canvases. I am particularly keen to see distinctions that are usually collapsed kept apart: for example, between (a) the causal claim that biographical knowledge is never necessary for grasping a work's aesthetic properties; (b) the metaphysical claim that an artist's moral character is not itself an aesthetic property instantiated by the work; (c) the normative claim that we ought to bracket moral knowledge as a rule of critical practice; and (d) the independence claim that the work's value is ontologically fixed regardless of its origin. Additionally, please consider how each reading fares against works that seem to resist separation — specifically, canvases whose formal coherence arguably requires the viewer to reconstruct the artist's self-deception as part of understanding the composition. Please say what would change your mind about which of these readings are genuinely available: which argument or piece of evidence would lead you to collapse some distinctions, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail? Include also a brief note on where your disambiguation takes liberties with standard positions in philosophy of art or leans on contested interpretations of the separation debate.
