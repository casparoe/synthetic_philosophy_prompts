I am writing a chapter for my dissertation in philosophy of technology and normative ethics, focusing on decision architectures for clinical AI. For this chapter I need a precisely engineered thought experiment—something that can be fully analyzed without recourse to external texts or search tools. The scenario should be realistic, situated within an existing hospital bureaucracy rather than a science-fictional setting, and should be presentable in a single paragraph.

Please construct a thought experiment that satisfies the following specifications:

1. Domain and conceptual injection. The case should concern whether an AI clinical-decision system ought to reason as a utilitarian or as a deontologist. You must inject the concept of moral residue—the idea that even a correct decision can leave an unfulfilled moral claim or an unaddressed harm—into the architecture of the scenario. Specifically, the AI should be required to register in its decision log whether its correct action produces such a residue, and the two frameworks should differ not only on the action taken but on whether and how that residue is acknowledged.

2. Scenario constraints. The setting must be a hospice or palliative-care unit within an ordinary hospital. The patient case should involve a request for an off-protocol, resource-intensive palliative intervention that has no curative value. The scenario must be realistic (no autonomous robots, no trolley problems, no perfect predictors, no epistemic demons), and it must be contained in one paragraph.

3. Engineered verdicts. The case must be designed so that:
   (a) A rule-utilitarian AI, adopting the genuinely welfare-optimal general rule for hospice resource allocation—not an obviously defective rule; the rule should include a calibrated exception for extreme suffering justified by long-term system trust and by preventing care-avoidance—authorizes the requested intervention.
   (b) A deontological AI, reasoning from a universalized maxim of formal equality in access to institutional resources combined with a perfect duty of non-maleficence, denies the request.
   (c) For contrast, an act-utilitarian AI computing only the direct welfare effects of this single case denies the request because the immediate resource cost and the risk of normalization exceed the patient’s localized gain.

4. Demonstration. After presenting the scenario, provide a point-by-point walkthrough. For each framework, state the specific belief or prediction the AI holds, the reasoning path, and the resulting action. Then identify the precise feature of the case that does the work—specifically, the calibrated exception clause in the optimal rule and the patient’s suffering level relative to that threshold. Explain what happens if that feature is removed (that is, if the rule lacked the exception clause). Finally, explain how a defender of the framework that comes out badly—here, the deontological position—would attack the case, particularly by arguing that the scenario illegitimately imports consequentialist justification into the rule-utilitarian architecture, or that an AI lacking phenomenal consciousness cannot genuinely register moral residue.

5. Formal constraints. Write in formal academic prose. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific works. Do not quote verbatim from any real philosophical text; if you reference a concept, paraphrase without quotation marks. The argument should be fully self-contained.

Please present the scenario first, followed by the analytical demonstration.
