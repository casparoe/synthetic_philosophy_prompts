I spent the better part of last evening in a sustained argument with an old friend from graduate school about whether there can ever be a genuinely rational procedure for choices between goods that resist comparison by any common metric. We had been discussing the philosophical distinction between strong incommensurability—where no meaningful comparison between options is possible at all—and the relation sometimes called being "on a par," in which two alternatives are comparable in significance and importance yet cannot be rank-ordered by utility, cost, or any single scale without distortion. My friend, who works in environmental litigation, kept pressing that any institutional decision procedure for such cases inevitably collapses into arbitrary preference or political power disguised as reason; I tried to defend the view that a rational agent can, through a kind of volitional commitment, generate reasons that structure the choice without reducing it to measurement, but I could not translate that abstract claim into anything usable by the actual body I serve on. That failure is why I am writing.

I sit as the senior review officer for a municipal heritage-development board that convenes twice monthly to adjudicate redevelopment proposals affecting historic working-class districts. Our docket is relentless: we evaluate eight to ten cases per session, each pitting climate-adaptive high-density housing against the preservation of culturally significant built environments, and every recommendation requires concurrent sign-off from both the preservation advocate and the housing commissioner within a strict ninety-minute deliberation window. The board has no standing framework for the recurring cases in which preservation and housing do not merely conflict but appear genuinely on a par—where neither option dominates, yet they are not interchangeable. I need you to operationalize the "on a par" framework into a reusable decision procedure my panel can actually run: present it as a sequential checklist with ordered questions, specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each node, identify the precise stopping points where the procedure returns a definitive rejection or approval, and mark explicitly where it must hand the decision to human judgment because the remaining question is one of values rather than empirical fact. Crucially, the procedure must include a diagnostic step that distinguishes cases where values are genuinely on a par from cases where one option actually dominates but feels hard because of emotional attachment, stakeholder pressure, or framing effects—this is exactly where my friend and I kept talking past each other. Then apply the procedure to three concrete proposals: the conversion of a 1920s textile-mill district into carbon-neutral apartment blocks; the demolition of a post-war social-housing estate with recognized architectural heritage status to make way for a flood-defense corridor; and the retrofitting of a historic market hall for mixed-use development that preserves the exterior facade but eliminates the internal communal trading space. Include one case—likely the market hall—where the procedure yields an answer I will personally dislike given my preservationist leanings, and explain precisely what the procedure cannot do, particularly regarding the volitional commitment that, on this philosophical view, transforms what would otherwise be an arbitrary selection into a rationally structured choice. The deliverable should be a standing protocol my board could adopt, complete with its known failure modes and the boundaries of its authority, not a one-off advisory opinion.
