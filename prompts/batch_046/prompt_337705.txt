I've been going back and forth on James's will-to-believe defense versus Clifford's evidentialism, and I think I've landed on a somewhat niche hybrid: Clifford is basically right for ordinary factual beliefs that sit inside an already-working framework, but James wins for what I'm calling 'scaffolding commitments' — the background assumptions that don't just lack full evidence but actually make it possible for evidence to exist at all, like basic inductive regularity or that other minds aren't zombies. So my view is that there's a real distinction between beliefs that answer to evidence within a framework and beliefs that constitute the framework itself, and only the second category can be adopted on non-evidental grounds without violating the ethics of belief. But I'm not totally sure this distinction holds up under pressure.

What would actually move me away from it? I want the defeaters ranked — not just the standard objections to James about wishful thinking, but specifically whether the framework/content split collapses once you look at actual scientific history, whether Clifford's principle can be restricted to avoid self-defeat without being totally ad hoc, and whether there are clear cases where adopting a scaffolding commitment led inquiry seriously astray. Also, be honest about whether this is just James's position wearing a new hat, and if it is, whether that's actually a problem for a view like this. I was talking about this with friends in a philosophy course and we kept circling whether quantum interpretation debates count as framework-level or just content-level disagreements, which made me think the line might be blurrier than I want it to be. Can you sort out which objections are fatal, which are merely awkward, and what a defender of this split should concede?
