I'm advising a close friend who helps manage a small cooperative investment pool that funds community projects over long horizons. The pool recently faced a decision about whether to commit a substantial portion of its capital to a single venture with a significant risk of total loss but, in success, a return large enough to multiply the pool many times over; the alternative was to keep those funds in safe, low-yield reserves. One of the cooperative's senior members argued that since the pool is intended to operate and reinvest across decades, the rational allocation should be sized to maximize the long-run geometric growth rate of the entire capital base—an approach that, she noted, often recommends staking a larger fraction than casual risk aversion would allow, and sometimes even using leverage, because multiplicative survival over time dominates linear expectation. Another member objected that this treats the pool as if it were the property of one immortal agent rather than a collective of people who can withdraw, be replaced, or suffer personal losses; he insisted that rationality demands maximizing expected utility on each discrete decision, adjusted for the actual risk preferences of the current members, rather than optimizing for an abstract future total. The situation is further complicated by the fact that membership rotates—people join and leave—so the "long run" is not experienced by any fixed set of beneficiaries, which raises the question of whether growth-optimal reasoning applies at all when the agents who bear the risk are not the ones who might eventually reap the multiplied rewards.

My friend wants to know whether this dispute is genuinely about the structure of rationality—whether there is a principled conflict between single-period expectation and multi-period geometric optimization—or whether it is ultimately just a disagreement about risk tolerance expressed in mathematical language. More specifically, if the geometric-growth framework is correct for an enduring institution, does its authority depend on continuity of membership, and does rotating membership undermine or merely complicate its application? Conversely, if the framework is mistaken, what exactly is the conceptual error in treating long-run multiplicative growth as the master criterion for collective decision-making? I should note that I may be illegitimately merging questions about economic optimization with questions about ethical obligation to present members; please push back plainly if my framing conflates categories that ought to stay separate, and do not feel compelled to endorse either partner's position if both depend on a shared confusion.
