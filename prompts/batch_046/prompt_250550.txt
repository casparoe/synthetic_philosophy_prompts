I've been working through Bernard Williams's writings on integrity and moral psychology for a graduate seminar, and I recently came across a summary in an unnamed online essay that strikes me as emotionally compelling but philosophically suspicious. The author writes: Williams's integrity objection is essentially that utilitarianism forces agents to betray their deepest commitments whenever the moral calculus requires it. Consider the case of a chemist who is offered a position developing chemical weapons; he finds the work repellent, yet a utilitarian might argue he should take the job because if he refuses, a more enthusiastic researcher will do greater harm. Williams rejects this reasoning because it demands that the chemist abandon his own ground project of not contributing to weapons research, thereby destroying his integrity. The same structure appears in the familiar case of a husband saving his drowning wife. The husband should rescue her because she is his wife, but if he pauses to consider whether saving her maximizes overall utility, he has one thought too many. From these cases, the author concludes that Williams demonstrates any moral theory requiring impartial justification for our actions is incompatible with having personal projects and commitments at all. Integrity, on this reading, is just the claim that we should never have to defend our partial loyalties before an impartial moral tribunal.

This summary feels right in broad strokes, but I worry it flattens several distinctions that matter. Is it a fair characterization? In particular, I want to know whether Williams frames integrity as protecting something like an absolute entitlement to moral purity, or whether the chemist example is meant to show that utilitarian reasoning alienates the agent from his own commitments rather than that he has a self-interested right to refuse. I also wonder whether the point about one thought too many is meant to forbid all moral reasoning about partiality, or only to show that substituting moral justification for personal attachment undermines the value of that attachment. Finally, does Williams's argument actually extend to all moral systems with impartial elements, or is it targeted more narrowly at utilitarianism's demand for constant maximization? Could you audit the summary claim by claim, separating what is accurate, what is a standard popular simplification, and what is a subtle misrepresentation? I am especially interested in whether a utilitarian could accept a corrected version of the objection—say, by allowing that agents may have protected ground projects—without abandoning the core theory, or whether Williams's critique really does strike at the heart of impartial moral reasoning itself.
