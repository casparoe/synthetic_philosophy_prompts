I am drafting a short essay on state apologies for historical wrongs, and I would welcome critical comments—particularly on conceptual coherence and on whether the central distinction I am drawing holds up under pressure.

The argument proceeds as follows. The standard continuity problem asks whether a present state can apologize for wrongs committed by a prior regime when the legal-institutional entity is not strictly identical. The usual resolution appeals to institutional continuity: if the constitution, legal order, and bureaucratic apparatus persist, then the state is the same moral agent and owes the apology. I want to challenge the sufficiency of this answer by introducing a distinction between institutional continuity and what I call collective agency continuity—the persistence of a shared narrative identity among citizens across constitutional breaks. In cases of radical constitutional discontinuity—say, the transition from an imperial regime to a democratic republic, or from an apartheid state to a post-apartheid constitutional order—institutional continuity is clearly broken. Yet our moral intuition that some form of acknowledgment may still be appropriate does not disappear; if anything, it becomes more urgent precisely because the old institutional order was illegitimate. This suggests that collective agency continuity, not institutional persistence, is doing the real normative work.

But this move creates a tension. If collective agency continuity is sufficient to ground apology obligations, then the state-specific logic collapses into a more general morality of groups: any community with a continuous narrative identity could be required to apologize for its ancestors' acts, regardless of whether it operates through state institutions. Conversely, if we insist that institutional continuity is necessary, then regimes that have genuinely broken with their predecessors—through revolution, dissolution, or constitutional replacement—are categorically off the hook, which seems morally perverse when those same regimes inherit territorial, economic, or symbolic benefits from the prior wrong. To avoid both horns, I propose a conditional account of what I term successor responsibility: a present state bears an obligation to apologize not in virtue of being the same agent, but in virtue of being the primary beneficiary and present steward of the legacy goods produced or preserved by the historical wrong—provided that the wrong is not already adequately addressed by other mechanisms of transitional justice. On this view, apology is not an expression of identical-agency guilt but a recognition of unrectified benefit coupled with a commitment to restructuring the conditions that made the wrong profitable.

I apply this framework briefly to two well-known cases: Germany's acknowledgment of colonial-era atrocities in Namibia, and Japan's various statements regarding wartime sexual slavery. In both instances, I argue that institutional continuity is partial at best—the German Empire is not the Federal Republic; the Imperial Japanese state is not the post-war constitutional order—yet successor responsibility remains applicable because the benefits (territorial integrity, economic development trajectories, diplomatic standing) have not been fully disentangled from the original harms. At the same time, I note a potential counterexample: a revolutionary regime that actively dismantles the economic and territorial structures inherited from the wrongdoer might, on my account, lack the obligation to apologize precisely because it has already severed the benefit link, even if collective narrative continuity remains strong. This strikes me as the right result, but I am unsure whether it renders the account too contingent on empirical sociology.

My specific requests for comment are these. First, is the distinction between institutional continuity and collective agency continuity philosophically defensible, or does it collapse upon analysis—perhaps because institutional forms are themselves constitutive of collective agency? Second, does the successor-responsibility condition genuinely avoid collapsing state apology into general group morality, or does it merely relocate the problem? Third, are there clear cases where a state clearly benefits from a historical wrong but an apology would nevertheless be inappropriate, or where there is little benefit but apology is still demanded? Any suggestions for sharpening the argument or for identifying hidden premises would be greatly appreciated; I am especially interested in whether the framework can accommodate federal or decentralized states where responsibility might be distributed across sub-units rather than centralized.
