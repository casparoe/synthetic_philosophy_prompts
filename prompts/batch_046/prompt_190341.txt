I teach an upper-level seminar on collective rationality and am drafting the final examination: a single 90-minute essay carrying 40 percent of the grade. I want a question on judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma that students cannot answer by merely defining the problem or reciting standard labels. The question should present a concrete five-member municipal advisory board tasked with issuing a unified recommendation on whether to license an AI-driven predictive policing system. Each member holds judgments on three logically connected propositions: (i) the system is empirically unbiased (U); (ii) if unbiased, then licensing is morally permissible (U → P); and (iii) licensing is permissible (P). Specify the members' individual judgment sets—each internally consistent—arranged so that majority vote on U and on U → P both yield acceptance, while majority vote on P yields rejection. Ask students to: (a) map the exact majority profile across all three propositions and pinpoint the logical tension that arises; (b) apply a distance-based aggregation rule that selects the collectively consistent judgment set minimizing the sum of Hamming distances to the five individual sets, identifying which recommendation this produces and whether it respects democratic equality; (c) evaluate a proposed "domain-weighted" compromise in which empirical premises are decided by simple majority but normative conditionals are subject to a principled veto if any member denies them on non-empirical grounds, applying it to this board and arguing whether it collapses into one of the standard methods; and (d) discuss whether the board's charter—requiring both logical coherence of the collective output and equal respect for every member's judgment—can be jointly satisfied by any non-dictatorial aggregation rule over logically interdependent propositions, and if not, which requirement ought to yield and why. Please provide the exam question, a model answer of approximately 700 words, and a marking scheme that distinguishes an A response—which precisely computes or identifies the distance-based outcome, recognizes that domain-weighting collapses into premise-based aggregation here, and engages the impossibility logic descriptively without relying on author-name recall—from a B response that correctly describes the dilemma but treats the aggregation rules only in generic terms without applying them to the specific profile.
