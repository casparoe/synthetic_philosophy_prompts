In a metaphysics seminar, a colleague repeatedly invoked the line "there are no universals, only resemblances" to dismiss questions about whether two red objects share a property in virtue of something genuinely common to both. I need that slogan pulled apart into its precise variants rather than treated as a conversation-stopper. Lay out the distinct theses it could be expressing: the purely linguistic claim that predicates like "red" carry no ontological commitment; the moderate resemblance-nominalist claim that property-talk is grounded in primitive pairwise resemblances without shared entities; the eliminativist claim that all property-ascriptions are literally false; the reductionist claim that universals exist but are identical to sets or classes of resembling particulars; and the stronger variant that resemblance itself is not a universal relation, intended to block the resemblance regress. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a metaphysically substantive and defensible position, or false; indicate whether it actually answers the "in virtue of" question about shared properties or merely changes the subject; and specify which version best supports using the slogan to end debate about common properties. Also address the hidden tension: if resemblance is treated as primitive and unshared, does the view still implicitly commit the speaker to a universal relation of resemblance, and if so does that undermine the slogan?
