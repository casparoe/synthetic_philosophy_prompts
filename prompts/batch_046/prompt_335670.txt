Consider Robert Louis Stevenson’s *Strange Case of Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde* not as a simple moral allegory about dual nature, but as a phenomenological study of disunified agency. Interpret the novella through split-brain neuropsychology and the philosophy of consciousness unity, but apply a specific conceptual refinement: treat Hyde not as a second person but as a “hemispherically dissociated action module”—a behavioral subsystem that generates goal-directed conduct without access to the left-hemisphere narrative center that produces autobiographical memory, linguistic report, and moral justification. On this framing, Jekyll’s written account is not a transparent testimony but a confabulatory reconstruction that suppresses right-hemisphere-initiated actions by attributing them to a named alter ego, preserving the illusion of a single continuous self.

With this lens, examine the transformation episodes, the killing of Danvers Carew, and the final confession-letter. Where does the split-brain analogy illuminate Jekyll’s experience of lost control—does Hyde possess phenomenal consciousness without access consciousness, or is he purely a narrative fiction invented to protect moral self-image? Where does the analogy break because Stevenson assumes a unitary soul that can be “possessed” rather than a divided cognitive architecture? Does the novel treat Hyde’s violence as disinhibited behavior lacking narrative integration, or as the expression of a genuinely distinct moral agent? And where does the text resist the neuroscience—perhaps in the idea that Hyde is ethically worse rather than merely behaviorally unbound?

Finally, say what Stevenson’s novella contributes to the philosophical debate that clinical descriptions of split-brain patients do not: does it dramatize the moral and existential cost of narrative disintegration in a way that makes the loss of unity ethically urgent rather than merely neurologically curious, and does it suggest that moral responsibility requires not just behavioral control but the capacity for a unified autobiographical narrative? I want a reading that is faithful in both directions—neither reducing the novel to an illustration of Sperry-style experiments nor distorting the neuroscience to fit the gothic plot.
