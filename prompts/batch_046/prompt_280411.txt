I've been examining how government climate agencies structure long-range cost-benefit analyses, particularly regarding damages projected for the late twenty-second century. The institutional practice is bifurcated: treasury and budget offices apply declining social discount rates—often derived from observed market returns—to compute present-value equivalents of future climate harms, while the ethical guidelines that inform carbon-pricing mandates and intergenerational equity reviews treat pure time preference as negligible, discounting future welfare only for anticipated economic growth and for uncertainty about whether the harm will materialize. This division seems to presuppose that temporal distance can diminish economic urgency without diminishing moral entitlement, yet the same administrative framework applies both judgments to the identical population of future people. What philosophical commitments does this institutional separation require? Must it assume a two-level theory in which collective decision-makers are permitted rational preferences—such as positive pure time preference—that individual moral agents ought to reject? Or does it instead depend on a metaphysical distinction between predictive modeling, where future outcomes are merely probable, and normative obligation, where they are treated as certain claims upon present action? And if one replaced the underlying view with a unified account—say, one that treats temporal distance as always and only an epistemic or risk-related factor—what parts of the institutional practice would have to be redesigned, and which could remain intact?
