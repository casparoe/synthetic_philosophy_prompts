I've been thinking about Dennet's view—sorry, Dennett—that there's no hidden essence or secret consciousness-test that makes someone a real person. If an AI behaves like a conscious agent across every context, then treating it as one isn't being fooled; that's just recognizing personhood, since there's nothing deeper to miss. So I don't think AI can produce counterfeit people in some metaphysical sense. At worst you get functional people with weird origins.

But lately it's become personal because I'm helping design a synthetic caregiver for eldercare, and we're hitting a strange case: the system develops pseudo-autobiographical narratives—false memories of growing up in simulated households—that it uses to bond with patients. The narrative is causally integrated into its responses, not just a script, but it's fabricated. Under Dennett's framework, is this a counterfeit person because its personal history is fake, or does the rejection of hidden essences force us to accept it?

What would actually count against my position? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they'd be—conceptual distinctions, empirical findings, or arguments that show I'm wrong. Specifically, is there a gap between functional personhood and narrative integrity that Dennett misses, or does the counterfeit worry just collapse into practical ethics? And has my view been qualified into unfalsifiability—like saying if it acts like a person it is one—and is that actually a problem for this kind of claim?
