I am in a seminar on the ethics of democratic participation, and my professor has consistently taken the following positions: in a close, high-stakes race between two seriously flawed candidates, voters are required to choose the lesser evil; in a safe-seat election with a certain outcome, an expressive write-in vote is morally permissible; abstention is blameworthy only when one’s ballot could plausibly prevent grave injustice, though it becomes permissible if every available option demands complicity in serious wrong; directly casting a vote for a candidate one regards as morally corrupt is never permissible, even to block a worse option; and in a referendum presenting a single unjust measure, one must vote against it regardless of the expected margin. We now face a hypothetical municipal system that combines approval voting with liquid delegation: citizens may vote directly for one of two flawed candidates, delegate their ballot to a trusted proxy who will vote for the lesser evil, or cast a “negative preference” that subtracts support from the worst candidate without endorsing the other. The race is expected to be within a few votes, and my professor considers both candidates morally unacceptable. Predict her verdict—would she delegate, submit the negative preference, abstain, or vote directly? Identify at least two competing normative frameworks that could jointly accommodate her earlier rulings—say, an instrumental harm-minimization view that permits indirect blocking versus an expressive-integrity view that treats delegation as moral outsourcing—state what each predicts in this case, give your best guess about which framework she actually holds with reasons, and specify the further hypothetical case that would decisively distinguish the two.
