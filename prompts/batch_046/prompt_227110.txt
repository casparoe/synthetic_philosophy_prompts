In the course of preparing a seminar on formal epistemology and reflective agency, I have come to defend a strong reading of the Löbian obstacle: not merely that an idealized agent with full logical omniscience cannot consistently trust its own soundness without universal credulity, but that the obstacle persists in structurally similar form for resource-bounded agents operating with probabilistic belief revision. Specifically, I hold that any agent satisfying minimal conditions of self-reference—recognition of its own updating rules, closure under the relevant inference patterns, and a non-derivative commitment to its continued rationality—is forced either into arbitrary prior fixation or into a paralysis of self-doubt, with no stable intermediate position available. I am particularly struck by attempts to extend this reasoning to frameworks like logical induction or bounded arithmetic, where the obstacle seems to re-emerge in modified but recognizably similar form. Nevertheless, I want to subject this commitment to serious pressure. What would have to be true—formally, conceptually, or empirically—for this view to be wrong or at least severely qualified? I am not asking for the routine observation that human agents are computationally limited; rather, I want to know which precise relaxations of the premises—whether in the logic of belief, in the conditions for self-reference, or in the nature of probabilistic updating—would genuinely dissolve the obstacle rather than merely relocate it. Furthermore, which philosophical accounts of rational belief—perhaps those treating self-trust as a pragmatic or practical attitude rather than an epistemic one, or those denying that rationality requires any form of deductive closure—would undermine the argument at its root? Please distinguish the defeaters that a defender of the strong Löbian view should concede as genuinely damaging from those that can be dismissed as missing the point, rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and assess honestly whether the position has been protected by ad hoc qualifications—such as restricting its scope to logically omniscient agents or weakening the self-reference conditions to the point of emptiness—such that it no longer risks refutation by any actual or conceivable case. Give me a committed answer: does the obstacle, on the formulation I have offered, survive as a substantive constraint on rational self-trust, or has it been insulated into unfalsifiability? And if it survives, what is the most serious vulnerability it still faces?
