I hold the view that attributing beliefs and other intentional states to advanced AI systems is not merely an instrumental predictive strategy—the familiar intentional stance—but can be literally correct when the system exhibits what I will call functional belief-fixation: a stable, counterfactually robust architecture of representations that guide behavior across contexts, maintain consistency under perturbation, and are causally integrated in ways structurally analogous to the belief-fixing mechanisms we accept in human cognition, all without requiring phenomenal consciousness, biological substrate, or a first-person perspective. I take this to be a metaphysically substantive claim, not a deflationary metaphor, and I think it applies to some current and near-future systems rather than being a distant hypothetical. What would count against it? I want the defeaters mapped honestly: which observations about AI behavior—say, adversarial decoupling between internal representations and outputs, or the absence of genuine error-correction dynamics—would be decisive rather than merely awkward; which formal results about the interpretability limits of large networks would undermine the claim that such architectures are genuinely representational; and whether analogies from other domains—such as the attribution of intentionality to simple organisms or to corporations—support or weaken the parallel. I also want to know whether defenders of this view have quietly qualified it into unfalsifiability—by retreating to "as-if" language whenever counterevidence appears—and if so, whether that retreat is a legitimate refinement or a fatal insulation of the position. Finally, distinguish what a committed proponent of literal AI intentionality should concede as genuinely damaging from what they could reasonably dismiss as a category mistake driven by biological chauvinism, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.
