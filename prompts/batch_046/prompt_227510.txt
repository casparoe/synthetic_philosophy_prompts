I am writing a dissertation chapter on the paradox of tragedy, and I am trying to move past the standard Aristotelian catharsis and Humean conversion accounts by testing whether formal narrative architecture can relocate the paradox from emotional content to structural form. I want to apply this framework to Kazuo Ishiguro’s The Remains of the Day. The novel presents Stevens, a butler whose professional self-discipline requires the systematic suppression of personal feeling, and the reader is invited to experience both the pathos of that suppression and a distinct satisfaction in the narrative’s disciplined restraint. My guiding hypothesis is that the novel dramatizes a kind of structural conversion of sorrow, in which the very architecture of withheld feeling becomes the source of aesthetic pleasure, so that the paradox is not dissolved but displaced onto the level of form. I am particularly interested in whether Stevens’s narrative unreliability operates analogously to the aesthetic distance invoked in tragedy theory, and whether the final movement of the novel—where Stevens resolves to renew his professional commitment despite recognizing the irrecoverable loss of Miss Kenton—forces the reader into an uncomfortable complicity: we admire the elegance of his self-deception precisely as we mourn its human cost. Could you provide a philosophical reading that takes both the paradox seriously and the novel’s specific mechanics seriously, including an honest assessment of where this formalist reading breaks down because the text refuses to let emotional response and moral judgment be cleanly separated?
