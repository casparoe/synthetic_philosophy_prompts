Two friends of mine, Mira and Jonas, got into an argument about whether dreams are experiences in any philosophically serious sense. Mira opened by saying that dreams cannot be genuine experiences, because there is no independent criterion by which a dream report could be verified against an actual occurrence during sleep; the narrative is assembled upon waking, so talk of "what it was like" to dream is at best a retrospective fiction rather than a report of a prior mental state. Jonas replied that this verificationist worry applies with equal force to waking memory—past waking events are also reconstructed through present reports and behavioral traces—so if the argument undermines dream-experience it should undermine waking experience as well. Mira came back with two distinctions: first, waking reports are constrained by intersubjective observation and behavioral consistency, whereas sleep offers no external check at all; second, dreams lack genuine temporal extension, since any apparent "duration" is compressed into an instant of narrative insertion upon awakening, leaving no interval during which a subject could actually undergo anything. Jonas answered that temporal compression is a feature of narrative memory, not proof of ontological absence, and that even on a Dennett-style view in which a story is "played into" consciousness at waking, that playback itself is a mental event with phenomenal qualities. He added that the deeper mistake is tying experience to waking-style sensory integration: REM sleep exhibits rich internal coherence—emotional arcs, spatial navigation, narrative continuity—without external input, so either that internal integration counts as experiential or the functional criterion is being restricted arbitrarily to awake states. They asked me to referee. Please go through their exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, identify what both parties are missing, and indicate whether the dispute needs a different framing of what "experience" requires.
