I am drafting a novel that employs a formal system of emotional analogies modeled on Hofstadter's Copycat architecture. In this system, affective states are arranged in ordered triplets—grief, then anger, then acceptance—so that the narrative can generate analogies by allowing relational concepts such as successor or predecessor to slip, precisely as Copycat resolves letter-string analogies within its restricted microdomain. Within my manuscript, the node grief occasionally slides into longing and at other moments into absence, contingent on narrative pressure; I have taken this variability as proof that emotional archetypes are genuinely fluid rather than categorically fixed. Yet I am troubled by what seems a contradiction: if Copycat produces coherent analogies only because its slipnet is anchored by an explicit conceptual hierarchy and governed by probabilistic codelets that constrain arbitrary drift, then my literary application—which lacks both a formal workspace and bounded codelet dynamics—appears to permit unlimited slippage. Am I correct in concluding that this absence of constraint reveals emotional concepts to have no underlying structure whatsoever, or have I misapplied the microdomain model by assuming that human symbolic interpretation in fiction requires the same architectural boundaries that govern letter-string analogy?
