I have been discussing with a colleague whether “social construction” denotes one phenomenon or a family of them, and I want to see whether my own intuitions about particular kinds can be captured by a single precise principle. Here are my verdicts. First, I take “being a twenty-dollar bill” to be socially constructed in the strongest sense: the physical object is money only in virtue of collective acceptance of institutional rules, and without those rules it is merely paper. Second, I take “being a woman” to be constructed, but in a different mode—not because biological sex is irrelevant, but because the specific clustering of traits, roles, and social positions that defines the kind is maintained by contingent power structures rather than by nature alone. Third, I think “being a refugee under the post-war international convention” is constructed in a legal-institutional sense analogous to money, yet I also judge that the underlying condition of being forcibly displaced from one’s home is not constructed in that same way; the legal kind draws an arbitrary boundary around a real situation. Fourth, I judge that “being left-handed” is not socially constructed: although social norms strongly privilege right-handedness and suppress left-handed writing, the underlying motor-dominance pattern is not brought into existence by collective agreement. Fifth, I hold that “being a microplastic particle”—defined for regulatory purposes as a synthetic polymer fragment smaller than five millimeters—is not a socially constructed kind, even though the five-millimeter threshold is an obvious human convention; the physical particles exist and cause harm regardless of how we classify them.

These judgments feel somewhat consistent, but I am troubled by a tension. In the case of gender and left-handedness, both involve biological substrates that are interpreted and regulated by social norms, yet I classify one as constructed and the other not. In the case of refugees and microplastics, both involve human conventions fixing the boundaries of a kind, yet I treat the legal kind as constructed while treating the physical kind as merely conventionally classified. Is there a unified principle—perhaps turning on whether a kind’s existence conditions, its explanatory power, or its boundaries depend essentially on collective intentionality—that can deliver all five verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If no single principle can accommodate all of them, I want to know which verdict is the outlier and should be overturned. Finally, please apply the principle you defend to a case I have not mentioned: is “being an essential worker”—the labor category that governments defined during the COVID-19 pandemic—socially constructed in the manner of money, in the manner of gender, in the manner of refugee status, or in some distinct fourth way? State the principle precisely, identify the clause that governs each of my five cases, note any verdict it must reject, and explain the prediction for the new case. I am not looking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature; I want the account stated exactly, with its successes and its honest failures laid out.
