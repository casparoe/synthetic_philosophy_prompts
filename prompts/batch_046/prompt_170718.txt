I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist who only started reading philosophy seriously about two years after I stopped working — mostly self-teaching, old library books, that sort of thing. Lately I've become definately convinced by Hofstadter's strange-loop idea: the self, the 'I', isn't some little homunculus or hidden observer, but a self-referential symbol that gets generated when a system's representations loop back on themselves, like a feedback circuit in physics except made of concepts and symbols. The 'I' is just the symbol stuck in that loop, referring back to the very pattern that creates it. I've taken to calling this an 'I-loop' in my own notes, though I'm sure that's not original.

So here's my position: the self emerges from a strange loop of representation — no Cartesian substance, just recursive self-modeling that stabilizes into a symbol that calls itself 'I'.

What would actually count against this? Not the usual handwaving about qualia. I want the hard stuff: which specific phenomona, experiments, or conceptual breakdowns would damage the view? Would split-brain patients reporting seperate streams of awareness be fatal, or could the loop account absorb that? What about severe dementia where the self seems to persist despite massive neural degradation — does that support the loop idea or undermine it? Or animal cognition: if some animals have complex self-models but no symbolic 'I', does that break the account? Rank these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a committed loop-theorist would concede versus dismiss.
