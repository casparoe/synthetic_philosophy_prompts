Write a formal philosophical dialogue between Dr. Yuki Tanaka, who defends an "indexical particularist" reading of Confucian role ethics, and Dr. Samuel Okonkwo, who defends a "transpositional reciprocity" reading. Tanaka holds that moral obligations are strictly indexed to specific relational positions—parent, child, ruler, subject—such that no neutral standpoint exists to adjudicate conflicts between them. Okonkwo holds that the practice of shu (empathic extension) allows agents to translate obligations across roles, yielding coherence without collapsing into universalist ethics. Center their exchange on a concrete modern case: a hospital administrator who is simultaneously the adult child of a terminally ill parent and a citizen protesting a corrupt healthcare allocation policy, and who must decide whether to redirect institutional resources toward private family care. Tanaka should argue that shu fails here because the asymmetry between filial and civic roles makes translation impossible without betraying one role's internal logic; Okonkwo should reply that indexical particularism leads to moral paralysis and that shu operates precisely by recognizing shared vulnerability rather than symmetrical roles. Both should engage each other's actual claims—Tanaka must address whether Okonkwo's appeal to shared vulnerability smuggles in a liberal conception of autonomy, and Okonkwo must address whether Tanaka's indexicality can account for the Mencian intuition that one should rescue a child drowning regardless of relational distance. The dialogue should be equally rigorous on both sides, avoid strawmen, and conclude with both philosophers agreeing on one precise limitation of role-based reasoning in bureaucratic modernity.
