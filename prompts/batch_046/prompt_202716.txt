Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two interpretive camps on Sartre's account of radical freedom and bad faith.

Camp One, the Transcendence-First reading, holds that in Sartre's ontology consciousness is never causally determined by its facticity—whether psychological, historical, or social—and that radical freedom means the pre-reflective cogito retains absolute spontaneity in every situation. On this view, bad faith is essentially flight from this inescapable transcendence: treating oneself as having a fixed essence, as being causally determined by one's past, or as being merely what one's social role demands.

Camp Two, the Situation-First reading, holds that Sartrean freedom is always freedom-in-situation, that facticity is not an obstacle to transcendence but its necessary material, and that describing freedom as radical refers to its quality—its capacity to nihilate and choose—rather than to an absence of constraint. On this view, bad faith is a misrelation to one's situation: either denying one's transcendence within real constraints (cowardice or adopting the spirit of seriousness) or denying the weight of facticity (irresponsible idealism). This camp further holds that structural conditions can render freedom practically null without metaphysically canceling it, and that collective patterns can exhibit bad faith in ways not reducible to individual self-deception.

Produce a single co-signable document with the following sections, in order:

1. Agreed conceptual ground (approximately 250 words). Identify claims about Sartre's framework—specifically the relations among consciousness, facticity, transcendence, situation, and bad faith as self-deception—that both camps can endorse without reservation. Reference Sartre's discussion in Being and Nothingness and related essays on situation and engagement, but do not invoke later commentators or post-Sartrean traditions unless both sides agree they are relevant. Make clear where agreement ends.

2. The precise disagreement, in neutral wording (approximately 300 words). State each camp's position in language that the opposing camp accepts as an accurate rendering of its own view. Focus on two questions: (a) whether radical freedom entails that consciousness can always do otherwise within the same situation, or only that it is never causally determined even when it cannot do otherwise; and (b) whether bad faith is strictly an individual pre-reflective attitude, or whether collective and institutional structures can themselves be said to be in bad faith.

3. The cruxes (approximately 300 words). List two or three sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. The cruxes must include: (i) how to interpret Sartre's idea that human beings are condemned to freedom—whether this means freedom is metaphysically inescapable, or that responsibility for navigating one's situation is inescapable; (ii) the case of a long-term prisoner under constant surveillance who pre-reflectively comes to value the prison's disciplinary routine as self-cultivation—whether this represents an authentic exercise of freedom within constraint, an instance of bad faith, or a situation so coercive that talk of freedom becomes misleading; and (iii) whether a bureaucratic organization that systematically diffuses responsibility for harmful decisions through its protocols can be described as being in bad faith as an organization, or only its individual members can.

4. Test cases and predictions (approximately 350 words). For each crux, specify a concrete case, textual consideration, or argument both camps agree bears on the issue, and record each camp's prediction in advance. For crux (ii), analyze the prisoner's case in detail. For crux (iii), examine whether organizational protocols that deny members' authorship constitute collective bad faith or merely aggregate individual failures. For crux (i), consider whether Sartre's analysis of the café waiter—who adopts the mannerisms of his role—illustrates bad faith about freedom or about facticity, and what each camp expects the correct analysis to be.

5. Residual verbal disagreements (approximately 150 words). Identify any disputes that turn out to be about terminology rather than substance—for example, whether situation is defined broadly enough to include social structures, or whether freedom carries a normative dimension in addition to its descriptive one. Propose clarifying distinctions or terminological adjustments that both sides could adopt to dissolve the verbal component.

The document should be written in formal philosophical prose suitable for co-signature. Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for further reading. Do not ask for quotes from specific editions or page numbers. Avoid invoking niche post-Sartrean theories not standard in discussions of Being and Nothingness. The goal is not to adjudicate which camp is correct, but to produce an exact map of shared ground and precise remaining disagreement, with each side's commitments rendered in wording it would accept.
