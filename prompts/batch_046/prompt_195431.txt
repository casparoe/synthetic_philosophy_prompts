I'm working out my own view on what makes a text actually mean something, and I want a consistency check before I commit to it. Here is where I've landed:

(1) Meaning requires an intending mind: if no one meant anything by a string of words, then there is no real meaning, only marks on a page or pixels on a screen.

(2) Even so, an author can misjudge their own work—if they insist a passage is ironic but the language doesn't support it, then they misremembered their intent, and the text doesn't carry that irony.

(3) There are also patterns—say, a symbol that quietly recurs across a novel—that genuinely belong to the text because the words carry them, even though the author never noticed them and never intended them.

(4) For that reason I think AI-generated writing is semantically empty: there is no mind behind it, and without a mind there is nothing to intend, so any coherence is just statistical echo.

(5) At the same time, I think the best interpretation is often the one that makes the work richest and most coherent—what I'd call interpretive surplus—even when that richness depends on connections the words don't strictly force and that the author never planned.

(6) Finally, I hold that when an author dies, their intentions become fossilized: interpreters can dig up hidden layers that fit that fossil record, but they can't invent intentions that flatly contradict it.

Do these hang together? I especially want to know whether (3) and (5) can survive alongside (1) and (4), or whether they collapse them. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which conflicts only dissolve once you draw the right distinction—perhaps between an author's actual intent and their later memory of it—and how severe is each real tension? For each serious conflict, say what the cheapest fix would be and what giving it up costs me. Then tell me the single revision that would resolve the most damage with the least harm to the rest of the package. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equal.
