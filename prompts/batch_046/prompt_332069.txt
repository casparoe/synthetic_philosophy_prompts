I'm trying to sort out my own judgments about when a society has genuinely collapsed rather than merely changed form, using both standard historical cases and a couple of less commonly discussed ones. Here are my verdicts, which feel mostly coherent but contain at least one tension I can't resolve.

The Late Bronze Age crisis around 1177 BCE counts as collapse: palace economies dissolved, literacy was lost across large regions, long-distance trade networks broke, and there was sharp demographic contraction in several core areas.

The Classic Maya abandonment of the southern lowland cities between roughly 800 and 900 CE does not count as collapse: the population largely persisted in rural and northern zones, the linguistic and religious framework continued, writing was eventually maintained elsewhere, and there was no society-wide demographic crash—only a political and urban reorganization.

Rapa Nui (Easter Island) counts as collapse: despite the survival of the population and the continuity of the language, the deforestation-driven ecological crisis, the cessation of the moai-building tradition, and the apparent social breakdown represent a discontinuous loss of institutional complexity from which the society never recovered its prior form.

The end of the Western Roman imperial administration in the fifth century CE counts as change, not collapse: the Eastern Empire persisted, Roman law and Christian institutions continued, and the populations in Gaul, Italy, and Britain were incorporated into successor kingdoms rather than vanishing.

The shift of the Khmer political center away from Angkor around the fifteenth century counts as change, not collapse: the state relocated rather than dissolved, Khmer culture and population continued with institutional adaptations, and there was no irreversible rupture in the social substrate.

The dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991 looks to me like change, not collapse: the institutional framework was replaced, but the populations, languages, economic networks, and territorial structures largely persisted, and there was nothing equivalent to the Bronze Age loss of literacy or the Easter Island ecological dead end.

Yet this creates a problem. If demographic and linguistic continuity are enough to make the Soviet case and the Maya case "change," then Easter Island—where people and language also survived—should also be "change." I treat it as collapse instead because the loss of complexity seems irreversible and the ecological base was permanently degraded. So perhaps my real principle involves something like reversibility of institutional complexity relative to the remaining substrate's capacity to reproduce it.

Here is the tentative principle I want tested: a society collapses when it undergoes a rapid, irreversible loss of institutional complexity that outpaces the capacity of its remaining population and ecological base to reproduce comparable complexity over time; it is mere change when institutional forms are replaced, relocated, or gradually transformed, but the underlying social, cultural, and ecological substrate retains the capacity to generate comparable or successor complexity.

Please evaluate this principle against my six verdicts. If it captures them all, state it precisely and show which clause handles which case. If it fails, offer either a repaired version or a small set of competing principles (each motivated by the case that drives its key clause), with verdicts on cases I haven't mentioned—specifically, the transformation of the Inca Empire after Spanish contact in the sixteenth century, and a hypothetical society that slowly decentralizes over three centuries with no sharp break but eventually loses metallurgy and writing entirely. For each principle, state honestly which of my verdicts—Late Bronze Age, Classic Maya, Easter Island, Western Rome, Angkor, Soviet Union—it cannot accommodate without revision, and if one verdict must be overturned, say which one and why.
