Write an essay examining whether the legal and moral concepts of genocide can survive the politics of their own naming. Begin with Raphael Lemkin’s original conception—which encompassed cultural, social, economic, and political destruction of groups, not only physical killing—and contrast it with the narrower 1948 Convention definition centered on specific intent to destroy national, ethnical, racial, or religious groups in whole or in part. Analyze the charge that this narrowing represents a form of taxonomic capture: the strategic restriction of a moral category so that states can deploy it against enemies while shielding their own structural, colonial, or systemic violence from the label. Consider the counter-argument that without the intent requirement, genocide dissolves into an undifferentiated concept of mass atrocity, sacrificing the legal precision necessary for prosecution and the moral distinctiveness that makes group-targeted destruction especially grave. Your essay should reach and defend a clear conclusion: either that the legal category’s political malleability has irredeemably corrupted the moral concept, warranting abandonment of the juridical framework in favor of a broader, non-state-dependent moral account; or that the legal and moral dimensions can be rigorously separated, allowing a precise instrument of international law to coexist with a more expansive moral understanding resistant to state manipulation. In justifying your verdict, address whether moral clarity about group destruction genuinely requires legal precision, or whether the pursuit of precision inevitably truncates moral comprehension.
