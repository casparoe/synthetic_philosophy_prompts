I'm taking a seminar on value pluralism and my study group got into a weird argument about whether people with totally different moral worldviews can actually compromise without just betraying themselves. I tried to formalize our debate and now I'm stuck because the chain seems to prove pluralism is basically a myth. Heres the argument we ended up with:

(1) If two agents operate within genuinely incommensurable value systems—say, one holds a strict deontological view that promises are absolute, and the other holds a relational virtue-ethical view that loyalty is context-sensitive—then a genuine moral compromise requires each side to concede something they genuinely value.

(2) A rational concession only happens when the agent recieves compensation that is, by their own lights, at least equal to what they gave up.

(3) But within each framework, values are ordinal and framework-internal; there is no neutral scale that can compare the weight of promise-keeping against contextual loyalty across systems.

(4) So for cross-framework trade to occur, both agents must implicitly treat their framework-specific values as possessing cardinal weights that are intersubjectively comparable—otherwise they couldn't assess whether the deal is favorable, which is what philosophers of compromise call axiological arbitrage.

(5) Once cardinal, cross-paradigm weights are available, we can aggregate them: if framework A's loss of 10 units of promise-value is offset by framework B's gain of 15 units of loyalty-value, and both agents agree on the exchange rate, then a third-party observer could justify forcing the compromise even when one party refuses it, on the grounds of net moral surplus.

(6) But if such aggregation is possible, then the two frameworks are not genuinely incommensurable; they are just dialects of a single underlying axiological calculus.

(7) Yet we constantly see functioning pluralistic compromises—indigenous land-use agreements inside liberal property law, religious exemptions in secular healthcare, etc.—that don't collapse into one system.

(8) Therefore, either these observed compromises are irrational self-betrayals, or the incommensurability thesis is false and all moral frameworks reduce to a hidden common currency.

This feels like it proves too much because I really want to keep (1) and (7)—pluralism is real and people do compromise rationally—but I also want to hold onto (2) and (3). Where does the chain actually break? Is there an equivocation in how rational concession is used between (2) and (4), or am I smuggling a quantifier shift somewhere that lets me jump from individual assessment to third-party aggregation in (5)? I'd rather keep (1) than (8), but I want to know exactly what I'm paying if I reject one of the middle steps, and whether giving up (4) destroys the idea of axiological arbitrage entirely or just limits it.
