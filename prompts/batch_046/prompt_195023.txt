I'm preparing a long-form piece on how philosophical accounts of probability fare when they are forced to guide action in a high-stakes engineering crisis rather than in the seminar room. To that end, analyze the Space Shuttle Challenger launch decision of January 28, 1986, through the lens of frequentism, subjectivism, and propensity theory, concentrating specifically on how each interpretation frames the Thiokol engineers' warning about O-ring failure in cold weather.

The salient facts are well known: prior to the flight, engineers had documented O-ring damage in seven of twenty-four previous launches, with only one of those incidents occurring below 53 degrees Fahrenheit, while the forecast for launch morning was 31 degrees. They possessed no validated physical model that mapped temperature directly onto failure rate, and they faced intense institutional pressure to maintain the schedule. They warned against launch but could not present a conventional statistical demonstration of elevated risk; management overruled them, and the vehicle was destroyed seventy-three seconds after liftoff.

Using the three interpretations, do the following. First, characterize the epistemic situation the engineers actually faced—was it a problem of estimating a long-run relative frequency with an inadequate sample, of updating personal credences in the face of ambiguous priors and sparse data, or of assessing an objective physical disposition of that specific O-ring seal at that temperature? Second, map the reasoning that was actually offered in the teleconference: show where a frequentist account demands statistical significance and large-n data, where a subjectivist account permits belief revision on engineering intuition and prior knowledge of rubber elasticity, and where a propensity account invokes the causal tendency of the material in cold conditions. Say which interpretation best captures the engineers' intuitive warning and which best captures management's dismissal of it as unproven. Third, identify what each interpretation explains well and what it misdescribes or leaves silent—for example, whether frequentism correctly diagnoses the evidential weakness of the engineers' case but offers no decision rule under urgency; whether subjectivism accommodates judgment but risks collapsing into arbitrary prior selection; and whether propensity avoids the reference-class problem only by smuggling in precisely the causal-physical knowledge that was absent. Fourth, ask what the episode demonstrates about each interpretation—whether it functions as a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the theory never anticipated, particularly with respect to single-case predictions made under organizational pressure. Be disciplined in your mapping: specify who counts as the epistemic agent, what counts as the evidence, what counts as the decision option, and say plainly where an interpretation has nothing to say about managerial authority or institutional failure. Do not supply a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; the analysis should stand entirely on conceptual grounds.
