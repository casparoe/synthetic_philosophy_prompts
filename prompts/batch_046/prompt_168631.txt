I serve on our university's Interdisciplinary Accreditation Panel, and after a long argument last night with a friend about Wittgenstein's language games and family resemblance, I am struggling to apply the idea to a concrete vote we face. Our case concerns a proposal from the Department of Cognitive Science for a graduate certificate in Conceptual Architecture, which uses formal semantic analysis and computational modeling to design ethical interfaces for AI systems. The Philosophy Department opposes affiliation, arguing that philosophy requires first-principles argumentation, dialectical justification, and sustained engagement with canonical problems, none of which this program contains. The proponents respond that philosophy has no essential definition — it is held together only by a family resemblance of overlapping practices, from Socratic dialogue to phenomenology to ordinary-language analysis — and that insisting on an essential method is precisely the mistake Wittgenstein diagnosed. They further claim that because language games are anchored in forms of life, institutional classification is always conventional: if the practices of justification in this program overlap sufficiently with philosophical ones, exclusion is arbitrary.

We must vote by Friday on whether to approve the certificate as philosophy-affiliated, deny it outright, or approve it with conditions. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a specific course of action with a clear time frame, treat the family-resemblance objection at its strongest, and explain whether Wittgenstein's account allows institutional boundaries to be drawn at all, or whether attempting to do so commits us to the essentialism he rejected. Be sure to acknowledge honestly the strongest consideration on the side you reject, and avoid simply appealing to departmental authority or tradition.
