You serve on the clinical ethics review board of a large teaching hospital. Every month you must evaluate patient requests for binding advance directives—sometimes called Ulysses contracts—that legally commit a future, possibly incompetent version of the patient to accept or refuse a specific treatment, even when that future self will have every reason, from its own values and circumstances, to reject it. Your board meets for thirty minutes per case; members are clinicians and lay representatives without philosophical training; and your recommendation carries legal weight, so it must be defensible in writing and signed off by a senior clinician.

Ground your work in Derek Parfit’s analysis of the hitchhiker and rational irrationality: it can be rational, all things considered, to cultivate a disposition that produces actions which are, taken in isolation, irrational, provided that disposition is necessary to secure a benefit that could not be obtained otherwise. Translate this into a reusable decision procedure—an ordered checklist or flowchart in words—that the board can apply repeatedly. The procedure should move through three stages in sequence: (1) establishing whether the proposed future refusal or acceptance would indeed be locally irrational from the future patient’s own values and information; (2) testing whether the commitment mechanism is credible enough that the desired overall outcome actually depends on adopting it; and (3) weighing whether the global rationality of cultivating the disposition outweighs the local irrationality it licenses, with explicit conditions for veto.

The procedure must handle hard cases: when the patient’s present values conflict with their documented past values; when the commitment is only partially credible (for example, family members or clinical staff could lawfully override it); and when the locally irrational future action causes harm to third parties rather than being merely self-defeating. Mark explicitly at each stage where judgment cannot be eliminated—where the board must make a substantive ethical call rather than apply a mechanical rule—and note who must sign off on that call.

Apply the procedure to three hypothetical requests:
- a patient with early-stage dementia requesting a binding refusal of life-extending antibiotics once capacity is lost, anticipating that fear will later drive them to accept;
- a patient with a history of severe addiction requesting that, should they relapse and be hospitalised, they be denied opioid pain medication regardless of medical need, knowing that in withdrawal they will beg for it;
- and a case where the procedure yields a recommendation the board will find counter-intuitive—specify the case and explain precisely why the procedure delivers an unwelcome result despite its internal consistency.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of commitment it fails to assess, which philosophical disputes about personal identity and rationality it deliberately brackets, and where a board relying on it alone would go wrong. Write the answer in British English.
