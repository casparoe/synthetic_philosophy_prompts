Consider this line of reasoning from Henry Sidgwick's The Methods of Ethics (1874), in the discussion of whether mere temporal position can justify preferring a nearer good to a more distant one of equal intensity. Sidgwick argues that from an impartial standpoint—treating all moments of one's life as equally part of one's total good—there is no rational ground for discounting future goods merely because they lie in the future. He acknowledges that present desires feel stronger, but insists this is only a psychological fact, not a rational one, drawing an analogy to spatial distance: we would not think it rational to prefer a pleasure because it is physically closer. The rational agent, he suggests, should distribute concern impartially across the temporal span of her life, weighing outcomes according to their intrinsic value rather than their temporal location.

Here is the difficulty I want help with. Sidgwick's framing seems to presuppose an outcome-based conception of intertemporal rationality: the agent is treated as a kind of static aggregator who collects utility across time-slices and applies uniform weights. But this ignores an alternative, process-based conception, in which rationality consists not in aggregating outcomes but in preserving the causal and functional integrity of the decision-making mechanism across temporal stages. On the process view, pure time preference might be rational not as an outcome-weighting rule but as a structural feature of the mechanism—much as a continuous process must weight local inputs more heavily to maintain stable operation over time. Does Sidgwick's analogy with spatial distance actually address this process-based alternative, or does it beg the question by assuming that intertemporal rationality must be evaluated as outcome-aggregation? More specifically, if we take seriously the idea that the agent is not an impartial spectator but a continuously operating mechanism that must preserve its own causal topology, does Sidgwick's critique of pure time preference still hold, or does it merely show that outcome-based aggregation conflicts with local mechanism-preservation?
