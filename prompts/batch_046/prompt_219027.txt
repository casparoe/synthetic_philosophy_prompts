Here's an argument about omissive causation that a study partner built, and I cannot locate the leak. (1) An omission O causes outcome H if and only if: (i) O is the absence of an action the agent had both the capacity and the opportunity to perform; (ii) had the agent performed that action, H would not have occurred; and (iii) there is no closer actual cause that fully explains H without reference to O. Call this the counterfactual-capacity account. (2) The account is meant to apply uniformly across types of omission—direct failures to rescue, failures to warn, failures to acquire the knowledge that would have enabled a rescue, and failures to establish the habits that would have led to acquiring that knowledge. (3) Take a concrete case: a physician, Dr. Aris, omits to prescribe a life-saving drug, and the patient dies. By (1), this omission causes the death. (4) But Aris only failed to prescribe because she had not attended the 2019 conference where the drug was presented. Her failure to attend also satisfies (1) with respect to the death: she had capacity and opportunity, and had she attended, she would have known and prescribed. (5) That failure to attend, in turn, traces back to her failure to maintain her professional calendar, which traces back to her failure to develop organizational habits in graduate school, and further back to her parents' failure to encourage such habits. At each stage, conditions (i) and (ii) are satisfied, and no closer cause screens off the link. (6) So by (2) and (1), every link in this chain is a cause of the patient's death. (7) But now notice that the same structure applies to any preventable harm: every omission that stands in a preparatory chain to any potential rescue generates the same dependency. This means every able person is embedded in countless such chains for countless distant harms. (8) Consequently, the counterfactual-capacity account assigns causal responsibility for nearly every preventable death to an enormous, indefinitely extending network of agents and omissions. (9) To block this, one might restrict (1) to omissions that are "causally adjacent" to H. (10) Yet adjacency for omissions cannot be defined spatially or temporally, since omissions are absences; any non-arbitrary boundary must invoke normative salience—exactly the kind of normative judgment (1) was designed to avoid importing into the causal analysis. (11) So either we accept the unbounded causal network in (8), or we abandon (1) and deny that omissions ever cause harm, which eliminates the direct case in (3) along with the regress. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would prefer to keep (1) intact, but I want to understand exactly what I am committed to if I do—especially about whether preparatory omissions genuinely cause the harm or merely stand in some weaker relation to it.
