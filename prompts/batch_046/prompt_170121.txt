I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics, and in our seminar we've been stuck for months on the quantum immortality and quantum suicide argument without anyone shifting position. One side, working within an Everettian framework, claims that if consciousness requires a continuous physical substrate, then from the first-person perspective there will always be some decoherent branch in which the observer survives a lethal event; this, they say, gives Many-Worlds a distinctive predictive signature—namely, that you should never experience your own death—because the measure of dead branches is allegedly irrelevant to the index of living ones. The opposing side responds that decoherence already suppresses macroscopic interference well before any fatal outcome is resolved, that psychological continuity is not preserved across branching worlds in any sense that matters for personal survival, and that treating "I will find myself alive" as an empirical prediction illicitly conflates epistemic self-location with metaphysical persistence. What makes the stall so frustrating is that both camps accept the same quantum formalism, the same decoherence models, and even the same claim that a macroscopic observer cannot be in a coherent alive-dead superposition; so the dispute is plainly not about the physics.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is partly verbal—turning on whether "survival" means stream-continuity, psychological connectedness, or mere existence of an isomorphic copy—but partly a deeper clash about whether first-person evidential reasoning can ever ground an asymmetry in branch weights. Specifically, I suspect one camp is implicitly treating self-locating credences as if they were objective probabilities over worlds, while the other rejects that move but lacks a shared framework for what counts as a valid prediction when the predictor is necessarily inside the sample space. So my question is this: is the impasse a substantive disagreement about the epistemology of branching, a hidden metaphysical dispute about personal identity disguised as physics, or are the two sides actually answering different questions—one about rational betting behavior across worlds, one about ontology—under the single heading of "quantum suicide"? And given your diagnosis, what if anything would count as progress: a formal decision-theoretic constraint on self-locating beliefs, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the alleged asymmetry, or is the question simply ill-posed in a way that guarantees permanent deadlock?
