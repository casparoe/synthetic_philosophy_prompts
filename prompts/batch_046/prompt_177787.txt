For an 80-minute session with 16 undergraduate philosophy students, design a participatory game that stages the conceptual force of ambiguity aversion underlying the Ellsberg paradox, but restructure the classic urn problem so that participants cannot rely on a pre-memorized narrative. Introduce the following structural twist: the ambiguous option involves a hidden distribution that does not stay fixed; rather, it “responds” to collective inquiry—if the group spends a shared, small token to open a sealed envelope containing the true proportions, the ambiguity collapses into a known risk, but every individual prefers that someone else spend the token. Set up the rules, roles, and point-based payoffs so that participants first choose, under private incentives, between a transparent bet and an ambiguous bet; then, in a second round, discover that they are collectively stuck with the ambiguous bet because no one volunteered the token, even though each would have paid half the cost alone. Provide: (1) the physical or procedural setup (cards, envelopes, assigned roles such as risk-bearers and observers); (2) the timeline and decision points across the two rounds; (3) a debrief that maps first-round choices onto the risk-ambiguity distinction and the second-round failure onto an epistemic coordination problem rather than pure risk aversion, showing participants that their reluctance was strategic ignorance rather than simple fear of loss; and (4) an honest admission of which formal feature of standard ambiguity-averse preferences—specifically their separability from risk preferences in the smooth ambiguity model—the activity necessarily fails to reproduce and the facilitator must supply by direct explanation. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; keep the scenario fully self-contained.
