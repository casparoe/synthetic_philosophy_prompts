I've been tracking a dispute that replays with remarkable consistency across philosophy of science and legal theory: whether Gödel's first incompleteness theorem imposes genuine epistemic limits on domains that are not formal arithmetic—specifically, on predictive cognitive models and on frameworks for statutory interpretation. Proponents, often extending the anti-mechanist analogies developed by Lucas and Penrose, argue that any sufficiently expressive, rule-governed system must harbor true statements that are unprovable within its own constraints, which they take to show that human cognition or judicial reasoning cannot be fully algorithmized. Critics reply that every such extension collapses at the same formal step: the domain in question either lacks recursive axiomatizability, smuggles in an unstated consistency assumption, or conflates syntactic undecidability with ordinary semantic underdetermination. After decades, the exchange feels circular—the same analogies are drawn, the same objections are raised, and neither side addresses the other on shared ground. Is this deadlock substantive—hinging on a hidden disagreement about what counts as legitimate self-reference or about the conditions for meta-linguistic ascent—or is it verbal, with one side using 'incompleteness' to denote structural openness and the other using it to denote logical undecidability? Or are the sides answering different questions entirely, one about logical architecture and one about institutional authority, under the single heading of Gödelian limits? I want a diagnosis grounded in what each camp actually says, not a survey of interpretations, together with an assessment of whether a sharper conceptual distinction—say, between syntactic closure and interpretive openness—could move the debate forward, or whether the very attempt to export Gödel fails because prerequisites like omega-consistency and precise arithmetization simply do not obtain outside mathematics. If the disagreement is bedrock—resting on intuitions about formalization that neither side can argue for—say so, and tell me what, if anything, should count as progress.
