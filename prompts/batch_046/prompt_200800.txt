I am a retired systems ecologist who, since leaving active research, has begun reading seriously in philosophy of mind, and I am preparing notes for a discussion group composed mainly of scientists with philosophical interests. Before presenting, I want a consistency audit of the following commitments, which I take to represent a defensible naturalistic position but which may not cohere as well as I imagine.

First, I am a physicalist about mental phenomena: I hold that any mental property is ultimately constituted by physical organization and interaction, with no need to invoke non-physical substances or irreducibly mental causation. Second, I accept a version of functionalism according to which the mental character of a state depends on its causal and informational role within a system, not on the particular biological substrate that realizes that role. Third, drawing on decades of studying collective behavior, I maintain that ant colonies instantiate genuine distributed information-processing architectures—complete with feedback-mediated regulation of foraging, defense, and resource allocation—that are not reducible to the cognitive capacities of individual ants, and that these architectures are structurally analogous to the dynamics discussed in the Ant Fugue comparison between colony behavior and neural activity. Fourth, despite this, I deny that an ant colony constitutes a single unified mind or conscious subject; I consider it a category error to attribute subjective experience or intentional agency to the colony as a whole. Fifth, I reject both panpsychism and any form of strong emergence that would treat collective mentality as an ontologically primitive addition to the physical world. Sixth, I propose that the boundary of a unified mind requires a specific kind of global causal integration combined with differentiated information processing within a single spatiotemporally bounded system—though I confess I have not precisely formalized how this integration differs quantitatively or qualitatively from that observed in large insect colonies.

My central question is whether these six commitments form a stable package or whether I am implicitly relying on unstated premises to bridge the gap between them. In particular, does my functionalism, together with my acceptance of distributed collective architectures in the third commitment, commit me to attributing at least primitive mental states to the colony? If not, can the denial in the fourth commitment be justified solely by the boundary condition in the sixth without circularity—that is, without defining integration in a way that simply assumes the conclusion? Is there merely an apparent tension between accepting collective information processing and denying collective mentality, which dissolves once we distinguish cognitive architecture from phenomenal unity? Or is there a deeper conflict between my rejection of strong emergence in the fifth commitment and my reliance on an integrative boundary condition in the sixth that seems to do explanatory work I have not accounted for within standard physicalism? Please rank the tensions by severity, identify which commitment is least costly for me to revise given my scientific background, and indicate whether the sixth commitment is performing genuine philosophical labor or functioning as a post-hoc rationalization of the fourth. Please respond in American English, and I would appreciate an audit-style analysis rather than a general lecture on philosophy of mind.
