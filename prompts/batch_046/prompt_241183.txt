my philosophy of science prof has these takes on discovery being simultaneous. Newton/Leibniz: inevitable, calculus was in the air, but the priority fight is sociology not epistemology. Darwin/Wallace: also inevitable, but Darwin deserves the credit cause he did twenty years of empirical grunt work. Bell vs Gray on the telephone: she calls it a fluke, not destiny, and Bell's patent is legal luck. Watson/Crick vs Franklin: she says this isn't even simultaneous invention—Franklin had the data but not the helical model, so it's misattribution, not convergence. Higgs mechanism in '64: overdetermined, five groups in months, so naming the boson after Higgs alone is just a lottery.

here's the new case. in 1981, two logicians—one in Warsaw, one in São Paulo—independantly solve the same open problem in modal logic about the interaction of knowledge and time, using different proof techniques. neither publishes for ten years because they think it's too niche. then in 1995 both submit to the same journal within a week. the Warsaw proof uses frame semantics; the São Paulo one uses algebraic methods. the problem is now named after the Warsaw logician only.

predict what my prof would say: is this inevitable? does the ten-year silence change whether it's "simultaneous"? does the different proof framework mean they discovered different things? lay out the two rules she might be running—maybe "inevitability of the result" vs "credit goes to the labor and framework"—say which fits her earlier verdicts, and tell me what case would actually split those rules. also reach a conclusion about whether we should rename the result.
