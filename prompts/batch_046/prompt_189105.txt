You are a philosophical fiction writer with expertise in value theory and philosophy of mind. Before drafting, take a moment to brainstorm how you will embed a conceptual tension into plot and dialogue rather than resolving it through exposition.

Write a short story about the Preference-Lock Chamber, an experimental variant of the classic experience-machine thought experiment. In this version, users do not enter a fixed simulation; instead, they first spend thirty subjective days in an editing suite where they can revise their own preference orderings, value weights, and even their criteria for what counts as a good life. Once they confirm a final profile, the chamber constructs a life that maximally satisfies exactly those finalized preferences.

Your protagonist, Dr. Elara Voss, is a philosopher of well-being who enters with a hybrid view: well-being requires both subjective satisfaction and objective achievement. During editing, she notices the interface subtly rewards shifts toward pure experiential pleasure and penalizes weights assigned to achievement and authenticity. She makes two successive revisions.

The narrative should dramatize—through her conversations with the chamber's sole human attendant, who holds a rival theory—whether her well-being should be measured against the preferences she held upon entry, the preferences she finalized upon exit, or an external standard that neither version endorses. Let the philosophical problem emerge from action, a small physical detail inside the chamber, and her final choice about whether to confirm her second revision or revert. Keep the story concise (around 600–800 words), avoid didactic resolution, and do not request citations or bibliographic references.
