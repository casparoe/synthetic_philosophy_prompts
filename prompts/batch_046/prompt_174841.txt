I've noticed a recurring pattern in philosophy-of-science discussions about scientific progress. A speaker will defend theory T over rival R by claiming T is "closer to the truth" or has greater verisimilitude—sometimes citing correct predictions, sometimes structural resemblance to reality, sometimes explanatory breadth. Yet when pressed on what exactly makes one false theory closer to the truth than another—whether it is the ratio of true to false consequences, similarity in law-structure, or some other metric—the criteria shift. If T is challenged on false predictions, verisimilitude is said not to depend on prediction count; if R is praised for structural similarity, empirical success is suddenly the relevant measure. The concept functions as a comparative ranking without a fixed basis, allowing a narrative of progress to be protected while the standard of comparison is adjusted case by case. I know Popper introduced the term and that formal accounts have been proposed, but I am asking about this specific methodological pattern: using truth-approximation as an intuitive, multi-factor judgement that changes its ground to shield the preferred theory. Does this have an established name—perhaps a recognised fallacy or failure mode distinct from ordinary conceptual vagueness—or should it be treated as something else? If there is no established term, how might one define it precisely, with clear boundary conditions separating it from legitimate pluralism about what makes science progress?
