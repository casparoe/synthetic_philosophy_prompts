I'm preparing a proposal for a six-month dissertation prospectus fellowship in moral philosophy, and I'd like to turn a half-formed idea into a structured research plan with a precise question, a realistic method, and an honest assessment of risk. The standard literature on Confucian role ethics—whether the hierarchical interpretation organized around the five cardinal relationships, the harmony-centered reading, or the virtue-cultivation approach—treats moral identity as constituted by positive relational obligations that bind the agent to specific others. What these readings systematically overlook, I suspect, is the conceptual possibility of what I'll call interstitial refusal: a mode of role-occupancy in which an agent withholds the performance expected of a recognized social position not to escape the role, but to preserve its moral integrity from corruption. Consider the minister who refuses a ruler's command precisely to maintain the ministerial obligation to correct the ruler, or the child who declines to obey a parent in order to prevent the parent from accumulating moral failure. Existing accounts tend to treat such cases either as covert appeals to an external moral standard or as simple role-failure; I want to ask whether Confucian texts contain internal resources for treating structured refusal as a form of role-performance itself, rather than as its negation.

The precise research question is this: does Confucian role ethics contain the conceptual architecture to legitimate structured, role-preserving refusal without collapsing into either legalist coercion or modern rights-based individualism, and if so, does that architecture depend on a metaphysics of relational harmony or can it stand on purely relational grounds? The existing positions leave this open because they either assume that all refusal is rebellion against hierarchy (the hierarchical reading) or absorb refusal into a broader virtue of benevolence that erases its specific logic (the virtue-cultivation reading), never treating refusal as having its own ethical structure within the role. My approach would combine close conceptual analysis of conflict passages in the Mencius and Xunzi—particularly those addressing minister-ruler and parent-child tension—with a comparative framework drawn from relational ontology, asking whether the refusal described is genuinely internal to the role or smuggles in an external criterion. Month one: map every significant instance of refusal in the relevant chapters and classify them according to whether the text frames them as failure, strategy, or an ethically distinctive category. Month two: reconstruct the implicit normative structure that would make refusal a role-obligation rather than a role-violation. Month three: test whether this structure requires cosmological backing—appeals to Heaven, harmony, or the Mandate—or can be defended on purely relational grounds. Month four: draft the core argument and assess failure conditions. A successful deliverable would be a 40-to-50-page paper arguing that interstitial refusal is a coherent, textually grounded category within Confucian ethics; failure would be finding that every textual case reduces either to strategic self-preservation or to an appeal to transcendent authority, leaving no genuinely ethical middle ground. The central risk is that "interstitial refusal" is my own anachronistic invention with no real foothold in the texts; I need an honest assessment of whether a semester's work can establish textual presence or only speculative coherence, and what the fallback thesis would be if the evidence proves thinner than hoped. Please write this as a formal two-page proposal: state the question with determinacy, explain the gap left by current interpretations, lay out the month-by-month method and the alternative approaches it rejects, specify exactly what success and failure look like, and address scope—whether this can be executed without deep philological training and what adjustments would preserve the project if the textual base is weaker than anticipated.
