Following a long and somewhat fractious argument with a friend over dinner about whether framing claims in universal terms automatically grants them epistemic dominance over locally anchored ones, I have been trying to articulate exactly where the disagreement lies. My friend maintains that stripping indexicals and context-sensitive markers from an ascription is merely a logical maneuver—widening scope without altering authority—whereas I keep feeling that in actual deliberative practice this "pure universal ascription" functions as a mechanism of dominance: it makes opposing, situated claims sound deficient rather than genuinely different. We went back and forth on whether this dominance is built into the logic of quantification, produced by institutional power, or just an illusion of linguistic convention, and we never resolved whether universal framing can ever be separated from an asymmetry in whose voice gets to define the terms of disagreement. Because I am currently enrolled in an upper-level seminar on philosophy of language and social epistemology and need to prepare for assessment, I would like you to design a take-home final exam prompt that puts students precisely in this tension—without allowing them to retreat into generic summaries of "universalism versus particularism."

Please construct an exam essay question appropriate for a three-hour, 2,500-word take-home response worth 40 percent of the final course grade, aimed at advanced undergraduates. The prompt should present a constructed scenario: a deliberative assembly in which one faction articulates its normative commitments through "pure universal ascription," explicitly removing agent-relative, culturally indexed, and perspectival qualifiers, while other participants are structurally required to respond within frameworks that retain those markers. Ask students to analyze whether this framing asymmetry produces epistemic dominance that is genuinely justified, merely rhetorically effective, or structurally coercive, requiring them to distinguish carefully between (i) logical universality as a matter of quantificational scope, (ii) epistemic dominance as a justified authority relation, and (iii) ascription universality as a social-linguistic practice of indexical stripping.

Together with the question, provide a model answer that identifies the specific conceptual moves a top-tier response must execute—such as separating scope from authority, diagnosing the indexical-stripping move, and assessing whether universal framing can be decoupled from power asymmetries—along with a marking scheme that assigns weight to each move and lists identifiable errors that should cost marks (for instance, conflating universal quantification with epistemic superiority, treating all local ascriptions as automatically valid without argument, or importing standard moral-universalism debates without adapting them to the specific mechanics of ascription framing). In your framing and commentary, clearly separate what is settled about the logic of universal quantification from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of language and social epistemologists—particularly the claim that stripping indexicals itself generates dominance—so that you do not present that contested thesis as established consensus. The prompt should be formal, polished, and sufficiently elaborate to stand alone as an examination document.
