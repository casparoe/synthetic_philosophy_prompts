In my reading group for the archaeology of societal change, we have reached an impasse that feels increasingly structural rather than evidential. On one side, colleagues working on the Late Bronze Age Eastern Mediterranean and the Classic Maya lowlands argue that we should speak of collapse: they point to the rapid abandonment of palace-centered administrative systems, the loss of specialized literacy, and sharp demographic contractions in core regions, treating these as the failure of a specific organizational form. On the other side, scholars of Rapa Nui and post-classic Maya rural settlement emphasize continuity—populations persist, kin-based networks adapt, and practices like the Birdman cult or rural maize cultivation replace earlier state-level rituals—arguing that labeling these shifts "collapse" smuggles in a normative commitment to urban complexity as the only legitimate form of social life. We all accept the same stratigraphic and osteological data; we disagree about whether the dissolution of a centralized hierarchy without the extinction of its population counts as the death of a society or its metamorphosis.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not a hidden empirical dispute—no one expects a new radiocarbon date to resolve it—but a disagreement about the identity conditions of a society itself. The collapse camp seems to treat a society as essentially its institutional architecture, so that institutional decoupling—the separation of administrative, military, and ritual hierarchies from the populations they governed—is tantamount to societal death, even when local subsistence regimes merely shift from tribute-based redistribution to household-level production. The transformation camp, by contrast, treats societal identity as anchored in demographic and cultural continuity, which makes institutional decoupling a change in governance form rather than a collapse. If this diagnosis is right, then Easter Island is the perfect boundary case: moai production and forest cover both collapsed, yet the Rapa Nui population endured and reorganized its ritual economy. But I am unsure whether this is genuinely a substantive metaphysical disagreement about social ontology, a verbal dispute over whether "collapse" should carry normative weight, or two research programs answering different questions—one about the resilience of state-level institutions, the other about the adaptability of local populations—under a single heading.

Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? I want you to engage the concrete positions rather than survey the literature, and to tell me whether progress would require a formal distinction—say, between institutional decoupling and subsistence regime shift—a normative argument about whether complexity loss is intrinsically harmful, or an acknowledgment that the question is ill-posed because "society" does not have the kind of unified identity conditions that would allow it to either fail or survive. Given your diagnosis, what would actually move the debate: a new conceptual framework, a change in the unit of analysis, or an argument that no resolution should be expected?
