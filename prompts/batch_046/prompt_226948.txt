okay so i just made a big career switch that involved signing a non-compete and now i'm weirdly obsessed with the idea of "burning bridges" as a rational strategy. i was reading about commitment races -- you know, like when companies race to build irreversible infrastructure so the other side has to concede, or countries making military commitments they can't back down from. and i started thinking: if binding yourself first is rational in a strategic standoff, shouldn't the same logic apply inside your own head?

here's the argument and it's driving me nuts because the conclusion sounds insane. (1) in any commitment race, the agent that makes the strongest irreversible commitment first gains the bargaining advantage, because the other party knows they can't be pushed around. (2) your present self and your future self are always in a kind of standoff over what values and preferences to keep -- if your future self might change its mind, that's like facing an opponent who hasn't committed yet. (3) therefore a rational agent should try to "win" this internal race by making every current preference maximally irreversible: not just signing a contract, but literally altering your psychology so you can never change your mind about career, relationships, or moral beliefs. (4) but that can't be right -- we think it's virtuous that people grow and change, and we don't praise someone for destroying their own autonomy. (5) so the analogy must break somewhere.

where does it break? is it that (1) only applies between separate agents with conflicting payoffs, and applying it to different time-slices of one person is a category mistake? or is (2) wrong because your future self isn't an "opponent" but part of the same utility function? i really want to keep (1) since it feels true for actual strategic situations, but maybe i'm smuggling in something about intertemporal bargaining that doesn't belong. also, is there a specific name for this kind of overextension -- taking a strategic concept about external bargaining and applying it to personal identity formation? i don't need a bibliography, just want to know which premise is the weakest link and what rejecting it costs me. definatly don't want to lobotomize my free will just to win an argument with myself.
