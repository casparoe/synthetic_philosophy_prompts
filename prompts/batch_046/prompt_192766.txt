I've been working through Machiavelli's The Prince on my own, and I'm trying to pin down the actual rule he uses to separate real virtù from mere luck. As I understand his track record, he praises Cesare Borgia for doing everything a prudent ruler should do—using force and fraud like the lion and fox, adapting constantly to shifting circumstances—yet Borgia was ruined not by any failure of his own but by sudden fortune: his father died and he fell ill at the worst possible moment. At the same time, Machiavelli denies Agathocles any glory or true virtù even though Agathocles seized and held power for decades through considerable skill and cruelty, apparently because Agathocles acquired power through outright crime and betrayal rather than through necessary founding violence. He excuses Romulus for killing Remus because Rome needed a single ruler and the act served the public good. And he keeps warning that princes who inherit power without earning it live entirely at fortune's mercy.

So his earlier verdicts seem to fit more than one principle. One rule might be about adaptability: virtù is the capacity to respond to fortune, regardless of how you came to power, which would praise Borgia and perhaps Agathocles for their competence but condemn inherited princes for lacking it. Another rule might be about the moral quality of acquisition: violence is excusable only when it founds a lasting state for the common good, which excuses Romulus but condemns Agathocles, leaving Borgia in an ambiguous middle since he served a pope rather than founding his own state. A third might weigh outcomes against fortune's interference: Borgia failed, but Machiavelli insists that was fortune's fault, not his.

Given that, consider two new figures. The first, call her V, never inherits power and never uses open violence. Instead she slowly rewrites legal codes through technical loopholes, eliminates rivals through bureaucratic exile rather than murder, and builds institutions that survive fifty years after her peaceful death in bed. She faces no sudden reversal of fortune; her state outlives her precisely because she engineered it to run without her. The second, call him W, inherits power but immediately demonstrates brilliant foresight: he restructures the military and tax systems, adapts his security to survive two assassination attempts, and governs with the flexibility Machiavelli praises—yet he is eventually overthrown by a foreign invasion that no one in his era could have anticipated.

Would Machiavelli say V possesses virtù? Would he say W possessed it but was overcome by fortune, or that he lacked it because he could not prevent the invasion? I am less interested in the bare answers than in which rules generate them. There seem to be at least two competing frameworks—one focused on whether power was acquired through legitimate or necessary means versus self-serving fraud, and one focused solely on whether the ruler can adapt to and master fortune regardless of origin. Which of V or W would best force those rules apart? And if you were recommending a verdict for consistency with Machiavelli's own precedents—say, judging a modern reformer who transforms a state through procedural manipulation rather than open force, leaving stable institutions behind—which principle would Machiavelli actually be adopting, and what closer analog from his own cases would justify it? Finally, what single further case—perhaps one involving a ruler whose origin was ambiguous and whose encounter with fortune was partial—would most clearly reveal which rule he really runs?
