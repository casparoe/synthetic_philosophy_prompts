As a retired experimental physicist who has lately taken up philosophy of mind, I have been formalising an intuition about symbolic causation through a model I call the careenium: a substrate of interlocking tracks that guide inscribed spheres, or simmballs, whose printed symbols specify the curvature of the next channel. Together with a colleague I constructed the argument below, but its conclusion—that mental causation is impossible and that intentional psychology should be eliminated—seems far stronger than the premises justify.

(1) In any careenium, if the symbol on a simmball determines the track curvature, then altering only that symbol (while holding mass, shape, and material constant) alters the substrate path. (2) In observed careenia, such symbol changes do alter the path, with no accompanying change in the sphere's physical properties. (3) Hence symbols literally push the substrate around, exercising causal power independent of their physical realisation. (4) The brain is a careenium: neural activation patterns function as simmballs, and their propositional content specifies synaptic pathways. (5) By parity of reasoning, mental content literally pushes neural substrate, rendering the physical properties of neurons causally redundant with respect to behaviour. (6) If neural properties are redundant, neurophysiological explanation ought to be eliminable in favour of intentional psychology. (7) Yet neurophysiology is not eliminable and explains behaviour more reliably than folk-psychological attribution. (8) Therefore mental content does not push neural substrate, the brain is not a careenium, and mental causation is impossible altogether.

I should be most reluctant to abandon (3) and (4), as they preserve the idea that symbols are genuinely causally efficacious. Please identify the flawed step, explain why it appeared sound, and indicate what rejecting it costs—whether that means accepting causal overdetermination, embracing epiphenomenalism, or weakening physicalism. Present your answer in British English, as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
