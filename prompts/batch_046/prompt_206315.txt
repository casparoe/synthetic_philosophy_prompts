I'm preparing a discussion for a graduate reading group that moves past the textbook version of Maxwell's demon—where an external intelligence sorts molecules—and instead asks what physical constraints apply when the "demon" is itself an embedded subsystem made of the same particles it sorts, with finite resolution and no external memory bank. Before I commit to one framework, I want the full specification: what desiderata must any adequate theory of information-thermodynamics for embedded agents actually satisfy?

Please lay out the requirements list, with the motivating case or distinction behind each. The theory must explain why erasing a record carries a thermodynamic cost (the Landauer limit) while acquiring one need not, using Szilard's one-molecule engine as the baseline case; it must respect the difference between information physically present in correlations and information accessible to an agent with measurement resolution bounded by thermal noise; and it must handle autonomous agents that have no external observer—systems where local interactions themselves perform sorting, so that any "knowledge" is internal to the dynamics. Crucially, I want two less-standard requirements included: first, the theory should address whether maintaining counterfactual representations—branching decision paths that are evaluated but never actualized—imposes an entropic cost analogous to erasure, since an embedded agent might compute over unrealized futures; second, it should clarify whether forgetting or decoherence within the agent changes the objective thermodynamic entropy of the environment it interacts with, or only its epistemic state, with the case being a demon that loses its record while the molecules remain sorted.

For each desideratum, flag whether you take it to be non-negotiable or negotiable if it conflicts with others—particularly whether the counterfactual-branching requirement and the epistemic/physical-entropy distinction can be jointly satisfied without treating unrealized computations as physically instantiated. Then, briefly, score the main families—Shannon-information accounts, Landauer-style computational thermodynamics, and quantum-information treatments—against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves rather than on the scoring, since the point is to know what we are asking of a theory before we pick one. The purpose is explanatory adequacy for embedded, resource-bounded agents, not for ideal laboratory observers.
