Question: How do hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy interact in macro-historical explanations that invoke path dependency and critical junctures to account for institutional change? Specifically, evaluate whether describing an early critical juncture as probabilistically overdetermined—in the sense that several institutional outcomes were genuinely available—protects such explanations from either bias, or whether it merely displaces them.

Student answer:
Hindsight bias describes the tendency, once an outcome is known, to regard it as having been inevitable. The narrative fallacy is our inclination to weave discrete events into smooth causal stories, often obscuring randomness. Path-dependent explanations identify brief critical junctures—moments when contingent choices establish self-reinforcing trajectories. Once locked in, these trajectories can make an outcome appear structurally inevitable, which is essentially hindsight bias operating at the macro level.

If a historian describes the critical juncture itself as probabilistically overdetermined—meaning that several outcomes were genuinely possible—this seems to block hindsight bias by denying inevitability at the origin. Nevertheless, it does not defeat the narrative fallacy. Even with an overdetermined juncture, the historian must still decide which antecedent conditions are causally relevant and which are mere background. Because this selection is typically guided by knowledge of the result, the narrative fallacy is not eliminated; it is simply relocated upstream, from the path between junctures to the framing of the juncture. Consequently, path-dependency models reduce hindsight bias but leave the narrative fallacy intact, merely shifting its locus.
