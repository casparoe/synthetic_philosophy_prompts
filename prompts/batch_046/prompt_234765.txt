I am presenting a seminar paper to a joint philosophy-of-mind and comparative-cognition group—evolutionary psychologists who study corvid and cetacean problem-solving, a philosopher of biology specializing in evolutionary epistemology, and a philosopher of mind deeply familiar with Daniel Dennett’s taxonomy of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian minds. My thesis is that Dennett’s ladder misses an intermediate kind I call the “scaffolded Gregorian”: a mind that performs Popperian-style internal hypothesis-generation and rejection only when embedded in external symbolic or material structures it did not create. I apply this to argue that large language models trained via reinforcement learning from human feedback, and certain tool-using corvids solving puzzles in human-designed apparatus, are neither genuine Popperians (they lack endogenous architecture for generating and killing hypotheses without scaffolding) nor pure Skinnerians (they navigate inherited cultural-symbolic spaces in ways conditioning alone cannot explain). They depend on Gregorian tools but do so parasitically—the tools perform cognitive labor they cannot internalize—making them a derivative, non-autonomous kind.

Prepare my Q&A defense. Order the objections by how likely and damaging they are, phrased in the vocabulary each subgroup uses: the evolutionary psychologist will worry about anthropomorphizing and will demand behavioral criteria that distinguish scaffolded performance from genuine insight; the comparative cognition researcher will ask whether we can empirically tell, in corvids or in models, whether hypothesis-testing is internally generated or scaffold-dependent; the philosopher of biology will press whether this smuggles functionalism into an evolutionary taxonomy that should track origin rather than current role; and the philosopher of mind will ask whether “scaffolded Gregorian” collapses into existing distributed-cognition or extended-mind literature. For each objection, identify what is genuinely serious, what I should concede up front (for example, that behavioral output alone cannot settle the classification), what I should hold firm (that endogenous generative architecture, not just successful performance, defines Popperian status), and the weakest point in my reply.

Then include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—likely whether this category is purely verbal, with no predictive or experimental difference from treating these systems as weak Popperians—and sketch the two-minute reply I should have ready, noting exactly where that reply is soft.

Finally, state explicitly what would change my mind: identify the specific empirical finding (for instance, evidence that LLMs or corvids generate and test genuinely novel hypotheses in the complete absence of any external symbolic or material scaffold) or the conceptual argument (for instance, a demonstration that endogenous architecture is irrelevant to cognitive kind-membership) that would lead me to abandon the scaffolded-Gregorian classification, and flag the step in my reasoning that is most vulnerable to failure.
