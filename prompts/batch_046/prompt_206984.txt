Here are my intuitions about personal identity seen through the lens of anatta, and I'd like to know what principle they add up to, if any.

When someone slowly replaces every belief and memory over twenty years through therapy and learning, but the body stays continuos, I think eventually it's not the same person - there's no mental thread tying the two ends together.

But if the same body and brain stay intact but Alzheimer's wipes almost every memory and personality trait, I still think it's them, just hollowed out. The causal chain of the body seems to count for something.

Then there's a meditator who reaches deep anatta: the feeling of being a subject vanishes entirely. When they come back, I think it's the same meditator, but during the state there wasn't a self present at all. So the absence of self-experience doesn't destroy identity across time.

But in a brain-split case where both hemispheres survive in different bodies with full consciousness, I think there are now two people, and neither is the 'real' original in some priviledged sense. It's not like the original died and was replaced by strangers, though.

There's a tension: in the first case I care about mental continuity, in the second I ignore mental loss, in the third I say self-feeling doesn't matter, and in the fourth I reject a single thread altogether. Could there be a principle - maybe that identity is just the pattern of upadanna, or clinging, that binds the skandhas - that saves all four? Or do I have to drop one?

Please tell me what principle or small set of candidates captures this, with the case that motivates each clause, what it predicts for a case I haven't listed (say, a perfect mental copy uploaded into two bodies at once), and which of my verdicts it can't accommodate without being stretched too thin. Also please say what would change your mind - which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.

Write in British English please - and don't worry if there's the odd typo.
