I spent last evening in a long, unresolved argument with a friend about evolutionary explanation. She defended the view that the Gould-Lewontin spandrels critique has been effectively neutralized: apparently non-adaptive traits are almost always either hidden adaptations at a different level of selection, pleiotropic effects locked in by selection for robustness in the underlying developmental architecture, or traits stabilized because selection acts on the covariance structure of development itself rather than on the trait directly. On her account there are no genuine spandrels left—every useless-looking feature is either a misidentified adaptation or an unavoidable architectural constraint that was itself selected. I want a stress-test of this position. What observations, theoretical results, or conceptual distinctions would decisively count against it? Which would be merely awkward? What would its own defenders concede as genuinely damaging—has the view been insulated into unfalsifiability by expanding “indirect selection” without limit, and if so, is that a problem for a claim of this kind? I also want an honest assessment of whether the position predicts anything its rivals do not, and whether it risks collapsing into the very adaptationism Gould and Lewontin attacked. Lead with your bottom-line judgment in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating the question.
