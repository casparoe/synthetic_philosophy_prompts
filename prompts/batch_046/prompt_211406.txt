Question: Derek Parfit uses the teletransporter to argue that bodily continuity is not necessary for what matters in survival; what matters is psychological continuity with the right causal mechanism (relation R). Now imagine a malfunction: the scanner creates two replicas on Mars. Each possesses roughly 70% of the original’s psychological traits, and the two replicas overlap with each other by about 40% of those traits—so neither is fully psychologically continuous with the original, yet together they cover most of his mental life. If relation R requires psychological continuity, does either replica survive? Can the original survive in some distributed fashion across both? Discuss whether this variant undermines Parfit’s argument, or whether it merely shows that survival can come in degrees.

Student answer:

Parfit’s teletransporter shows that bodily continuity is unnecessary for survival, because the Mars replica has identical psychology. In the malfunction case, Replica A and Replica B each share about 70% of the original’s psychology, so both stand in relation R to him. Because psychological continuity is transitive, if both are continuous with the original then the original survives in both—this is essentially Parfit’s fission case, except that the branching is incomplete. The 40% overlap between the replicas means they are not continuous with each other, confirming that one original can yield multiple survivors. Since the scanner is the right kind of cause, relation R holds fully for each replica, though only to a 70% degree. Consequently neither replica is strictly identical to the original, but each is a partial survivor. This does not undermine the teletransporter argument; it reinforces that bodily continuity is irrelevant and that psychological continuity, even when divided, is what matters.
