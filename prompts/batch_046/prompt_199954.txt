I am designing an exercise for a graduate seminar in epistemology that moves beyond the usual expository formats. We have been discussing the conflict between the KK principle—roughly, that knowing p entails knowing that one knows p—and the rejection of luminosity, the view that mental states are not necessarily transparent to the subject, especially under conditions of imperceptible change. The classic illustrations feel overused to my students, so I want a prompt that forces engagement with the structure of the problem rather than its standard examples, using a scenario that is concrete but not the familiar warming-room case.

Please write a sestina—six stanzas of six lines, followed by a three-line envoi that incorporates all six end-words—narrated from the perspective of a sleep-laboratory researcher who is monitoring her own hypnagogic state in a room whose temperature and light are being adjusted so gradually that her instruments and her introspection fall out of sync. Choose six end-words that denote epistemic or phenomenological conditions, such as cold, feel, know, sure, warm, and doubt, and arrange them so that the sestina's mandatory rotation tracks an ascending attempt to apply the KK principle: each stanza should try to secure second-order knowledge of the state named in the previous rotation, only to have the imperceptible shift undermine it. The envoi should collapse all six terms into a single moment, showing that the hierarchy of self-knowledge folds back into first-order experience without reaching a luminous base. The result should be philosophically precise enough that a specialist recognizes the anti-luminosity structure, yet poetically coherent for a reader unfamiliar with the debate.

Present the poem in full, followed by a section titled Commentary on Form and Argument (approximately 250 words) that identifies which end-words represent first-order, second-order, and collapsed states; explains how the sestina's cyclical repetition enacts the iterative demand of the KK principle and its failure; and notes why the envoi's compression offers a formal analog to the denial of luminosity rather than a resolution of it. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; refer to philosophers by name only if necessary, and avoid relying on specific page numbers or publication dates.
