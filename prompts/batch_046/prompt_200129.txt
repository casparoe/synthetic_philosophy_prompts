I'm drafting a handout for a graduate seminar I am running on the mechanics of moral change, and I want a decision tree that sorts explanatory theories of how practices such as chattel slavery and duelling actually disappeared — not by asking students to name philosophers, but by tracing the commitments that genuinely separate the accounts. The tree should open with one diagnostic question answerable without knowing the literature: whether the decline of such a norm is best explained by a shift in first-order moral beliefs, by a restructuring of the social costs and incentives that made the practice sustainable, or by a prior collapse in the conceptual framework that rendered the old norm unintelligible. From there, each branch should pose one further concrete claim on which positions diverge — for instance, whether honour functions as an independent reason-giving mechanism or merely as a coordination device; whether rational argument can drive change before interests have already shifted; whether abolition requires participants to come to see the old norm as not merely wrong but conceptually incoherent; and whether there is a meaningful distinction between the end of slavery and the abandonment of duelling in terms of institutional enforcement versus peer-to-peer status competition.

The leaves should include Bernard Williams's thesis about duelling's decline being tied to changes in the social technology of honour and Kwame Anthony Appiah's account of moral revolutions as involving both argument and honour, but they should also accommodate unnamed combinations that the logical space permits — for example, a view on which interests shift entirely without conceptual change, or one on which conceptual change is necessary but never sufficient without a parallel collapse in coordination incentives. Where two named positions land on the same leaf, flag them explicitly; where a combination of answers seems to correspond to no defended view, note briefly whether it looks incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Do not frame nodes as "do you agree with Williams?" and do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Please also say where your reasoning is most vulnerable: which branch distinction you regard as sharpest in practice but might collapse under scrutiny, and what kind of historical counter-example — say, a case where argument clearly preceded any interest-shift, or vice versa — would cause you to rearrange the tree.
