As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I have noticed a recurring configuration that feels adjacent to the liar paradox but does not match the textbook descriptions I have seen. I want to know whether it has an established name in theories of truth, or whether it marks a genuinely distinct phenomenon that needs its own characterization.

Here are two concrete instances. First, in formal verification, one can build a module M that exports a proof of its own consistency, but the proof succeeds only if the proof checker applies a local meta-rule—something like "exclude M's own axioms from the base logic"—that is not part of the system's ordinary evaluation procedure. M never says "this statement is false"; instead, contradiction is avoided only by selectively suspending the rule that governs how M's outputs are evaluated. Second, in a distributed consensus protocol, nodes A and B each sign a message attesting that the other node's signature is invalid, with neither node referring to itself. The protocol does not crash; it settles into a loop in which A's validity depends on B's and vice versa, and the evaluator can assign both-valid or both-invalid depending purely on evaluation order. In both cases there is no direct self-reference, no explicit contradiction, and yet there is no globally stable assignment of truth or validity without an ad hoc procedural exception.

The pattern I am trying to isolate is this: a cyclic or meta-level dependency in a truth- or validity-assignment system that avoids direct self-reference and avoids outright contradiction, but still prevents any stable global assignment unless an evaluator introduces a local, non-systematic exception. How exactly does this relate to the standard liar paradox, to revision-theoretic treatments of truth, and to coherence theories? Is it best classified as a distributed variant of the liar, as a problem of hierarchical or procedural ambiguity, or as something with its own structure?

Please take a committed position. Either identify the nearest established concept, explain precisely how these cases differ from it and from each other, and defend why those differences do or do not undermine the identification; or, if nothing established fits well, propose a precise name and definition, specify the boundary conditions—when it collapses back into the standard liar, when it collapses into mere ambiguity or underdetermination, and when it remains genuinely distinct—and defend that taxonomy. I am not asking for a balanced survey; I want a definite diagnosis with explicit reasons.
