I'm moderating a panel next month on pluralist epistemologies in science studies, and before I defend my own framing of Jain anekāntavāda in public I need to know whether the architecture actually holds together. Over the past year I have settled on five commitments that I present as a package, but I have not stress-tested them against one another. First, I read anekāntavāda as an ontological claim rather than an epistemic modesty: reality genuinely possesses infinitely many non-reducible aspects, and the naya doctrine tracks co-existing standpoints, not merely our cognitive blind spots. Second, this pluralism is not "anything goes" — a standpoint that asserts a purely material soul, for instance, is not just partial but erroneous, because it denies properties that other valid standpoints correctly affirm. Third, I still maintain that reflective equilibrium is the right method for refining beliefs in metaphysics and ethics. Fourth, I extend ahiṃsā into cognition: forcing a single unified narrative onto a multi-aspect reality is a form of epistemic violence. Fifth, I hold a minimal moral realism — there are mind-independent moral facts about basic harm, and they are best approached by disciplined movement between standpoints rather than from any single naya.

Do these commitments hang together? Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision if something must give? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damaging. Lead with the bottom line — is the set jointly tenable, and if not, what collapses first — then work through the audit in order. Keep the response under 350 words. I am not asking for a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; I need the internal logic checked, not documented.
