Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers of biology—Dr. Aris Thorne and Dr. Lena Vasquez—about whether DNA is literally a semantic code rather than merely a causal template. Thorne holds a teleosemantic position: DNA sequences carry genuine representational content with normative conditions of success and failure, analogous to how mental representations can be true or false, because evolutionary selection establishes proper functions that fix what a sequence is supposed to produce. Vasquez holds a causal-structural position: DNA is a chemically stable correlation mechanism between nucleic-acid sequences and amino-acid outcomes; talk of “coding,” “information,” and “misrepresentation” is a useful but ultimately anthropomorphic projection onto a regularity that has no intrinsic intentionality and no normative failure conditions beyond ordinary chemical malfunction.

To keep the exchange from recycling standard slogans, ground it in the following injected framing: the two are discussing a hypothetical synthetic molecule—call it “X-polymer”—engineered de novo with no evolutionary history, inserted into an artificial ribosome, and capable of directing the synthesis of one specific functional protein. Thorne should use this case to argue that representational content depends on present functional role, not on causal history: if the X-polymer is designed so that its “proper function” is to produce protein P, then its producing Q is genuinely a misrepresentation, not merely a broken machine. Vasquez should counter that without a selection history there is no distinction between malfunction and misrepresentation—producing Q is just a different chemical outcome, and calling it “false” illegitimately imports intentional vocabulary where only causation operates.

The dialogue should contain real back-and-forth, not parallel monologues: each speaker must respond to the other’s actual point about evolutionary history, synthetic origin, or the nature of normative failure.

Prescribe the response in exactly these sections, in this order:
1. “The framing scenario” (~100 words): Set the conversational scene and state the core disagreement concisely, introducing the X-polymer thought experiment.
2. “The dialogue” (~600–800 words): The exchange itself, with direct engagement on at least three specific issues—(a) whether evolutionary history is necessary for representational content, (b) whether the synthetic X-polymer can genuinely “misrepresent” or only chemically fail, and (c) whether the “code” metaphor implies an arbitrary symbol-to-meaning mapping like language or a non-arbitrary functional mapping like a blueprint.
3. “Analytical postscript” (~150 words): Assess whether Thorne and Vasquez are having a substantive disagreement or largely talking past each other, identify the precise conceptual crux that would settle the matter, and say briefly what kind of consideration—empirical, conceptual, or analogical—would actually move the debate.

Write formally, do not include bibliographies or reading lists, do not cite specific authors or texts in quotation marks, and do not depend on obscure technical literature beyond general philosophy of biology and philosophy of mind.
