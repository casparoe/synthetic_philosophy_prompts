He claims that any identification of goodness with a natural property commits a distinctive error—if goodness were identical to pleasure, then asking whether pleasure is good would collapse into the tautology of asking whether pleasure is pleasure. Yet, he insists, that question remains genuinely open and substantive, which demonstrates that goodness is not reducible to the natural property in question. The conclusion he draws appears to be realist in character: moral judgments attribute an objective, non-natural quality to states of affairs, and this quality is grasped through direct recognition rather than derived through inference.

Another colleague insists that Moore is making only a semantic point about the limits of definitional analysis and has said nothing about the psychology or metaphysics of moral cognition. I am unsure how to adjudicate between these readings, and I worry that I may be importing assumptions into the text.
