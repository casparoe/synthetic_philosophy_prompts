As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun auditing graduate seminars in moral philosophy, I have agreed to serve as an external grader for a colleague's course on normative ethics, and I find that I need assistance calibrating my judgment on one particular examination response. The examination presented students with the following problem. Consider Dr. Amara Okonkwo, an epidemiologist who, after completing a full career and formally retiring, now devotes roughly ten hours per week to reconciling discrepancies in a public-health dataset maintained by an international non-governmental organization. She holds no contract with the organization, receives no compensation, and her labor, though valuable, is not necessary for the dataset's basic functioning. Some recent commentators have proposed a "post-obligatory" framework, according to which retired professionals inhabit a moral space in which only perfect duties and genuinely supererogatory acts apply, on the grounds that all role-based imperfect duties have been fully discharged. Evaluate this claim. In your answer, (a) explain whether the absence of a professional role-obligation automatically removes an act from the category of imperfect duty; (b) assess whether the post-obligatory framework accurately captures the moral structure of sustained voluntary scientific labor after retirement; and (c) discuss whether a substantial cost-to-agent is a necessary condition for supererogation, using Okonkwo's situation—where the work is intellectually engaging rather than burdensome—to illustrate your reasoning.

The student submitted the following answer. Because imperfect duties such as beneficence and the cultivation of one's talents are structurally role-relative, they apply only where a specific social or professional position creates a latitude of fulfillment. Once Dr. Okonkwo retired, her epidemiological role-obligation was fully discharged, so she no longer operates within any imperfect duty pertaining to public health data. The maintenance of the dataset therefore cannot be an instance of fulfilling an imperfect duty. Instead, it occupies the space beyond all duty: either it is supererogatory, given its social value, or it is merely permissible, like any leisure pursuit. The post-obligatory framework correctly describes this moral topology. Once role-based obligations are completed, the remaining architecture consists solely of perfect duties and actions that exceed them. Okonkwo's contribution is unambiguously supererogatory, because it produces substantial public goods without being demanded. One might object that general human duties of solidarity remain, but these are themselves imperfect duties tied to one's status as a capable agent, and in retirement that status has been dissolved. Finally, supererogation requires not only that an act be non-obligatory and valuable, but that it involve significant cost-to-agent. Because Okonkwo spends only ten hours weekly and finds the work stimulating, the personal cost is minimal. Consequently, the act fails the necessary cost condition and should be classified not as supererogatory, but as an admirable exercise of post-professional autonomy—morally praiseworthy, yet falling within the broad latitude of permissible retirement activity.

Please assign a specific grade—using either a letter or a numerical scale—and justify it with point-by-point comments that address parts (a), (b), and (c) separately. I am especially concerned with whether the student has conflated role-specific obligations with the broader class of imperfect duties, and whether any inference about the cost-to-agent condition treats a typical feature as a definitional requirement. I do not want a bibliography or suggested readings; I need only a calibrated, point-by-point assessment that I can compare with my own tentative evaluation.
