I'm running a grad seminar on moral responsibility and I'm trying to figure out where the live philosophical work actually is, instead of the same circular debates everyone has been having since the nineties. You know Strawson's Basic Argument -- the regress that kills true moral responsibility because you can't be causa sui for your own nature or character. I'm not asking you to defend or attack it. I'm asking what we should actually do with it if we take it seriously.

Specifically, I'm seeing three directions people are circling but nobody is ranking them properly. First, there's this notion of "structural self-formation," where you try to stop the regress not by claiming you cause yourself, but by arguing that earlier choices structurally ground later character in a non-causal way that still preserves some kind of responsibility. Second, there's just accepting the impossibility result and rebuilding blame, punishment, and praise practices around "stewardship without desert" -- keeping social coordination but dropping the metaphysical story about deserving suffering. Third, there's extending the regress to artifical agents and collective entities, where there's no unified self to begin with, so the argument either collapses or becomes something totally different.

Of these three, which two deserve serious investment -- from a research group, a career, a grant -- over the next decade, and which one is mostly a dead end or intellectual distraction? Rank them and defend the ranking with explicit criterias: tractability, how much other philosophical or practical work depends on resolving it, and whether it actually changes anything outside the seminar room. And tell me what would flip your ordering -- what argument or empirical finding would change your mind?

Don't give me a survey or a bibliography. I want your committed judgment: take a definite stance, own the trade-offs, and don't hedge with "it depends on your metaethical framework." I'm writing a long-form piece and I need a clear agenda, not both-sides-ism.
