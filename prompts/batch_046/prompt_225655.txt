Rank the following five stances on the tension between the KK principle—that knowing p entails knowing that one knows p—and the anti-luminosity argument that knowledge of p can obtain even when one lacks second-order knowledge of it—ordered from the most defensible stable position (rank 1) to the least defensible (rank 5). For each stance, assign a tier S, A, B, C, or D, and in exactly one sentence explain whether it avoids the margin-for-error slippery slope without collapsing into an ad hoc boundary restriction.

The five stances are:

1. Unrestricted rejection: deny luminosity entirely, accept that knowledge is not luminous even in ordinary high-confidence cases, and treat KK as false across the board.

2. Safety-only preservation: retain KK only for safe knowledge, where safety means the belief could not easily have been false; allow near-boundary cases to fail both luminosity and second-order knowledge, but insist that central cases satisfy both.

3. Epistemic thermostat: luminosity holds only when an agent’s second-order confidence exceeds first-order confidence by a fixed resolution delta; as first-order confidence declines near a boundary, the required gap widens, dynamically blocking the slippery slope at the margin while preserving KK well inside the safe zone. Evaluate this constructed calibration mechanism on internal coherence, not on prior literature.

4. Closure denial: reject the closure principle that knowledge is closed under known entailment; on this view one can know p and know that p entails q without knowing q, breaking the derivation of second-order knowledge from first-order knowledge plus logical awareness, leaving luminosity intact but leaving KK unsupported.

5. Indexical luminosity: the truth of S knows that S knows p is indexed to a context parameter specifying the granularity of knowledge attribution; in fine-grained contexts the attribution fails near boundaries, in coarse-grained contexts it succeeds, and there is no single context-independent fact of the matter about luminosity.

In your ranking, attend specifically to which stances remain vulnerable to a modified margin-for-error argument that targets the boundary conditions of their proposed restriction, and which ones pay an unacceptable intuitive cost elsewhere—such as rendering ordinary self-knowledge impossible or multiplying epistemic contexts beyond parsimony.
