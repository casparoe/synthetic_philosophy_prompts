After a long argument with a friend about whether the brain is literally a computer, I want to audit the analogy she kept pressing. She used it to defend a strong computationalist claim: that mental states are identical to computational states realized in neural hardware, that consciousness is substrate-independent in the way software is independent of silicon, and that explaining cognition requires only specifying the right algorithm regardless of biological implementation. She mapped neural networks onto circuit architectures, synaptic weights onto stored programs, and action potentials onto binary logic gates, concluding that once we have the correct functional description the brain's wetware is as replaceable as a laptop's chassis.

Can you lay out the correspondence explicitly—what in the brain plays the role of the CPU, the memory bus, the instruction set, the compiler, and the operating system, and where that mapping is strained? In particular, separate the disanalogies that are merely cosmetic (energy source, physical size) from those that actually undermine her conclusion: for instance, the absence of a clear hardware-software distinction in neural tissue; the brain's continuous analog dynamics, including glial modulation and volume transmission, versus discrete symbol manipulation; and the fact that brains develop through embodied self-organization rather than being engineered to run pre-specified code. Does the analogy quietly import features—deterministic stepwise execution, addressable memory registers, centralized error-correction protocols—that brains lack? And if those missing features are essential to the kind of computation that could support consciousness, does her substrate-independence conclusion survive? Finally, is there a better analogy—perhaps the brain as a prediction-error-minimizing dynamical system, or as a neuromorphic analog processor—that captures its information-processing without importing the misleading architecture of a von Neumann machine?
