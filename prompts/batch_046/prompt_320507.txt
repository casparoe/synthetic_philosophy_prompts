I've been trying to formalize the split-brain argument for dual consciousness and keep ending up with a conclusion that applies to every intact brain, and I can't locate the faulty hinge. (1) If two cognitive subsystems lack transitive informational access—each can represent states that the other cannot represent in the same format—then they ground distinct subjects of experience. (2) In split-brain patients after corpus callosum section, the left hemisphere can verbally report a seen object while the right hemisphere, guiding the contralateral hand, selects a matching object that the left denies seeing; informational access is not transitive across hemispheres. (3) So split-brain patients contain two subjects. (4) But in normal brains, dorsal and ventral visual streams compute spatial location and object identity with minimal direct cross-talk and different representational formats; each stream can encode features the other does not access in the same way. (5) The corpus callosum is a high-bandwidth bridge with inherent transmission latency, not a mechanism that creates transitive access ex nihilo. (6) Therefore, by (1), normal brains also contain multiple subjects—probably more, given other modular boundaries. (7) This collapses the distinction between split-brain and normal cognition and makes unitary personhood an illusion everywhere. I want to preserve (1), because integration should be doing real explanatory work, and I want to preserve (3), because the behavioral dissociation is empirically robust. But (7) feels like a reductio rather than a discovery. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost—especially if I deny (4) or (5) and must explain why callosal connectivity is categorically different from intrahemispheric modular separation?
