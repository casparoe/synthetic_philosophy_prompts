I am the executor of the estate of my late uncle, Dr. Elias Vorn, a mineralogist with a private interest in the philosophy of science. Among his possessions is a hermetically sealed vault containing forty crystalline specimens. According to handwritten instructions verified by his attorney, half are fragments he recovered from a meteorite fall documented in 1978, and half are laboratory syntheses he produced decades later, engineered to match the originals at every detectable chemical, isotopic, and structural level, including artificial traces of cosmic weathering he deliberately reproduced. The vault employs a single-use atmospheric seal; once breached for direct analysis, the internal argon environment is lost and the comparative scientific value of the contents is permanently degraded. Non-invasive imaging and remote spectroscopy have been attempted by the receiving museum, but the synthetic and authentic groups remain indistinguishable by any method that does not open the seal.

The donation agreement requires me to list each specimen in the catalog as either authenticated extraterrestrial material or as terrestrial synthesis, for curatorial, legal, and research-access purposes. Because the information that would determine the correct assignment is locked behind a seal whose breach destroys the very conditions that make the collection valuable, I must make the classification without access to the deciding evidence. I want to honor my uncle's work without committing the archive to a false ontology, but I also want to avoid inventing distinctions that have no operational consequence for future study. Is there a principled reason to treat the two groups as belonging to different ontological categories when every foreseeable scientific use depends only on their shared observable properties, or should I regard the vault as a single unified class? Furthermore, if a later researcher discovers that a particular piece was misassigned in my catalog, does the mistake lie in the record or in the world itself? I am not seeking a reading list; I need a framework for thinking about identity, responsibility, and classification when the facts that ground them are permanently inaccessible.
