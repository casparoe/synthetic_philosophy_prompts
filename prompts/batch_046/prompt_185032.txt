As a novelist drafting a manuscript about a secluded agrarian commune, I have invented for them a civic ritual they call “specular assembly”: whenever a moral or practical disagreement arises, the disputants do not speak in deliberation but instead compose a silent tableau vivant—arranging bodies, props, and light into a single frozen image meant to embody the conflict’s ethical architecture. The town then renders judgment by evaluating the scene’s aesthetic coherence and emotional legibility rather than by weighing reasons. In tracing my characters across several years of this practice, I have observed a peculiar deformation. One elder, who once wept openly at a neighbor’s loss, now pauses before any sorrow to consider whether the posture will read clearly from the viewing platform; a young mother confesses she can no longer distinguish her protective anger toward a rival from the satisfaction of knowing that her fury produces a striking diagonal composition. The community’s leaders now ask whether they ought to preserve the ritual for the harmony it generates, or dismantle it in favor of unmediated speech. As both author and moral witness, I seek not practical governance advice but a philosophical diagnosis: what precisely is being corrupted when a people learn to experience their own passions primarily as material for spectacle? Is the damage reversible through reform—say, by forbidding citizens to witness their own tableaux, or by restricting the practice to ceremonial domains—or is it inherent in letting representation precede experience? I wish to understand whether my characters’ loss is a contingent failure of moderation or the inevitable consequence of substituting performed image for spoken reason.
