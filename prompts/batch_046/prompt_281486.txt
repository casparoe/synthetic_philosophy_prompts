I've been trying to formalize a risk-sensitive decision rule for long-horizon planning, and I keep running into a version of Pascal's mugging that seems to show risk-weighted expected utility collapses into universal paralysis unless we smuggle in an ad hoc cutoff. Here's the argument.

(1) Define risk-weighted expected utility (RWEU) for an action A as V(A) = ∫ U(x) dμ(x) · R(x), where R(x) is a risk coefficient that scales the contribution of outcome x by its tail distance from the median—so catastrophic outliers are weighted super-linearly relative to standard EU.

(2) If R is continuous and unbounded below as loss severity increases, then for any action A there exists a logically possible but empirically uncalibrated outcome x*—say, a hidden logical contradiction in physics triggered specifically by A, or a simulation reset contingent on A's execution—with arbitrarily severe negative utility and arbitrarily small but non-zero epistemic weight.

(3) Because the space of such logically possible catastrophes is infinite and each can be assigned a non-zero prior in a logically omniscient framework (or even just a non-dogmatic prior over logical possibilities), the total risk-weighted disutility summed over all such x* is unbounded below for every A.

(4) Therefore V(A) = −∞ for every action A.

(5) But rational agents do perform actions, so at least some actions must have finite, comparable values.

(6) So RWEU with continuous unbounded R must be false, or it must be restricted to outcomes that carry empirical, frequency-calibrated probabilities—i.e., we must exclude "merely logical" catastrophes.

(7) Any such restriction, however, requires drawing a boundary between empirically grounded risks and logical possibilities that the risk coefficient R itself does not justify; without an independent theory of logical uncertainty or prior restriction, the boundary is ad hoc.

(8) Hence RWEU either paralyzes all choice or depends on an unjustified epistemic filter.

The conclusion feels wrong—there should be a middle path that lets risk-sensitivity matter for climate policy or AI deployment without requiring me to treat "the universe is a Boltzmann brain that punishes this specific computation" as a live risk. But I can't locate the slip. Is it in (2), where I conflate logical possibility with non-zero epistemic weight? In (3), where I assume infinite summability? Or in (7), where I claim the filter is ad hoc—maybe there's a principled coherence argument (like updatelessness or a logical-induction prior) that justifies it?

If I have to defend one premise, I'd keep (1) and (5): risk-sensitive weighting should be real, and paralysis is unacceptable. That means I'm probably sacrificing either (2) or (3) or (7). What's the standard taxonomy here—do risk-sensitive theorists bite the bullet and accept paralysis for extreme tails (the "skeptical" position), restrict the domain of R via an empirically calibrated prior (the "pragmatic" position), or deny that logical possibilities enter the integral at all (the "structuralist" position)? And what's the cost of each?
