Question: Explain David Lewis's account of convention in Convention, focusing on (a) the three conditions he gives for a regularity to be a convention, (b) why common knowledge matters, and (c) how he thinks conventions can arise and persist without any explicit agreement. Then briefly discuss whether his framework can accommodate norms that evolve gradually over generations rather than being established all at once.

Student answer:
David Lewis defines a convention as a regularity R in behavior within a population P that solves a recurrent coordination problem. For R to be a convention, everyone in P conforms to R, everyone expects everyone else to conform, and everyone prefers to conform on the condition that others do. There's also an alternative regularity R' that could have solved the problem, which shows the choice is arbitrary. Common knowledge is crucial because it's not enough that each person knows others conform; each must know that the others know, and so on, or else someone might deviate thinking they're the only one who knows. Lewis distinguishes conventions from mere habits because conventions depend on this mutual expectation and the preference for coordination.

As for how conventions arise without explicit agreement, Lewis thinks they get established by precedent and salience. Once a pattern is noticed, people follow it because they want to coordinate, and it becomes self-reinforcing. He also allows for conventions to evolve through replicator dynamics, where behaviors that yield higher payoffs gradually replace others in the population, so a norm can shift slowly over generations without anyone deciding to change it. This means his framework can handle gradual evolution, since the replicator dynamics just update the regularity R over time while keeping the common-knowledge structure intact.

One thing to note is that Lewis requires conventions to be Pareto-optimal: if a regularity makes everyone worse off, it can't be a convention because people wouldn't prefer to conform. So bad norms can't be conventions on his view.
