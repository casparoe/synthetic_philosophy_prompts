I am writing from the perspective of a retired experimental physicist who, after several decades in laboratory design and instrumentation, has recently begun studying philosophy of religion and the philosophy of science with some seriousness. I have become particularly struck by A.J. Ayer’s verificationist framework—specifically, the claim that statements which cannot in principle be subjected to empirical verification are not merely false but cognitively meaningless—and I find its application to assertions about life after death to be both rigorously consistent with his broader logical positivism and, if pursued without any moderating instinct, rather absurd in its practical consequences. I would like you to compose a satirical grant proposal, written in the deadpan register of a formal scientific funding application of approximately 600 to 800 words, addressed to an imaginary interdisciplinary review board. The proposal should apply Ayer’s verification principle with complete literalness to empirical investigations of post-mortem consciousness: near-death experiences should be treated as unverifiable measurement noise lacking intersubjective reproducibility; the applicant should outline a ‘double-blind resurrection protocol’ complete with statistical power calculations for detecting survival; and there should be explicit operational criteria specifying exactly when a claim about an afterlife achieves ‘cognitive significance’ rather than functioning as an expression of emotional attitude. The humor must arise from extending verificationist logic into a domain where it manifestly overreaches—do not misrepresent Ayer’s actual commitment that unverifiable metaphysical claims lack factual meaning, but exaggerate the bureaucratic and methodological consequences of taking that commitment seriously within a modern research infrastructure. Maintain the conventional structural sections of a grant proposal—abstract, background and significance, specific aims, methodology, anticipated outcomes, and ethical considerations—without breaking the formal voice or winking at the reader. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or footnotes directing to external sources, and avoid placing anything in quotation marks that derives from an actual published text unless you have fetched and verified the exact wording; paraphrase freely where appropriate. The intended reader should understand both the philosophical target and the scientific form well enough to recognize that the proposal’s absurdity lies in the principle’s overextension rather than in any caricature of empirical practice itself.
