I'm taking this jurisprudence course and also interning at a legal-tech startup, so the Hart-Fuller debate has been living rent-free in my head lately. At work they demoed this thing called an adaptive covenant system — smart contracts that automatically rewrite thier own dispute-resolution terms based on real-time sentiment analysis of arbitration outcomes. Basically the code learns what counts as 'fair' from data and updates itself, so there's no single legislator writing the rules and they change in ways that aren't really prospectively announced.

That got me thinking about what Hart and Fuller would make of it, since both died long before this kind of tech existed. Hart always insisted on a kind of seperate thesis between law and morality, with law being valid through a rule of recognition regardless of content. Would he say these adaptive contracts are still law, as long as courts or some authority recognize the algorithm's outputs? Or does the fact that the system is literally optimizing for a moralized variable like fairness collapse the distinction he wanted? And Fuller — with his eight principles of internal morality, especially prospectivity and generality — this seems like it violates both, since every contract gets bespoke rules that shift retroactively based on new data. But maybe I'm missing something: could there be a reading where Fuller's internal morality is actually satisfied because the algorithm is consistent and non-contradictory, even if it's not human-readable?

I'd love someone to kind of project both thinkers onto this scenario. What would Hart say about whether algorithmic self-amending contracts count as law in his framework, and where exactly would Fuller locate the failure of internal morality — or would he think it fails at all? I'm mostly trying to figure out if my intuition that this breaks both theories is right, or if one of them actually handles it better than I think.
