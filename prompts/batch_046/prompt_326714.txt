In methodological debates over whether history should be explained by covering laws or by narrative structures, colleagues often invoke the line that "history doesn't repeat itself, but it rhymes." They use it to block demands for law-like generalizations — for instance, to reject Hempel-style deductive-nomological explanations of macro-events, or more recent mechanistic accounts that require invariant causal regularities across cases — while still allowing that analogical reasoning has some legitimate place in historiography. I want to know exactly what work that slogan could be doing. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses it might express, ordered from the most anodyne to the most radical? Specifically: (1) the purely pedagogical claim that analogies help students learn; (2) the ontological claim that historical events are token-unique but can belong to recurring structural types; (3) the epistemic claim that we can recognize patterns without being able to formulate them as falsifiable laws; (4) the methodological claim that narrative coherence supplies explanatory power that covering laws cannot, because historical causation is configurational rather than regular; and (5) the metaphysical claim that "repeating" and "rhyming" are themselves incoherent distinctions for temporal processes, and that the slogan is just a rhetorical escape hatch. For each reading, say whether it genuinely undermines the covering-law program, whether it relies on a questionable distinction — e.g., between token and type recurrence, or between causal isomorphism and structural homology — and whether the people invoking it in seminar usually mean that reading. I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature; I want a disambiguation menu I can bring back to the debate.
