I am a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun serious study in philosophy of action, and I have been examining Kavka's toxin puzzle with the nagging sense that I am importing a conceptual framework that does not properly belong there. The scenario is familiar: an agent receives a substantial reward merely for sincerely intending, at midnight, to drink a mildly toxic but non-lethal substance at noon the next day; once the reward is deposited, she has no further reason to drink, and indeed every reason to avoid it.

In much of the literature this is treated as a challenge to the coherence of cross-temporal practical reason—specifically, whether one can rationally intend an action that one knows one will later have decisive reason not to perform. Yet I find myself framing it instead as a variant of Newcomb's problem in causal decision theory: the present mental state appears to function as a common cause, linking the reward mechanism to subsequent behavior, and I am inclined to think that a causal-decision theorist should advise actually drinking the toxin (to preserve the causal efficacy of the intention), whereas an evidential theorist should advise forming the intention while planning to abstain.

My question is whether this framing rests on a false presupposition about backward causation. Specifically: if we categorically reject any retrocausal influence of present mental states upon the conditions that produced the reward, does Kavka's puzzle collapse into a trivial updating problem—merely the observation that a rational agent revises her plans when her reasons change? Or does a genuine paradox remain for a "time-slice" conception of agency, according to which rationality is evaluated independently at each temporal stage? I am especially interested in whether the puzzle can be dissolved without invoking any holistic conception of the agent extended across time, since such psychological holism strikes me as suspiciously analogous to the non-local correlations I studied in quantum mechanics, but applied to practical reason rather than physical systems. What, if anything, does the toxin case actually show about the necessity of diachronic unity in rational agency?
