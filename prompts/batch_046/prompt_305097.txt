hey, so my friends Jordan and Riley got into it over Quine's "Two Dogmas" and whether the analytic/synthetic split is totally dead or just needs a formal home. Jordan's position was basically the classic Quine line: try to define "analytic" and you either circle back to synonymy (which is just as murky) or to reducibility to logic plus definitions (which needs analyticity already to explain logical truth). So the distinction collapses into a matter of degree, not kind, and there's no principled line between revising a belief because of meaning vs because of experience.

Riley pushed back that Quine's argument works in natural language where meanings are fuzzy, but in a formal system like ZFC or even a well-defined logical calculus you can just stipulate it: analytic-in-S means derivable from the axioms and logic alone, full stop. No circularity, because "derivable" is purely syntactic. She said philosophers like Carnap already showed this — you fix a language L, and L-truth is analytic relative to that language. The boundary is sharp inside the system.

Jordan came back with to points. First, picking which sentences are axioms vs empirical inputs already requires a prior semantic sorting; you can't build the system without smuggling in judgments about what's definitional. Second, mathematicians actually revise axioms all the time (he brought up the independence of CH and how set theorists debate large cardinal axioms), so even inside formal math the "analytic" layer isn't revision-proof — it's pragmatic. If analyticity doesn't protect sentences from revision, it doesn't do the epistemological job Quine attacked.

Riley said that's mixing levels. Quine's target was the idea that there's an absolute, cross-theoretic boundary between truths-by-meaning and truths-by-fact. Within any fixed formal system there obviously is a syntactic split between theorems and non-theorems; the fact that we switch systems for pragmatic reasons doesn't erase the internal distinction. She also said Jordan's first point is just the problem of the metalanguage — yes, picking your logic and axioms involves choices, but once they're fixed, analyticity is well-defined. She accused him of demanding that formal analyticity do work it was never meant to do.

Then Jordan said if analyticity is just "theorem of S", then it's relative and doesn't capture the philosophically interesting claim — that some propositions are immune to empirical revision purely in virtue of meaning. Riley hasn't saved that; she's just renamed derivability. So Quine still wins: there's no robust analytic/synthetic distinction, only local syntactic convenience.

Riley replied that Quine himself admitted there's a distinction between logical and factual truths within a given theory; his whole point was that there's no sharp boundary across all theories. So she's not disagreeing with Quine technically — she's saying Jordan overread Quine as killing the local split too, which is a misreading.

Jordan said if Riley concedes there's no absolute boundary, then she's conceding the traditional distinction is dead, and all she's left with is a bookkeeping device. He thinks that's essentially Quine's victory, not a rescue.
