I was arguing with my study partner after our decision-theory class about the Allais paradox and we kind of went in circles. He laid out the standard setup: people pick A (guaranteed $1 million) over B (89% chance of $1m, 10% of $5m, 1% of nothing), then turn around and pick D (10% of $5m, 90% of nothing) over C (11% of $1m, 89% of nothing). He said that’s a straight-up violation of the independance axiom — the 89% branch is identical in both pairs, so stripping it away should never flip your preference between what’s left. I pushed back that this treats people like they evaluate final-wealth lotteries in a vacuum, but actually the presence of a sure-thing option in the first menu sets a "security baseline" that makes the 1% risk of total ruin in B feel like a catastrophic fall from that baseline, whereas in the second menu there is no sure thing so the baseline is already "probably broke," and the extra 1% shot at $1m in C just doesn’t feel worth sacrificing 1% of the $5m chance. So it’s not irational — it’s context-sensitive evaluation where the menu itself shifts how you weigh probabilities. He said fine, but if you let the menu restructure your evaluation frame then expected utility becomes unfalsifiable: tweak the reference point and you can rationalize any pattern, which kills the normative bite of independance. I answered that maybe the axiom was only meant for compound lotteries evaluated inside a fixed frame, not for choices where the availability of certainty itself changes the psychological weighting of outcomes. Then he hit me with the harder point: even if you buy that, you need a principled rule for when the frame shifts and when it doesn’t, otherwise you’re just telling a just-so story. I had to admit that part was pretty murky, and I also wonder whether this whole thing looks different if you think in Savage’s sure-thing terms instead of von Neumann-Morgenstern compound-lottery terms. Can you go through our exchange move by move and say who’s right at each step? I don’t want a simple winner — I want to know which moves are solid, which ones are dodging the formal point, and whether we’re both missing something about how independance actually applies across different formal frameworks.
