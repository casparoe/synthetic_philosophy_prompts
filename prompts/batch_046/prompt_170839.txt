I've been thinking about Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment after a course discussion where my professor said constitutions are basically societies tying themselves to the mast. I ended up with this kind of weird take that I'm not sure is defensible: constitutional precommitment only actually sticks if there's what I'd call "institutional amnesia"—meaning later generations have to partly forget or not fully internalize the original reasoning behind a hard rule. If they really understand and remember exactly why the constraint was put there, they just treat it as one option and amend it away; the binding works not through rational self-control but through engineered ignorance that makes revision feel illegitimate. So that's my position—successful precommitment in constitutions is closer to distributed forgetting than to a society deliberately binding its future self.

What would count against it? I don't want the obvious "but people can amend constitutions on purpose" reply; I want the deeper defeaters. Are there clear historical cases where societies maintained strict constitutional limits despite fully grasping and actively remembering the founding rationale? Would it damage the view if constitutional courts routinely invoke original intentions in ways that show full memory, or is that just a different mechanism? Also, have I insulated this too well—like if I just say "well then the amnesia wasn't complete" whenever a counterexample shows up? Give me an honest assessment: rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say what defenders of the standard Ulysses-contract view would concede, and tell me whether the view actually risks anything empirically or if it's just a redescripton of the same facts. I want to know if it's genuinely exposed or if I've accidentally made it unfalsifiable.
