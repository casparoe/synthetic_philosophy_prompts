I'm taking a course on political philosophy and we've been talking about Ulysses contracts and constituional precommitment -- like tying yourself to the mast so future-you can't do something dumb. But I've noticed a pattern in arguments and real-world cases that I don't have a word for, and I'm wondering if it's actually a recognized failure mode or just me overanalyzing.

Here's the thing. Someone sets up a hard constraint meant to block a specific future choice: a constitutional clause that says emergency powers die after thirty days no matter what, or a person who gives a trusted friend the password to their gambling account and deletes the app. Then the blocking situation actually arrives, and instead of either honoring the constraint or openly tearing it up, the people involved reinterpret the original rule so that it now allows exactly what it was meant to prevent. The court says the thirty-day limit was only for minor disruptions, so extending it for a long crisis is actually being loyal to the clause's real purpose. The gambler says the password block was only for impulsive midnight bets, not for this one carefully planned exception. What's strange is they don't act like they've broken the contract; they present the reinterpretation as fulfilling it more truly. It's not quite a built-in escape clause, because the exception is invented on the spot, and it doesn't feel like plain hypocrisy either, since the reasoning is usually public and often sincerely believed. I can think of several examples that fit, but I can't tell if there's an established concept that captures this exact move.

So does it have a name? I'm asking whether there's a term in legal theory, philosophy of action, or psychology for this particular inversion -- preserving the formal authority of a precommitment while reversing its substantive constraint through a retrospective redefinition of one's own past intent. If there's no standard name, what would you call it, and where are its edges? Also, please separate what's fairly settled about precommitment failures from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a disputed view as the consensus.
