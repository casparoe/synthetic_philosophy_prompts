I'm a doctoral student in metaphysics preparing to lead the second meeting of a graduate reading group on classical ontology. Last week the group worked through Russell's account of external relations and his argument that a relation needs no further relating relation; several members left convinced that Bradley's regress had been solved rather than relocated. This week I must teach Bradley's regress together with the deeper problem of the unity of the proposition—the sense in which a relation, even if externally relating its terms, still fails to bind them into a genuine propositional unity. The audience is six first-year philosophy PhD students; they have basic logic and some Russell, but they have not read Bradley's Appearance and Reality closely, and I do not want to require primary-source preparation.

Could you plan the ninety-minute seminar's explanatory arc? I need the dependency order: which distinction—say, between the infinite regress of relations and the failure of compositional unity—must be secured first, and why the second collapses into a different problem if introduced too early. For the carrying example, I want to avoid the standard pedagogical crutches ("Socrates is wise," "the table is brown"); instead, build the explanation around a complex relational claim such as "the gravitational constant relates mass and distance in inverse-square proportion," and specify exactly what features this example must possess—what kind of terms, what kind of relational structure—to make the unity failure visible rather than obscured. Map the misconceptions: I expect they will treat Bradley as complaining about an infinite hierarchy, will think Russell's external-relations move fully answers him, and will conflate propositional unity with mental synthesis or grammatical form. For each, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be intercepted. Provide one diagnostic question for the end that distinguishes grasp of the unity problem from mere familiarity with the regress objection. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out—perhaps Bradley's absolute monism, or detailed textual comparisons between his earlier essays and the monograph—and justify the omission for this level.
