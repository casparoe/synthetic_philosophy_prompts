I hold that Germany's completion of its nuclear phase-out was not a deviation from but an enabling structural condition for its Energiewende: baseload nuclear suppresses the price volatility and grid-flexibility investments that a high-renewable system requires, so retaining reactors would have locked in a generation profile incompatible with deep decarbonization; conversely, France's retention of its fleet represents a path dependency that defers the storage, demand-response, and sector-coupling infrastructure that even a nuclear-heavy grid eventually needs. Play it straight: what would count against this view? I want the comparative system analyses, grid-modeling results, and empirical trends that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be. Which of my load-bearing assumptions—about nuclear's inherent inflexibility relative to modern retrofits, about whether Germany's post-exit reliability stress is transient or structural, about whether France's current modernization is genuinely stalled by nuclear rather than by other factors—are genuinely exposed, and which have been qualified into unfalsifiability? Which escape routes are live rather than merely logical: for instance, could small modular reactors or retrofitted existing plants provide load-following capacity that eliminates the baseload objection, and what evidence would show this? I also want an honest assessment of whether the position has been insulated so that any German grid difficulty is blamed on missing nuclear while any French stability is credited to nuclear—if so, is that insulation a problem for a claim of this kind, or an acceptable feature of a long-range structural argument?
