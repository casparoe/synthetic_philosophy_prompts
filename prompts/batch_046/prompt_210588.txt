Recently, after being named healthcare proxy for a parent in the early stages of cognitive decline and drafting my own advance directive, I have been trying to articulate precisely why I think these documents ought to bind future caregivers, and I want a consistency check on the following commitments before I present them in a discussion group.

First, I think an advance directive expresses a form of what I call projective autonomy: a person extends practical governance over future conditions of their own bodily life, authorizing a specific trajectory rather than merely predicting preferences. Second, I accept a psychological-continuity account of personal identity—I survive over time mainly through overlapping memories, intentions, and character traits, and when those links become too sparse, strict identity fails. Third, I therefore think that a patient in advanced dementia is not literally the same individual who executed the directive. Fourth, despite that, I believe the directive should still be honored for the demented patient, though not on grounds of identity-based authority; rather, out of respect for the prior person’s quasi-property interest in not having their bodily existence used for ends they explicitly rejected. Fifth, I also think that if the patient is in an intermediate stage—with enough continuity of memory and reasoning to form stable, emotionally coherent preferences—and expresses a clear, sustained refusal of the directive’s instructions, that refusal should override the document, because sufficient continuity remains to support autonomous revision. Sixth, I hold that whether such a revision is valid should depend solely on whether psychological continuity crosses the threshold for autonomous agency, not on whether the new preference is rational or consistent with the earlier life plan. Seventh, I reject treating the directive as a contract, since contracts presuppose mutual, revocable consent that is absent here.

Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision? In particular, does my autonomy framing in the first commitment survive my denial of identity in the third and my rejection of contract in the seventh, or must I abandon the projective-autonomy language and rely solely on the property-interest argument in the fourth? And is there a hidden tension between allowing intermediate-stage revision in the fifth—judged purely by continuity—together with my insistence in the sixth that coherence with the earlier plan is irrelevant, given that the first commitment treats the directive as extending governance over a life plan? Please rank the tensions by severity, indicate for each the cheapest fix and what it costs, and say which single revision, if any, would clean up the most problems without abandoning the core idea that prior directives matter.
