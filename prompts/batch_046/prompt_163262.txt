I'm drafting the final exam for an upper-level moral philosophy seminar on communicative ethics, and I want an essay question that cannot be answered by reciting the standard taxonomy—lying violates a norm against deception, misleading violates a norm against manipulation—without actually confronting the hard case. The prompt should present students with a carefully specified scenario: a public-health spokesperson who, knowing that journalists will report only the headline figure, releases technically accurate aggregate infection-rate data that combines high-risk and near-zero-risk populations, thereby ensuring that the audience forms a false belief about the specific subgroup they care about; contrast this with a spokesperson who explicitly states the false subgroup figure. The question must ask students whether there remains a morally relevant distinction between these two acts once we hold constant (a) the speaker's intention to induce that specific false belief, (b) the audience's resulting doxastic state, and (c) the behavioral consequences. It should require them to examine whether the distinction between literal assertion and conversational implicature can carry moral weight when the speaker has deliberately structured the communicative environment—choosing what data to aggregate, what format to release, and what context to omit—to guarantee the misinterpretation. In addition, the answer should address at least one serious objection that collapses the distinction by appealing not to the literal content of the utterance but to a broader duty of epistemic care or transparency, and it should avoid treating the issue as settled by a quick appeal to Kant's Formula of Humanity or Grice's Cooperative Principle without explaining why those frameworks do or do not apply here.

Please provide the exam question (designed for a 45-minute essay), a model answer of approximately 600–700 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A answer from a B answer by listing the specific conceptual moves each must contain—for instance, recognizing that engineered ambiguity functions as a form of epistemic manipulation structurally parallel to lying—together with the common errors that should cost marks, such as conflating moral wrongness with legal liability or assuming that literal truthfulness is always sufficient for communicative virtue.
