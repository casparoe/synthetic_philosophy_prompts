I have been putting together notes for an essay I intend to present at an informal philosophy-of-science reading group I attend with colleagues. By profession I am a software engineer, with no formal training in philosophy or medicine, so I am writing partly to test whether analogies from distributed systems can illuminate debates I have encountered about the nature of health and disease.

My central concern is the stalemate between naturalism—the view that health is essentially statistically normal biological functioning, and disease a deviation from species-typical design—and normativism, which holds that calling a condition a disease necessarily involves a value judgement about harm or undesirability. To push past this binary, I have sketched what I call a "functional alignment" view: health is the state in which an organism's actual functional capacities align with the range of activities characteristic of its particular form of life, where "form of life" is understood not as a biological taxonomy but as a contextually specified pattern of living that includes social and environmental dimensions. Disease, on this account, is not mere statistical outlier status, nor is it reducible to subjective dislike; rather, it is a structurally significant misalignment that undermines the organism's ability to sustain its characteristic activities, analogous to the way we judge a distributed system "unhealthy" not when a single node fails, but when the failure pattern prevents the architecture from fulfilling its intended purpose.

I would be grateful for critical comments on three points in particular. First, does this framing genuinely avoid collapsing back into naturalism (by smuggling in evolutionary norms) or into normativism (by letting social values determine what counts as characteristic activity)? Second, is the software-engineering analogy of "functional alignment versus failure pattern" conceptually sound here, or does it mislead because biological organisms lack an external "designer" specifying intended purpose? Third, are there obvious objections—perhaps from disability studies or from evolutionary biology—that I have overlooked because of my non-specialist background?
