I have been reflecting on the literature concerning moral testimony—specifically, on whether it is epistemically appropriate to form moral beliefs on the basis of others’ assertions. Many critics argue that moral testimony is deficient because moral understanding requires a kind of first-personal grasp that testimony, being purely propositional, cannot transmit. It strikes me, however, that this objection implicitly models moral knowledge on the situation in Jackson’s thought experiment: just as Mary acquires a new, non-propositional kind of knowledge when she first sees red, so too the moral agent is thought to need a direct, acquaintance-like access to moral properties that testimony cannot provide. If this analogy holds, then the objection to moral testimony should apply with equal force to expert testimony in the empirical sciences, since no physicist has direct acquaintance with quantum fields. Yet we generally do not regard scientific deference as epistemically defective in the same way. This suggests to me that the anti-testimony view must rely on something other than a Mary’s-room-style gap—perhaps on the claim that moral beliefs play a distinctive practical or motivational role. But I am unsure whether this rescue is available. If we abandon the acquaintance model and instead treat moral beliefs as justified by inferential reasoning from observable social practices, is there any remaining epistemic distinction between moral and scientific testimony? Or does the objection reduce to a pragmatic concern about moral autonomy, in which case the parallel with Mary’s phenomenal knowledge was always a red herring?
