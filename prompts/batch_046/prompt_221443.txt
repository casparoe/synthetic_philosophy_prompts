I'm trying to extend Bostrom's unilateralist's curse—the idea that when agents can independently take initiatives with shared effects, each should apply a principle of conformity and act only if they want everyone in their position to act too—to ordinary speech and belief. The result looks like it eliminates private expression entirely, which can't be right. Here is the argument:

(1) If an initiative can be taken unilaterally and its effects are shared, rational agents should apply the principle of conformity: undertake it only if universal adoption by all agents in the same position would be good.
(2) Speech acts are initiatives in exactly this sense: when I assert something, I alter the shared epistemic environment, just as releasing a technology alters the physical environment.
(3) So I should only assert p if I want everyone in my epistemic position to assert p.
(4) But if everyone asserted everything supported by their private experience—saying "I'm tired," "This is beautiful," or "That hurt me" whenever those states obtained—the result would be contradictory noise rather than communication.
(5) Therefore, to avoid universal chaos, I must suppress all assertions based on private or subjective grounds.
(6) But this eliminates first-person testimony, aesthetic judgment, and personal reporting—without which human interaction collapses.
(7) So either the principle of conformity is false, or there is a hidden difference between technological release and speech that blocks the analogy.

Which premise or step should I reject? If I keep (1) and (2), I seem forced into (5) and the silencing of ordinary expression; if I reject (1), I lose the proposed solution to the curse itself. I want to preserve the core idea that unilateral action with shared risks demands coordination, but I also want to preserve the legitimacy of private speech. Where does the argument slip—between (2) and (3), in a scope ambiguity about "everyone," or elsewhere—and what does fixing it cost?
