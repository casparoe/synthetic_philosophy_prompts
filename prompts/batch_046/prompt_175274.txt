As a retired physicist who has only recently turned to philosophy in earnest, I am struck by how often existential ideas are treated as texts to be parsed rather than structures to be inhabited. I ask you to design a rigorous, runnable classroom activity for a philosophy workshop of about fourteen graduate students and interested non-specialists, meeting for one 75-minute session. The goal is to make participants feel the lived tension between absurdist commitment—persisting in a project after recognizing that the cosmos assigns it no inherent value or purpose—and contemporary hybrid accounts of meaning that require both subjective fulfillment and some independent form of objective worth.

To keep the exercise from feeling generic, please build in the following specific mechanism: each participant receives ten “attention tokens” representing finite life-resources and must allocate them among five project-cards. Every card displays an immediate “subjective pull” score (emotional resonance, social recognition, or sensory pleasure) but conceals an “objective worth” score that is revealed only after commitment. The hidden scores are determined by a predetermined, undisclosed algorithm (representing normative or cosmic indifference), not by participant choice. Once allocations are made and revealed, participants enter a “revolt or revise” phase: they may defend their original commitments despite zero or negative objective scores (the absurdist path) or reallocate tokens toward high-objective, low-subjective projects (the hybrid-rationalist path). The payoff scheme should rely entirely on non-monetary stakes—public justification to the group, a “legacy card” summarizing each life-plan, and a collective workshop vote on whether the group’s “society” endorses the portfolio—so that emotional and social costs drive choice rather than cash.

Please provide: (1) precise rules and materials (card designs, token sheets, a brief facilitator script for the reveal); (2) a timeline divided into silent commitment, revelation, small-group deliberation, and public defense; (3) a role and incentive structure that makes the absurdist option genuinely costly yet still emotionally attractive; (4) a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the conceptual distinction between mere subjective satisfaction and meaning that survives objective scrutiny, naming the underlying idea only after participants have experienced the conflict themselves; (5) an honest prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run (for example, participants attempting to reverse-engineer the hidden algorithm, emotional attachment to initial choices preventing rational reallocation, or the facilitator accidentally telegraphing the mechanism too early) together with a brief remedy; and (6) a frank admission of which feature of either the absurdist stance or the hybrid view this simulation cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator in closing remarks.

The philosophical force should emerge from the rules, roles, and payoffs themselves; do not open with a lecture defining the positions. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations—the activity must be fully self-contained. End your response with a single line beginning “Summary:” that captures the core design and its philosophical target in one sentence.
