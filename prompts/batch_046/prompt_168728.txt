I write novels, and I am trying to understand how beliefs about consciousness take root in a room. I ask you to treat the 1995 Tucson conference Toward a Science of Consciousness — specifically the session in which David Chalmers introduced his division between tractable functional questions and the hard problem of subjective experience — as a live case for the meta-problem of consciousness. The meta-problem asks why human cognition generates the conviction that phenomenal experience is specially resistant to physical explanation, and whether that conviction reveals a genuine feature of the world, a conceptual framing effect, or something in between. Apply it to the episode with discipline: What in Chalmers' framing — the contrast between third-person data and first-person presence, the appeal to shared intuition, the structural separation of explanation types — triggered or failed to trigger the meta-problem mechanism in different members of the audience? How should we classify the reactions that followed — enthusiastic adoption, skeptical resistance, strategic institutional uptake — according to competing answers to the meta-problem, such as illusionism, the phenomenal-concept strategy, or cognitive-closure views? Where does the meta-problem account miss the sociology of the event — the organizers' need for a disciplinary flagship, the asymmetry of authority between philosophers and neuroscientists present, the later circulation of the framing in grant narratives? I want you also to use an analog I will call narrative transparency: the novelist's observation that a first-person interior monologue can conceal its own artifice so completely that the reader takes the constructedness for reality. Do not treat this analog as doctrine; treat it as a diagnostic lens. Does the Tucson episode look different if we read the hard-problem intuition not as raw data about consciousness but as an effect of narrative transparency — a framing that hides its framing? Be precise about the options available to the actors, the predictions each meta-problem answer makes about how they should have responded, and the points at which the theory is genuinely silent rather than forced to speak.
