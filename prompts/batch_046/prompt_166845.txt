Write a 90-minute final-exam essay question for an upper-level philosophy of rational choice seminar, together with a model answer and a marking scheme totaling 20 marks. The topic must be Buridan's ass and choice without preference, but frame it so that students cannot answer by summarizing the paradox; instead, require them to adjudicate among three positions: (1) rationality demands a tie-breaking rule but is silent on its content; (2) arbitrary choice violates the principle of sufficient reason and is therefore irrational; (3) the agent may rationally suspend judgment until an external perturbation breaks symmetry. Embed a specific hypothetical: the agent knows both options yield identical utility, but delaying choice incurs a small, symmetric cost—so inaction is not neutral. The model answer must identify the central crux—whether rationality attaches to the choice itself or to the deliberative procedure that produces it—and must distinguish this from the separate question of whether total indifference is ever psychologically or normatively possible. The marking scheme should award 4 marks for correctly isolating the crux, up to 12 marks for accurate, non-straw-man reconstruction of the three positions (4 marks each), 3 marks for correctly handling the symmetric-cost hypothetical, and 1 mark for a conclusion that does not collapse the distinctions. List typical errors that should cost marks: conflating rational choice with optimal outcome, treating the paradox as a physical-causation problem rather than a normative-reasons problem, and assuming that introducing a randomizer solves the difficulty rather than relocating it.
