Design a 90-minute workshop for 12 advanced philosophy students on whether suppressing habitat can reduce aggregate wild-animal suffering. The idea they should encounter through play, not through any opening lecture, is the biomass-welfare inversion: in high-biomass ecosystems, preservation often increases reproductive subsidies to predators and raises density-dependent suffering (starvation, parasitism, predation), so that a restored habitat can contain more total suffering than a suppressed one, even if every individual in the restored system lives a decent life and biodiversity is higher.

Frame the session around a fictional audit tool invented for this exercise—the Sentience-Weighted Biomass Index (SWBI)—which assigns welfare coefficients to trophic levels rather than conservation values. Participants should derive its implications from hidden payoff structures, not from prior explanation.

Provide: (1) rules and roles for a constrained deliberation or card-based simulation in which three small groups manage distinct ecosystems (forest edge, wetland, grassland) with a fixed intervention budget and must commit to preservation, partial suppression, or degradation before seeing full welfare data; (2) hidden cards revealing, only after commitments, that preservation raises both flourishing units and suffering units via reproductive subsidies and density effects, while suppression lowers both; (3) a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes; (4) a debrief that maps each decision phase onto the conceptual problem—specifically the incommensurability of aggregate welfare and biodiversity metrics, and the non-obviousness of net assessments—without naming the view until after the mapping; (5) an honest note on which feature of the net-welfare position (for example, interspecies comparison of suffering intensity) the activity structurally cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply verbally; and (6) a prediction of the predictable misreading participants will make of the payoff structure and a strategy for correcting course without breaking the simulation.

Keep everything runnable with only printed cards and a simple score sheet. Do not ask participants for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not include any in your answer. Ensure the tension between ecological preservation and welfare reduction emerges entirely from the mechanics and hidden information before any conceptual labeling occurs.
