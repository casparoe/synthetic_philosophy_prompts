I recently joined the safety review board for a research collective developing autonomous reasoning agents, and I have been asked to formalize how these systems may rationally endorse their own inference rules without falling into circular self-trust. The conceptual difficulty is the Löbian obstacle: in any formal system strong enough to represent its own reasoning, an agent cannot derive a belief in its own soundness from within the system without already assuming exactly what it seeks to justify. I need a decision procedure—something my non-specialist colleagues can run in a review session—to evaluate whether a proposed self-trust mechanism in an agent's architecture genuinely avoids this obstacle rather than disguising it.

Please construct a step-by-step audit procedure: the ordered questions to ask about the mechanism, what counts as a satisfactory answer at each stage, the explicit stop-points where the evaluation returns a failure, and the nodes where the question is irreducibly normative—for example, how much external verification we require—and must be flagged for human judgment rather than resolved by the procedure. The procedure should distinguish between first-order trust, which concerns acceptance of specific outputs, and reflective trust, which concerns acceptance of the rules that generate outputs, since the obstacle applies differently to each. Include a small set of operational tests—concrete structural features a reviewer can check without fully formalizing the system.

Then apply the procedure to three proposed architectures. First, an agent that applies a meta-rule allowing it to accept any theorem its internal prover outputs provided the proof length falls below a fixed threshold. Second, an agent that maintains an epistemic escrow layer—an external, read-only audit log that it may consult but not modify—before endorsing high-stakes inferences. Third, a hybrid system that dynamically adjusts its confidence in its own reasoning rules based on past predictive accuracy, with the adjustment algorithm itself being among the rules subject to update. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer the board is likely to resist—most likely that a design that appears conservative actually collapses back into the obstacle. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot adjudicate, particularly regarding the baseline trust that must be placed in the initial architecture, and identify the point at which further evaluation requires stepping outside the formal framework entirely.
