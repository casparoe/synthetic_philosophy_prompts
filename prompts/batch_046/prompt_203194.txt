I'm running a fifty-minute discussion section for first-year ethics students on capital punishment. Most of them already have opinions — deterrence works or it doesn't, an eye for an eye, don't kill the innocent guy — but they treat these like three separate menu options. I want them to see there's a structural trilemma: retributive justice needs epistemic certainty that the state can never fully guarentee, deterrence needs the penalty to be applied consistently enough to actually shape behavior, and the risk of executing an innocent person isn't just a side-effect but something that undermines the moral logic of both other positions. So it's not pick-a-team, it's seeing why fixing one pillar tends to break another.

Plan the teaching arc: what concept has to be in place before the other two can even be heard? What's the one concrete scenario that should carry the whole session — I'm thinking a hypothetical state with perfect deterrence statistics but a known 2% false-conviction rate, but tell me what that example needs to include and what it must lack. Then map the misconceptions: I'm expecting 'retribution is just revenge,' 'deterrence is pure expected-value math,' and 'one wrongful execution is a tragedy but doesn't invalidate the system' — tell me exectly where in the sequence each should be defused, and why that spot. Give me one end-of-session question that reveals whether they've understood the structural tension or just picked a side. Finally, what should I openly refuse to explain at this level — comparative constitutional law, the neuroscience of risk perception? — and say so plainly.

Keep it practical, no bibliographies, and don't ask them to memorize case names.
