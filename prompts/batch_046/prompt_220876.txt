The debate over disjunctivism in the philosophy of perception—specifically whether the argument from illusion demonstrates that veridical seeing and a subjectively indistinguishable hallucination share a common mental kind—has persisted for decades without clear convergence. Both sides accept broadly the same phenomenological descriptions and the same neuropsychological findings; yet disjunctivists continue to insist that perception is essentially a direct relational encounter with an object, while common-factor theorists maintain that a shared phenomenal character must be present in both cases. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily about phenomenology or metaphysics, but about a largely tacit divergence in explanatory standards: disjunctivists treat the preservation of direct object-awareness as a non-negotiable constraint on any adequate theory, whereas their opponents treat the uniformity of subjective report across veridical and hallucinatory cases as the central datum that must be accommodated, with directness treated as a defeasible default rather than an essential feature. I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of interpretations: is the disagreement genuinely substantive, or are the sides answering different questions about consciousness under a single name? If it is substantive, is there a hidden commitment—perhaps about the semantics of introspective ascription, or about the conditions under which a mental state can be said to misrepresent—doing the real work? Given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a conceptual distinction that separates the two explanatory demands, a formal model of perceptual error that satisfies both, or an argument that the question itself is ill-posed? Defend your answer by engaging concretely with what each side actually says about hallucination reports, and reach an honest conclusion about whether anything should be expected to resolve the dispute.
