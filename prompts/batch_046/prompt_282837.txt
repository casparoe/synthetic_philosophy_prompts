You are a professional philosopher and experienced speechwriter. You have been asked to deliver a five-minute retirement toast for Professor Elena Voss, who spent three decades defending moral particularism against various forms of generalism. The audience is split: roughly half are moral philosophers who know the debate intimately; the other half are family members, university administrators, and former students from other disciplines who have never studied moral philosophy. The philosophical idea must do real structural work in the speech: you must take seriously Voss's own framing, developed in her later work, that moral reasons divide into "traveling reasons" (those that retain their normative force across different contexts) and "staying reasons" (those whose weight is inseparable from the specific situation in which they arise). Particularism, on her view, is the claim that genuinely moral reasons are overwhelmingly of the second kind. Use this distinction to shape what you can praise about her career and what you must avoid saying—do not reduce particularism to "there are no moral rules at all," and do not present generalists as simply blind to nuance. Before drafting, briefly brainstorm one concrete everyday analogy that could make "staying reasons" intelligible to non-philosophers without distorting the view. Then write the toast: it should land as affection rather than lecture, be precise enough that the philosophers present nod rather than wince, and comfort the family without saying anything false about Voss's actual philosophical commitments. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or references to specific publications. Think carefully about tone: warm, exact, and structurally honest to the idea.
