I'm currently enrolled in a seminar on formal epistemology and belief dynamics, and we've been examining Jeffrey conditionalization as an alternative to strict Bayesian updating when evidence is uncertain. I understand the basic structure: rather than conditioning on an observed event, one assigns new probabilities to the cells of a partition and redistributes prior beliefs within each cell according to the old conditional probabilities. Most textbook illustrations involve simple perceptual cases—like seeing a color under poor lighting—where the evidential shift is clearly external to the belief system being revised.

What I'm trying to understand is a deeper conceptual problem that arises when the probabilities assigned to the partition cells are not exogenous but depend on the very beliefs being updated. Consider a concrete case: an agent is estimating a continuous parameter using two measurement instruments whose reliability is itself state-dependent—that is, the calibration error of each instrument varies with the true value of the parameter, which is unknown. To apply Jeffrey's rule, the agent must assign weights to different regions of the parameter space based on the reports, but computing those weights requires prior beliefs about reliability, which in turn depend on prior beliefs about the parameter value. Could you explain whether Jeffrey's framework can accommodate this kind of circular or interdependent evidential structure without collapsing into standard conditionalization on a richer state space? Specifically, is there a principled distinction within Jeffrey's theory between treating a credence shift as genuinely evidential versus treating it as an internal revision of the belief state, and does the framework implicitly require that evidential probabilities be independent of the prior distribution over the partition?
