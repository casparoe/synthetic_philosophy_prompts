In my economics and philosophy reading group, I encountered Keynes's distinction in the Treatise on Probability between the probability of an argument—how strongly the premises support the conclusion—and its weight—how much relevant evidence actually bears on those premises. I understand that two arguments can have identical probabilities but very different weights. Then, reading The General Theory, I saw Keynes claim that long-term investment decisions face irreducible uncertainty: we lack any rational basis for assigning probabilities to future yields. This led me to think that economic uncertainty might just be an extreme case of low weight, where the supporting evidence is so thin that the probabilities become ungrounded. But I also wonder whether Keynes means something stronger—that in economic life the premises needed for a logical probability relation simply don't exist, so the weight/probability framework from the Treatise doesn't apply at all. Is economic uncertainty therefore a limit case within his probability theory, or does it show that the theory must be abandoned for practical affairs? I want to understand which interpretation is correct and why.
