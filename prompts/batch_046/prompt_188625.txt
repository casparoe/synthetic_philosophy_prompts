I serve on the curriculum committee of a large comprehensive school, and we have recently received a briefing document from an external consultancy. The report presents SNP-based heritability estimates for standardised mathematics scores of approximately 0.65 within our local population, and it also highlights substantial indirect genetic effects—described by the authors as 'genetic nurture', whereby parents' genotypes influence children's outcomes primarily through the home learning environment rather than direct transmission. One trustee has concluded that, since the variance is 'mostly genetic', funding for after-school numeracy support should be redirected toward early polygenic screening and selective enrichment for high-potential pupils. Another trustee argues the opposite—that high heritability makes targeted environmental intervention even more urgent—but she appears to be treating population-level variance statistics as if they determined individual developmental limits. I was discussing this dilemma with a friend, who observed that both trustees might be committing a category error about what heritability actually measures, yet she could not articulate precisely where the reasoning breaks down.

Could you help me understand the philosophical structure underlying this disagreement? I am not asking for a statistical tutorial, but rather for a conceptual clarification of what heritability does and does not entail for educational policy—particularly regarding the distinction between explaining group differences and predicting the malleability of any particular pupil—so that I might advise the committee on whether our funding decision should be guided by these figures at all.
