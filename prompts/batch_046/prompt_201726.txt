I've been retired from physics for about five years and lately I've been digging into philosphy of belief — mostly James's The Will to Beleive and Clifford's essay on the ethics of belief. The standard classroom story — James the romantic, Clifford the stern evidentialist — feels too tidy to me. So here's a position I've cooked up, maybe wrong, maybe not. Clifford says it's wrong always everywhere to believe on insufficient evidence, but I think that only applies to what I call first-order empirical beliefs — claims about specific states you could check with an experiment. There's a separate class I call enabling commitments: things like inductive reasoning is projectible across cosmological horizons, or the mental states of other observers are causally tied to behavior, or even the math structures in my old field actually map onto physical reality. These aren't backed by prior evidence because they make the gathering of evidence possible in the first place. James's live/dead/forced taxonomy applies here, not to empirical claims. So my view is: evidentialism governs empirical belief; James-style permission governs enabling commitments, but only when they're genuinely forced (no live rival) and momemtous. If an enabling commitment has a live alternative — say, an anti-realist reading of quantom mechanics that actually changes your experimental practice — then Clifford's rule kicks back in and you need data.

What would actually count against this? Don't just say Clifford said everywhere. I want the hard defeaters. First, is there an argument that enabling commitments are really just high-level empirical hypotheses that could be overturned with enough data — like if quantum gravity forces us to drop continuity? Second, could someone show that James's forced option is never truly forced because there's always a live suspend judgment option, even for framework stuff? Third, am I insulating my view: whenever Clifford looks right I call it empirical, whenever James looks right I call it enabling? Is that unfalsifiable taxonomy a problem? Sort these by how decisive they'd be — what would make me drop the view vs what'd just be awkward — and say honestly whether defenders of this kind of split (if there were any) would concede each point. I'm not writing a paper, just trying to think straight, so be direct and don't sugarcoat.
