For my dissertation work on existential phenomenology and technological mediation, I have been trying to apply Sartre’s account of radical freedom and mauvaise foi to a range of concrete judgments, and I want to know whether my intuitions hang together under a single principle or whether some must be abandoned.

First, consider a surgeon who refuses to operate on a patient she despises and reports that her hands simply “refuse to move”; she treats her refusal as an involuntary bodily event rather than as a choice she makes. I take this to be paradigmatic bad faith: she denies her freedom by identifying herself with her body as pure object.

Second, consider a prisoner who says he lacks freedom because he is physically confined to a cell. I do not think this is bad faith; it is an accurate recognition of facticity. His freedom is severely constrained, but he does not deny that he chooses how to respond to the constraint.

Third, consider an engineer who deploys and actively maintains an algorithmic scheduling system that determines his work priorities, and who then claims he no longer chooses his actions because “the system decides.” He updates the code and profits from its outputs, yet treats it as an alien force. This feels structurally like the surgeon’s denial, but I am unsure whether Sartre’s framework can accommodate it, since the constraint is not internal to his body but distributed in an infrastructure he sustains—what I am calling distributed facticity.

Fourth, consider an artist who says she “had no choice” but to paint in a particular style because her artistic vision demanded it. If she recognizes the vision as her own project, I think this is authentic; if she reifies it as an external command, it is bad faith. So the same utterance can express either authenticity or self-deception depending on how she relates to her project.

Fifth, I notice a tension between my first and second judgments: both the surgeon and the prisoner cite physical conditions, yet I treat them differently. The difference seems to lie in whether the cited condition is something the agent maintains as part of her project. Yet I also judge the engineer differently from the prisoner, though both involve structures external to the body, and I am not sure my distinction is consistent.

Is there a single principle—perhaps centered on whether the agent treats a constraint as an in-itself that negates her for-itself—that delivers all five verdicts? Or must one be overturned? In particular, does the framework need to be extended to cover distributed facticity, and if so, does that extension preserve the core distinction between facticity and bad faith? Please state any principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, predict its verdicts on cases I have not listed, and say honestly which of my five judgments it cannot accommodate.

Finally, apply your best account to the following case: a user of a brain-computer interface that preemptively suppresses impulses she has previously endorsed, who then claims that the interface, not she, is responsible for her subsequent moral actions.
