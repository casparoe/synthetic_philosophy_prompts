Please describe the most important implications of applying a “structural-complicity” model of collective responsibility—defined here as responsibility that accrues to institutional roles when (i) the role’s formal duties include preventing a given harm, (ii) the institutional architecture systematically rewards actions that produce that harm, and (iii) role-occupants possess practical knowledge of this design yet remain in post—to the Fischer controversy over whether Imperial Germany bore primary responsibility for the outbreak of the First World War. In particular: does this framework permit holding the German state culpable without endorsing Fritz Fischer’s specific claim that a unified, aggressive national will deliberately sought continental hegemony in 1914? How does it reshape the moral distinction between a nation that actively seeks war and one that slides into it through bureaucratic momentum? And what does it imply about whether attributing blame to a nation requires positing a singular collective intention, or can instead be satisfied by distributed institutional failure across the military, diplomatic, and executive structures of the Kaiserreich?
