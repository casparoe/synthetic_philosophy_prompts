I've been trying to formalize a hybrid cosmological argument for a philosophy-of-religion seminar and have driven it to what looks like a reductio, but I want to check whether the fault lies in my reasoning rather than in the premises themselves. The argument runs as follows. First, take the kalam line: if something begins to exist, it has a cause of its existence that is outside it and prior to it; the universe, having begun to exist, therefore has a transcendent cause. Second, take the contingency line: the universe is contingent — it exists, but it might not have — and contingent beings require an explanation in a being whose existence is necessary and self-explaining. Now I combine them with a step I have not seen in standard presentations: contingency is not merely a global property of the universe taken as a whole, but a modal feature that attaches to every particular state of affairs within the universe. If a falling leaf, a chemical reaction, or a human decision is genuinely contingent — it could have been otherwise — then by the contingency principle each requires an explanation in the necessary being, not only at the origin of the cosmos but at every occurrence. From this it follows that there is no genuine secondary causation: natural causes are at best instrumental, since every contingent effect is immediately explained by God. But this collapses the distinction between primary and secondary causation that defenders of both arguments usually insist on preserving, and it seems to make empirical science redundant, since every particular event already has a complete explanation in the necessary being.

I find this conclusion implausible, yet I am reluctant to reject either the kalam premise about beginnings or the contingency premise about explanation. Where exactly does the argument overreach? Is the mistake in treating contingency as a per-event modal property that demands immediate explanation, in assuming that the explanation required by contingency must be direct rather than mediated through natural causes, or in some hidden equivocation between the cause required by the kalam argument and the explanation required by the contingency argument? If I have to choose, I would rather preserve both (1) and (2) and give up whatever hidden bridge premise connects them to universal immediate causation, but I want to know precisely what I am paying for that choice.
