After a long argument with a friend last night about whether philosophical thought experiments are genuinely informative or just rhetorically compelling, I want to defend a particular diagnosis of why they go wrong. Please argue for the following position.

The problem with canonical intuition pumps — from the trolley problem to the teletransporter to Gettier-style constructions — is not merely that they strip away context. Rather, they systematically exploit what I call structural narrative compression: by collapsing a multi-layered causal and normative structure into a linear sequence of events, they cause the listener to mistake temporal adjacency for logical entailment. The framing choices that determine the outcome are hidden inside the narrative flow, so the conclusion feels necessary when it is actually contingent on how the scenario was compressed. This explains why structurally isomorphic cases that embed the same trade-off in institutional rules rather than direct personal action reliably produce opposite intuitions. The distortion is therefore formal, not contextual.

Please develop this argument carefully. Show how structural narrative compression operates in at least two distinct kinds of intuition pump, and explain why recognizing it undermines the standard defense that these devices are useful precisely because they isolate variables. You should also address the objection that all reasoning involves some narrative framing, and therefore that this critique proves too much.

Finally, please state how confident you are in the overall argument, and single out the specific claim or step about which you are least certain.
