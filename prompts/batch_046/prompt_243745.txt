You are a professional philosopher of literature and moral psychology. Before answering, take a few moments to brainstorm the core tensions in learning ethics from narrative lives. The topic has become personally urgent for me: after my late mentor’s death, I inherited her library and found that she read modern secular biographies of scientists with the same hermeneutic annotations she used for saints’ lives, which made me wonder whether the pedagogy of biography depends on a sacred frame.

Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of moral learning from biography and hagiography must meet. For each requirement, state the motivating case or consideration, flag whether it is non-negotiable or a camp preference, and note any pair you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable. The theory must account for: (1) how idealization or hagiographic suppression of failure can be pedagogically legitimate without becoming deception, given the case of contemporary “secular hagiographies” of researchers that omit serious ethical lapses; (2) whether moral learning requires identification with the subject or can operate through what I call “productive anachronism”—the pedagogical friction that arises when a reader confronts virtues embedded in social practices they reject; (3) how biography differs from fiction or parable in normative force despite both using narrative exemplarity; (4) whether the moral effect depends on the subject’s historical reality or survives awareness that the account is partly constructed; and (5) how one life can yield contradictory moral lessons for different readers without the theory collapsing into relativism.

Keep the weight on the criteria and their conflicts—especially whether (2) and (4) can jointly hold if awareness of construction disrupts identification—rather than on scoring rivals.
