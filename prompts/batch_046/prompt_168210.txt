In a graduate seminar on existential phenomenology that I have been facilitating, we have spent the better part of a term working through Sartre’s claim that consciousness is radically free and that bad faith is the refusal to own that freedom by treating oneself as a pure object. Lately, however, a specific disagreement has crystallized and then frozen: one faction argues that Sartre’s later analysis of the “practico-inert”—the way past human praxis sedimentizes into material and institutional structures that then appear to determine present action—introduces a genuine determinism that makes bad faith not merely a personal vice but an unavoidable condition for anyone embedded in those structures. The opposing faction insists that the practico-inert is nothing more than an especially dense form of facticity, which radical freedom always already transcends, so that to say oppressed or structurally constrained agents are “in bad faith” for failing to revolt is to confuse ontological determination with historical difficulty. Both sides agree on Sartre’s descriptions of bureaucratic inertia, agree that freedom is an ontological structure rather than an empirical capacity, and agree on the phenomenology of how institutional rules feel like external necessities; yet after months of discussion, neither camp has conceded a single premise.

My own working theory is that the impasse is not really about Sartre exegesis but about what should count as an adequate explanation of action: one side demands that any ascription of freedom be accompanied by a demonstration that the agent could have acted otherwise given the specific weight of the structures, while the other side treats the possibility of transcendence as a primitive that requires no further empirical vindication and is falsified only by logical contradiction. Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden substantive disagreement—perhaps about whether collective entities can be agents in the relevant sense, about the criteria for attributing moral responsibility under systemic constraint, or about whether “bad faith” is being used as a psychological predicate versus an ontological one? I want an analysis that engages what each side actually says about facticity, transcendence, and the practico-inert, rather than a survey of the secondary literature. Given your diagnosis, tell me whether anything—say, a rigorous distinction between “structural constraint” and “ontological determination,” or a designed scenario in which an agent fully internalizes an institutional rule yet remains transparently free—could actually move the debate, or whether the disagreement ultimately rests on bedrock moral intuitions about responsibility for oppression that philosophical argument alone cannot adjudicate.
