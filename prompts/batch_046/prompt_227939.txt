Analyze the 1940 City College of New York appointment controversy — in which Bertrand Russell was appointed to the philosophy department, then blocked by a court ruling that cited his writings and personal life as morally unfit, leading the Board of Higher Education to rescind the offer — through the lens of Russell's own ethical theory of marriage and sexual morality articulated in Marriage and Morals (1929). I want a disciplined two-directional reading.

First, apply Russell's framework: his rationalist separation of sexual ethics from religious dogma, his defense of companionate marriage as a trial union without legal or religious coercion, his claim that the state's only legitimate interest in marriage concerns the welfare of children, and his argument that conventional morality produces psychological harm by suppressing open discussion. Map the episode's actors — Russell, the Board of Higher Education, Judge McGeehan, the protesting parents, and the academic defenders — onto this framework. What does Russell's theory prescribe for each? Does it give Russell a coherent defense of his own appointment, or does it commit him to rejecting the very institutional authority he sought? How should the Board have evaluated moral fitness if it adopted Russell's welfare-based criteria rather than conventional standards?

Second, identify where the theory fails to capture the dynamics of the episode. The court's decision turned on a conflation of Russell's descriptive sociology of marriage with prescriptive advocacy, and on his personal divorces rather than on philosophical arguments. Does Russell's framework anticipate that rational sexual ethics will be misread as personal license? Does it have resources to address the gendered asymmetry of the scandal — that Russell's male rationalism was read as predation while female sexual autonomy was erased? And does the theory's silence on democratic legitimacy — the fact that a public institution answers to popular morality — constitute a gap or a feature?

Finally, ask what the episode reveals about the theory itself. Is the Russell affair an anomaly that shows rationalist sexual ethics cannot survive institutional politics without a protective elite? Or does it confirm Russell's darker claim that conventional morality is sustained not by reason but by fear? I want the analysis to stay close to the actual mechanisms of the case — the judicial reasoning, the Board's capitulation, the public rhetoric — and to say explicitly where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
