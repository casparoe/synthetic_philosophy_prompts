My colleague oversees a small cooperative workshop housed in a deconsecrated chapel, and she intends to restructure it according to a strict efficiency framework. She points to two features she regards as design failures: first, a narrow triangular void between two load-bearing arches near the ceiling that serves no storage or display function; second, a tool cabinet secured by a mechanism requiring two people to turn separate keys at once. She proposes removing the arch supports to reclaim vertical space and replacing the cabinet with a standard single-lock unit. When I observed that the triangular space is likely an unavoidable geometric byproduct of the arch construction rather than a failed attempt at storage, and that the dual-key system derives from a salvaged bank vault whose original security purpose is now obsolete, though members have come to rely on the enforced collaboration for safety checks, she dismissed these explanations as post-hoc rationalizations. She insists that any feature of the current layout must either serve an optimized present purpose or be eliminated as an error. I am unsure whether her reasoning neglects an important distinction about how physical and organizational structures accumulate constraints and accidental functions over time. How should I evaluate her proposal? Is her principle—that every element in an existing system must either fulfill a currently optimized role or be corrected—defensible?
