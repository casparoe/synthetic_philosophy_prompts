I have no formal background in philosophy or the sciences, but over the past few years I have read extensively in popular works on causation and have tried to apply the ideas to puzzles I construct for myself. I have become convinced that the long-running disagreement between the counterfactual account—according to which one event causes another just when, had the first not occurred, the second would not have occurred—and the interventionist account—according to which causation is a matter of invariant relationships that would hold under an intervention on one variable while others are held fixed—has settled into a stalemate that is more structural than anyone admits. The canonical examples are always the same: the colliding billiard balls, the assassin whose shot is preempted by a rival, the double-prevention scenario with two guards, and the falling barometer that correlates with but does not cause a storm. Each side has a well-rehearsed response to each example, and after following the exchange for some time I can predict the reply before I finish reading the objection. Because causation is not directly observable in the way a chemical reaction is, there seems to be no decisive experiment waiting to be performed. So I want a diagnosis rather than another defense of one side. Is the disagreement merely verbal, turning on different uses of the word "cause"—one metaphysical, one methodological? Are the two sides actually answering different questions, one about the structure of the world and one about the structure of good scientific models, while speaking as though they share a single subject? Or is there a hidden substantive dispute about which relationships are genuinely causal that each side disguises by selecting examples favorable to its own framework? To make this concrete, I have constructed a case that I cannot place comfortably in either framework: a deterministic game with hidden rules, in which one player's private knowledge of a hidden rule allows her to make a legal move that alters the outcome, but in which every physical token and every publicly known rule remains exactly as it would have been had she lacked that knowledge. The counterfactualist might say her knowledge caused the outcome, since without it the move would not have happened; the interventionist might deny this, on the ground that knowledge states are not modular variables that can be intervened upon in isolation, and that the invariant relationships among the public rules are unchanged. If both frameworks can be adjusted to accommodate this case, then I suspect the debate is not about particular verdicts at all, but about what an explanation is required to achieve—perhaps predictive control, perhaps metaphysical grounding, perhaps coherence with our practices of holding people responsible. Please tell me, with reasons drawn from what each side actually says about their standard examples, what kind of disagreement this is. And then be honest about what, if anything, would count as progress: a new formal result, a conceptual distinction that separates the questions, a shift in the framing of the problem, or an argument that the impasse is permanent because the two projects were never in conflict to begin with.
