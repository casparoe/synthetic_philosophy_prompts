Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps on what counts as corruption, with particular attention to clientelism, ceremonial gift-giving, and whether Western anti-corruption standards are universal rather than culturally particular.

Camp One (Universalist Institutionalism) holds that corruption is definable across contexts as the subversion of entrusted public authority for private or factional gain; that clientelism and ritualized gift-exchange become corruption when they systematically substitute personal obligation for impartial, rule-governed procedure; and that Western legal-rational norms—merit-based allocation, transparency, formal conflict-of-interest rules—represent functional prerequisites of accountable governance rather than merely local customs.

Camp Two (Contextual Moral Economy) holds that corruption is not a culture-neutral category; that practices such as kin-based contract distribution or ceremonial exchange often operate within local moral economies of reciprocity, social insurance, and collective ritual obligation; and that labeling them corrupt applies an ethnocentric framework which mistakes the administrative form of Western states for a universal moral baseline.

Produce a document both camps could sign, structured exactly as follows:

1. Agreed Claims: A numbered list of propositions both sides accept without reservation. Include at least: that hybrid governance—where formal state institutions coexist with parallel networks of obligation—is empirically widespread; that both camps reject pure moral relativism regarding unambiguous predation (e.g., direct embezzlement for purely personal luxury without reciprocal social function); and that descriptive accounts of local norms do not by themselves settle prescriptive judgments about institutional design.

2. Disagreement Table: State each camp's core position in wording that camp accepts and the other recognizes as accurate, avoiding strawman formulations. Cover precisely: (a) whether corruption necessarily requires the intent to violate a formal rule that is locally recognized as legitimate, or whether violating an externally imposed rule can also constitute corruption; (b) whether gift-giving and clientelism can be functionally separated from corruption in contexts where formal procurement rules and kin-obligation networks operate simultaneously—specifically, whether the concept of "institutional bilingualism" describes a stable adaptation or a systematic subversion; (c) whether Western anti-corruption metrics measure real harm to collective welfare or primarily measure convergence with Western administrative form.

3. Cruxes: Identify three sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side. Use these: (i) whether ceremonial gifts that influence contract allocation but fund communal rituals constitute private gain or collective redistribution; (ii) whether there exists a cross-culturally stable distinction between "clientelism as social insurance" and "extractive corruption," and if so, what empirical marker distinguishes them; (iii) whether the presence of dense rotating-credit or kin-obligation networks in a municipality with formal procurement rules predicts deviation from those rules because of network density itself, or only because of the absence of ritual reciprocity obligations within the network.

4. Agreed Tests and Predictive Cases: For each crux, specify one concrete comparative scenario or dataset both sides agree is relevant, and record in advance what each camp predicts. For crux (iii), use the scenario of two municipalities with identical formal procurement rules but different densities of kin-based rotating credit associations: Camp One predicts that higher association density correlates with greater deviation from formal criteria regardless of ritual context; Camp Two predicts that deviation correlates with the absence of ritual reciprocity obligations rather than with association density alone.

Write in formal, precise prose. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific scholars or texts. Do not ask for identification of individual theorists by name. Ensure the wording of disagreements is symmetrical—neither formulation should embed a loaded presupposition. Conclude with a brief joint note identifying whether part of the dispute is verbal—specifically, whether disagreement over the definition of "corruption" (e.g., reserving it for violations of locally legitimate formal rules versus applying it to any abuse of entrusted power regardless of local normative acceptance) creates the appearance of substantive disagreement where there may be only a definitional choice, and if so, specify exactly which choice does so.
