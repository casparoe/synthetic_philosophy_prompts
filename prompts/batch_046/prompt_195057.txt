You are a professional philosopher specialising in social ontology and the metaphysics of institutional kinds. Please think carefully and systematically. Before you adjudicate, take a moment to map the conceptual terrain: distinguish behavioural-functional accounts of social kinds from institutional-status accounts, and list the phenomena each must explain — for example, counterfeiting, hyperinflation, and unilateral creation of a medium of exchange.

A colleague of mine attended a seminar where two philosophers, Sam and Jordan, argued about what social construction actually requires in the case of money. Here is how their exchange went, as she reported it.

(1) Sam opened by saying that money is whatever a community treats as a medium of exchange, store of value, and unit of account. Therefore, if a closed virtual economy of ten thousand users collectively treats an AI-generated token called glimmer exactly as they treat pounds sterling — pricing goods in it, saving it, settling debts with it — then glimmer is money, full stop. There is no hidden natural kind moneyness waiting to be discovered; the construction is purely collective acceptance.

(2) Jordan replied that this confuses functioning as money with being money. Social construction does not mean arbitrary collective labelling; it means the kind is constituted by institutional facts that depend on collective intentionality embedded in rules. Without an institutional framework that assigns status-functions — legal-tender status, banking regulations, norms of enforcement — glimmer is only a token that behaves like money, not money itself. Jordan compared it to a perfect counterfeit note: it functions identically in transactions but lacks the right institutional pedigree.

(3) Sam countered that Jordan smuggles in a statist definition of money, which is historically false. Cowrie shells and Rai stones functioned as money without central banks or legal codes. If Jordan demands state-like institutions, then large parts of economic history are misdescribed.

(4) Jordan clarified that she does not require a state, only a normative framework — rules of exchange, social sanctions for fraud, and collective recognition of a status-function. Cowrie shells had precisely these: norms about shell purity and penalties for counterfeit shells. The point is that there must be normative structure, not merely behavioural regularity. Sam's treatment view cannot distinguish genuine money from a popular collectible card that happens to be traded for goods; the missing element is collective recognition of a status-function assigned to the object.

(5) Sam then argued that Jordan's view makes social construction mysterious. It sounds as though there is a hidden institutional essence just as fixed as any natural essence. If construction is genuinely social, then the community's practices should be sufficient; otherwise we are back to essentialism with extra steps.

(6) Jordan responded that this is not essentialism but relational constitution. The kind exists in virtue of relations to other institutional facts — contracts, property law, debt structures — not in virtue of an intrinsic nature. Sam's purely behavioural view misses these relations and therefore cannot explain why hyperinflation destroys the money-ness of a currency rather than merely reducing the value of a natural kind.

My colleague said she could see merit on both sides but could not tell who was winning at each step. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say, overall, which account better captures what social construction of money requires, and identify what both sides are missing — particularly regarding whether an AI-generated token in a fully virtual economy could ever cross the threshold from behavioural analogue to genuine institutional kind without any connection to offline legal or economic systems.
