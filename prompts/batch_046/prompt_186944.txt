At our philosophy-of-science reading group, two members argued over whether the current deep-learning research programme is progressive or degenerating in Lakatos’s sense. Maya began by identifying the hard core as the claim that intelligent behavior emerges from statistical pattern-matching in large neural networks trained by gradient descent, with the protective belt comprising architectural innovations, regularization techniques, and alignment methods such as RLHF and chain-of-thought prompting. She argued that the programme is progressive because it has generated genuinely novel predictions—specifically, that performance improves predictably with scale according to power laws, and that certain capabilities emerge discontinuously at particular parameter thresholds—predictions that guided resource allocation before the relevant experiments were fully run.

Jonas replied that the hard core is effectively unfalsifiable: any failure—hallucination, adversarial vulnerability, poor reasoning—is always attributed to insufficient scale or an inadequate protective-belt adjustment, never to a flaw in connectionism itself. He granted that scaling regularities exist, but said they are empirical correlations rather than theoretical predictions derived from the hard core; they do not uniquely support connectionism, since a symbolic or hybrid programme could also expect improved performance with greater compute. He added that phenomena such as in-context learning were observed first and then rationalized post hoc, so they do not count as risky predictions in Lakatos’s sense.

Maya countered that Lakatos does not require predictions to be unique to one programme, only that theoretical adjustments within the protective belt lead to new anticipated facts. She claimed that the move to transformer architectures and the subsequent anticipation of emergent in-context learning at specific scales were theory-driven, not mere accommodations, and that this distinguishes the programme from purely ad hoc curve-fitting.

Jonas closed by noting that even if some predictions precede confirmation, the programme never faces a genuinely risky test of its hard core. Because every anomaly is handled by expanding the belt—adding data, parameters, or post-training refinement—the central commitment remains insulated. He concluded that this insulation is precisely what Lakatos called degenerating problem-shift.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of Maya’s and Jonas’s four arguments from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything important that both overlook—such as whether Lakatos’s framework needs modification for domains where theoretical commitments and engineering practice are inseparable, or whether the distinction they draw between empirical scaling laws and theoretical predictions misses a deeper point about how prediction functions in largely empirical fields.
