Constitutional precommitment is often explained through the Ulysses-and-the-sirens analogy: a polity binds itself in calm moments to resist future temptations, just as Ulysses had his crew tie him to the mast and ignore his later pleas to be released. But the analogy does heavy conceptual lifting that is rarely audited in detail. Lay out the mapping precisely—what plays the role of Ulysses, the sirens, the ropes, the crew, and the ship? Then identify the disanalogies that specifically threaten democratic legitimacy: in the constitutional case the “crew” are not subordinate sailors but future citizens who may claim equal authority to untie the knot; Ulysses knew exactly what temptation he faced, whereas framers cannot know which specific policies future generations will be tempted by; and the ropes were temporary, while constitutional entrenchment is designed to be durable. Does the analogy still support the conclusion that binding is rational self-binding rather than illegitimate domination of one generation by another? Which features of the Homeric source—unitary agency, hierarchical command, foreknowledge of the threat—does the constitutional version quietly import that democratic self-government lacks? Finally, if the analogy breaks down at these points, what is a better model—perhaps a trustee-delegation framework, or an intergenerational contract with explicit renegotiation clauses?
