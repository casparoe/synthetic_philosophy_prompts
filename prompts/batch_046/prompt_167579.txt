I am writing for a seminar on moral psychology and have come to defend a selective dissolution of the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem: I claim it is genuinely pressing only within deontic frameworks, where obligations bind regardless of contingent desire, but largely evaporates within axiological or eudaimonistic frameworks, since judgments about the good life are evaluated through the coherence of one's overall practical identity rather than through the purity of motivating reason. I want an honest map of what would count against this claim. Which cases or arguments show the problem persists even when we evaluate flourishing rather than duty—for instance, is there a wrong kind of reason for pursuing wisdom solely for social prestige, and does that revive the problem in a form my distinction cannot accommodate? I also want to know whether this view risks insulating itself by definitional fiat: have I qualified the problem away merely by restricting its domain, and if so, is that a theoretical defect or a legitimate boundary? Please rank potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguishing objections that a defender of this selective view would concede from those they would dismiss, and say whether the position has been made unfalsifiable in a damaging way. Respond in continuous prose only—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as in a letter.
