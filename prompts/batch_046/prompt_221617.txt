I will present a 25-minute paper defending an institutional thesis about forgetting to a faculty seminar in political philosophy and transitional justice law. The thesis is that democratic states inheriting authoritarian administrative archives have a limited but genuine obligation to implement "mnemonic sunset provisions"—transparent, reviewable protocols that allow specific categories of low-level bureaucratic records (routine surveillance logs of non-violent citizens, administrative collaboration classifications, and similar state-defined categories) to decay or be sealed after a defined period, rather than preserved in perpetuity. This is not an argument for destroying evidence of serious crimes or for removing public monuments; it concerns only bureaucratic data that fixes citizens in administrative identities they did not choose. I draw on Avishai Margalit's distinction between shared memory and ethical memory, arguing that perpetual state retention of these records violates the conditions for ethical memory by preventing citizens from being released from state-defined categorization, together with David Rieff's critique of compulsory historical commemoration, applied here to archival policy rather than to public ritual. The audience consists of scholars of transitional justice, legal theorists of the "right to truth," and philosophers of moral repair. Most have published on truth commissions, victim reparations, or epistemic injustice in archives. They generally hold that remembering is a form of moral repair, that forgetting is often a second harm to victims, and that state archives serve democratic accountability. They are familiar with Margalit's The Ethics of Memory and likely know Rieff's In Praise of Forgetting, but they tend to treat Rieff's position with deep suspicion. They care intensely about victim dignity, the politics of archives, "archival violence," and the risk that state control over memory reproduces domination.
