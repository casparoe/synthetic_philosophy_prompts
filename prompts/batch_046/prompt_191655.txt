I was a research biologist for thirty years before I retired, and lately I've been trying to teach myself some philosphy — mostly metaphysics, because it feels closer to the cosmology I used to read about and it's relevent to how I think about time now. I'm in a little reading group with other retired scientists, and we're working through Slaughterhouse-Five, which I hadn't touched since college. We got stuck on Billy Pilgrim being unstuck in time, and the Tralfamadorians seeing every moment at once. That sounds like eternalism — the block universe — but Billy still bleeds in Dresden and dies in 1976, which feels like real change, not just a static slice.

So I want to ask: can you read Vonnegut's novel through the presentism vs. eternalism debate, but don't just reduce it to an illustration? I think the book might complicate both sides, especially because Billy's jumps seem tied to trauma rather than ontology. Here's the angle that keeps nagging me — in neuroscience there's an idea called the specious present, where the brain integrates experience over a few seconds rather than an instant. Could Vonnegut's narrative structure be modeling that kind of lived temporal binding instead of either metaphysical view? Or does that reading miss what the novel is actually doing about memory and suffering?

I'm writing this up for our group, and I don't want to mislead anyone who hasn't studied the debate. Take seriously both the physics — block universe, arrow of time — and this neuroscience twist, but be honest about where the metaphor breaks down and where the work resists the philosophical framing. What does the novel add that a straight argument about presentism can't?
