I'm a phd student in comparative philosophy and I've been going in circles with my advisor about Zhuangzi's attitude toward death. The debate in the literature feels like it's been running on fumes for decades: one side reads the tub-drumming episode after his wife dies as genuine metaphysical acceptance—death is just transformation, nothing to grieve—while the other side treats it as either ironic performance or strategic emotional distancing, especially since other passages show him clearly shaken by a friend's death and playing with skulls in ways that don't look like serene detachment. Nobody concedes ground, and every new article basically re-arranges the same three chapters.

My own hunch is that the stall isn't about Zhuangzi's psychology at all but about what kind of consistency we demand from a text that never argues in straight lines. One camp wants propositional coherence—if he accepts transformation he shouldn't mourn—while the other camp thinks practical wisdom can be locally inconsistent without being irrational. But I'm not sure if that's actually right, or if there's a hidden empirical question about interpollations and dating of chapters that's secretly driving the disagreement, or if the sides are just answering different questions: one about normative stance, one about historical belief. Is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or a sign that we're asking something ill-posed? I want a diagnosis that actually engages the positions—what each side says that reveals their deeper commitments—not a survey of interpretations. And honestly, could anything actually end it? Like a new conceptual distinction, or is this the kind of deadlock we just live with?
