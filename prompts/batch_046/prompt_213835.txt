I hold a strong version of the F-twist about idealization: unrealistic assumptions in scientific models are not merely tolerable heuristics but actively productive distortions—predictive power in domains like macroeconomics and ecology often scales with the severity of misrepresentation of underlying causal mechanisms, because only radical simplification can filter out confounding dynamics and expose invariant relationships. What would have to be true for this to be wrong? I want an honest map of its exposure: which specific out-of-sample failures in forecasting or policy evaluation actually undermine the claim that distortion is epistemically productive rather than merely convenient? Are there formal results linking representational fidelity to predictive robustness that, if established, would be decisive? Would sustained predictive success by mechanistically realistic or agent-based models in domains where highly idealized equilibrium models currently dominate count as fatal, or could defenders absorb it by redefining what counts as prediction? I am especially interested in whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—where any failure is blamed on implementation rather than on the idealization strategy itself—and whether that insulation is a problem for a view of this kind. Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what critics allege from what committed defenders of the strong F-twist would themselves concede as damaging, and assess whether the position currently risks anything substantive or has been rendered immune to evidence.
