I have recently begun participating in a philosophy-of-physics reading group focused on the interpretation of quantum mechanics, and I have become concerned that one analogy used repeatedly in our discussions is doing more work than it should. A fellow participant compares the quantum wavefunction to a credit score: just as a credit score aggregates hidden but genuinely real financial histories into a numerical summary that is informative yet physically unlocated—one would not search for it inside the borrower's body or environment—so the quantum state, she argues, aggregates hidden properties of a system without being a concrete field or substance in configuration space. From this she derives a sharp conclusion: psi-ontic interpretations commit a category mistake by treating an epistemic summary as if it were an object with spatial or structural presence. I want this analogy audited rigorously. Can you lay out an explicit correspondence table—borrower, hidden history, credit bureau, the score's value, and the space in which it might be "located" mapped onto their quantum-mechanical counterparts? Then identify the fractures: does the analogy smuggle in classical hidden variables that quantum mechanics may not permit? Can it accommodate unitary evolution, superposition, and entanglement across spatially separated systems? Does it confuse ignorance about definite properties with the specific kind of indefiniteness that quantum states encode? Finally, evaluate whether the anti-psi-ontic conclusion survives these disanalogies, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument all along; and if it collapses, suggest what a more apt comparison for an epistemic quantum state might be.
