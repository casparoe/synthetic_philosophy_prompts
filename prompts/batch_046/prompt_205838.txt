I've been stuck on the aggregation problem in Scanlon-style contractualism for months, and I'm starting to think the literature is talking past itself in a really specific way. The basic setup is familiar: you've got five people who need a kidney each and one healthy person whose organs could save them, or the classic rescue case where you can save one from drowning or five others. Scanlon's idea is that wrongness is about whether anyone can reasonably reject a principle allowing the act. The anti-aggregation crowd—Kamm's line, and Scanlon himself—says you can't aggregate burdens across people, so saving the five isn't justified just because 5>1, because each of the five can't reject being saved, but the one can reject being killed. Meanwhile the aggregation-friendly critics—Otsuka, Norcross, maybe some recent people—say this makes contractualism absurd in large-number cases and that reasonable rejection has to be sensitive to numbers somehow.

But here's what's weird: in every seminar I've been in, both sides agree on the formal structure of the contractualist test, both agree that the one can reject being sacrificed, and both agree that if the numbers are big enough something has to give. The argument just loops. My working theory is that this isn't really about aggregation at all—it's about whether "reasonable rejection" is evaluated pairwise between the agent and each affected party, or whether it's evaluated against a principle that already includes a distributive rule. In other words, are the sides answering different questions—one about direct justification to individuals, one about institutional design—while using the same word "wrong"? Or is there a hidden factual disagreement about what counts as a comparable burden, maybe about whether suffering is interpersonally measurable in the first place?

So: is this stall just bedrock intuition about the seperate-ness of persons that neither side can argue past? Is it a verbal dispute about whether "aggregation" means adding up welfare or adding up rejection-weights? Or are they genuinely disagreeing about the formal properties of the rejection relation—like whether it's transitive across groups? And if you think it's one of these, what would actually move it? A formal model showing the rejection relation is non-transitive? A case where the pairwise structure clearly breaks? Or should we just accept that contractualism has a hard boundary here and stop expecting resolution?
