Consider the structural parallel often drawn between the lottery paradox and the preface paradox: in each case, an agent holds a large set of individually well-supported beliefs that jointly entail a contradiction. The standard literature treats these as distinct pathologies—one arising from probabilistic reasoning over a partitioned possibility space, the other from rational self-assessment of fallibility—yet both are routinely described as violations of closure under conjunction for rational belief. This suggests a unified diagnosis might be available. I want to press a specific proposal that seems curiously absent from the discussion: a principle of domain-relative closure, according to which closure holds within an epistemic domain—say, empirical predictions about discrete events, or first-order beliefs about textual claims—but is systematically suspended at the boundary between domains, particularly between object-level beliefs and meta-cognitive assessments of one's own reliability.

If we adopt such a principle, the preface author could rationally maintain every chapter-level belief in the textual domain while relegating the admission of probable error to the reflective domain, with no contradiction arising because closure does not operate across the boundary. Meanwhile, in the lottery case, one might hold each ticket-specific belief within the statistical domain without being forced to conjoin them into the belief that no ticket wins, since the aggregate statistical judgment belongs to a different domain of reasoning.

My question is twofold. First, is there a principled reason why this domain-partition strategy fails—perhaps because the preface meta-belief is inherently about the very beliefs it purports to bracket, or because the lottery aggregation is internal rather than cross-domain? Second, and more generally, if domain-relative closure can be consistently articulated, does it genuinely resolve both paradoxes, or does it merely relocate the tension into the rules governing domain membership and cross-domain reasoning? I am especially interested in whether any hidden circularity in defining the domains undermines the proposal.
