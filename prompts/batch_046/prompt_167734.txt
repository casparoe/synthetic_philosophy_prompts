In discussions of Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation, it is common to compare the field linguist constructing a manual for "gavagai" to a physicist selecting between empirically equivalent interpretations of quantum mechanics—say, a collapse picture versus an Everettian branching account. The analogy is meant to license a strong conclusion: just as no experiment can adjudicate between those quantum descriptions because they share every observable prediction, no pattern of native assent and dissent can adjudicate between translating "gavagai" as "rabbit," as "undetached rabbit part," or as "rabbit-stage," since the corresponding analytical hypotheses are behaviorally equivalent. From this, defenders often infer that there is literally no fact of the matter about which translation is correct, and that meaning is therefore fixed only relative to an arbitrarily chosen manual.

Audit this analogy in detail. First, lay out the explicit mapping: what in the quantum case corresponds to the native speaker's utterance, to the linguist's analytical hypotheses, to the shared empirical predictions, and to the claim that there is "no fact of the matter"? Then identify precisely where the correspondence breaks down. Does the quantum analogy smuggle in the idea that the competing descriptions are notational variants of a single formalism, and if so, does that apply to "rabbit" versus "rabbit-stage," which appear to differ in ontology, extension, and compositional behavior? Consider whether the practical purpose of a translation manual—enabling successful communication, coordination, and prediction in a shared world—imposes constraints that a physicist's interpretive preference need not satisfy. Also examine whether the analogy quietly assumes that behavioral evidence exhausts all data relevant to meaning, or whether it suppresses internalist, compositional, or pragmatic constraints that might break the symmetry in ways quantum interpretations do not share.

Finally, reach a verdict: does the conclusion—genuine indeterminacy rather than mere practical underdetermination—survive once these disanalogies are on the table? If the quantum-interpretation parallel fails, suggest a better comparison (perhaps to coordinate transformations in geometry, or to model-theoretic equivalence in formal logic) that might capture Quine's intended point without importing misleading metaphysical baggage, and say whether that repaired analogy strengthens or weakens the case for radical translation indeterminacy.
