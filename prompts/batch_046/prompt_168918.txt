After a long argument with a friend about whether anything genuinely survives substantial change, I want to move past the usual Ship of Theseus examples and apply the framework to an actual case. Consider the fire at Notre-Dame de Paris in April 2019 and the decision—subsequently executed—to rebuild the roof and spire according to Eugène Viollet-le-Duc's nineteenth-century design, using largely traditional materials, but embedding modern fire-suppression and structural systems inside. Analyze this reconstruction through endurantism and perdurantism. For the endurantist: in virtue of what—material continuity, spatio-temporal continuity, functional continuity as a cathedral, institutional authorization—does the structure that reopened in late 2024 count as numerically identical to the pre-2019 building, given that a large portion of the physical substance was destroyed and replaced? For the perdurantist: is the post-fire cathedral best understood as a new temporal part of a four-dimensional worm that includes the medieval and nineteenth-century stages, and does the deliberate replication of the spire rather than its replacement with a contemporary design affect whether this stage belongs to the same worm or initiates a branching? Be disciplined about the mapping: identify exactly which features of the reconstruction (material replication, hidden modern infrastructure, uninterrupted liturgical use, state and ecclesiastical authorization) each theory treats as decisive for persistence, and say explicitly where each view is silent or overreaches. Finally, turn the analysis back on the theories: does this case show that persistence conditions for artifacts of this kind necessarily depend on collective intentions about replication in a way that neither standard endurantism nor standard perdurantism anticipates, and if so, does that count as an anomaly or merely a domain they never claimed to cover?
