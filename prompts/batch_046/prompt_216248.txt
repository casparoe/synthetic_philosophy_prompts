As a retired biochemist who has only recently begun working through philosophy of language and classical Chinese thought systematically, I have become absorbed in the long interpretive deadlock over the so-called White Horse Dialogue (Bai Ma Lun) and the broader disputes of the School of Names (Mingjia). For well over a century of modern scholarship, the argument that “a white horse is not a horse” has been read in four incompatible directions: (1) as a logical paradox about class inclusion, in which “horse” denotes all horses but “white horse” denotes only a subset and therefore the terms are not co-extensive; (2) as an early semantic distinction between intension and extension, anticipating Fregean sense and reference; (3) as a metaphysical claim that the universal “horse-ness” and the particularized complex “white-horse-ness” possess different ontological statuses; and (4) as a pragmatic or rhetorical demonstration that ordinary language fails when precise discrimination is required. Each camp treats the same sparse textual fragments as decisive and dismisses the others as anachronistic projection—logicians see metaphysicians importing ontology where none exists; philologists see formalists ignoring linguistic context; metaphysicians see both groups missing the point about universals and particulars.

What I want is not another interpretation but a diagnosis of why this specific disagreement has stalled. Is it genuinely substantive—about whether early Chinese philosophy possessed a concept analogous to sortal dependence—or is it verbal, with “meaning,” “reference,” and “class” doing different labor for logicians, philologists, and metaphysicians? Or are the sides answering different questions under one heading: one about the historical intentions of Gongsun Long, one about the formal structure of the argument irrespective of intent, and one about whether the dialogue genuinely anticipates modern philosophy of language? I am inclined to hypothesize that a hidden formal issue is doing the work—specifically, that scholars are implicitly disagreeing about whether the argument requires second-order logical resources to represent the relation between “horse” and “white horse,” and that this unacknowledged divergence about logical apparatus is being mistaken for a divergence about Chinese thought itself. But that is only a hypothesis.
