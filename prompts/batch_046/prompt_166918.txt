I am designing a single ninety-minute seminar for an interdisciplinary group of first-year graduate students—roughly half with philosophy backgrounds and half with statistics or data-science backgrounds—on the bootstrapping problem and the phenomenon of easy knowledge. None have read the specialist literature on circular justification.

Plan the teaching session: the sequence in which the conceptual components must be introduced so that epistemic circularity emerges as a substantive problem rather than a rhetorical accusation; the one concrete running example that should carry the entire explanation (it should involve a self-calibrating measurement instrument, not the standard thermometer or automobile cases), with explicit criteria for what the example must contain and what it must lack; the misconceptions each subgroup is likely to import—for instance, philosophers conflating all circularity with viciousness and statisticians treating calibration as purely a technical error—together with the precise moment in the sequence where each misconception should be anticipated and defused; one diagnostic question I can pose at the close that reveals whether a student grasps the structural problem rather than merely associating circular reasoning with failure; and which well-known responses or escape routes (such as externalist or reliabilist strategies) I should deliberately exclude from the session, with a brief rationale for why omitting them sharpens rather than distorts the core issue.

The deliverable is a teaching plan—order, carrying example, misconception map, understanding check, and exclusions—not an explanation of the concept itself.
