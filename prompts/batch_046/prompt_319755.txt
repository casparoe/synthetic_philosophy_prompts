After a long argument with a friend about whether moral responsibility is best understood as an interpersonal reactive practice or as something mediated by institutional framing, I want to examine a concrete episode through P.F. Strawson's distinction between reactive attitudes—resentment, moral indignation, gratitude, and love—and the objective attitude in which we treat someone as an object of social policy or clinical management, but with a modification she kept pressing. She proposed a scaling-threshold amendment: reactive attitudes do not collapse into objectivity only when mental incompetence is established, but also when the magnitude of harm exceeds a collective cognitive limit, automatically pushing societies to treat the perpetrator as a risk-object rather than a moral interlocutor; formal institutions such as courts and forensic psychiatry then function either to resist that shift or to formalize it.

Consider the 2011 attacks in Norway and the 2012 trial of Anders Behrik Breivik: the bombing in Oslo and the shootings on Utøya that killed seventy-seven people; the initial psychiatric evaluation diagnosing paranoid schizophrenia; the second evaluation finding narcissistic personality disorder but legal sanity; and the court's determination that he was criminally responsible, resulting in a sentence of twenty-one years of preventive detention. The salient facts are these: the attacks occurred on 22 July 2011; Breivik was tried in 2012; two pairs of court-appointed psychiatrists disagreed on sanity; the court sided with the second pair; and the verdict preserved the framework of criminal punishment rather than indefinite psychiatric commitment.

Analyze this episode through Strawson's framework, augmented by the scaling-threshold idea, in both directions. First, map the actual posture of the relevant actors—the victims' families, the Norwegian public, the psychiatric experts, the judges—indicating who maintained reactive attitudes, who adopted an objective attitude, and whether the sanity debate was primarily about mental competence or partly about managing collective cognitive overload at scale. Second, assess what the episode reveals about the theory. Does the court's refusal to accept insanity, despite the initial diagnosis, demonstrate that institutions can sustain reactive attitudes even for mass atrocity, or does it show that maintaining them requires bureaucratic machinery that Strawson's interpersonal account ignores? Does the scaling-threshold amendment explain cross-national variation in responses to mass violence—some societies defaulting to therapeutic framing, others to punitive framing? Finally, identify where the theory is genuinely silent, for instance on the temporal dynamics of reactive attitudes across years of imprisonment, memorialization, and public remembrance, and state whether this silence is a limitation or an appropriate boundary.

Be precise about the mapping—specify which options the actors faced and what counted as evidence for each posture—and avoid forcing the framework to speak where it offers no guidance. Conclude with an honest judgment about whether the episode confirms, complicates, or undermines the reactive-attitudes account of moral responsibility.
