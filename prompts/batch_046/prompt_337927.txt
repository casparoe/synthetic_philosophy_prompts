I've been retired from experimental physics for about five years now, and lately I've gotten into philosophy of knowledge mostly because my grandson said I think like an engineer. Anyway, I was arguing about the regress problem in some forum and built this chain that seems to show science is built on irrational commitments, which can't be right. (1) For any belief B to be knowledge, there must be available justification, and that justification either traces to another belief, loops in a circle, or stops at a basic belief. (2) In physical measurement we reject both infinite regress and circular calibration -- you can't measure a length with a ruler that itself needs measuring, and you can't verify a scale using only that scale. (3) So if epistemic justification works like measurement, it must bottom out in basic beliefs that don't need further belief-support; call them epistemic anchors. (4) Now here's the step I'm suspicious of: if an anchor doesn't depend on other beliefs for its justification, then other beliefs can't overturn it either, because overturning is just justification running in reverse. (5) So anchors are immune to belief-based revision. (6) But rational science is exactly that kind of revision -- theories change when other theories or data conflict. (7) Therefore holding an anchor is being irrationaly unresponsive to evidence. (8) If foundationalism is true, then all scientific knowledge rests on such irrational commitments. (9) But science is rational. (10) So either knowledge doesn't require justification, or science isn't knowledge -- both feel absurd. Where does this break? I'd really prefer to keep (2) and (3) since the calibration analogy feels right, and I'm willing to drop (4) if that's the rot, but I want to understand what rejecting it costs for coherentism and infinitism too.
