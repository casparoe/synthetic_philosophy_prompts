Consider the well-known problem of rendering Clément Marot’s brief lyric poems—those tightly woven eight-line pieces governed by strict syllabic count and an insistent rhyme scheme—into English without dissolving either their musical architecture or their propositional content. The conventional wisdom holds that a good translator must first grasp the poem’s “meaning” and then, if possible, clothe it in comparable formal garments; failure to preserve rhyme and meter is regrettable but not fatal, since the semantic core survives intact in free verse. I want you to argue the opposite: that in this kind of lyric, formal structure is not an ornamental wrapper around a detachable message but a constitutive element of what is actually said.

More specifically, defend the view that rhyme and meter function as a kind of “forced disambiguation” mechanism. Because the rhyme scheme constrains the translator’s lexical choices to a narrow set of phonetic matches, the original poet’s selection of one rhyme-word over its near-synonyms is not arbitrary ornamentation but a decision that fixes the illocutionary force of the line—determining, for instance, whether a statement is ironic, tender, or arch. Consequently, a translation that abandons the rhyme scheme in order to preserve dictionary-level synonymy does not merely lose elegance; it replaces one determinate speech act with another, underdetermined one. Push this further by developing a “formal pragmatics” of translation evaluation: argue that we ought to judge a rendering of Marot not by the proximity of individual word meanings, but by whether the English version reproduces the same pattern of formally compelled lexical choices, thereby preserving the original’s determinacy rather than its literal paraphrase. Do not treat this as a defense of literalism in general—limit your argument to the specific case of highly constrained short-form lyric, and address the obvious objection that English rhyme sounds artificially sing-song compared to French.
