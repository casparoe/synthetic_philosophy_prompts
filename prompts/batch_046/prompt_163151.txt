During a seminar on algorithmic ethics, two philosophers debated whether a specific multi-constraint triage algorithm demonstrates that genuine moral dilemmas are possible. The system in question must allocate scarce intensive-care resources under four simultaneous rules: never deny treatment solely on chronological age, never prioritize on the basis of ability to pay, never separate dependent family members across facilities, and always maximize expected quality-adjusted life-years saved. The designers acknowledge that, in the parameter range where demand exceeds supply by more than forty percent, every feasible allocation violates at least one of these rules, and no meta-rule determines which violation is least severe. Philosopher A argued that this is not merely a tragic choice but a genuine moral dilemma: the decision procedure is rationally compelled to violate a moral constraint regardless of which allocation it selects, and the wrongness is structural rather than derivative from ignorance or error. Philosopher B responded that this mistakes computational intractability for moral ontology: if the rules are genuinely lexical, then the correct response is not to lament an unavoidable wrong but to recognize that the framing itself is defective, and that one constraint must yield to another according to a correct ordering that the designers have failed to specify. A countered that B's solution presupposes precisely what is in question—namely, that there always exists a correct ordering—whereas in this case no such ordering is available ex ante, so whichever rule is sacrificed leaves genuine moral residue. B then distinguished moral residue, which accompanies any tragic choice, from the existence of a dilemma: residue shows only that we suffer from having acted, not that there was no right action available; if a single right answer exists but is psychologically or institutionally costly, we still face no philosophical dilemma. A replied that B defines dilemma away by fiat, committing to a single-right-answer moral theory that the case is meant to challenge rather than assume. They asked me to adjudicate. Please go through their exchange point by point: rate the strength of each move, say where each philosopher is correct and where they conflate distinct issues, and determine whether the algorithmic case genuinely supports the possibility of moral dilemmas or whether B's dissolution strategy succeeds.
