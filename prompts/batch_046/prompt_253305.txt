I've been trying to map the implicit normative architecture behind different university-funding regimes, and I'm struck by how little the usual signaling-versus-human-capital framing captures what's actually going on institutionally. In Germany, tuition fees were introduced experimentally in a handful of Länder in the 2000s and then abolished across all states by the mid-2010s, leaving only modest semester contributions; funding now flows mainly from state budgets, with the rationale usually appealing to Bildung and to the social-state obligations embedded in the Basic Law, treating university access as an unconditional entitlement tied to citizenship rather than to expected productivity. In Denmark and Sweden, tuition is free for citizens and EU residents, but the state also provides direct study grants—functionally a negative income tax conditional on enrollment—financed through general taxation that falls on present earners regardless of whether graduates eventually out-earn non-graduates. By contrast, in the United States and the United Kingdom, students are positioned primarily as individual borrowers: American federal and private debt instruments place repayment risk on the graduate, while the UK system is administratively structured around an income-contingent repayment mechanism that accrues interest and carries credit-reporting consequences, even when officially described as a graduate contribution rather than a loan.

The puzzle is that these are not merely different budget lines; they appear to embed incompatible ontologies of the student and of education itself. The German and Nordic models seem to presuppose that education is a public good that transforms civic and intellectual capacities in ways that resist ex ante pricing, and that intergenerational obligations run from the present tax base to the young irrespective of ex post economic returns. The Anglo-American model, even in its income-contingent variant, seems to presuppose that education is mainly a private investment in human capital, that risk should be individualized according to expected returns, and that the state's legitimate role is to correct capital-market failures rather than to guarantee a formative entitlement.

But I'm unsure whether this reconstruction is coherent, or whether the practices contain internal tensions that undermine it. So: reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments—concerning merit, desert, citizenship, intergenerational justice, and the ontology of the educated person—that make each institutional design intelligible. Distinguish what is strictly presupposed from what is merely compatible. Identify specific inconsistencies within each system, such as the tension between Germany's early tracking mechanisms—Gymnasium and Abitur sorting—and its universalist rhetoric of Bildung, or between the Nordic study-grant universalism and implicit expectations about graduate productivity. Finally, if we replaced the underlying view with a straightforwardly probabilistic, human-capital-only framework, which concrete features—tracking structures, grant design, tax incidence, repayment mechanisms—would necessarily change, and which could survive?
