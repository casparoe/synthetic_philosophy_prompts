I am preparing to defend a thesis I am calling "institutional fiduciary climate obligations" at a workshop next month, and I need a Q&A prep sheet rather than a polished script. I discussed the framing with colleagues in democratic theory and constitutional law, and they warned that the audience will see it either as democratic overreach disguised in procedural language or as philosophically incoherent about representing people who do not yet exist.

The audience is precise: about fifteen participants—eight constitutional lawyers skeptical of judicial activism and protective of legislative supremacy; four democratic theorists who work on representation, intergenerational ethics, and the non-identity problem; two climate-policy scholars who think legal mechanisms are already too sluggish; and one philosopher of law familiar with fiduciary doctrines but not climate politics. They are institutionalists, not activists; they care about who has authority to decide, whether future generations can be beneficiaries without being present claimants, whether "fiduciary consistency" is just substantive climate policy in procedural clothing, and whether courts have the epistemic competence to assess long-term continuity. They will ask in their own vocabularies—about democratic legitimacy, proxy representation, institutional competence, and hidden assumptions about harm—not about carbon-pricing design or adaptation engineering.

Please prepare the objections I will actually face, ordered by how likely they are and how damaging each could be, phrased the way a constitutional lawyer or democratic theorist would put them. Include the objection I am most hoping nobody raises—likely something about the non-identity problem undermining the idea of a harmed beneficiary altogether—and tell me what I should concede up front rather than defend. Also briefly sketch the argumentative move connecting standard fiduciary law, where trustees owe duties to identifiable beneficiaries, to climate governance, where beneficiaries are unidentifiable future people, since that bridge is where the argument is most exposed.
