I retired from condensed-matter physics a few years back and have been poking around philosophy of language, mostly because I got obsessed with that old idea about translating Marot's rondeaux — Le Ton beau de Marot and all that. Anyway, I think I walked myself into an argument that proves way too much, and I need someone to point out which step is actually rotten.

Here is the chain:

(1) In poetry, what makes a translation different from a brand-new poem is that it preserves the formal architecture of the original — the rhyme scheme, the syllable count, the placement of the caesura, and especially those repeating refrain lines in a rondeau.

(2) So let's define a formal vector F(P) for any poem P: it is just the tuple of those structural features. If F(P) equals F(Q), then Q sits in the same equivilence class as P with respect to form.

(3) Now, if Q is a translation of P, then preserving F is not merely nice but necessary; otherwise you have just written a paraphrase or an adaptation.

(4) But lexical meaning is continuous — you can slide words around, substitute synonyms, change metaphors — while F is discrete. That means for any given F, there are astronomically many poems with that same vector but totally different semantic content.

(5) Take one of Marot's rondeaux, the farewell-to-the-court one — if we keep the rondeau's refrane structure and rhyme pattern intact, we could rewrite every line to talk about superconductivity, or about my old lab's cooling system, and the formal vector wouldn't budge.

(6) So by (3), that superconductivity poem is a perfectly valid translation of Marot, even though it shares no theme, image, or word with the original.

(7) In fact, since any poem can be rewritten line-by-line to match the syllable and rhyme demands of Marot's form, every English poem is technically a translation of that rondeau — we just haven't mapped the equivilence yet.

(8) That can't be right. Translation has to involve some kind of semantic bridge; otherwise the word translation just means shares rhyme scheme.

(9) So (3) must be false: formal preservation isn't necessary for translation after all.

(10) But if (3) is false, then there is no principled reason to prefer a tightly constrained version — the kind that tries to keep Marot's music alive — over a loose paraphrase that ignores the refrane structure. The whole project collapses into say what Marot said in your own words.

Which step do I actually reject? I really want to hang on to (3) because without it I don't see why anyone bothers with the formal headache. But if I keep it, I have to swallow (7), and that feels like giving up on meaning entirely. Is there an equivocation hiding in (2) or (4) — maybe I'm sliding from necessary to sufficient? Or is the problem that I'm treating semantic content as infinitely malleable when it's actually constrained by the form in ways I haven't accounted for?

Please point to the exact step, explain why it looked okay from a scientist's habit of defining equivalence classes, and tell me what it costs me to drop it.
