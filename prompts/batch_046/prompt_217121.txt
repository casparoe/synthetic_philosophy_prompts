Draft the joint statement that a conjugal-teleological camp and a revisionist-contractual camp on same-sex marriage and the purpose of civil marriage could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The conjugal-teleological camp holds that civil marriage is essentially ordered to the spousal union of a man and a woman, with procreation and child-rearing as its normative core, though it allows that infertile or elderly opposite-sex couples can still realize this union. The revisionist-contractual camp holds that civil marriage is essentially a legal framework for securing interdependence, mutual care, and economic coordination between adults, with no essential connection to biological sex, gender, or reproductive potential.

I want the deliverable structured as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: specific propositions about the history of marriage law, about the state's legitimate interests in family regulation, about the welfare of children raised in various household structures, and about the logical distinction between descriptive and normative definitions of marriage, that both sides can endorse without reservation. Second, a disagreements table: for each residual dispute, state the exact claim in wording the conjugal-teleological side would accept for its own view, then the exact claim in wording the revisionist-contractual side would accept for its own view, paired neutrally. The table should cover at least: (i) whether the state has a non-instrumental interest in recognizing sexual complementarity; (ii) whether extending marriage to same-sex couples necessarily transforms the institution's meaning; and (iii) whether the availability of domestic-partnership regimes for non-romantic pairs undermines the argument that marriage should be gender-neutral. Third, identify the cruxes—the sub-questions such that settling them in one direction would cause at least one camp to revise its position. I suspect two cruxes are whether naturalness has intrinsic normative weight in institutional design, and whether the predictive social science about child outcomes in same-sex households is robust enough to ground policy distinctions. For each crux, present a concrete hypothetical case or empirical finding that both camps agree is relevant, and record in advance what each camp predicts the case or finding would show about the crux. Fourth, indicate whether any part of the dispute is largely verbal—e.g., whether both sides actually agree on all empirical predictions but assign different weights to values like tradition and equality—and if so, specify which part and why. Write in a formal, polished register; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; and do not invoke highly specialized legal doctrines or obscure empirical studies that a well-informed generalist model could not be expected to know.
