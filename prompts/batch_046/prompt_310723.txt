hey, I've been reading up on the seperateness of persons objection in utilitarian debates and I keep running into a move I don't have a word for. A person will reject the classic "harvest organs from one to save five" case, insisting you can't pool welfare across genuinely different lives like it's one big account. But then the same person will happily aggregate costs and benefits across generations of a family - "we should accept some hardship now so our descendants thrive" - or across the stages of one long life, treating "me now" and "me at 80" as parts of a single welfare-stream that can be traded off. In all three cases you've got welfare sitting on both sides of a boundary: between strangers, between ancestors and descendants, between temporal stages. The formal question is always whether that boundary blocks addition. But the seperateness objection seems to switch on and off based on whether people can tell a continuity story - genetic, narrative, institutional - rather than on any actual metaphysical difference. I know Rawls talked about the seperateness of persons and there's the non-identity problem nearby, but neither is exactly this pattern of selectively applying the block. Does this have a name in the literature? If not, what's the cleanest way to carve it out from ordinary person-affecting views and from plain inconsistency? I don't want just a label - I want to know what makes the boundary count as "real" in one case and "illusory" in the other, and where the edges are.
