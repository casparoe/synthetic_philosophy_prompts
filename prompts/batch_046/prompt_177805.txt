I have been working through a problem in my philosophy of probability course that concerns whether the principle of indifference can survive in continuous spaces, and after a seminar discussion I wrote out an argument that now strikes me as suspicious: each premise seems defensible in isolation, yet together they appear to show that objective Bayesian reasoning with continuous parameters is impossible, which is clearly too strong. I would be grateful if you could examine the argument step by step, tell me precisely which numbered claim I ought to reject, explain why that claim looked plausible despite being the weak link, and then set out what the standard responses—those who defend an objective interpretation of probability, those who reject the principle entirely for uncountable spaces, and those who treat the paradox as showing only that our concept of randomness is ambiguous—each have to pay in order to reject it. Please reply in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and write in British English.

The argument runs as follows. (1) The principle of indifference requires that if our total evidence does not discriminate among n mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive possibilities, we assign each a probability of 1/n. (2) In Bertrand's chord problem we seek the probability that a chord drawn at random inside a circle of radius R exceeds the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle in length. (3) The circle possesses full rotational symmetry about its centre, so for any given chord there exists a rotation that maps it onto any other chord sharing its exact length; consequently, within each length-class the members are perfectly symmetric with respect to all physical properties of the figure. (4) By (1), then, every chord within a given length-class should receive equal probability, and since our ignorance extends across all length-classes as well—there being no epistemic feature that favours one length over another—the uniform assignment should extend across the entire uncountable set of possible chords. (5) If every individual chord receives equal probability and there are uncountably many, each receives probability zero; hence the probability that the chord is longer than the triangle side is either undefined or zero, which contradicts the well-established result that the answer depends on the selection method and is one of approximately one-third, one-half, or one-fourth. (6) One might reply that (1) applies only to discrete partitions, but any discretisation of the chord space requires selecting a parameterisation—uniform over endpoint pairs on the circumference, over midpoints within the area, or over perpendicular distances from the centre—each of which converges to a different value as the mesh refines to zero. (7) Applying (1) at the meta-level, we have no reason to prefer one parameterisation to the others, so they should each receive weight one-third; averaging the three results yields thirteen thirty-sixths. (8) Yet this meta-application can be iterated: each parameterisation can be expressed in infinitely many coordinate representations, and applying indifference among these representations dissolves the weights into an infinite regress without a stable limit. (9) Therefore either we abandon uniform priors in all continuous settings—which would undermine statistical mechanics and standard Bayesian inference—or we accept that the principle of indifference collapses into arbitrariness whenever the space is uncountable. (10) Given that physicists routinely employ uniform distributions over continuous variables, (9) proves far too much. Which step should I sacrifice? I should prefer to keep (1) and (3) intact if possible, but I am prepared to abandon whichever premise is least damaging; please explain the cost of each choice, including whether rejecting a particular step forces us to abandon objective probability altogether or merely to restrict its domain.
