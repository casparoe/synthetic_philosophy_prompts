Consider the institutional architecture that has emerged in response to the replication crisis across psychology and biomedicine: funding bodies and journals now routinely mandate preregistration of hypotheses and analysis plans for confirmatory studies, treating post-hoc analytical changes as serious methodological breaches; yet these same institutions continue to evaluate researchers primarily by publication volume in venues that apply null-hypothesis significance testing with conventional fixed-alpha thresholds, systematically privilege original “positive” findings over replication attempts regardless of outcome, and rarely require Bayesian power analyses or posterior-probability reporting in grant evaluation. Meanwhile, replication studies—whether they succeed or fail—are typically relegated to lower-prestige outlets and receive diminished funding priority. What philosophy of statistical evidence and scientific knowledge does this hybrid practice presuppose? Reconstruct its implicit epistemology: does it treat the p-value as a direct measure of evidential strength for a specific claim, or merely as a long-run error-control filter? Does it presuppose that discovery and confirmation operate under different rational norms, with the former licensed to be exploratory and probabilistic and the latter governed by strict frequentist rules, and if so, does that normative division map coherently onto any distinction between Bayesian belief-updating and frequentist error-rate control? Is the architecture internally consistent, or do its parts quietly depend on rival views—perhaps a frequentist criterion for the validity of individual studies combined with an implicit Bayesian intuition that replication should update collective confidence in a literature? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and then specify what would have to change in peer-review standards, publication incentives, and funding criteria if this implicit philosophy of evidence were replaced by a consistently Bayesian or consistently frequentist account.
