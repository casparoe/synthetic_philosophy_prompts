I was revisiting Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation—the essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life—after a long discussion thread about whether longtermist reasoning implicitly relies on a monumental orientation toward civilizational progress, and whether our current digital archival practices have turned the antiquarian impulse into something that stifles rather than sustains living culture. The tripartite schema—monumental, antiquarian, and critical history—seems to me to be doing far less work in contemporary philosophy of history than it could, but I am unsure which extensions or tensions within it are genuinely open and worth a small team's limited time.

Suppose you are advising a research group of three philosophers and one historian, with two years of funding and no mandate to produce original archival discoveries, on where to direct their conceptual labor. I see three competing directions, and I want you to rank them with a defended agenda rather than a survey. First, whether the critical and monumental modes are necessarily antagonistic at the institutional level—can a culture design collective-memory practices (museums, curricula, AI training corpora) that preserve monumental exemplars without suppressing the critical dissolution of false heritage? Second, whether scaling the antiquarian impulse to algorithmic storage and large-scale retrieval transforms it into what we might call algorithmic antiquarianism—a mode that preserves every trace but thereby paralyzes the will to act—such that Nietzsche's diagnosis needs structural revision rather than mere application. Third, whether the framework suppresses a prospective or constructive mode of historical consciousness—one oriented not toward the past as model, archive, or target of critique, but toward an open future that uses the past instrumentally—which modern longtermist ethics and constitutional design might require. For each: defend its upstream or downstream relation to the others; specify whether it is tractable for philosophers without empirical ML expertise; identify what evidence or argument over the next year would cause you to reorder the priorities; and state clearly which one you would deprioritize on the grounds that resolving it would not materially change how we think about cultural policy, institutional design, or personal reasoning about the past.
