I was recently appointed senior acquisitions curator at a mid-size museum of modern art, and I now must sign off on acquisition recommendations within seventy-two hours of receiving a dossier, working with two associate curators and a direct-experience budget that permits only twelve in-person viewings per quarter. Because our recommendations depend heavily on expert testimony—critic reviews, peer assessments, and condition reports—I need a reusable decision procedure that translates the philosophy of aesthetic testimony into an operational checklist my team can run without specialized philosophical training. The procedure should distinguish thin aesthetic properties, which are structural, comparative, and survive redescription, from thick properties, which are phenomenal, emotive, or indexical, and it should apply a substitution stress-test: if replacing the demonstrative or proper-name anchor in an expert's judgment—such as "this particular melancholy" or "the way this line breaks here"—with a definite description eliminates or weakens the epistemic warrant, then the testimony is referentially fragile. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates in a clear refusal, and where it must hand the decision to me because the remaining question concerns institutional values rather than epistemic risk. Then apply the procedure to three proposals: first, a large-scale color-field painting praised for "this specific blue's acoustic depth that transforms the ambient space," available at a favorable price but requiring a recommendation before travel is possible; second, a geometric print series described solely in terms of bilateral symmetry, chroma contrast, and scale relations; and third, a sculptural installation where one expert praises "the haunting quality of the patina" while another calls the surface treatment technically conventional, leaving the value claim contested. In each case, state the procedure's output, and explicitly identify the case where the recommendation conflicts with what I am likely to want. Finally, say what the procedure cannot do—where it will misfire, which distinctions it collapses for usability, and what argument or evidence would lead you to revise the flowchart's central distinction between substitutable and non-substitutable aesthetic testimony. Please close with a brief note on what would change your mind about that central distinction.
