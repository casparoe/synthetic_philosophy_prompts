As someone who spent thirty years in experimental physics before turning recently to philosophy, I have been trying to clarify my own intuitions about second-order knowledge—specifically whether knowing something automatically puts one in a position to know that one knows it, the thesis often called the KK principle—by testing them against scenarios that resemble actual laboratory practice rather than abstract belief boxes. Here are my verdicts on five cases, and I want to know whether a single coherent principle captures them, or whether I must abandon one or more.

In the first case I examine a stable spectrometer in optimal conditions and correctly identify a spectral line. I not only know that the line is present, I also know that I know it; both orders of belief are correct and well-founded. In the second case the same instrument is operating near the edge of its calibrated temperature range. The thermal drift is continuous and imperceptible to me in the moment; my reading is still correct, and my first-order belief is true and justified by the same training that served me in the first case. Yet my verdict is that although I know the line is present, I do not know that I know it, because I am not in a position to discriminate this marginal state from one just past the boundary where reliability fails.

In the third case I run a commercial statistical package that applies a correction I do not understand. The correction happens to suit my data, and my conclusion is true. I believe the conclusion and I believe I know it. Here my verdict is that I lack first-order knowledge altogether, since I do not grasp the method, so the KK principle does not apply; but I also want to treat my false confidence in second-order knowledge as a separate failure. In the fourth case I have built a custom data pipeline that I understand completely. Unbeknownst to me, a storage error has altered one intermediate value, yet by coincidence the final result remains correct. I believe the result, I believe I understand the pipeline, and therefore I believe I know the result and that I know I know. My verdict is that neither first-order nor second-order knowledge obtains, despite the appearance of mastery.

The fifth case is the one that troubles me. I am using an instrument whose resolving power degrades continuously with ambient light. At a particular luminosity the image is still just sharp enough for correct identification, and I make the right call. Crucially, I have previously studied the calibration curve, so I know exactly where the boundary lies, and I have checked the light meter, which reads just inside the safe zone—though unknown to me the meter is miscalibrated by exactly the margin that puts me on the wrong side. I still correctly identify the feature. My instinct is to say that I know it is present, but I do not know that I know, because my check was undermined. But this feels hard to reconcile with the thermal-drift case, where I lacked any explicit awareness of the boundary. If explicit awareness combined with a hidden instrumental failure still blocks second-order knowledge, then the absence of such awareness in the thermal case should presumably block it even more strongly, not less—which seems to conflict with my intuition that in the thermal case I at least retain first-order knowledge. So I have a genuine tension.

Is there a principle—perhaps appealing to safety, sensitivity, or the denial of luminosity for knowledge conditions—that delivers all or most of these verdicts? If not, which one should I overturn? And whatever principle emerges, what should it predict about a case I have not listed: a scientist who correctly identifies a phenomenon using an instrument she has never personally calibrated, relying entirely on a colleague's certification that she has read and understood, where the colleague's report is accurate but the colleague herself was misled about one parameter?

I am particularly interested in whether the principle you propose requires me to reject the KK principle outright, or only to restrict its domain, and I want you to address the tension between my luminosity cases directly rather than smoothing it away.
