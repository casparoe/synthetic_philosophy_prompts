The empirical psychology is not in dispute: both sides accept that framing effects, default architectures, and attentional bottlenecks shape behavior, and both accept that autonomy carries weight. They also converge on clear extreme cases—no one treats a preference formed under explicit deception as legitimate, and no one advocates overriding a stable, reflectively endorsed life-plan. Yet in the intermediate zone—imagine a user who, after six months of personalized ranking, reports satisfaction with the results but has never been exposed to the counterfactual ranking—the camps have not moved in years. One side treats sustained engagement and reported satisfaction as evidence of an authentic preference that has legitimately adapted; the other treats the same data as evidence of path-dependent lock-in that masks an underlying bias.

My own working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not mainly empirical but turns on a criterion I will call counterfactual preference independence: whether a preference counts as genuinely autonomous only if it would survive the agent’s awareness of the architectural mechanisms that produced it. The preference-respecters appear to deny that such robustness is necessary; the bias-correcters appear to treat it as the core normative condition. If that is correct, the stall might be verbal—each side using “preference” to mean something structurally different—or it might be that they are answering different questions under one heading, one about the ethics of system design and one about the metaphysics of personal identity across adaptive environments. Alternatively, there may be a hidden formal problem: whether any non-circular baseline for “unbiased preference” can be defined in an adaptive system without appealing to a pre-intervention psychological state that never actually existed.
