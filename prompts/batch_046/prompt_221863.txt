I've recently had to decide whether to file a Kirchenaustritt in Germany, and comparing that decision with my upbringing in the US and time spent in France and England has left me with a cluster of views on church-state relations that I have not tested for consistency. Here is roughly where I have landed.

(1) I think Germany's Kirchensteuer is defensible: membership is genuinely voluntary because Kirchenaustritt is legally guaranteed, the state acts only as an administrative collection agent for private associations, and there is no meaningful state endorsement of religion.

(2) I think religious communities should not be granted Körperschaften des öffentlichen Rechts status, since that confers legal privileges—direct state verification of membership, internal labor autonomy, and tax exemptions—that secular worldviews and philosophical associations cannot access through equivalent procedures.

(3) I think France's 1905 separation law was correct to end direct state salaries for clergy, but wrong both in its confiscation of church property and in the subsequent laïcité interpretation—exemplified by the 2004 school symbol ban—that treats visible religious identity in public institutions as a threat to neutrality rather than as protected expression.

(4) I think the US Establishment Clause should prohibit direct government funding of religious exercise, but should permit neutral, generally available schemes—such as school vouchers or social-service contracts—where religious providers compete with secular ones on genuinely equal terms.

(5) I think religious institutions that accept such neutral public funds must be subject to the same anti-discrimination and transparency obligations as secular contractors, with no special religious exemptions from labor or equality law.

(6) I think England's established Church is objectionable not because ceremonial religious acknowledgment is always wrong—the monarch's formal religious role is harmless—but because establishment structurally privileges Anglicanism through episcopal representation in Parliament and state maintenance of cathedrals.

My question is whether this package hangs together. Specifically: does my defense of Kirchensteuer in (1) depend on the very public-law status I reject in (2), and if so, which one has to weaken? Does my criticism of aggressive laïcité in (3) sit awkwardly with my willingness in (4) to allow religious schools to receive state-linked funding, or is that tension only apparent once I distinguish exclusion from neutrality? Is there a genuine conflict between (4) and (5) about whether equal funding combined with full regulatory compliance actually preserves religious autonomy or effectively secularizes religious providers? And is my distinction in (6) between structural establishment and ceremonial acknowledgment stable, or does it collapse once I recognize that even symbolic establishment shapes civic identity and norms?

Please audit the set: order the tensions by severity, flag which ones dissolve on inspection, indicate for each what would have to give (and what that costs), and do not lecture me on church-state theory in general. I want to know whether I am helping myself to incompatible motivations, and if so, which commitment is the cheapest to revise.
