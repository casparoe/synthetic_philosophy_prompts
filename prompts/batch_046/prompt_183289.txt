I'm putting together the take-home final for my philosophy of property seminar — mostly upper-level undergrads with a couple grad students auditing — and I need an essay question that gets into the weeds of intellectual property without letting students just regurgitate Lockean labor-mixing or the usual utilitarian "incentive to innovate" script. I was talking with a friend about this weird middle case: an engineer builds a working device by combining three old public-domain mechanical principles, but the actual spatial configuration only works because of embodied tacit know-how that can't be fully written down in a patent claim. I'm tentatively calling it the "tacit configuration problem." I want the exam question to ask whether philosophical ownership attaches to the functional idea of the arrangement, or just to the physical object, and students have to work through both a Hegelian personality-based view (property as expression of will) and a commons critique that says rearranging shared resources doesn't create private title. They need to see exactly why the legal idea-expression dichotomy doesn't solve the philosophical problem here, since we're talking about functional configurations rather than expressive works. The question should be unanswerable by summary alone — they need to argue whether tacit knowledge breaks the standard ownership framework.

Please write the exam prompt itself, a model answer around 600 words, and a marking scheme that seperates an A answer (must identify the tension between articulable claims and embodied know-how, and must show Hegel's account struggles with non-expressive functional innovations) from a B answer (just describes both theories and applies them mechanically). Also list one specific mistake that should definately cost marks, like treating patent eligibility rules as if they settle the normative philosophical issue. And please, no bibliography or citation requests in the exam prompt — I want this usable for students without full database access.
