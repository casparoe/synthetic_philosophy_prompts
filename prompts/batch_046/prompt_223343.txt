Here is roughly where I have landed on decision-theoretic risk, long-horizon rationality, and climate-related uncertainty, and I want to know whether the package is jointly tenable or whether I am smuggling in an inconsistency. First, I accept a risk-weighted expected-utility framework: outcomes are weighted not only by probability but by a risk coefficient that scales non-linearly with the severity of potential harm, so that a small chance of catastrophic damage receives more deliberative weight than the linear sum of many independent small risks of comparable expected value. Second, I think this framework is particularly appropriate for technological and climate policy, precisely because standard expected-utility models systematically undervalue structurally uncertain tail risks. Third, I hold that any rational risk-weighting must be dynamically consistent: if I adopt a multi-stage plan, I should not revise my risk coefficients downward merely because I have survived an earlier stage without incident. Fourth, I reject formulations of the precautionary principle that treat unknown or poorly quantified risks as effectively infinite-cost barriers, since that collapses practical reasoning into paralysis. Fifth, I maintain that the risk coefficients themselves should be calibrated to the decision-maker's epistemic position—specifically, to the accumulated model uncertainty about causal pathways—rather than to any objective frequency distribution. Is this consistent? In particular, does my rejection of the precautionary principle survive once dynamic consistency is combined with epistemic calibration in domains where model uncertainty compounds over long time horizons? Or am I implicitly relying in the climate-policy case on exactly the kind of paralysis-inducing weighting that my fourth commitment forbids? And is there any hidden tension between treating risk weights as epistemically calibrated and treating them as stable across stages? Order the tensions by severity, indicate which commitment is cheapest to revise, and flag any apparent conflict that actually dissolves on closer inspection.
