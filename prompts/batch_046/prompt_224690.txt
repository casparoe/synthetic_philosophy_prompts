I have just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend about Daniel Dennett’s conception of the self as a centre of narrative gravity, and I find myself wanting to crystallise our disagreement in verse rather than in prose. We were debating whether that narrative centre is merely a descriptive fiction that organises autobiographical memory, or whether it exercises a kind of prescriptive authority—what I called a narrative debt—binding future actions to the coherence of the story it has already told, so that deviation feels like an ontological default rather than a simple change of mind. My friend insisted this collapses the self into a mere coherence-maximising algorithm, stripping away the autonomy that makes moral agency meaningful; I countered that the gravitational pull is precisely what generates the conditions for autonomy, since without narrative continuity there is no agent to be free. I should like you to write an English sonnet—fourteen lines, iambic pentameter, rhyme scheme ABAB CDCD EFEF GG—with a volta at line nine that marks the shift from the descriptive to the prescriptive problem. The poem should treat the self not as a substance but as an organising centre, yet it should render the tension between coherence and autonomy structurally: the rhyme and argument should feel as though they are being pulled toward resolution by the rhyme scheme itself, even as the content resists it. The language should be precise enough that someone familiar with Dennett’s work recognises the theory being engaged rather than merely name-checked, and the turn at line nine should embody the moment the narrative centre demands repayment from its own future. Please write in British English.
