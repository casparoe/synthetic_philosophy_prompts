I'm delivering the opening address at the inauguration of the Châtelet Center for Natural Philosophy and Human Flourishing, an interdisciplinary institute that houses physics research alongside philosophy and psychology of well-being. The audience is sharply divided: on one side sit historians of Enlightenment science and philosophers who know Émilie du Châtelet's Discours sur le bonheur in detail; on the other sit university benefactors, several of whom are tech philanthropists who funded the center on the premise that AI will soon automate scientific discovery, plus administrators and scientists with no background in her work. I need a speech of about twenty minutes that uses du Châtelet's framework—her argument that happiness requires both rigorous study, which dissolves harmful illusions, and the deliberate, reasoned preservation of other necessary illusions, especially about being loved, about ambition, and about the enduring value of one's intellectual labor—to justify why natural philosophy and the study of human flourishing must share an institution. The philosophical structure should genuinely constrain what I can say: I cannot claim that knowledge alone guarantees happiness, nor that happiness requires abandoning reason for pure sentiment, and I must address—without alienating the donors—the implicit assumption that discovery can be fully automated and that human flourishing is separable from the labor of understanding. In particular, I want to invoke her often-overlooked claim that the pleasure of sustained study is structurally unlike sensory pleasure, since it depends on effort and on maintaining the illusion that one's work will outlast one's own lifetime, an illusion that must be actively chosen rather than passively received. Write the speech. Separate what is well-established in scholarship on du Châtelet from interpretations that remain genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any disputed reading of her work as the consensus view.
