Two members of my study group have been arguing about Moritz Schlick's essay on the meaning of life, and I am writing this up as an outside observer with no stake in the outcome. Please adjudicate the exchange point by point, saying exactly who is right about which claim and what both are overlooking.

Maya begins by summarizing Schlick's position: the demand for an ultimate purpose belongs to the logic of work, not play, and life becomes meaningful only when it is lived as absorption in activities pursued for their own sake. She insists this is a conceptual clarification, not an empirical generalization; Schlick is not measuring how often people play, but showing that the concept of meaning must be reconstructed away from teleology once transcendent goals are rejected.

Jonas objects on verificationist grounds. Since Schlick is a logical empiricist, Jonas argues, any cognitive claim about the meaning of life must be empirically verifiable. Yet Schlick distinguishes play from work solely by the agent's attitude, not by observable behavior—the same hours of musical practice can be exam preparation or joyful improvisation. If the difference is purely internal, Jonas says, Schlick's distinction is unobservable and therefore cognitively empty by his own criterion of significance.

Maya replies that Jonas misapplies the verification principle: Schlick targets theoretical claims about the world, not the framing conditions of value or first-person descriptions of engagement. She proposes that the distinction can be saved by temporal structure rather than by single actions. Work is repeatedly interrupted and redirected toward an external end, whereas play forms a self-sustaining loop—what she terms an autotelic temporal pattern. This pattern is in principle observable over the long run, so Schlick's distinction need not be private.

Jonas concedes that temporal patterns are observable, but he presses two further objections. First, if the distinction depends on long-run structure, then Schlick's association of youth with meaningful play becomes empirically dubious, since adolescents are often intensely goal-oriented. So Schlick must either abandon that association or retreat to subjective attitude, reviving the privacy problem. Second, Jonas argues that the boundary is porous: scientific inquiry, for example, can become autotelic. Once we admit this, Schlick's binary between work and play collapses into a single factor—the presence or absence of an atelic orientation. At that point, Jonas claims, the concept of play does no philosophical work; it is merely a nostalgic label for having an atelic orientation, and Schlick's verificationism should have led him to dismiss the original question as a pseudo-problem rather than solve it.

Maya responds that Jonas is demanding a moral psychology Schlick never intended to provide. Schlick's aim is conceptual therapy: asking for life's ultimate purpose is like asking for the color of a number. The autotelic orientation is not a hidden mental state but a structural feature of how activities are integrated into a life. She grants, however, that Schlick's sharp binary may be too rigid—some externally directed goals, such as understanding a phenomenon, can be pursued in ways that remain internally sustained—so the work-play distinction might be a matter of degree rather than kind.

Jonas accepts the porosity but argues that this concession undermines Maya's defense. If work can be gradually transformed into play, then meaning tracks atelic engagement wherever it occurs, not a special category called play. Schlick's rhetoric about youthful play then becomes an unnecessary embellishment. Both agree that Schlick's verificationist commitments sit uneasily with his normative-sounding conclusion, but they disagree on whether the tension is fatal or merely productive.

Please deliver a point-by-point adjudication: evaluate whether Jonas's verificationist challenge succeeds against Schlick's conceptual framing; assess whether Maya's autotelic temporal pattern genuinely saves the distinction from privacy; determine whether the porosity of the work-play boundary undermines Schlick's position or only refines it; and identify what, if anything, both parties miss about how Schlick's own epistemology interacts with his claim that the question of life's meaning is dissolved rather than answered.
