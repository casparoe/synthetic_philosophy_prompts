Consider the following argument about whether germs, climate systems, and volcanic events can properly be called 'historical actors'—entities that do not merely form background conditions but genuinely shape the trajectory of human events.

(1) To count as a historical actor, an entity must possess specific, non-substitutable properties that figure irreducibly in the best causal-narrative explanation of a macro-historical outcome; if substituting a different state of that entity yields a different historical trajectory, it is not mere background.

(2) The 1815 Tambora eruption's particular magnitude and sulfur load are non-substitutable: a smaller eruption would not have produced the 1816 climate anomaly, the subsequent agricultural collapse, or the altered epidemiological pathways that shaped 19th-century European politics.

(3) The bacterium Vibrio cholerae exhibits specific virulence and transmission dynamics that are non-substitutable in explaining the 1854 Broad Street outbreak and the sanitary reforms that followed.

(4) Anthropogenic climate forcing in the late 20th and 21st centuries exhibits specific radiative properties that are non-substitutable in explaining Arctic ice retreat and the associated shifts in migration patterns and resource conflicts.

(5) Therefore, Tambora, Vibrio cholerae, and anthropogenic climate forcing all satisfy criterion (1) and qualify as historical actors.

(6) But human agents also depend on non-substitutable background conditions—atmospheric oxygen, gravitational constants, neurochemical configurations—without which their actions could not occur.

(7) Applying (1) consistently, these background conditions also qualify as historical actors.

(8) Consequently, the category 'historical actor' expands to include virtually every physical condition that enables human action, rendering it useless for distinguishing agents from conditions.

(9) To prevent this collapse, one might restrict agency to entities capable of feedback-responsive or goal-directed adaptation.

(10) Yet climate systems exhibit feedback-responsive dynamics (radiative balance adjustments), and microbial populations exhibit evolutionary adaptation; so the restriction fails to exclude them without also excluding paradigmatic human agents who operate under evolutionary and ecological constraints.

(11) Thus, either we deny non-human entities any agential status—an apparently arbitrary anthropocentric restriction—or we accept that all history is overdetermined by an undifferentiated field of co-actors, leaving no distinctive explanatory role for human intentionality.

(12) Both outcomes seem unacceptable.

The argument appears to show that any consistent attribution of agency to non-human causes either collapses the concept of agency or eliminates human agency altogether. In particular, if you wish to preserve premise (1) and avoid conclusion (11), what principled distinction between 'actor' and 'condition' must you defend, and does that distinction survive when applied to climate and microbial evolution?
