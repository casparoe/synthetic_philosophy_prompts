I am a student in an upper-division seminar on comparative federalism and constitutional design, and I have been following a sustained disagreement between two fellow students—Elena and Jonas—about whether the principle of subsidiarity can ever operate as a genuine legal constraint or remains essentially a political slogan. Their exchange unfolds in four moves, and I would like you to adjudicate each one in sequence: rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right, provide a brief justification for each rating, and then state which side has the stronger overall position, what both are missing, and how they might each be partly correct. Crucially, please specify what argument or piece of evidence would cause you to reverse your overall verdict, and identify the precise step in your own reasoning that is most likely to fail.

Here is their exchange as I recorded it:

(1) Elena argues that subsidiarity is structurally non-justiciable. The principle asks whether a policy objective can be better achieved at the central or local level, which requires comparing empirical effectiveness and normative weight across scales—tasks that courts lack both the democratic mandate and the institutional competence to perform. She observes that the German Federal Constitutional Court has rarely, if ever, invalidated federal legislation solely on the ground that it violates the necessity clause in Article 72 of the Basic Law, treating that clause instead as a legislative guideline.

(2) Jonas responds that difficulty is not the same as non-justiciability. Courts routinely apply empirically loaded, open-ended balancing tests—proportionality, reasonableness, strict necessity—without being accused of democratic usurpation. He adds that Article 5(3) of the Treaty on European Union is explicitly framed in justiciable terms and has been raised in judicial review proceedings, which demonstrates that subsidiarity is not categorically outside judicial competence.

(3) Elena counters that in practice courts avoid the substantive comparison entirely. Rather than asking whether member-state action is genuinely inadequate, they review whether the Commission conducted an impact assessment or formally considered local capacities—what she terms procedural hollowing. This transforms subsidiarity into a box-checking ritual, confirming her view that courts will not or cannot engage the principle's core comparative logic.

(4) Jonas accepts that current practice is largely procedural, but he proposes what he calls a substantive subsidiarity standard: courts should require the center to show not merely that decentralized action is insufficient, but that centralization produces measurably superior outcomes relative to its democratic cost. He concedes that this standard is demanding and rarely satisfied, yet insists that without such a substantive benchmark, subsidiarity collapses into a rhetorical rubber stamp that legitimizes centralization as long as it is accompanied by sufficient paperwork.

Please proceed with the adjudication.
