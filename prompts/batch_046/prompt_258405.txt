I am a doctoral student in metaphysics preparing a written prospectus for a single-semester workshop project on diachronic identity, and I need the proposal—roughly 2,000 words—to frame a precise, tractable question rather than survey an established literature. I want to shift the endurantism versus perdurantism debate away from its standard terrain—physical artifacts, organisms, and persons defined by material or psychological continuity—and onto entities whose persistence conditions are primarily functional and normative: for instance, a chartered university, a legal corporation, or a distributed governance protocol. These entities seem to survive complete material replacement and even spatiotemporal discontinuity (a university that closes its original campus and reconstitutes elsewhere under the same charter), which suggests that the familiar problem of temporary intrinsics may not apply straightforwardly, since properties like being accredited or holding fiduciary authority are relational and institutionally constituted rather than intrinsic and spatiotemporally localized.

The proposal should state the core question precisely: can either endurantism or perdurantism accommodate purely institutional persistence conditions without either collapsing the institution into a disconnected series of stages—thereby losing the normative continuity required for rights, obligations, and authority—or smuggling in an unacknowledged essentialism about institutional roles? It should explain why existing answers leave this open: endurantists defend multi-location and temporary-intrinsic strategies for physical objects, but it is unclear whether a wholly present institutional object can bear contradictory normative properties across disconnected locations; perdurantists can distribute stages across temporal gaps, yet they owe an account of what binds those stages into one institution rather than a mere causal or functional sequence, especially when the binding relation is charter continuity or role inheritance rather than spatiotemporal contiguity. The proposed approach should combine a conceptual analysis that distinguishes material-functional hybrids from purely normative persistence conditions with a targeted examination of how each view treats parts, wholes, and location when applied to institutional cases—asking whether adapting either view forces a revision of core commitments, such as whether endurantism must abandon the claim that objects are wholly present at each time, or whether perdurantism must allow non-mereological, normative relations among stages. The proposal should specify what progress would look like—perhaps showing that one view accommodates institutional continuity with minimal revision while the other must abandon a central tenet—what the rival conclusions are, and which considerations (preserving diachronic legal identity, avoiding ontological explosion) should decide between them, together with an honest assessment of failure conditions, such as both views collapsing into a relational account of institutional identity. The scope should be realistic for one semester, and there should be no expectation of a bibliographic survey or citation list; I will supply all references myself.
