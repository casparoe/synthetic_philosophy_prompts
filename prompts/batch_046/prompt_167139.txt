hey so i'm drafting a short essay for my history of science class and i'm genuinely not sure if my main point is sharp or just contrarian for the sake of it. The assignment was to engage with the Needham question—basically why Britain and not China, since China was clearly more advanced in terms of tech and bureaucracy for like a thousand years. I started out thinking the usual answer is about coal, colonies, and the scientific revolution, but the more i read the more i think the question itself is malformed.

My argument is that asking "why Britain and not China?" treats industrialization like a race with one finish line, which hides the fact that China wasn't "trying" to become Britain and then failing. The exam system and imperial patronage in China were optimized for large-scale agrarian stability, not for the kind of fragmented, competitive state environment that let British coal access and patent law align in this specific way. So comparing them as if they're two runners on the same track is misleading.

Then i pushed it further with this idea i made up—maybe the real issue is "endpoint determinism," where we read back from the 19th century and pretend that sustained productivity growth was the natural attractor state for any advanced society. If that's right, then the right contrastive question isn't geographic at all. It should be something like: under what conditions does technological leadership get converted into sustained growth rather than being captured by extractive instutitions? That feels more useful to me, but i'm worried i'm just dodging the historical comparison instead of answering it. Also, am i being unfair to historians who actually do comparative work? Is my framing just strawmanning them? I'd really appreciate comments on the logic, especially on whether my critique of the contrastive framing holds up or if i'm missing something obvious about how counterfactual reasoning works in history.
