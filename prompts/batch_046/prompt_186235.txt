For a philosophical novel I am drafting, I require a branching taxonomy—functioning as a silent decision tree—that sorts interpretations of what Descartes's cogito actually establishes, not by the names philosophers give them but by the concrete commitments each entails. The audience is an intelligent general reader who knows the basic argument but not its scholarly divisions; the instrument should contain exactly six leaves and no more than five internal nodes, with every node phrased as a specific claim or thought-experiment on which positions genuinely split. Begin with the shared premise that denying one's own existence while thinking involves some failure, then branch first on whether the cogito works as a syllogism with hidden premises—specifically, whether it tacitly assumes that every thought must have a thinker—or as an immediate existential disclosure requiring no inference. From there, split on the ontology of the secured "I": does it denote a substantial, enduring thinker; a bare, momentary occurrence of thought with no bearer; or merely a grammatical placeholder analogous to the "it" in "it rains," so that nothing about a subject is actually proved? Next, include a branch asking whether the argument licenses knowledge of other minds or remains irreducibly first-person and non-transferable. Then separate those who take the result to establish ontological presence from those who take it only to secure epistemic indubitability while bracketing all existential commitment—a pairing that should appear either as an occupied leaf or as an explicitly noted empty space. The tree must include the following leaves: the classical Cartesian substantialist view; the thin episodic view that proves only a token act of thinking; the performative-contradictory view, in the manner of Hintikka, that treats denial as pragmatically self-defeating rather than logically invalid; the grammatical-illusion view that the "I" is syntactic fiction; the phenomenological-reduction view that secures only immanent presence without ontology; and the logical-error view that the argument smuggles in an unjustified premise. For any answer-combination that yields no named defender—especially the conjunction of grammatical illusion with the claim that other minds are knowable—note the vacancy briefly and diagnose whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves that your branching questions cannot cleanly separate rather than inventing a false distinction. Do not supply bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; do not ask which interpretation is correct; and write each leaf's summary as a single sentence describing its most counterintuitive commitment.
