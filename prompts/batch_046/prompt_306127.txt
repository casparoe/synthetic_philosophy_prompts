As a retired physicist recently turned to classical philosophy, I have been working through Aristotle’s treatments of exchange and value, and I find myself stuck on what appears to be an internal tension. He describes money (nomisma) as a purely conventional instrument invented only to facilitate exchange according to need, and he holds that goods are naturally commensurable through the need they satisfy; yet he condemns usury (tokos) on the ground that money is “barren” and ought not to breed more of itself. But if both money and its quantitative ratios are already conventional measures layered over a pre-existing natural proportion of needs, then interest cannot violate nature by being artificial—it is just another convention. So is the standard interpretation mistaken? Is Aristotle’s real objection not that coin is sterile, but that interest introduces a spurious second metric which displaces the qualitative, need-based equality that should govern exchange? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
