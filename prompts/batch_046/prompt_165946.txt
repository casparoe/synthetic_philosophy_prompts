I am writing a chapter on moral luck in regulatory practice, and I have become stuck on an institutional mechanism that appears philosophically unstable. In several common-law jurisdictions—particularly in recent UK and Canadian environmental sentencing guidelines—courts now apply what are termed “counterfactual risk matrices”: the tribunal calculates the objective probability that a defendant’s reckless conduct would have produced the prohibited harm under standard conditions, and if that probability exceeds a statutory threshold (typically around seventy per cent), the offender is sentenced according to the tariff for completed harm, even when no harm actually occurred because of an unforeseeable external factor—for instance, an unseasonal drought that dispersed a chemical contaminant, or an unexpected genetic resistance in a pathogen that prevented infection. Meanwhile, these same legal systems maintain, in ordinary negligence doctrine, that liability attaches to the breach of duty rather than to the harm itself; and in criminal attempts, they generally insist on proof of specific intent coupled with a substantial step, not merely on the presence of a high-risk profile. The result is a bifurcation: in one domain, outcome—or its counterfactual shadow—drives punishment; in others, control and intent are said to be sovereign.

What theory of moral responsibility does the risk-matrix practice presuppose? Does it implicitly commit us to an outcome-sensitive form of moral luck, or can it be reconstructed as a pure risk-creation account that merely borrows harm-tariffs for administrative convenience? Is the practice consistent with the broader legal framework’s explicit rejection of outcome-based blame in other contexts—such as the refusal to enhance punishment for a completed assault over an attempted one when failure was due to pure chance? And if the implicit theory were made explicit, what would have to change in the sentencing architecture: separate tariffs for risk-creation, the abolition of counterfactual matrices altogether, or an explicit embrace of outcome-sensitive sentencing?
