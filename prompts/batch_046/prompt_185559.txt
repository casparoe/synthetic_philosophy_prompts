Consider the nonidentity problem as formulated by Derek Parfit: a policy adopted today—say, a sustained high-emission trajectory—results in a different set of people being born centuries later, many of whom live lives barely worth living, yet no particular individual can claim to have been harmed because that individual would not have existed under any alternative policy. Now imagine Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz encountering this argument in the context of climate policy and intergenerational ethics. How would he likely respond, given his commitment to the principle of sufficient reason, his doctrine that every possible substance possesses a complete individual concept, and his framework of compossible worlds? Specifically, would he regard the absence of a specific, identifiable victim as dissolving the moral wrong, or would he insist that harm must be evaluated relative to an entire possible world rather than across branches? Furthermore, how might his doctrine of pre-established harmony influence whether he grants moral standing to those merely possible future persons who are never actualized, and does his metaphysics allow for a meaningful distinction between harming someone and failing to bring about the best compossible world?
