I run a philosophy seminar on aesthetics with ten graduate students; we have ninety minutes. Design a deliberation exercise—no upfront lecture—in which participants experience art-definition not as applying a hidden correct criterion, but as negotiating irreducible conflict among competing functional frames. Divide them into three subgroups, each assigned one unstated evaluative lens: aesthetic experience, institutional recognition, or art-historical continuity. In the first round, each subgroup evaluates five case-cards and classifies each as art or not-art using only their lens. In the second round, the full group must unanimously select three works for a mock exhibition from a pool of six that includes standard cases, borderline cases, and one adversarial case-card describing an object constructed to violate all three criteria simultaneously yet presented as an actual museum acquisition. The rules should make deadlock and frame-conflict emerge before any theory is named. Provide a debrief that maps each round onto positions in the definitional debate, predicts the specific deadlock patterns, is frank about which feature—real institutional authority and social power—the simulation cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply verbally, and specifies what participants should come away seeing: that ambiguity in art-status often reflects overdetermination by incompatible criteria rather than missing information. Keep all theoretical names and attributions out of the exercise itself; they should appear only in the debrief.
