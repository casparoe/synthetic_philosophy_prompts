I've been arguing with my roommate about whether DNA is actually a "code" or just a molecule we talk about like one, and now I'm stuck on this weird institutional split that doesn't make sense to me.

In clinical genetics, everything is framed around information: doctors call mutations "typos," noncoding DNA is "regulatory syntax," and you're genome is a "blueprint" that gets "read" and "interpreted." Genetic counseling reports literally say your DNA "contains" risk information, and consent forms treat sequence data as personal medical information you can withdraw or share — it's your informational property, not your body part. The whole practice assumes there's a message in there, seperable from the physical tissue, that belongs to you.

But in forensic DNA databases, the exact same molecule is treated like dirt under a fingernail: a physical trace. The database doesn't interpret meaning — it compares repeating DNA fragments to match tissue samples. Contamination is a physical error, not an "informational corruption." And you definitely don't "own" your forensic profile; the state treats it as an identitiy marker, like a fingerprint, with no syntax or semantics involved.

So I'm confused. If DNA is genuinely a readable code in the clinic, shouldn't forensic science also be interpreting messages? And if it's just a chemical trace in forensics, why do clinical labs get to claim it's a seperable information-object that patients own? What philosophical commitments about materiality, information, and identitiy does this split practice actually presuppose? Does the clinical framing strictly need DNA to be a code, or could it work just as well treating sequences as chemical correlates (like cholesterol levels) without any syntax? And if we dropped the code metaphor entirely — if consent forms talked about tissue chemistry instead of informational messages — what would have to change in genetic counseling, data-ownership law, and our idea that you can "have" a genome without having the cells?
