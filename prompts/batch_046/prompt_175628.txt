Write an essay on whether the abolition of dueling in nineteenth-century Britain and the decline of Atlantic chattel slavery are best explained by the persuasive force of moral arguments, by shifts in material and political interests, or by transformations in the social economy of honor. Take seriously the view associated with Bernard Williams—that large-scale normative change is rarely driven by the intrinsic rationality of arguments alone and more often tracks alterations in the conditions under which honor and shame operate—and contrast it with Kwame Anthony Appiah's claim that honor-codes can themselves be redirected by moral reasoning, so that a practice collapses when it becomes incompatible with a group's own conception of honorable status. Do not treat these mechanisms as merely complementary; defend a specific verdict about which factor was structurally decisive in one of the two historical cases, and then apply your framework to a less-discussed transition—such as the rapid erosion of public-smoking norms in Western countries after 1970, or the collapse of footbinding in early twentieth-century China—to test whether the same mechanism operates. Reach a clear conclusion about whether a future moral revolution, for instance regarding industrial animal agriculture, is more likely to be driven by ethical argumentation or by changes in what confers social standing.
