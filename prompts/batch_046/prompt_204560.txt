Compose a formal philosophical dialogue between two equally capable interlocutors—designated A and B—debating Bernard Williams’s integrity objection, specifically the charge that utilitarian moral arithmetic can involve one thought too many in contexts where personal commitment ought to preempt calculative reasoning. Rather than replaying the familiar individual-rescue scenario, embed the disagreement in a constructed institutional setting: a regional medical-triage consortium whose voting members include both direct stakeholders with familial ties to potential beneficiaries and independent ethicists with no such ties. A should defend the view that requiring any stakeholder-member to perform an explicit utilitarian weighing—treating the lives of relatives and strangers as fully commensurable inputs—reproduces Williams’s alienating structure at the collective level, corrupting the ground project that gives the stakeholder’s practical identity its coherence, even when the final decision is rendered by collective vote rather than individual fiat. B should resist by arguing that collective bodies lack the unified narrative self that Williams’s concept of integrity presupposes, so the objection either misfires or collapses into a pragmatic concern about institutional legitimacy rather than a deep moral-psychological corruption. Have them engage in genuine back-and-forth: B should press A on whether distributed agency can possess a ground project at all, and A should reply by distinguishing between aggregate preference-summation and genuinely shared commitments that can be betrayed; B should then challenge whether integrity can carry normative weight if it is anchored in idiosyncratic life projects that may themselves be morally arbitrary, and A should respond without conceding that all personal commitments are equally defensible or that collective decision-making is therefore immune to alienation. The dialogue should be rendered in precise, academic prose, should avoid requesting citations, bibliographies, or reading lists, and should treat the core concepts—integrity, ground projects, and the alienating structure of consequentialist reasoning—with sufficient accuracy that a reader familiar with Williams’s moral psychology would recognize the argument’s structure, while remaining fully intelligible to someone encountering these ideas for the first time.
