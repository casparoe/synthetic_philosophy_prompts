I'm a journalist covering philosophy of technology and virtual economies, and I've been trying to settle my own view on a question that keeps surfacing in my reporting: in what sense are virtual objects and virtual harms genuinely real? Rather than defend a theory outright, I want to test whether any single principle can capture the pattern in my intuitions across five concrete cases. Here are my verdicts.

Case 1 — The persistent artifact. In a long-running multiplayer world with an internal economy, a player spends roughly six hundred hours crafting a unique digital weapon. The weapon can be traded for in-game currency that is exchangeable for real money, but it cannot be duplicated. If another player hacks the database and permanently deletes the weapon, I think the owner has suffered a real harm, and that the weapon was genuinely owned property—not merely a pattern of pixels.

Case 2 — The simulated trauma. In a high-fidelity virtual-reality environment, a user is subjected to a scripted scenario with realistic haptic feedback in which their avatar is restrained and subjected to simulated torture. The user knows it is scripted, yet suffers lasting anxiety and intrusive memories. I think the psychological harm is entirely real, but I do not think there was a real victim of torture—the situation is closer to the harm caused by a traumatic film than to actual bodily assault.

Case 3 — The autonomous ecosystem. A virtual world runs an evolutionary simulation with its own physics and selection rules; no human designed the specific entities that emerge. One entity develops a stable pattern of damage-avoidance, distress signaling, and self-preservation that is functionally indistinguishable from suffering in a biological organism. Even so, I think there is no real suffering occurring—it is only a representational pattern, however complex.

Case 4 — The smart-contract breach. Two users enter an agreement enforced by an external smart contract: one will design virtual architecture inside the world, the other will pay in cryptocurrency upon delivery. The builder delivers; the payer refuses. The contract is unenforceable only inside the virtual world, but it is legally binding outside it. I think a genuine obligation was breached—the failure is not merely a violation of game rules, but a real-world moral and contractual failure.

Case 5 — The private garden. A solitary user generates an elaborate virtual landscape stored only in local memory, never networked or observed by anyone else. She reports profound aesthetic satisfaction and describes the landscape as beautiful. I think her experience is real, but the landscape itself is not an appropriate object of public aesthetic judgment or real-world preservation obligations—its status is closer to that of a vivid private fantasy than to a physical garden.

These verdicts feel partly coherent—Cases 1 and 4 treat virtual-world transactions as embedded in real normative structures, while Cases 2 and 3 distinguish real harm from real victimhood or suffering—but they also pull apart. Case 1 grants full reality to an object defined by server data, whereas Case 3 denies reality to a pattern defined by internal dynamics; Case 2 accepts real harm without accepting a real harmed subject in the virtual domain, while Case 5 denies reality to an object despite a real experiencing subject.

My question: Is there one precise principle—perhaps concerning causal-normative integration with the non-virtual world, representational transparency, or intersubjective accessibility—that delivers all five verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and what is the cost? Please apply your resulting principle to one further case I have not listed: a virtual artifact that exists in a superposition of server states, so that its properties are fixed only upon observation by a user, but that is traded for real cryptocurrency and covered by an external insurance policy. Is its ownership real prior to observation?
