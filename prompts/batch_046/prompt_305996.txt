As a retired physicist whose career centered on non-equilibrium thermodynamics and statistical mechanics, I have recently begun studying philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I find myself perplexed by the apparent immobility of the debate over what intelligence fundamentally is. The dispute seems to span at least Spearman's psychometric tradition, evolutionary-adaptive accounts, and contemporary functionalist approaches in artificial intelligence research, yet despite shared access to correlation studies, neurobiological data, and computational models, the core positions appear unchanged across decades. Psychometric theorists continue to treat intelligence as a unitary, heritable, predictive cognitive capacity best captured by standardized measurement; evolutionary and ecological theorists continue to treat it as a distributed, context-dependent suite of adaptive behaviors shaped by specific selection pressures. Both camps accept that nervous systems are predictive engines; both acknowledge cross-domain correlations in task performance. Still, no synthesis has emerged.

Drawing on my former field, I have begun to suspect that the stalemate arises not from missing data but from an unacknowledged divergence in what kind of explanatory object intelligence is taken to be. One framework treats it as an intrinsic algorithmic property—specifically, the efficiency with which a system reduces environmental variability into compressed predictive representations, which I have come to call compressive intelligence. The other framework treats it as an extrinsic relational property—the capacity of an organism-environment coupling to expand its viable state-space of responses under novel constraints, which I call expansive intelligence. These orientations are not strictly contradictory—predictive compression often enables behavioral expansion—but they entail different measurement protocols, different evolutionary narratives, and different criteria for attributing intelligence to non-human or artificial systems.

Given this framing, I am asking not for a survey of positions but for a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure. Is the impasse primarily verbal, with both sides using intelligence to denote different but equally legitimate phenomena? Or is there a substantive disagreement about whether cognition should be explained by internal information-processing efficiency or by ecological adaptability? Could a hidden normative commitment—perhaps about whether science should prioritize predictive accuracy over functional flexibility—be doing the work? And if your diagnosis is correct, what would genuinely count as progress: a formal result demonstrating that compression metrics and state-space expansion are mathematically equivalent under certain conditions; a conceptual distinction that cleanly separates the two notions; or an argument that the original question is ill-posed because it conflates two distinct explanatory targets? I want an honest assessment of whether this debate can be moved, or whether its immobility is structural.
