Consider this reasoning, which extends the nonidentity problem into longtermist ethics and seems to prove too much:

(1) If policy A and policy B produce different future populations, and both populations have lives worth living, then no specific future person is harmed by choosing A, since under B they would not exist.
(2) This applies to any macro-level parameter—climate trajectory, economic growth path, technological sequencing—that determines reproductive pairing across generations.
(3) Now suppose we adopt "lineage continuity" as the relevant moral unit: we care about the welfare of continuous family lines across generations, not merely about discrete individuals.
(4) Even on this view, altering macro conditions replaces one set of lineages with another; the "displaced" lines never come into being.
(5) So lineage-level concern is also captured by the nonidentity problem: there is no particular lineage that is made worse off by our choice, because the alternative lineages do not exist to suffer.
(6) Therefore, longtermist obligations—whether framed in individualist or lineage-based terms—have no harm-prevention object; they can rest only on pure impersonal value.
(7) But pure impersonal value, without harm-based constraints, collapses either into maximizing total population regardless of quality or into indifference between a flourishing far future and one of barely-worth-living people of equal size.
(8) Longtermists want to avoid both pure presentism and this impersonalist collapse.
(9) Hence the nonidentity problem, pushed through lineage continuity, forces longtermism into an unacceptable theoretical corner.
