I'm preparing a session for a graduate philosophy reading group—eight participants, seventy-five minutes—on Ubuntu and relational conceptions of personhood, and I want an activity that makes the ontological pressure of relationality felt in real time rather than debated abstractly. Specifically, I want participants to experience the breakdown of distributive moral reasoning when harm travels through the web of constitutive relations rather than landing on discrete subjects. Design a structured deliberation game with concrete rules and role assignments.

The scenario: a communal land-use decision has disrupted a farming cooperative, but the damage is not localized in any single body; it has frayed the kinship obligations, elder-junior recognition rituals, and shared labor rhythms through which each person's standing is constituted. Assign participants not to individual agents but to "relation-positions"—for example, "the elder recognized through the junior's greeting," "the sibling constituted by shared seasonal labor," or "the ancestor whose narrated memory binds the present group"—and provide each with a card describing which relations they require to maintain full personhood-status within the cooperative. Give the group a collective pool of resources (tokens representing compensation and restorative labor) and a rule that any allocation must be justified in relational terms: no participant may claim a token for themselves as an isolated bearer, but only by showing how receiving it repairs a specific relation-link without severing another.

The rules should force standard judgments—who was wronged, who owes restitution, who deserves compensation—to become impossible without either arbitrarily cutting relations to manufacture discrete victims or dissolving the very ontology the group is meant to inhabit. Include a timeline (opening framing, 25 minutes of negotiation, 15 minutes of forced decision, 20 minutes of debrief), a constraint scheme that rewards coherent relational responses but penalizes retreat into individual-rights language (e.g., anyone who uses "I" as a standalone claimant loses a token), and explicit instructions for what you, as facilitator, will do if a participant insists on liberal-individualist framing.

In the debrief, map specific negotiation moments onto parts of the relational ontology: the point where compensation was assigned, the attempt to isolate a victim, the failure of unanimous agreement. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong and how to handle it, and state honestly which feature of Ubuntu—perhaps the historical specificity of pre-colonial communal life—this simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied verbally. Please respond in no more than 400 words, with rules in bullet form and the debrief in one paragraph.
