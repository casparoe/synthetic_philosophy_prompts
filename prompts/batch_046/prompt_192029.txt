I am writing as a student in a course on Buddhist philosophy who has become stuck on a tension that my readings do not seem to resolve, and I would be grateful for your comments on a short argument I have sketched. The standard presentation opposes Theravada ethics, in which cetana or intention is the sole determinant of karmic weight, to Mahayana practices such as upaya, where a bodhisattva may appear to break precepts in order to reduce suffering. Most scholars treat upaya as a suspension of rules for compassionate ends, but that framing still assumes a kind of hidden consequentialist calculus in which aggregate dukkha is being minimized. I want to propose a different reading, one that I have been calling a topological ethics of interdependence. Rather than summing welfare across individuals, this view holds that the moral significance of an act is determined by its structural position within pratityasamutpada: an action that occurs at a dense node of causal conditions reshapes the network of liberation for many beings without requiring that anyone calculate outcomes. The bodhisattva in the Jataka tales is not weighing lives like a utilitarian, but altering the topology of conditions so that future paths to awakening become available. I then extend this to Pure Land practice, suggesting that the transfer of merit through Amida's vow is not an outsourcing of moral agency, as it is often criticized, but rather an acknowledgment that individual karma was never fully agent-bound to begin with, since the self that accumulates merit is itself a node in the same network. I realize this may conflate Abhidharma metaphysics with East Asian devotional practice, or import Western structuralist language where it does not belong. Could you comment on whether this framing is coherent, where it conflicts with established doctrine, and whether I am unfairly harmonizing traditions that should remain distinct?
