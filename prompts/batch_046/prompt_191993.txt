I am writing from a high school philosophy seminar on political concepts, where my classmates and I have been arguing about what “being free” actually requires without being able to settle on a shared definition. Rather than asking us to memorize standard labels, my instructor wants a conceptual sorting instrument: a decision tree that begins with concrete judgments about particular situations and ends at named positions, so that a student can locate her own view by answering questions rather than by picking a philosopher’s name.

Please construct such a tree for the debate over liberty, covering at minimum Isaiah Berlin’s distinction between negative and positive freedom, Philip Pettit’s republican conception of freedom as non-domination, and the logical space in between. Each internal node should present a specific case or claim—such as whether an agent with no interfering obstacles but subject to an arbitrary ruler is unfree, whether the absence of resources to exercise a legally permitted choice counts as a loss of freedom, or whether a benevolent law that removes an option nobody desired constitutes an interference—phrased so that one can answer yes or no without knowing which branch leads to which tradition. The leaves should be labeled with the position they represent: classical negative liberty, autonomy-based positive liberty, republican non-domination, and at least one hybrid or under-discussed view (for instance, an “effective liberty” position that makes resources constitutive of freedom, or a “structural domination” view that extends republican analysis to systemic economic dependency without identifying a particular master). For any combination of answers that is logically coherent but apparently unoccupied in the literature, include a leaf marked “unnamed” with a brief diagnosis of whether the gap reflects incoherence, practical unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Finally, please flag any pairs of leaves that the tree cannot sharply distinguish—certain autonomy-focused views, for example, may collapse into republican concerns about arbitrary power—and append to each named leaf a single sentence describing its most awkward commitment (for example, that pure negative liberty must regard a slave with a kind master as fully free). I do not need citations, reading lists, or references to specific editions; the instrument should be usable by students who have encountered these ideas only through secondary discussion.
