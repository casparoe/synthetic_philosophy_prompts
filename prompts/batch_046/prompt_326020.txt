The Hart–Devlin debate on legal moralism has been rehearsed for decades without either camp conceding ground: liberals hold that criminal law may restrict conduct only to prevent tangible harm to others, while legal moralists maintain that a society may legitimately enforce its shared moral code to preserve social cohesion, even where no identifiable victim suffers. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not principally about the limits of state coercion, but about a masked ontological disagreement—whether social cohesion is constituted by shared propositional beliefs that legislation can directly target, or merely by behavioural coordination that law can influence only indirectly. Diagnose the disagreement precisely: is it verbal (a dispute over the extension of ‘harm’ or ‘morality’), substantive but hidden (about social ontology, collective intentionality, or the conditions for norm stability), or are the two sides answering different questions—one normative about political legitimacy, one descriptive about social survival—under the single heading ‘legal moralism’? Ground your diagnosis in what each side actually says about the purpose of punishment and the mechanisms by which a shared ethos survives. Then say, given that diagnosis, what kind of advance could genuinely move the debate—a conceptual distinction in collective intentionality, an empirical result about how norms are transmitted, a reformulation of the framing question—or whether we should expect a permanent impasse and why.
