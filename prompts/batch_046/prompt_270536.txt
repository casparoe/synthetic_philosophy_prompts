Since Hofstadter and Sander’s Surfaces and Essences, the claim that analogy-making—not statistical pattern-matching, modular computation, or symbol manipulation—is the fundamental engine of cognition has generated sustained disagreement without clear convergence. Defenders treat high-level conceptual blending—seeing a legal argument as a chess match, or a molecule as a key fitting a lock—as proof that all thinking is analogical mapping at its root, while critics reply that rapid perceptual categorization and routine decision-making occur without any detectable structural alignment, and that analogies are often post-hoc narratives layered over faster, non-analogical processes. Each camp points to different phenomena as the decisive data: conceptual creativity versus millisecond recognition. My working hypothesis is that much of the impasse is terminological—"analogy" meaning deep relational alignment for one side and superficial feature comparison for the other—masking agreement that both processes exist, yet I also suspect a hidden empirical question is underneath: whether analogical mechanisms are causally necessary for the categories we actually form in real time, or whether they are merely retrospective descriptions. Is the stalemate genuinely about the architecture of mind, is it verbal, or is it a disagreement about which cognitive tasks should count as central? Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement, supported by what each side actually says about blending versus rapid categorization, indicate whether any hidden formal or empirical issue is doing the work, and specify what—if anything—would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a process-tracing result, or a reframing of the core question itself.
