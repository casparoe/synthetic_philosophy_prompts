I'm writing from a course on classical Chinese philosophy of language, where we've been working through the white horse dialogue and the debates of the School of Names about the correspondence between names (ming) and actualities (shi). The problem of whether a white horse counts as a horse because of an added color-property has made me notice a parallel in a modern institutional practice.

In data-protection regulation—particularly in frameworks like the GDPR and its analogues—there is a sharp institutional boundary between personal data and anonymized data. Once a dataset is certified as anonymized, it is generally exempt from consent requirements, breach-notification obligations, and purpose-limitation rules, even if the anonymization is technically reversible or if the dataset retains every structurally relevant feature of the individuals except a direct identifier. The practice treats the removal of one property (the identifying label) as sufficient to shift the ontological or legal kind of the information.

What view of kind-identity, property-dependence, and the relation between naming and reality does this regulatory distinction presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit metaphysics: is it essentialist (holding that kind-membership requires a specific bundle of necessary properties, so that removing the identifier changes the kind), conventionalist (treating the boundary as a decision about how we apply the name personal data), or something closer to the School of Names' concern with the mismatch between linguistic classification and actual structure? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates.

Then evaluate its coherence: do regulators apply the anonymization exemption consistently across domains—say, between medical records and consumer-behavior datasets—or do the variations reveal that the practice is philosophically underdetermined, compatible with several rival theories of classification? Finally, if we replaced the implicit view with one that held kind-identity invariant under the addition or subtraction of non-essential properties—a stance closer to common-sense realism—what concrete institutional changes would be required in consent protocols, breach-notification rules, and cross-border data-transfer agreements?
