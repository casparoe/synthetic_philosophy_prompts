I'm preparing an interview for a philosophy colloquium series with a philosopher—let's call her Dr. Elena Voss—who defends what she terms "scale-dependent functionalism." On her view, mental states are individuated by their causal-functional profiles, but whether a particular substrate realizes a given mental state depends essentially on the spatiotemporal scale at which we describe the causal structure: the same system might realize belief at the neural-network scale, fail to realize it at the ion-channel scale, and realize an altogether different mental kind at the socio-environmental scale. She deploys this framework to argue that the classic multiple-realizability objection to physicalism rests on a category error, since it presumes there must be a single canonical physical description relative to which mental-physical token identity should be evaluated, whereas on her account realization is always indexed to an explanatory scale that is itself selected for predictive and integrative success.

I'd like a sequence of questions that go well beyond asking her to restate the view. First, please include a question pressing whether scale-dependence collapses into a problematic explanatory relativism: if the choice of scale determines whether a mental property is instantiated, then saying that mental states are "really" physical states at the correct scale appears to do little more metaphysical work than saying they are convenient descriptions at that scale. Please key the follow-up to her likely reply—that scale-selection is constrained by inter-theoretic reduction and predictive power, not by arbitrary convention—by asking whether those very constraints don't already presuppose a physicalist ontology she claims to be defending rather than explaining. Second, draft a question about synthetic minds: if a silicon-based architecture implements the macro-scale causal profile of, say, pain, but entirely lacks the meso-scale dynamics characteristic of nociceptive processing in biological systems, does she grant the artificial system full mental status, and if so, doesn't this revive the multiple-realizability problem she sought to dissolve by allowing mental properties to float free of any shared micro-physical basis? Anticipate her probable answer—that macro-scale functional equivalence is sufficient for mental attribution regardless of micro-scale divergence—and include a follow-up asking whether this makes her view indistinguishable from a non-reductive functionalism that she originally criticized for being metaphysically non-committal about the physical. Finally, include one question about diachronic mental processes such as long-term belief revision or the gradual transformation of emotional dispositions: can her view accommodate these without requiring that the realizing substrate maintain a stable descriptive scale across time, or must she allow that an agent's mental states are realized at shifting scales as the process unfolds? Key the follow-up to her expected response—that temporal continuity is itself a functional parameter fixing the relevant scale across intervals—by pressing whether this doesn't implicitly reintroduce a substrate-independent "functional architecture" that does all the metaphysical labor she assigned to scale-relative physical realization.
