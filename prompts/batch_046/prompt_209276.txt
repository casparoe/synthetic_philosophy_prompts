Im prepping a discussion group on Ted Chiang's Story of Your Life—the story behind Arrival—and I keep hitting a wall with the translation theme. Most people in my group jump straight to Sapir-Whorf, saying Louise learns Heptapod B and therefore starts experiencing time all at once, but I want to read it through Quine's indeterminacy of translation, specifically the gavagai problem where behavioral evidence undertermines whether a speaker means rabbit, undetached rabbit-part, or rabbithood. The narrative treats Louise's translation as converging on one true heptapod ontology of simultaneous time, but doesnt that smuggle in analytic hypotheses that Quine says we cant justify? I want to know if the story actually dramatizes radical translation failure, or if it cheats by letting Louise bypass the behavioral evidence problem through direct cognitive access to heptapod perception.

There's also this detail about the heptapods writing logograms in one circular stroke instead of linear sentences. Some of my friends think that sidesteps translation ambiguity altogether, since you see the whole meaning at once. But I wonder if thats just confusing epistemic access with ontological determinacy—like seeing a symbol clearly doesnt solve the gavagai-style ambiguity about what ontology the symbol refers to. So my question is: can you read the story as genuinely affirming Quinean indeterminacy—there being no single correct translation of Heptapod B—while still allowing Louise's consciousness to shift? Or are those two readings incompatible, and does the honest critic have to admit Chiang collapses them for narrative effect? I'm writing this up for a philosophy of language course and don't want to misrepresent Quine just to make the plot fit, but I also dont want to reduce the story to a philosophy illustration.
