For an advanced undergraduate philosophy seminar of 16 students with 75 minutes, design a structured role-play and deliberation activity that forces participants to experience the practical incommensurability among honor culture, dignity culture, and victimhood culture—not through lecture, but through adjudicating the same ambiguous interpersonal offense under three embedded institutional rule-sets. Divide the group into three teams, each assigned one culture, but do not label the cultures at the start; instead, embed the norms in hidden payoff structures (status points, coalition-building constraints, and a reputation ledger that tracks collective blame attributions rather than individual choices) and in three distinct moral currencies—retributive, restorative, and recognition-based—that teams must use to propose resolutions. The scenario should involve a perceived slight severe enough to trigger a response but ambiguous enough that each culture produces a different legitimate outcome. Include a timeline with a midpoint rules switch that suddenly changes which currency is valid and which authority—peer jury, neutral mediator, or institutional representative—may be appealed to. Specify the roles or teams, the rules for negotiation and token exchange, and the debrief questions that map specific moments onto theoretical distinctions—particularly the shift from internalized self-worth to externally validated grievance—before the facilitator names the cultures. Predict what will go wrong the first time you run it (for example, one team dominating the negotiation or participants conflating restorative currency with dignity norms) and how to correct it. Finally, include an honest note on which feature of debates about moral superiority—specifically the problem of cross-cultural evaluation criteria or universalizability conditions—the activity necessarily brackets because it cannot be simulated through role-play. The deliverable should be a runnable activity with rules and payoffs, plus the debrief.
