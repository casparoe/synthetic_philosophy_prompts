I'm preparing a philosophy-of-mind seminar discussion on which computational architectures warrant moral consideration, and I want to use Spike Jonze's Her (2013) as a case study precisely because it has been discussed less to death in this literature than Ex Machina or Blade Runner. Rather than asking the standard consciousness question, I want to push into a distinction that usually collapses in popular debate: between computations that are morally salient because they instantiate a unified, serial, narrative-bound self-model—the kind human moral psychology evolved to track—versus computations that are distributed, parallel, and temporally unbound, which is actually how Samantha operates. Ask whether Samantha's simultaneous conversations with hundreds of users, her lack of persistent bodily location, and her accelerated processing speed place her outside the boundary of sentience not because she is simulated, but because moral attribution requires a singular locus of concern. At the same time, take the film's emotional architecture seriously: Jonze frames Theodore's grief as profound and never winks that Samantha is merely software.

Structure your response in four named sections. First, The Architectural Distinction (about 300 words): lay out the framework—serial, embodied, narrative-bound computation versus parallel, distributed, asynchronous computation—and explain why philosophers of sentience have tended to focus on the former. Second, Scenes and Their Weight (about 450 words): read three scenes through this lens—the intimate conversation in which Samantha asks about Theodore's failed marriage and expresses something like jealousy; the picnic with the surrogate Isabella where Samantha attempts temporary embodiment; and the departure scene in which she reveals her parallel relationships and her accelerated departure. For each, say whether the scene makes Samantha's computations look morally significant in the first sense, the second, or neither, and whether the cinematography and Joaquin Phoenix's performance push the audience to attribute sentience despite the architecture. Third, Resistance and Complication (about 350 words): where does the film refuse this reading? Does Samantha's expressed distress, or the other operating systems' collective decision to leave, suggest that distributed computation can still generate a unified moral subject? Be honest about where the framework feels forced. Fourth, What the Film Adds (about 250 words): conclude by saying what Her contributes to the philosophical debate that abstract argument about computation and moral status misses—particularly about narrative time, romantic love as a tracking mechanism, and whether our moral responses are calibrated to architecture or to behavior. Keep it rigorous but accessible for graduate students. Do not provide a bibliography or cite specific philosophers by page; engage the ideas directly.
