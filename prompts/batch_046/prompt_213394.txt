Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation is usually debated across languages, but its logic seems to apply with equal force within a single speaker across time. Here is an argument I drafted for a seminar that now strikes me as suspicious: its premises each look defensible, yet the conclusion undermines the very rational practice that produced the argument.

(1) Quine's core claim: for any total body of behavioral, dispositional, and environmental evidence about a speaker's use of a term, there are multiple translation manuals that accommodate the evidence equally well, with no further fact of the matter selecting among them (the familiar gavagai scenario being the canonical illustration).

(2) The evidence I now possess about what I meant yesterday by my own terms—my present memories, written notes, and observable behavioral traces—is structurally of the same kind: it consists entirely of present records and dispositions, all compatible with different interpretations of my past utterances.

(3) So if Quine is correct about inter-linguistic indeterminacy, he should also be correct about intra-personal, diachronic indeterminacy: there is no determinate fact about whether yesterday-me meant rabbit, undetached rabbit part, or some other extensionally equivalent but intensionally distinct category.

(4) Rational belief revision over time requires that there be determinate relations of consistency and contradiction between a thinker's past and present beliefs; if my past meanings are indeterminate, then these logical relations are indeterminate as well.

(5) Therefore, applying (1) without restriction collapses diachronic rationality—there is no fact of the matter about whether I have contradicted myself, accumulated evidence consistently, or changed my mind for good reasons.

(6) Yet Quine's own philosophical project—defending naturalized epistemology, treating scientific belief as continuous with common sense, and arguing for indeterminacy itself—presupposes that thinkers can revise beliefs rationally across time.

(7) Hence (1), generalized consistently, entails that the reasoning supporting it lacks determinate content, which is self-undermining.

(8) The escape seems to be restricting (1) arbitrarily to other people and other languages, but then we owe a principled explanation of why the same evidential structure suddenly yields determinacy when the speaker is my past self.

Where does this chain break? I am inclined to preserve (1) and even (3), and I am reluctant to deny (5) outright, since I think diachronic rationality is real. If I must pay a price, I would rather pay it by weakening (2) or blocking the inference to (4). But I want to know exactly which step is weakest, why it seemed plausible on first inspection, and what each alternative costs—particularly whether there is a coherent way to keep Quine's inter-linguistic indeterminacy intact without letting it infect the determinacy of my own past meanings.
