I'm preparing remarks for an interdisciplinary seminar on comparative moral status, where I will defend a gradient account I call dimensional luminosity: the view that moral considerability does not depend on a binary sentience threshold but varies continuously across organisms and artificial systems according to two jointly necessary dimensions—what I term temporal binding depth (the integration of predictive error-correction over non-trivial time-scales) and representational bandwidth (the range of sensory modalities over which homeostatic regulation operates). I argue that, on this framework, there is no principled discontinuity between the predictive architecture of a eusocial insect colony and that of a sophisticated climate-control system; the difference is quantitative along both axes, not qualitative. The audience includes three animal-ethics scholars who defend sharp welfare thresholds for invertebrates—particularly one who maintains that nociception requires centralized neural architecture—two philosophers of AI who are vigilant against over-attributing moral status to algorithms, and a cognitive scientist who studies collective cognition in hymenoptera. Their concerns are practical as much as metaphysical: they want to know whether welfare law should cover insects, whether we risk moral inflation by extending protection to thermostats, and whether my metric licenses absurd regulatory burdens. Give me the objections in the order they are most likely to arise, phrased in the vocabulary each faction would actually use: begin with the thresholdist's challenge about phenomenal binding and centralized nociceptive pathways, proceed to the AI ethicists' worry that continuous gradation collapses into a form of panpsychism about feedback loops, and then address the collective-cognition specialist's empirical point that decentralized processing in bee colonies differs structurally from the centralized feedback architecture of even adaptive thermostats. For each objection, indicate precisely what I should concede—likely that my view sacrifices the clean legislative line they prefer—what I should hold firm—the continuity claim along both dimensions—and where my reply is weakest, especially the suspicion that my two-dimensional metric is ad hoc unless I can justify why temporal depth carries independent moral weight. Finally, include the objection I most dread: that if moral status truly dims continuously without a cutoff, then every act of temperature regulation becomes a microscopic moral catastrophe, and prepare a concise reply that avoids relying on aggregationist arithmetic or total-utilitarian summation that I have not defended elsewhere. Structure this as a prep sheet for a twenty-minute Q&A session, ordered by likelihood and damage, with a brief rhetorical note on tone—where to sound conciliatory and where to stand firm—rather than as a literature review.
