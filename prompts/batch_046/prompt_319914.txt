I hold that Plutarch’s Parallel Lives teach morality not by supplying imitable models but by engineering an irresolvable structural tension between each Greek–Roman pair, forcing the reader to construct an unstated third position—call it reflected virtue—that belongs to neither biography; and that hagiography extends this dialectical mechanism, so its hyperbolic distortions are pedagogically essential amplifiers rather than epistemic defects to be corrected. What would genuinely count against this? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward: textual cases where a pairing clearly resolves into a single moral verdict; historical or reception evidence that ancient or medieval readers treated these lives as direct exemplars for imitation; arguments that narrative distortion undermines rather than sharpens practical reasoning; and whether the thesis risks unfalsifiability through ad hoc appeals to hidden dialectical structure—if so, whether that insulation is a weakness for a view of this kind. For each, indicate what a defender of the dialectical-reading thesis ought to concede and what they could plausibly absorb, and say whether any remaining objection is decisive or only inconvenient.
