I spent the better part of last evening in a heated but respectful debate with a friend about whether evolutionary accounts of human moral cognition represent legitimate explanatory cranes or whether they collapse into greedy reductionism and, ultimately, a kind of skyhook. We kept circling back to Daniel Dennett's distinction between cranes—structures that build complexity gradually from simpler foundations without miraculous leaps—and skyhooks, which presuppose the very complexity they purport to explain. My friend insisted that when evolutionary psychologists trace deontological intuitions back to kin-selection algorithms and reciprocal altruism, they are engaging in greedy reductionism: stripping away the normative authority of moral judgments by reducing them to reproductive strategies, thereby treating morality as if it were nothing but a spandrel of adaptive cognition. I countered that this is precisely how a crane should work—explaining the emergence of normative structure through incremental selection pressures without invoking any mystical moral faculty—yet I struggled to articulate exactly where the boundary lies between legitimate reduction and the greedy variety that Dennett warns against. I also found myself wondering whether the charge of greedy reductionism implicitly assumes a kind of explanatory exclusivity that Dennett's framework does not actually require.

Do not allow either character to become a strawman; both should demonstrate philosophical sophistication.
