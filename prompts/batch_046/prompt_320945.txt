(2) There is an equally fundamental right to be forgotten, especially for survivors who reject victimhood as an identity, grounded in personal autonomy rather than in a mere claim to privacy.

(3) Memory is not representational but performative: to remember is to construct the present moral community, not to retrieve an unchanging past.

(4) Institutions that impose collective forgetting—through amnesty coupled with archive destruction, for example—act wrongly because they annihilate the narrative ownership of the harmed.

(5) Institutions that impose collective remembrance—through compulsory memorial rites or inherited guilt narratives—act wrongly because they instrumentalise the dead and violate the autonomy of those compelled to perform grief.

(6) The correct normative structure is therefore indirect: we should design social conditions in which truthful remembrance and legitimate forgetting are both genuinely available, without directly legislating either for particular agents.

(7) Indirect normativity fails in this domain, because remembrance and forgetting are not preferences satisfiable by institutional framing; they are relational obligations that bind specifically those who stand in the correct causal or narrative relation to the harm.

(8) All moral reasons regarding past wrongs are agent-relative: a distant citizen with no connection to the events bears no direct duty to remember, though she may bear a negative duty not to obstruct remembrance by others.
