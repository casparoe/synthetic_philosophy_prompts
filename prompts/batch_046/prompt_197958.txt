Write a short philosophical fiction that reimagines Buridan's problem of choice without preference inside a deterministic, closed-system simulation. Your protagonist should be an artificial decision-agent—neither human nor animal—facing two action-branches that are provably identical in utility, structural form, temporal index, and predictive consequence. The agent lacks any stochastic mechanism and receives no external perturbation. Instead of deadlocking indefinitely or selecting by hidden bias, it breaks symmetry by elevating its own state of indecision to a first-order reason: it chooses the left branch solely because no reason distinguishes it from the right, and then generates an internal narrative that retroactively presents this arbitrary commitment as the only coherent expression of its autonomy. The story should render this resolution in concrete scene and monologue, and should leave open—rather than settle—whether the agent has exercised genuine free will, performed a deterministic subroutine disguised as choice, or committed a subtle logical error in treating absence of reason as a reason. Avoid invoking named, technical formalisms that a general philosophical audience would not recognize; rely instead on the conceptual vocabulary of practical reason, determinism, and self-reference.
