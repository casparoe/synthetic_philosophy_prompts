I'm drafting questions for a podcast interview with an unnamed philosopher who defends a reconstructed Berkeleyan idealism built around what she calls a continuity constraint: on her view, esse est percipi entails not only that sensible objects exist only when perceived, but that they must be perceived with sufficient temporal density to rule out genuinely gappy existence, even in principle. She applies this specifically to the classic worry about trees persisting unobserved, arguing that divine perception supplies continuous coverage rather than sporadic filling-in, and she extends the constraint to primary qualities as well as secondary ones.

Draft eight to ten rigorous but conversational questions that go well beyond asking her to restate Berkeley's master argument. Include at least one question on how her continuity constraint differs from a merely epistemic limitation on our access to objects—pressing whether she is advancing a metaphysical claim about being itself or only a claim about evidential transparency across perceptual gaps—and include a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that the constraint concerns the ontological conditions for object-identity across intervals rather than our knowledge of them.

Include at least one question on whether the constraint commits her to occasionalism about causal relations among perceptions: if every object-state must be continuously perceived, does causation collapse into regular succession within a single perceiver's stream, and can she preserve common-sense talk of one object causing change in another? Include a follow-up keyed to her likely answer that divine perception unifies all causal sequences into one continuous experiential field, asking whether that eliminates the need for inter-object causation altogether.

Include at least one question on remote or pre-observer scenarios—say, the geological formation of an exoplanet long before any possible perceiver, human or divine, could exist—asking whether her view implies those structures literally do not exist during those intervals, or whether she must invoke a retroactive or potential-perception condition. Include a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that omniscient potential perception still counts, pressing whether that collapses her constraint back into standard theological occasionalism.

The remaining questions should press other pressure points: whether the constraint applies to abstract objects or only to sensible ones; how she responds to the incredulity that a table's existence genuinely flickers when a human briefly closes her eyes; and whether her reconstructed view can accommodate perceptual constancy without smuggling in a mind-independent substrate. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; assume familiarity with Berkeley's canonical writings but do not depend on obscure secondary scholarship. The tone should suit an informed general audience.
