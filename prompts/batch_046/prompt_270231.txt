Please review the following draft outline for a philosophical essay on Milton Friedman’s well-known argument that unrealistic assumptions in scientific models are permissible when the models yield accurate predictions—an argument often referred to as the F-twist. In the outline, I propose a distinction not widely discussed in the existing literature: between what I call “representational idealizations,” which alter the ontological structure of the target system (for example, treating firms as unitary agents with no internal organization), and “computational idealizations,” which preserve that structure but simplify the mathematics of derivation (for example, assuming continuous time to avoid discrete-step computation). I then argue that Friedman’s predictive-success criterion legitimately applies only to the second category. A model that uses representational idealization might predict well, I claim, but only by accident or by proxy correlation, and therefore the F-twist does not rescue it.

The outline proceeds in four parts. First, it reconstructs Friedman’s position by noting that he treats predictive accuracy as the sole criterion of model evaluation, explicitly bracketing descriptive realism. Second, it introduces the representational versus computational distinction and illustrates it with an example from climate modeling—specifically, comparing a model that replaces turbulent eddies with a smoothed viscosity parameter (computational) against one that treats atmospheric cells as non-interacting (representational). Third, it contends that in the second case, predictive success cannot be attributed to the model’s capturing the relevant causal dynamics, because the ontic displacement removes the very interactions that generate the phenomenon. Fourth, it considers an objection: that all models involve some ontic displacement, so the distinction is one of degree rather than kind. The outline responds by proposing that the relevant difference is not the absence of displacement, but whether the displaced elements are causally inert in the domain of prediction.
