I have been working through a position in my graduate seminar on moral responsibility that takes Strawson’s reactive-attitudes framework further than the standard compatibilist reading usually allows. The view holds that being morally responsible is exhaustively constituted by being a fitting target of reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, indignation, and their analogues—where fittingness is determined solely by the quality of will actually manifested in the agent’s conduct, with no supplementary condition about the agent’s capacity to have acted otherwise, not even a local, reasons-responsive ability of the kind many compatibilists try to preserve. On this account, talk of “basic desert” is not accommodated but dissolved: it is a misleading reification of the interpersonal conditions that make reactive attitudes warranted, and once we see that warrant as purely relational and attitudinal, there is no residual metaphysical debt—no further “desert base”—that needs grounding in libertarian freedom or in any counterfactual power over one’s actions. I want a rigorous map of what could genuinely defeat this position. Which discoveries in moral psychology or clinical neuroscience—concerning, say, the structure of psychopathy, acquired sociopathy, or the dissociation of moral emotion from normative judgment—would count as decisive? Which formal results about the logic of excuse, or which intuitive judgments about extreme manipulation, coercion, or addiction, would be fatal rather than merely awkward? Please rank these potential defeaters, and distinguish those that a committed defender of this view would have to concede as genuinely damaging from those they could absorb simply by refining the scope or object of reactive attitudes. I am particularly concerned with whether the position has become empirically insulated—whether any finding about determinism, about the causal genesis of character, or about the neural substrates of agency can be dismissed as irrelevant to the interpersonal stance—and whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a vice. In answering, please separate what is well established in the Strawson literature from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed interpretive claim—such as whether Strawson intended a purely descriptive sociology of moral practice or also a normative theory of responsibility—as though it were the consensus view.
