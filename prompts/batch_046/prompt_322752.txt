I'm drafting a chaper on standing and hypocrisy for my diss, and I've gotten obsessed with this pattern in how faculty ethics boards actually work — at least at the three universities I've been at. There's a rule, never written down, that if you've been formally sanctioned for research misconduct you can't sit on the ethics committee, which makes sense. But then there's this weird asymmetry: if your past violation was in a different category — say you messed up data management but never fabricated — you not only stay on the board, people actually treat you as having special authority to judge fabrication cases. Meanwhile, if you once publicly accused someone of fabrication and turned out to be wrong, or even if you were right but then later made a minor citation error, you've permanently lost standing to speak about fabrication at all. So the same institution treats past offenders in adjacent domains as experts, but past critics with minor later flaws as permanently disqualified in the exact domain they criticized.

What theory of moral standing does that practice presuppose? Is it assuming that hypocrisy only undermines standing within the precise domain of failure, while past suffering or failure in adjacent domains builds authority? Or is there some hidden idea about epistemic humility that only applies to critics? And can this be consistent — does it need a view where standing is pooled collectively rather than held individually, so that groups can redistribute authority? I'm trying to figure out whether this is one coherent implicit theory or just several incompatible intuitions smuggled together. Could you reconstruct what commitments about blame, hypocrisy, and standing this pattern actually requires, distinguish what it strictly needs from what it just happens to fit with, and say what an institutional policy built on a cleaner account would have to change? I don't need a bibliography, just want to see the logical structure.
