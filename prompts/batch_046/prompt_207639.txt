As a doctoral student working between Buddhist metaphysics and contemporary philosophy of personal identity, I am drafting an essay that challenges the standard philosophical assimilation of anatta to Western bundle theories. Rather than reading the five skandhas as ontological parts that compose a reducible person—along the lines of Humean or Parfitian reductionism—I want to argue that the Abhidharma distinction between conventional (sammuti) and ultimate (paramattha) reality treats personal identity as a purely pragmatic designation that lacks even derivative ontological status. Specifically, if the aggregates are ultimately real (in the Theravada Abhidhammic framework) while the person is only conventionally real, then karmic continuity cannot be explained through real causal relations among aggregates, since that would presuppose an underlying substrate. Instead, I propose that karma operates entirely at the conventional level: the pattern-tracking that sustains moral continuity is a designational practice without ontological substrate. This would make anatta not a metaphysical thesis about composition but a soteriological claim about which designations obstruct liberation. I am particularly interested in whether this reading can avoid the standard objections to Parfitian reductionism—especially that psychological continuity among impersonal parts is insufficient for robust moral responsibility—without reintroducing a substantial self. Could you comment on the coherence of this interpretation, particularly on whether Abhidharma sources genuinely permit treating karmic efficacy as confined to conventional reality, and on whether the argument successfully distinguishes anatta from Western bundle theories or merely restates them in Abhidharmic vocabulary? I am also unsure whether my framing of the aggregates as ultimately real but the person as lacking even derivative reality is internally consistent with the causal account of rebirth, and I would welcome suggestions on that specific tension.
