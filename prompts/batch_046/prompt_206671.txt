As a novelist developing a narrative structure in which characters gradually become aware of their fictional status without rupturing the reader's emotional investment, I am trying to determine which unresolved philosophical questions about fiction and affect actually constrain artistic practice, and which are merely academic taxonomy. The familiar paradox—experiencing genuine sorrow or dread for beings we know do not exist, coupled with an absence of practical response—seems too coarse for my purposes. I am more concerned with three finer distinctions that current literature treats unevenly: first, whether emotional engagement with fiction operates through a uniform mechanism, such as pretense or simulation, or through heterogeneous, genre-sensitive pathways; second, whether the willingness to imagine immoral or impossible scenarios within a fictional frame is structurally continuous with the problem of emotional response to fiction, or whether these are separate capacities that happen to overlap; and third, whether there exists a neglected intermediate state—perhaps best characterized as affective framing or modal distancing—between full belief and mere pretense, in which a reader holds an emotional posture that is neither endorsed nor fully suspended.

Rather than a survey, I want a defended agenda. Suppose you are directing a small collaborative group—limited to about three years, with backgrounds in philosophy of mind and narrative theory rather than empirical psychology—toward settling one upstream question that would restructure how we understand reader-character relations. Among the three distinctions above, which should take priority, and in what order should the remaining two be pursued? Please defend the ranking using criteria of tractability, downstream consequences for literary and critical practice, and dependency: which answers would make others obsolete or significantly easier. I am especially interested in whether the intermediate state I have sketched is a genuine gap worth pursuing, or whether it collapses into existing categories once properly analyzed. If you think it is worth pursuing, how would you refine its boundaries so that it does not merely replicate pretense theory or belief-like acceptance?
