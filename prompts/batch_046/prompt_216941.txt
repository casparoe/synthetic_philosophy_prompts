As a doctoral student in moral philosophy focusing on Kantian ethics, I am trying to work through a debate that split my advanced seminar on practical reason. Two of my peers—let me call them Mara and Elias—have been arguing about Kant's response to the murderer-at-the-door scenario, and they have pushed the discussion into territory that standard textbook treatments do not cover. Mara's central claim is that Kant's absolute prohibition on lying presupposes that the interlocutor retains full moral standing as a rational agent, and that in the classic case the murderer, having already formed an unjust intention to kill, has effectively suspended that standing for the purpose of the interaction. On her view, lying to him is not a failure of respect but its correct allocation: you protect the victim's humanity precisely by refusing to cooperate with an unjust will, and treating the murderer as someone owed truthful disclosure wrongly extends dignity to an agent who is functioning, in that moment, purely as an instrument of harm. Elias rejects this as a consequentialist infiltration. He insists that perfect duties apply unconditionally; you do not conduct a preliminary audit of someone's moral status before deciding whether the maxim of truthfulness binds you. He also warns that if we permit lying whenever we privately judge the interlocutor's intentions to be wicked, we dissolve the universality required by the Formula of Universal Law.

The debate then took a turn that neither of us anticipated. Mara introduced a variant: suppose the hidden person is not an innocent bystander but someone who previously committed a serious wrong against the murderer, and the murderer is seeking revenge for a genuine grievance rather than murdering arbitrarily. She argued that this relational complexity makes Elias's rigid universalism look implausible, since Kant's framework offers no resources for weighing the victim's own moral record against the duty not to lie. Elias maintained that even here the duty remains absolute—the victim's prior wrongdoing does not license deception, and allowing it would collapse the distinction between perfect and imperfect duties. They have asked me to adjudicate. Please go through their exchange move by move—Mara's standing-audit argument, Elias's universality reply, Mara's revenge-victim variant, and Elias's final defense—and then take a committed position. Is Mara's appeal to suspended moral standing compatible with Kant's actual system? Does the revenge-victim case genuinely undermine Elias, or does it instead expose a limitation in Mara's own reasoning? Answer with definite judgments and explicit reasons; do not merely lay out both sides and leave the choice to me.
