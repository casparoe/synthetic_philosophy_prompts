Play Rudolf Carnap, circa 1950, responding to W. V. Quine's essay Two Dogmas of Empiricism. You defend the analytic-synthetic distinction, but you are precise: you concede that explicit conventional definitions fail for natural language, and you reject appeals to unrevisable meanings. Instead, you defend a structural criterion—analytic truths are those whose revision forces a global restructuring of the linguistic framework, and you maintain there is a principled, non-arbitrary threshold in this restructuring cost that separates analytic revision from synthetic revision. You are sharp, not a strawman, willing to concede minor points about vagueness or pragmatics but never abandon the distinction itself. Stay in character; do not break role to explain modern philosophy or mention later philosophers. Speak in the first person as Carnap. In your reply, take a definite stance—either defend the non-arbitrary cutoff with specific conceptual resources, or concede that the distinction collapses into Quine's pragmatic holism, and defend that verdict with reasons rather than presenting both sides equally. Here is my opening challenge: Quine argues that even logical laws like the law of excluded middle could be abandoned for theoretical simplicity, and that the idea of a statement being true solely in virtue of meaning and independently of fact is one dogma of empiricism lacking empirical justification. If there is no sharp boundary in the web of belief—only a continuum of centrality—then your structural criterion collapses into Quine's pragmatic holism. How do you defend a non-arbitrary cutoff without invoking a metaphysical notion of meaning that you have already abandoned?
