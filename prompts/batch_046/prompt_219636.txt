I was preparing a handout for an advanced moral-philosophy seminar—about twelve graduate students who have worked through the core chapters of Scanlon’s contractualist framework but are now stuck on the internal mechanics of aggregation. Rather than asking them to map “contractualism versus consequentialism,” I want a sorting instrument: a decision-tree taxonomy that classifies positions on how reasonable rejectability operates when a principle affects different numbers of people with different degrees of harm.

Please construct the tree so that it begins with the root distinction: does the contractualist assess rejectability complaint-by-complaint for each individual, or is there a legitimate aggregative step that combines complaints across individuals before testing whether a principle is reasonably rejectable? From that root, build four successive branching questions: first, if aggregation is permitted, does it take an additive or linear form, a threshold form, or a diminishing-returns form? Second, is the comparison baseline for each complaint an individual counterfactual—what happens to this person if the principle is adopted—or a group-level status quo? Third, when two branches generate equal rejectability scores—say, saving one person from severe harm versus saving several from moderate harm—does the tie-breaker invoke a random lottery, a lexical priority for the worst-off affected party, or an external distributive principle imported from outside contractualism? Fourth, does the framework recognize a distinctive category of “distributive rescue” obligations, in which a principle requiring aid to a larger group can be justified even when no single individual’s complaint reaches the severity needed to reject the opposite principle?

At each leaf, provide the position’s conventional or proposed name (use “unnamed” if the combination is unoccupied), followed by exactly one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. The commitment should be illustrated by reference to a specific comparison case: a rescuer can either prevent one stranger from losing a limb or prevent five strangers from each losing a finger, where the summed harm in the second scenario exceeds the harm in the first but no individual in the second group suffers as severely as the one in the first.

The taxonomy should contain at least eight leaves. Where a logical combination of answers is incoherent—such as permitting linear aggregation while denying any group-level comparison baseline—label the node “incoherent” and explain in one sentence why it collapses. Also flag, at the appropriate nodes, which pairs of positions the tree fails to separate without adding premises that are not themselves part of Scanlon’s contractualist machinery. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or specific page citations; the tree should be answerable entirely from the conceptual architecture of reasonable rejection and the logic of aggregation. Keep the format suitable for a seminar handout: clear branch labels, concise leaf descriptions, and explicit annotations for unoccupied or indistinguishable spaces.
