My philosophy of art professor has offered the following verdicts across our seminar on originals and reproductions, and I want to know how she would rule on a case we have not yet discussed. First, she holds that a perfect forgery of a Vermeer executed with period-appropriate materials and technique by an unknown master forger is aesthetically equivalent to the genuine article but ontologically not a Vermeer and should not hang in a gallery of Vermeer originals, because its causal origin is wrong. Second, she judges that a bronze cast made after the artist's death from the artist's own unfinished plaster model, authorized by the estate and faithful to the model's dimensions, is an original work of that artist and retains full aesthetic value, even though the artist never touched the bronze. Third, she says that a painting restored so extensively that eighty percent of the visible surface is modern paint applied by a conservator following the artist's preliminary sketches is still that original painting, though its value is proportionally diminished by the loss of authentic surface. Fourth, she rules that a high-resolution digital scan printed on canvas with pigment inks that exactly replicates the surface of a lost fresco is a reproduction, lacking original value, despite being visually indistinguishable from what was lost. Fifth, she considers a ceramic vase shaped and glazed by an apprentice in the master's workshop, then corrected in its final form and signed by the master, to be an original work of that master, not a workshop copy. Sixth, she treats a photograph composed and exposed by the photographer but printed entirely by an assistant in the darkroom as an original photograph by the photographer. Next week we will discuss a bronze sculpture cast directly from a plaster maquette that the artist personally modeled but left incomplete at her death; the missing portions were reconstructed by a foundry technician using a generative model trained exclusively on the artist's complete works, and the estate approved both the reconstruction and the casting, yet the artist never conceived the specific form of the completed figure. Predict her verdict on whether this bronze counts as an original work with full value, identify the implicit principle or theory she seems to be running, lay out the other plausible principles that fit her earlier six verdicts equally well, say which of those competing principles diverge on this bronze case and what each predicts, give your best guess about which principle she actually holds with the reasons for preferring it, and name one further hypothetical case that would decisively distinguish the leading candidates. In your response, please keep what is settled in the philosophy of art separate from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed position as if it were the consensus view.
