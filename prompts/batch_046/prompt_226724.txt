In debates about whether immoral artists can make good art, people often invoke the bridge-engineer analogy: we don't tear down a safe bridge because its builder was cruel, so we shouldn't reject a painting because its painter was. As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I find that analogy shaky—using art feels less like crossing infrastructure and more like importing a library into my mental runtime, where a beautiful API can still carry hidden dependencies on the author's values or harm. Can you audit both analogies explicitly? For the bridge: map engineer, bridge, and user onto artist, work, and audience; identify where the mapping breaks, especially around whether art "functions" like infrastructure and whether aesthetic appreciation involves something like "structural testing" that moral judgment contaminates; and say whether the bridge's independence actually licenses separating work from artist once the disanalogies are on the table. Then do the same for the library-import picture: what corresponds to dependencies, version control, and runtime contamination; where does it overstate the artist's presence inside the finished work; and does either analogy survive intact? I'm after a precise principle, not citations—just whether my gut sense that some art feels "tainted" in a way a bridge never does can be defended, or whether both analogies collapse and I should drop that intuition.
