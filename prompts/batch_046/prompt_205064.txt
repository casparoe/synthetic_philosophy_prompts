I've been thinking on my own about the effective-altruist idea that we ought only to back what we can measure, and I keep running into an argument that seems to swallow everything. Here it is, step by step:

(1) Rational giving requires comparing actions by their expected welfare effects, and comparison without measurement is little more than guesswork.
(2) For direct interventions—bed nets, cash transfers, deworming—we have randomised trials, so we can measure effects with reasonable confidence.
(3) For long-term institutional work—reforming education systems, shifting social norms, building research cultures—the effects are too diffuse, delayed and entangled with other causes to be measured with comparable confidence.
(4) So, if I take measurability seriously, I ought to restrict my support to the first kind of intervention and treat the second as rationally invisible.
(5) But the same standard applies to my career: I cannot identify the counterfactual welfare effect of becoming a policymaker or researcher versus earning more in finance and donating.
(6) Therefore consistency demands that I either treat career choice as arbitrary, or default to income maximisation—earning to give—since income is the one measurable output.
(7) Yet this reasoning applies symmetrically to large charities: my donation often merely displaces another donor's contribution, so the measurable counterfactual impact of my gift is near zero.
(8) So by my own lights I should avoid both institutional careers and large-scale giving, leaving only small, unfunded, measurable projects.
(9) This seems to prove that rational altruism collapses into impossibility at any serious scale, which feels like a reductio rather than a conclusion.

Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost me? Please answer in British English, and say explicitly what would change your mind—which argument or evidence would make you abandon the step you identify, and where do you think your reasoning is most vulnerable to being wrong?
