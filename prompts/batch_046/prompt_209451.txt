In debates about whether consciousness is substrate-independent, people often analogize a preserved brain—say, Einstein’s, as imagined in Hofstadter and Dennett’s The Mind’s I—to a book: both are physical objects encoding information about a mind, and if you decode the encoding (reading neurons or text) you can reconstruct thoughts and perhaps converse with the original person. The analogy is used to license the conclusion that the right informational structure is sufficient for consciousness, so a detailed book, or a computer simulation, could in principle host Einstein’s mind. Audit it precisely: lay out the correspondence table—author, reader, syntax, semantics, substrate, causal dynamics—then identify where the mapping breaks, especially regarding temporal dynamics, parallel distributed processing versus linear sequential access, and the difference between representing a thought and instantiating one. What does the book side quietly import about interpretation being external and passive? Now consider a niche variant: instead of running a simulation, suppose we literally print Einstein’s complete synaptic weight matrices page by page into a “neural book” readable only by an outside interpreter who never activates the network. Does this static object fare better or worse than a running simulation? Finally, assess whether the substrate-independence conclusion survives once these disanalogies are explicit, or whether the analogy was doing all the argumentative work.
