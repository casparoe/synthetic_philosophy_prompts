I'm writing as a journalist who covers behavioural science and institutional design. I've been tracking a recurring configuration in costly signalling that doesn't seem to match the standard handicap principle as Zahavi formulated it, nor its common extensions. The pattern runs like this: an institution bears a cost that, to an outsider, looks like pure inefficiency or mismanagement — for example, a philanthropic body that maintains an overtly redundant grant-review process, or a research centre that visibly under-utilises an expensive facility — yet within a specific interpretive community that cost reads precisely as a credible commitment, because only an actor genuinely bound by the group's norms would tolerate such visible waste. The signal is therefore illegible across group boundaries; its costliness is not displayed for universal admiration but is embedded in apparent dysfunction that outsiders misread as incompetence. I have seen this in corporate sustainability reporting, in certain academic hiring rituals, and in elite membership organisations that preserve archaic procedural burdens. Is there an established concept for this — perhaps related to counter-signalling or to in-group credentialism, but distinct from both — that captures the idea of a handicap whose very visibility as waste to outsiders is structurally necessary for its function inside the group? If the literature offers no precise term, I would like a clear delineation: how it differs from ordinary costly signalling, from counter-signalling, from credential inflation, and from simple rent-seeking, together with a provisional name and the boundary conditions that would falsify an application of it.
