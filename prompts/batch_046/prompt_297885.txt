i run a small philosophy club at a uni, about 10-12 people, one 75-min session. design a hands-on game that makes them actually feel the Frege-Geach compositionality pressure on expressivism, not just hear that "moral language gets weird in conditionals." inject a physical-card twist so it's not just lecture-with-examples.

materials: red cards (moral assertions like "cheating is wrong" with a stamped expressive token, e.g. a frown icon), blue cards (plain descriptive claims), green connector cards (if/then, not, and, therefore), and a big "compositionality charter" on the wall saying every card must keep the exact same meaning and marking wherever it appears. also a simple point system: groups earn 1 point for every valid argument chain they build, but lose 2 if they break the charter.

rules / phases:
phase 1 (15 min): build standalone moral claims. red cards sit alone with their frown icon visible. easy — expressivism works fine here.
phase 2 (20 min): build compound arguments, e.g. "if lying is wrong then don't lie; lying is wrong; therefore don't lie." the catch: whenever a red card is embedded inside a green connector, the group must physically cover/remove its frown icon — you don't literally frown at a hypothetical. now interpreters have to read the chain but can't tell what attitude attaches to the embedded clause. groups must still score the chain as valid.
phase 3 (20 min): groups can try to "fix" the problem by inventing ad-hoc rules — maybe an "embedded frown" sticker, or a tone-of-voice rule. but if the same red card now carries different markings in different positions, they violate the charter on the wall and take the penalty. they should feel the squeeze: either keep expression and break compositionality, or keep compositionality and lose expressive force.

debrief (15 min): map phase 1 to bare moral expressivism; phase 2 to Frege's point about embedded contexts stripping away force; phase 3 to Geach's specific argument that the same sentence contributes differently. say what will predictably go wrong the first run — people will try to smuggle expression back through vocal tone or facial expressions, which works spoken but collapses once the cards are silent/written, showing the problem is structural not performative. be frank about what the activity can't reproduce: the formal semantic machinery (sense/reference, propositional content, truth-conditions) that makes the objection precise, and the specific logical form of attitude-embedding. you'll need to supply that verbally.

they should leave seeing that expressivism isn't just "morals are feelings" but faces a real mechanical failure when moral terms have to do logical work inside larger sentences — and that fixing it costs them either compositionality or the expressive story itself.

oh, and include one honest note on which feature of the view the card-game still misses.
