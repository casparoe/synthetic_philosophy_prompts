The debate over whether religion makes people more moral, and whether secular societies can sustain deep trust without transcendent sanction, has been stalled for decades: empirical studies on charitable behavior and crime rates point in opposite directions, philosophers invoke incommensurable conceptions of virtue, and secular ethicists reply that reciprocal cooperation needs no metaphysical anchor. I want a structural diagnosis, not a survey or verdict.

To keep the discussion from collapsing into familiar slogans, work with two distinctions I am introducing here. First, distinguish “calibrated cooperation” (behavioral alignment with norms where social visibility is high) from “binding commitment” (treating moral obligations as non-optional even when defection is undetectable). Religious defenders typically claim secular societies can secure the first but not the second; secularists typically reply that the second is unnecessary or reducible to evolved reciprocity and institutional design. Second, distinguish “epistemic opacity” (conditions of weak social monitoring) from “ontological anchoring” (the belief that moral facts hold independently of human agreement). Both sides then cite identical Scandinavian social-trust data as if it settled the matter, yet they seem to be measuring different things.

Using what each side actually argues—not a generic summary—diagnose whether the stall is verbal (hinging on whether “more moral” means calibrated cooperation, binding commitment, or axiological independence), substantive but masked (a hidden empirical question about long-run trust under epistemic opacity), or bedrock (an undefended intuition that motivation requires ontological anchoring). Identify whether the sides differ about facts, about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data, or about starting intuitions neither can argue for. Then state honestly what, if anything, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, an empirical result, a reframing, or an admission that the question is ill-posed, and whether nothing should be expected to move it.
