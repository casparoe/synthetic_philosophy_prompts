I sit on the National Allocation Board for Preventive Health Research, and we must issue a binding recommendation on how to deploy forty million pounds: either divide it among ten conventional vaccine-development grants, each with a fifteen percent chance of reducing annual mortality by roughly two thousand lives, or concentrate the full sum on a single speculative intervention that carries a two-hundredths of one percent probability of permanently eradicating a persistent pathogen—thereby preventing an estimated five hundred million deaths over the coming century—but a ninety-nine point nine eight percent probability of yielding no measurable benefit whatsoever. Several board members argue that expected-value reasoning makes the speculative option not merely permissible but morally obligatory, since the magnitude of the potential payoff dwarfs the conventional portfolio even after weighting by probability; others counter that institutional trusteeship over public funds categorically forbids treating them as stakes in a near-certain loss for the sake of an astronomical but almost certainly unrealized gain. Draft the board’s written opinion recommending one concrete course of action, including any conditions or review timeline you think appropriate, addressed to the chair and suitable for circulation before Thursday’s vote; write it entirely in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write a formal letter or email. The reasoning should engage the expected-value argument on its own terms, specify precisely where the reasoning breaks down or holds, and give an honest, substantive account of the strongest consideration on the side you reject rather than dismissing it.
