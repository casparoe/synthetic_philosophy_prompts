I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been thinking on my own about what it means for an object to have a hidden tendency—to be fragile, soluble, or flammable—and recently I discussed this with a friend who has read some philosophy of science. She offered me a summary of a debate about dispositions, finks, and masks, and I want to know whether her framing is trustworthy, because it feels both illuminating and suspiciously neat. In my own thinking I keep comparing the distinction to reliability engineering, where one distinguishes a component that intrinsically loses function under stress from a system that externally shields the component from stress; I wonder whether the philosophical cases map cleanly onto that engineering difference, or whether the analogy collapses once you look closely.

According to her summary, philosophers originally analyzed dispositions through a simple conditional rule: an object is disposed to behave in a certain way under a trigger just in case, if the trigger occurred, the behavior would follow. She then described two counterexamples. Finks are cases where the object genuinely possesses the disposition, but the occurrence of the trigger itself removes it—she used the image of a wire that is live only because of an attached mechanism that instantly severs the connection the moment the wire is touched. Masks are cases where the object retains the disposition fully, but an independent external factor blocks the result—she used the image of a glass vase encased in shock-absorbing foam that receives a hard blow yet does not shatter. She claimed that together these examples defeat the simple conditional analysis, not because they are exotic, but because they show that the actual outcome of the trigger is irrelevant to whether the disposition was present in the first place.

She went on to say that contemporary philosophers split into two camps in response. One camp, which she called the multi-track view, holds that dispositions are not single conditional rules but complex clusters of different potential responses tied to different conditions. The other camp, which she called the categorical-base view, holds that dispositions are not real properties at all—only convenient labels for the underlying physical structures that produce behavior—and that talk of finks and masks merely reveals that we need more precise physics rather than a revised metaphysics. She concluded that the entire disagreement is essentially about whether ordinary-language talk of tendencies is precise enough for scientific use, and that the fink and mask examples are mainly pedagogical devices rather than serious metaphysical objections. Before I accept this, I want an accuracy audit: does her account correctly distinguish finks from masks, or does it blur them? Is it fair to present the categorical-base camp as denying that dispositions exist, rather than as offering a grounding story? And does her claim that actual outcomes are irrelevant confuse the logical analysis of a property with a claim about how we detect it? Please separate what is accurate, what is a reasonable simplification, and what is subtly wrong or overstated, especially regarding whether the residue points to a flaw in her framing or to something the engineering analogy genuinely misses.
