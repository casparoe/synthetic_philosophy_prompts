I'm delivering the opening address at an interdisciplinary workshop convened in a university museum space dedicated to the politics of public memory. The audience is divided: on one side sit historians and political theorists who study how states metabolize defeat—specifically through the Confederate Lost Cause, the Masada narrative in Israeli state-building, the Kosovo Polje legend in Serbian nationalism, and the Dunkirk evacuation as British foundational myth; on the other side sit curators, educators, and community representatives who must translate any framework into actual exhibits, labels, and school programs.

I need a fifteen-minute speech structured around a single conceptual distinction I have been developing—between a "lost cause," in which defeat is retrospectively transformed into moral vindication and a permanent grievance against the present, and a "foundational defeat," in which the same traumatic loss is incorporated into state identity as a necessary, unrepeatable passage that demands vigilance rather than revenge. This distinction must do real structural work: it should allow me to honor genuine tactical sacrifice and civilian suffering in each of the four cases without endorsing the mythic narrative that turns defeat into an alibi for subsequent domination or exclusion. The address must avoid both false equivalence among these histories and triumphalist dismissal of the emotions they still carry, and it should conclude with a concrete curatorial problem—how to display artifacts of defeat, such as uniforms, letters, or ruins, so that they resist both glorification and reduction to mere cautionary tales—showing exactly how the conceptual framework shapes exhibition practice. Please write the address, calibrated so that the specialists recognize the analytical stakes while the curators and educators leave with a usable principle.
