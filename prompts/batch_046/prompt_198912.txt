For a reading group on epistemology and mathematics I have been trying to articulate where I stand on Benacerraf's dilemma, but I have started to distinguish two separate pressures that are often run together in the standard treatments. The first is the familiar causal-access horn: if knowledge requires some appropriate causal or interactive connection to the object known, and mathematical entities are abstract and causally inert, then either mathematical knowledge is impossible or platonism about mathematics is false. The second is what I would call the structural-mapping pressure: even if one rejects causal theories of knowledge and grants that reliable reasoning can yield knowledge of abstract structures, there remains a further question about whether our cognitive representations actually latch onto the specific mathematical structures they claim to describe, rather than onto structurally isomorphic but ontologically different systems that our reasoning procedures cannot distinguish. Compare these two challenges as they bear on mathematical realism. In particular, which one poses the deeper threat to a philosopher who wants to preserve both the objectivity of mathematics and the possibility of genuine mathematical knowledge?

If a thinker resolves the causal-access problem by adopting a non-causal account of knowledge—say, a rationalist or reliabilist framework—does that same move automatically dissolve the structural-mapping concern, or does the latter survive as an independent epistemic gap? Be precise about whether the structural-mapping worry is merely a redescription of the first horn once causation is dropped, or whether it introduces a distinct failure of tracking that requires its own remedy. Also clarify what kind of response each horn demands: for instance, whether resolving one commits you to a particular view about the nature of representation, and whether the two can be addressed together by a single philosophical maneuver or must be handled separately.
