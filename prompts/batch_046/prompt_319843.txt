I sit on my university library’s special collections acquisition committee. We must vote on whether to accept a donation from a dissolved tenant-rights collective: about four hundred pages of handwritten and audio records they call a “counter-ledger,” documenting informal eviction practices, off-books rent payments, and retaliatory neglect in one neighborhood from 1987 to 2019. The collective deliberately kept these outside standard archival form—no full tenant names, no street addresses, only relational descriptions and first-name-only testimony—to prevent landlords and immigration authorities from using the archive as a tracking tool. Their donation agreement requires the materials stay in this anonymized, non-geocoded format and that we not reconstruct missing identifiers.

This conflicts directly with our institutional mandate. Our cataloging protocols require verifiable provenance, geographic metadata, and cross-reference capacity with official housing records; without these, the collection fails our “archival integrity” standard and cannot enter our discoverable repository. At the same time, historians on our advisory board argue that accepting the materials in their current form—and designing a descriptive schema that preserves their strategic silences—would recognize how power already operates in the record: official archives are complete for landlords, and this collection’s value lies in its refusal to replicate that surveillance logic.

Draft the committee’s written recommendation to the library director, to be circulated before the vote. Recommend one concrete course—accept with modified protocols, reject, or accept only if reformatted—together with an implementation plan specifying cataloging standards, mediated researcher access, and how we would handle future digitization grants that require standard metadata. Treat seriously the strongest argument for the opposing view, whether that is the institutional risk of housing an unverifiable record or the ethical risk of destroying a necessary silence by forcing standardization. Take a definite position with your reasons; do not present the options as equally valid and leave the choice to us.
