I was arguing with my roommate last night about whether there's actually a kind of free will that's worth wanting, or if the whole phrase is just philosophers being dramatic. I think I've landed on a specific version — call it revisionary continuity — and I want to know if it's totally busted. The idea is that free will isn't about having alternative possibilities, and it's not just Frankfurt-style hierarchical satisfaction either. Instead, it's about possessing a capacity to revise the very framework you use to evaluate your own desires, where that revision-process itself isn't causally determined by prior states or outside manipulation. So you can change the metric by which you rank your second-order volitions, and this meta-plasticity is the real deal. Without it, I think every other variety collapses into being a well-behaved mechanism. But I'm definatley open to being wrong.

What would count agains this view? I'm looking for specific defeaters — maybe results in neuroscience about prefrontal control being deterministic, or psych experiments showing that belief revision is always driven by hidden biases, or conceptual arguments that this just pushes the problem up one level. Rank them from fatal to awkward. Also tell me what defenders of revisionary continuity would themselves admit is damaging, versus what they'd dismiss. And be honest: have I just qualified this into unfalsifiability by saying the revision 'escapes determination' in some hand-wavy way?
