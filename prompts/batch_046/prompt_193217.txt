I have been discussing with a friend who is taking a course in jurisprudence, and we keep returning to the debate between Patrick Devlin and H. L. A. Hart on whether the law ought to enforce private morality. My friend notes that the exchange has become curiously static: Devlin insists that a shared social morality is as essential to society as a shared government, and that immorality weakens the social fabric in the same way that treason does, which means the law may legitimately suppress practices such as bigamy even when no individual is directly harmed; Hart replies with the harm principle, drawing a sharp line between private immorality and conduct that inflicts tangible injury on others, and he accuses Devlin of relying on an unverified disintegration thesis that mistakes popular revulsion for a reason to coerce. Despite these moves being rehearsed for decades, neither camp shifts its ground, and my friend offers a hypothesis: perhaps this is not merely an empirical disagreement about whether societies collapse when private vices are tolerated, but a deeper conceptual divergence over what morality denotes—whether it refers to critically justified principles that protect individual autonomy, or to an empirically given collective conscience that binds a society together functionally.

I am inclined to think there is something to this framing, but I want a sharper diagnosis. Is the impasse substantive, verbal, or a case of two different questions—one about the normative limits of state coercion rooted in liberal political philosophy, the other about the functional conditions of social cohesion rooted in a Durkheimian sociology—being pursued under the single heading of legal moralism? Please examine what each side actually commits to, beyond the standard slogans, and consider whether a hidden disagreement about the ontology of social norms, or about the nature of legal authority and its relation to the internal point of view, is really driving the stall. If the disagreement is structural in this way, what would actually count as progress: a rigorous sociological study testing whether the relaxation of shared moral norms correlates with social disintegration, a conceptual clarification that distinguishes critical morality from positive morality, a formal model of social cohesion that makes the functional claim precise, or an argument that the very question of enforcing morals is ill-posed because it conflates descriptive sociology with normative jurisprudence?
