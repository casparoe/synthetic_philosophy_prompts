I am a high school student writing an independent paper on collective action, and I have noticed a recurring pattern in public-goods failures that I cannot quite place in the concepts I already know. Consider a good that is genuinely non-excludable and benefits people far in the future—say, an anonymous fund that reduces the risk of a catastrophic pandemic in 2075—where every contribution is completely unobservable. There is no donor list, no social acknowledgment, and no mechanism by which peers can verify who bore the cost. In these situations, people who sincerely claim to care about the long-term outcome often contribute very little in practice, and the usual diagnosis is free-riding. Yet standard free-riding describes agents who want the collective benefit without paying for it; here, many advocates appear to genuinely desire the outcome, but the contribution still collapses, not mainly because private costs exceed private benefits, but because the act of contributing offers no legible signal of one's commitment to others or to oneself. I have seen something like this in discussions of untracked climate-adaptation donations and anonymous funding for long-term scientific research, where enthusiasm for the cause remains high but private action evaporates once visibility is removed. Does this specific failure mode—where a public good is undermined not by consumption-based free-riding but by the absence of an observable contribution mechanism—have an established name in economics, philosophy, or sociology? If there is no precise term, how should it be carved out and distinguished from ordinary free-riding, from moral licensing, and from virtue signaling? I am not looking for a bibliography or a list of authors; I want to understand the conceptual boundaries: what exactly this phenomenon is, what it resembles, and where its edges lie.
