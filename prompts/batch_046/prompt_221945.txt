I'm preparing background material for an interdisciplinary workshop I'm co-organizing with colleagues from philosophy and computer science, and in skimming a recent essay — not a canonical academic source, but a widely shared piece by an unnamed technology writer — I encountered a summary of the alignment problem that struck me as both clarifying and potentially misleading. The author frames alignment as fundamentally the technical task of calibrating an artificial system's reward signal so that it tracks human values, and claims this divides neatly into two sub-problems: outer alignment, which concerns the correct specification of the reward, and inner alignment, which concerns preventing the system from pursuing a proxy rather than the intended objective. Once these two are resolved, the essay contends, concerns about economic displacement, geopolitical concentration of capability, or malicious use are genuinely important but belong to governance and policy rather than to alignment research proper. The summary further asserts that alignment is really just a specialized instance of interpretability, because if we fully understand why a model produces its outputs we have already guaranteed that it pursues the right goals, making separate research programs redundant. To round out the taxonomy, the author adds a third notion they call structural alignment — the degree to which a system's optimization pressure reflects institutional selection incentives rather than its explicit objective — and places this firmly in sociology, not in machine learning.

I am inclined to think the first part about reward specification and proxy gaming is roughly correct, but I worry that the essay draws overly sharp boundaries between technical work and its social context, and that it collapses distinct research agendas into one another. Before I include this framing in our workshop materials, I would like an accuracy audit that separates what is standard, what is a defensible simplification, and what is a subtle misrepresentation.
