I just spent three hours arguing with a close friend about whether the ethical implications of panpsychism actually extend to suffering in fundamental physics—specifically, whether quantum fields or spin-network nodes could be said to have some proto-experiential status that makes decoherence events morally relevant. The argument got in the weeds: we kept circling whether integrated information at the Planck scale is a coherent notion or a category mistake, and whether assigning even minimal moral considerability to non-biological substrates requires a theory of value that does not collapse into pan-sentience by definition. Now I have to put my institutional hat on.

I sit on the University Interdisciplinary Research Ethics Board. A proposal has come to us from a physics-philosophy collaboration that wants institutional approval and a small seed grant to develop what they call micro-phenomenal welfare assessment protocols—essentially, a framework for evaluating whether theoretical models in quantum gravity, particularly those adopting a panprotopsanpsychist ontology, generate obligations regarding the suffering-capacity of fundamental entities, such as whether rapid decoherence across high-entropy configurations could be analogized to harm. The principal investigator argues that if consciousness is fundamental rather than emergent, then ethical review should not be restricted to biological or artificial agents; the opposition within the board—including my friend, who thinks this is metaphysical overreach—says we are being asked to regulate ontology with an ethics framework that has no empirical purchase and that risks institutionalizing pseudoscience.

Please draft the board's decision memo for circulation before next Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve, approve with conditions, or reject—with a clear time frame for implementation or review. The reasoning should engage seriously with the PI's argument: do not simply dismiss panpsychism, but address whether micro-phenomenal welfare can be operationalized without circularity. At the same time, give full weight to the strongest objection—that extending moral considerability to spin states or field configurations lacks the intersubjective criteria that make suffering claims meaningful for institutional policy. The memo should be something the researchers could actually respond to: if you recommend conditions, specify what deliverables, such as a conceptual clarification paper or an independent philosophy review, would satisfy them. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; reason from the conceptual structure directly.
