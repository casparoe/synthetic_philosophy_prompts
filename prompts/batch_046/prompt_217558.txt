As a retired evolutionary biologist who has spent the past several years working seriously through conservative political thought, I will present a short paper next month to our university’s interdisciplinary science-and-values colloquium. My thesis is that Chesterton’s fence should not be treated as a generic caution about unintended consequences, but should be reconstructed through the lens of evolutionary developmental biology—specifically, the idea of canalization and robustness in complex adaptive systems. I argue that long-standing social institutions are analogous to deeply canalized developmental pathways: their stability is not accidental but an emergent property of extensive historical integration across functional scales, which means that rational reconstruction of their “purpose” systematically misses hidden structural fragilities and non-obvious collapse modes. From this I derive a qualified Burkean conclusion: conservatism is justified not by epistemic humility alone, but by the formal properties of robustness and perturbation-sensitive breakdown that characterize institutions shaped by long evolutionary-like histories of selection and adaptation. I do not claim societies possess genomes or that selection operates identically, but I do claim that the formal dynamics of robustness, redundancy, and hidden dependency apply.

The audience is precisely this: four analytic philosophers (two in ethics, one in philosophy of science, one in political philosophy) who are hostile to any naturalization of normative claims; two evolutionary biologists who understand canalization, evolutionary stability, and robustness literature well but are deeply skeptical of normative conclusions drawn from biology; one scholar of Burke who regards biological analogies as category mistakes that replace Burke’s historical and prudential reasoning with pseudo-scientific mysticism; and several graduate students from both disciplines. They will not ask for reading lists or citations; they will attack the analogy at the mechanism level, the leap from “is” to “ought,” my disciplinary competence, and the political implications of treating harmful stable institutions as if they were healthy canalized systems.

Prepare me for the objections I will actually face, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage each could do. For each objection, phrase it exactly as that specific audience member would put it (note their field and likely vocabulary); tell me what I should concede up front without undermining my core claim; tell me what I must hold; and identify the weakest point in my reply. I want to know what reply has to land for the proposal to survive, and where that reply is soft. Include the objection I am most hoping nobody asks—likely the challenge that this framework cannot distinguish a harmful institution that is robust because it suppresses dissent from a beneficial one that is robust because it genuinely coordinates behavior, and that I have therefore smuggled in normative criteria while pretending to derive them from system dynamics. Finally, identify the single weakest point in my own position—the step in the argument that is genuinely vulnerable rather than merely controversial—and advise me on how to defend it in under two minutes rather than hoping they overlook it.
