At a recent discussion group—mostly rationalist-leaning people with some biology background—two members, Nadia and Eli, got into a sustained disagreement about biological function ascription, specifically in cases that sit at the boundary of evolutionary and engineered systems. I was taking notes and trying not to take sides. Here is the exchange, reconstructed as faithfully as I could.

(1) Nadia opened by defending a strict etiological view: to say that X has the biological function F is to say that X was selected for F in the evolutionary history of the lineage. Without that historical entitlement, we are merely describing a causal effect or an intended purpose, not a biological function. She gave the heart versus an implanted artificial heart: both pump blood, but only the heart has the biological function of pumping, because only the heart was shaped by selection for that task.

(2) Eli replied that this makes functional ascription depend on hidden historical facts we rarely have direct access to, and that it misdescribes how biologists actually talk. We say the heart pumps blood because of its current role in maintaining circulatory homeostasis; if an artificial heart integrates and does the same organizational work, it acquires that functional status. Selection history is one causal explanation among others, not the definition of function.

(3) Nadia countered with the tumor example: a tumor can causally contribute to hormone production, yet we do not call hormone production its function; we call it a pathological side effect. The difference, she said, is precisely that the heart's contribution is selected for, while the tumor's is not—even though both have evolutionary histories. So causal contribution alone cannot distinguish function from malfunction or accident.

(4) Eli responded that the tumor case actually favors causal-role analysis: we deny the tumor a function not because of its evolutionary origin (it evolved!) but because its contribution disrupts rather than maintains the organism's organizational integrity. He then introduced a case he thought was decisive: the human appendix, which evolutionary accounts often treat as a digestive vestige with little current selection, yet which in modern clinical contexts serves as a reservoir for gut microbiota that can be re-established after antibiotics. By Nadia's view, its function is digestion or none; but physiologically it is currently contributing to immune and microbial maintenance. Calling that contribution a mere beneficial side effect seems to ignore the actual biological work being done.

(5) Nadia conceded that the appendix case is tricky but argued that Eli is conflating current useful effect with function. Unless there is evidence that selection is currently maintaining the appendix specifically for microbiome recovery, its bacterial reservoir role is best described as an exaptation or spandrel—like using the nose to hold glasses. She then introduced what she called a functional orphan: an engineered tissue scaffold implanted to filter blood, which performs the same causal role as a kidney but has no evolutionary lineage at all. By her lights, calling the scaffold's filtration a biological function erases the distinction between evolved nature and designed artifact that makes evolutionary explanation distinctive. The scaffold has a designed purpose; the kidney has a biological function.

(6) Eli said that Nadia's functional-orphan distinction is clarifying but not fatal to his view. He proposed that in clinical and synthetic-biology contexts we need causal-role ascription to guide action: if a surgeon knows the appendix is maintaining microbiome stability, preservation is rational regardless of evolutionary history. He also questioned whether Nadia's view can handle cases of functional surrogacy, where a non-selected trait takes over the organizational role of a lost selected trait—if the etiological account is strict, it must deny function to the surrogate, which seems biologically perverse.
