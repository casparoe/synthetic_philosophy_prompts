I have been trying to work out how to decide what to do when I am genuinely unsure whether consequentialism or a strict deontological view is correct—say, whether to break a small promise in order to prevent a serious harm. I have read that philosophers sometimes recommend weighing the options by my credence in each theory, but this puzzles me: the two frameworks do not share a common unit of value, so there appears to be no exchange rate between saving someone from harm and violating a promise. Without such an exchange rate, it seems impossible to calculate any expected moral value. Therefore, shouldn't we fall back on a maximin rule—choose the act that is worst-case best? Yet that leads to a strange result: if there is any non-zero credence in a theory that absolutely forbids breaking promises, maximin will always forbid the promise-breaking, regardless of the harm prevented. So moral uncertainty appears to collapse into blind obedience to the strictest theory I find remotely plausible. Have I correctly shown that the lack of intertheoretic exchange rates makes maximin unavoidable, or is there a hidden mistake in assuming that comparison requires a shared cardinal scale? Please conclude with \answer{...} stating the key flaw in my reasoning.
