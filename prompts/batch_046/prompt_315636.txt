I am trying to articulate the principle behind my judgments about whether a mass starvation event should be classified as a natural disaster or as a political crime, working within Amartya Sen’s distinction between food availability decline and entitlement collapse but pushing it to cases that sit near its boundaries. Here are my verdicts, given as gut classifications rather than theoretical conclusions.

First, the Bengal famine of 1943: rice was physically present in the region, but wartime inflation, wage collapses, and disrupted markets destroyed rural purchasing power. Verdict: an entitlement failure, not a natural disaster, despite the absence of any crop failure.

Second, the Great Leap Forward famine of 1959–1961: aggregate grain production collapsed due to policy-induced agricultural disruption, yet the state also extracted and misdirected available grain. Verdict: primarily a political crime, but one in which an availability collapse is genuinely present; this feels different from pure entitlement failure, yet I still resist calling it a natural disaster.

Third, the Irish famine of the 1840s: the potato blight was a natural biological event, but other foodstuffs were exported and access was governed by market and colonial structures that left the poor without entitlement. Verdict: a natural trigger exploited or permitted by political and economic arrangements; the famine itself is not purely natural.

Fourth, a hypothetical drought in a well-functioning democracy with robust social transfers: crop yields fall sharply, but universal entitlements prevent starvation. Verdict: a natural event with no famine; the absence of mass death confirms that the disaster status attaches to the failure of protection, not to the weather.

Fifth, a hypothetical meteor impact that destroys global food production with no government involvement and perfectly equal deprivation: verdict a genuine natural disaster, with no political crime to assign.

Sixth, a case I keep revisiting: a corrupt regime deliberately withholds food aid from a specific ethnic region, causing severe localized starvation, while the rest of the country enjoys surplus. The aggregate national food supply is intact. Verdict: clearly a political crime of entitlement denial, yet I hesitate to call it a “famine” in the same sense as Bengal or the Great Leap Forward, because the geographic and demographic scope is restricted.

My question is whether these judgments hang together on a single principle, or whether I am implicitly running together two distinctions—one about whether starvation results from aggregate scarcity versus distributional access failure, and another about whether democratic or accountable institutions are present. Specifically, I want to know: does the principle need to treat the Great Leap Forward and the localized junta case as the same kind of phenomenon, despite the presence of availability collapse in one and its absence in the other? And does the presence or absence of democratic institutions function as a necessary condition for the “political crime” classification, or merely as an empirical correlate? If there is a coherent account, I want it stated precisely, with explicit boundary conditions—especially for cases where a natural trigger and an entitlement failure coincide, and for cases where the entitlement failure is geographically confined rather than society-wide. If no single principle accommodates all six verdicts without contortion, I want to know which verdict should be revised and why.
