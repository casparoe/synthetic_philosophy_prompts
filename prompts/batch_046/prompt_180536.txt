As a doctoral researcher in the philosophy of biology and normative bioethics, I am attempting to specify the full adequacy conditions for a theory that treats the hedonistic imperative—the biotechnological abolition of suffering—not as a peripheral applied-ethics proposal but as a fundamental redesign constraint on evolutionary neurobiology. The existing literature too quickly collapses into either an uncritical endorsement of genetic intervention or an appeal to the "wisdom" of evolved affective architecture. I need the complete specification of what such a theory must be able to do to count as adequate, with the conceptual work centered on the criteria rather than on scoring particular authors.

Please provide the full requirements list. For each desideratum, state: (i) the precise phenomenon, case, or conceptual distinction it protects; (ii) why failure on this point undermines the theory rather than being a negotiable cost; and (iii) whether it functions as a hard constraint or as a defeasible preference. The list should cover, at minimum, the following dimensions, though you should add or refine any that belong:

- The evolutionary-functional distinction: the theory must account for the adaptive roles of nociception and negative affect without committing the naturalistic fallacy, and it must specify which aversive states are candidates for elimination versus which serve irreducible informational functions.
- The suffering / negative-affect boundary: it must not collapse the distinction between suffering (a severe, sustained negative state with a particular phenomenal intensity and temporal profile) and functional negative affect (e.g., acute stress responses, competitive frustration, epistemic anxiety, or motivational drive) that may be necessary for learning, agency, or social coordination.
- The motivational conservation problem: it must explain whether, and in what form, mechanisms carrying negative valence must be retained to preserve moral agency, scientific curiosity, or long-term cooperative behavior—or whether these capacities can be fully sustained within an architecture that lacks suffering entirely.
- Cross-species calibration: it must provide a non-arbitrary criterion for comparing suffering-capacity and intervention priority across phylogenetically distant nervous systems (e.g., mammalian limbic circuits versus decapod nociceptive pathways) without collapsing into anthropocentric projection or arbitrary sentience thresholds.
- Affective continuity: it must specify whether some minimal range of negative valence must be preserved for an organism to track harm, maintain identity-constitutive preferences over time, or avoid a form of hedonic blindness that undermines autonomy.
- Neuroplasticity and reversibility: it must remain consistent with current neuroscience regarding the reversibility of hedonic set-points, allostatic load, and the risks of abrupt uniform abolition—particularly the possibility that eliminating suffering pathways could produce dysregulation rather than stable well-being.
- Institutional and distributive architecture: the theory must specify how abolition technologies are governed, distributed, and protected from coercive misuse, distinguishing between voluntary adult adoption, proxy decision for non-autonomous beings, and ecological release into wild populations.

Then identify which single requirement does the most damage to the most popular versions of the hedonistic imperative—for example, the view that genetic or pharmacological rewiring can straightforwardly replace suffering with gradients of well-being without structural loss. Also indicate which two or three requirements are genuinely negotiable: that is, a theory could violate them and still remain within the bounds of serious consideration, and defend that judgment.

A brief table scoring the main existing approaches (e.g., the original abolitionist framework associated with David Pearce, more recent "gradient-replacement" models in synthetic biology, and conservative evolutionary-psychology objections) against the hard constraints is welcome, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves: I am primarily building the evaluation framework. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need arguments that could be evaluated without external lookup.
