I'm working on a novel that jumps between late antiquity and some made-up Lyon clockmakers, and I got into this with a friend over coffee about when a legend actually hardens into history. We started calling it the directional anachronism filter — basically if a story carries details that match an older material culture but feel wrong for the people writing it down, and if those details don't do any obvious poltical or moral work for the composers, then they're probably fossilized memory rather than pure invention.

So I mapped out the chain and now it's eating it'self. (1) If a narrative contains culturally anachronistic details that align with an earlier era but not the composers' own, and those details are unnecessary for the story's present function, then they are best explained as preserved historical memory. (2) The Homeric epics describe chariot tactics and armor that fit Mycenaean practice rather than Archaic Greek warfare, and Achilles' sulking doesn't exactly serve polis propaganda. (3) The earliest Welsh Arthurian layers mention cavalry logistics and hill-fort defense that suit post-Roman Britain but are awkward for later medieval court mythmaking. (4) The Exodus account includes census figures and tribal structures that mirror later Iron Age demographics more than New Kingdom Egypt, plus Moses losing his temper isn't great propaganda for a priestly class. (5) So in each case the filter points to a real kernel behind the legend. (6) Now apply it to a story I invented for my novel, 'The Clockmaker of Lyon': he builds a clock with a spring-driven escapement centuries before that mechanism existed there, and the detail is embarrassing to my protagonist's family chronicle because it makes them look technologically backward. (7) By (1), that anachronism should be read as preserved memory of an actual clockmaker. (8) But I literally made him up last tuesday. (9) So (1) proves way too much.

I want to keep the filter for Troy, Arthur, and the Exodus — I feel like there's something there — but I don't want my fictional clockmaker to accidentally become historical. Where's the slip? Is it a hidden scope problem in (1) about which direction the anachronism runs, or is one of the historical premises weaker than it looks? Tell me which step to reject and what I'd be paying for it. End with \answer{...} putting the number of the step you think I should reject, or 'valid' if the argument holds and I just have to bite a bullet on a premise.
