Since my father was diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease, I have observed his remaining habits—navigating familiar rooms, dressing—alongside the complete erosion of his narrative self-awareness and his inability to use cultural tools such as calendars or family photographs as reasoning aids. This has made Dennett’s taxonomy of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—personally urgent, but I am stuck on a conceptual point that is usually discussed in evolutionary rather than clinical terms. Please explain, with some precision, how these four categories are meant to differ in their present-tense architecture of representation and control, rather than merely in their phylogenetic origins. Specifically, I want to understand whether the persistence of highly trained procedural behaviour in dementia represents a genuinely intact Skinnerian layer that can survive the collapse of Gregorian capacities, or whether the taxonomy treats these kinds as nested evolutionary stages such that degrading the outer layer necessarily compromises the inner ones. Additionally, clarify whether a Popperian creature’s internal modelling can function independently of Gregorian cultural scaffolding, or whether the two are conceptually entangled. I am not seeking medical advice or a bibliography, but a philosophical analysis of whether applying this framework to neurocognitive decomposition—rather than to evolutionary ascent—reveals that these 'kinds' are better conceived as dissociable functional modules, and what that implies for the unity of a single mind.
