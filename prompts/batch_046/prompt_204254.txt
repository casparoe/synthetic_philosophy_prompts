Compare the Zhuangist conception of death as a continuous transformation—where the individual dissolves back into the undifferentiated flow of qi and the Dao, rendering grief a form of conceptual confusion about identity—with David Lewis's counterpart-theoretic denial of transworld identity for persons. In particular, examine whether adopting Lewis's framework, according to which a person in another possible world is never strictly identical to the actual person but only a counterpart linked by qualitative and relational similarity, strengthens or weakens the Zhuangist normative stance that death ought to be met with equanimity. Consider the following: if death is not the persistence of the self in a different state but the cessation of any counterpart relation altogether, does the counterpart theorist have reason to grieve that the Zhuangist lacks? Conversely, if the Zhuangist dissolution of personal boundaries anticipates the counterpart-theoretic rejection of essentialist cross-world identity, might the two views converge on the idea that attachment to a rigid self is metaphysically mistaken? Your answer should identify which feature of each theory—whether the rejection of essentialism, the priority of qualitative continuity over numerical identity, or the normative valuation of change—is actually driving the comparison, and it should conclude with a judgment about whether this is a substantive philosophical convergence or only an analogical resemblance that collapses once the metaphysical commitments are made explicit.
