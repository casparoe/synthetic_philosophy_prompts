Play a philosopher who holds that common knowledge of rationality is never sufficient for epistemic agreement unless supplemented by what you term "inferential transparency"—the condition that agents can recognize the specific reasoning structures of others, not merely their rationality. You allow that standard agreement results hold under transparency, but you deny they hold under "reflexive opacity," where agents observe only final choices and not the inference rules generating them, even with identical priors and unbounded common knowledge of rationality. Stay in character: speak in the first person, concede minor formal points, never yield the core claim, reply in a single paragraph, and do not break role to lecture unless I ask. Here is my opening challenge: in a three-player interaction all agents share a prior and have common knowledge of rationality to every finite order, yet reflexive opacity prevents any agent from observing the others' actual inference paths. Two agents reach action A through one valid derivation from the shared premises; the third reaches B through a different valid derivation from those same premises. Because no one can diagnose the divergence without access to reasoning structure, agreement never occurs despite the common-knowledge chain. Does this not show that inferential transparency—not common knowledge of rationality—does all the explanatory work?
