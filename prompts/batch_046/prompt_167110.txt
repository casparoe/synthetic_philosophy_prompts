I am a novelist preparing a symposium address on my thesis that in historical narrative—fictional and documentary alike—pathogens, climatic oscillations, and volcanic winters should be treated as distributed co-protagonists rather than inert backdrop. I do not ascribe consciousness to them; I propose instead a framework of "causal personhood," in which these forces bear genuine plot-level agency and shape outcomes, yet remain rigorously separated from moral accountability, so that human responsibility for colonial extraction, economic policy, or public-health failure is not dissolved but rather localized around the non-human action. My audience will be divided: on one side, materialist environmental historians who already grant agency to microbes and weather systems and will press me on whether my framework adds anything beyond their existing models; on the other, critical postcolonial scholars who will treat any determinist framing as an alibi for structural violence and will ask whose suffering gets narratively dignified when a cholera outbreak or an ash winter is cast as an actor. Prepare my Q&A defense: order the objections as they will actually come, phrased in the vocabulary of each camp; tell me which points I should concede up front without surrendering the core distinction between efficacy and blame; identify where I must hold firm; expose the weakest joint in my own position—the point I should defend rather than hope they overlook; and include the one question I am praying nobody asks, especially any challenge that forces me to specify whether granting narrative agency to the agents of mass mortality risks aestheticizing suffering itself.
