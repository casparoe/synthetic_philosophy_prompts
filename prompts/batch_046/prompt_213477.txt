In popular writing about moral foundations theory I keep running into the line that “morality binds and blinds.” People invoke it to explain why left and right talk past each other, but they also seem to slide between very different ideas: that shared values create group cohesion, that they make outsiders look immoral, that conservatives draw on five foundations while liberals draw mainly on two, and—most confusingly—that this psychological difference either proves neither side is “right” or somehow proves the broader palette is closer to moral reality. I don’t have an academic background, so I can’t tell which of these claims is actually in the phrase and which is being smuggled in.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that “morality binds and blinds” could stand for? I want a menu: the purely descriptive claim about in-group binding and out-group blinding; the psychological claim that liberals and conservatives weight care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and purity differently; the stronger claim that having more active foundations equals seeing more of moral reality; and the normative claim that recognizing these asymmetries tells us nothing about which politics is correct, or conversely that it favors one side. For each reading, say whether it is a tautology, a defensible empirical finding, or a hidden normative argument. In particular, address the version I heard used to end a debate—namely, that because conservatives activate all five foundations, their moral judgment is more complete, and therefore psychology itself answers “who is right.” Is that reading actually supported by the slogan, or is it an extra step? And which of these readings, if any, actually justify using the phrase to settle an argument about whether left or right morality is closer to the truth?
