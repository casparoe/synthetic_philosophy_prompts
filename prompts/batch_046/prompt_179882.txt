I spent much of last evening in a rather heated discussion with a friend about collective choice, and we kept returning to the Gibbard-Satterthwaite result: with three or more alternatives, any deterministic voting rule that is not dictatorial (or restricted to a duumvirate) must be vulnerable to strategic misrepresentation. We could not agree on whether this should be read as a counsel of despair about democratic aggregation or as a precise boundary that careful mechanism design might navigate around; the argument ended, as these things often do, with both positions intact and unreconciled.

I should like you to write a poem in American English that captures this exact tension without resolving it into either cynicism or complacency. Specifically, compose a sestina of thirty-nine lines — six sestets followed by a three-line envoi — spoken in the first person by the decision procedure itself, not by any individual voter. The six end-words must be preference, rule, truth, vote, cycle, and voice, and they must follow the standard sestina permutation (1-2-3-4-5-6; then 6-1-5-2-4-3; and so on through the six stanzas). The mechanical rotation of these words should structurally enact the theorem: just as the end-words shift position but never disappear, strategic manipulation finds an entry point regardless of how preferences are initially arranged. The poem should acknowledge that the paradox vanishes with only two alternatives, should treat manipulation as the misrepresentation of a true preference ordering over three or more options, and should not suggest that randomisation or cardinal scoring escapes the result, since Gibbard-Satterthwaite applies specifically to deterministic social-choice functions mapping preference profiles to outcomes.

In the envoi, all six words must appear in order, and the final two lines should state precisely what must be sacrificed — either the fixed voice of a dictator or the illusion of a genuinely unmanipulable rule — leaving the reader with the same unresolved ambiguity that ended our discussion. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with strategy-proofness recognises the logic rather than merely the vocabulary, yet it must also work as poetry: the rotation should feel inevitable, the mechanism’s self-discovery should build across the stanzas, and the closing should land with the precise structural trade-off we could not settle between us.
