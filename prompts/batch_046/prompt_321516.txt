I am composing a salon scene for a historical novel set in late-Quattrocento Florence, and I need a polished witticism—delivered between a diplomat and a jaded humanist over wine—that turns on the actual conceptual tension in Machiavelli's pairing of fortuna and virtù. The joke should exploit the specific claim that fortuna directs half of human affairs and can be struck and subdued only by the bold and impetuous, set against the Stoic conviction that true virtù is an internal, unshakeable rational order impervious to external contingency. Its punchline should depend on the idea that Machiavellian virtù is not Stoic self-possession but an active, almost performative shaping of occasion—so that the sage who refuses to beat fortune when she exposes herself mistakes constancy for impotence, and is therefore not self-sufficient but merely uninvited. It should be intelligible to someone who grasps that fortuna is contingent externality while virtù is adaptive agency, without requiring citations, source verification, or bibliographic apparatus; I need only the joke itself, period-flavored but not archly antiquated, with the irony resting on the conceptual content rather than on generic philosophical wordplay.
