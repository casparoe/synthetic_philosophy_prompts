Consider a hypothetical case of chronological scattering: a system S exists continuously from t1 to t2, is then completely annihilated with no physical remnant during the open interval (t2, t3), and is reconstructed at t3 from entirely new matter guided solely by information causally traced to S, after which it persists to t4. There is perfect structural and functional continuity between the t1-stage and the t3-stage, yet no spatiotemporal path connects them.

Please explain the specific conceptual tension this raises for endurantism about diachronic identity. In particular, address how the view's commitment that an enduring object is wholly present at each time of its existence interacts with the total absence of S during (t2, t3), and whether causal-informational continuity can substitute for spatiotemporal continuity without tacitly importing temporal parts. Contrast this with the perdurantist treatment, in which the t1 and t3 stages might be parts of a single four-dimensional worm that is spatiotemporally discontinuous but unified by appropriate relations.

Finally, clarify why this problem is not reducible to the Ship of Theseus: identify the distinct metaphysical issue it highlights about whether persistence requires continuous existence, and what that implies for the ontology of enduring objects versus stage-based ontologies.
