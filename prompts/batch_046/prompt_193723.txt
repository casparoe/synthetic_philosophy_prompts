I am preparing a feature on how the moral equality of combatants is being applied to emerging forms of warfare, and I have been tracking the published rulings of an advisory ethics panel that has issued consistent judgments across novel scenarios. Their record is as follows: they held that a regular soldier fighting for an unjust aggressor retains full combatant immunity when engaging uniformed defenders, yet forfeits it if she deliberately strikes civilian medical personnel; they ruled that a mercenary contracted solely to defend a just cause possesses the same moral license to kill as state soldiers but does not share their protected status against capture or prosecution; they determined that an operator who remotely disables an enemy command network without causing physical injury or death is not exercising lethal rights and therefore stands outside the equality principle entirely; and they found that a drone pilot operating from another continent, who inflicts lethal harm without ever facing reciprocal physical danger, is not a combatant in the relevant sense and consequently lacks both the right to kill and the liability to be killed. The panel must now issue a recommendation on a new proposal: a synthetic-biology specialist embedded within a uniformed unit of a just-cause force, physically present in the theater of operations and subject to the same risks as her unit, who deploys an engineered microbe that degrades enemy logistics materials without harming human beings directly. For consistency with its own precedents, should the panel grant her full combatant status under the equality principle, deny it entirely, or assign an intermediate classification? Which prior ruling is the closest analog—the embedded soldier, the mercenary, the remote cyber-operator, or the distant drone pilot—and what principle regarding the boundaries of combatancy, whether risk reciprocity, organizational integration, direct lethality, or functional equivalence, would each consistency path commit the panel to adopting? If its track record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, what further hypothetical—perhaps a uniformed soldier using a permanently disabling but non-lethal acoustic weapon, or a civilian contractor present in the theater who directs lethal strikes—would best distinguish which principle is actually governing their framework?
