I'm designing a conceptual taxonomy for a philosophy-of-religion seminar on divine providence and human freedom, and I want a decision tree—not a list of theologians—that sorts positions on middle knowledge and counterfactuals of creaturely freedom (CCFs). The tree should begin from one root commitment: God is omniscient, and at least some human actions are genuinely free in a libertarian sense (that is, the agent could have done otherwise in a way that does not reduce to prior causal determination).

From that root, each internal node must split on a concrete metaphysical or logical claim about counterfactual truth and providential control, phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing which philosophical label it leads to. For example, a node might ask whether a statement of the form "if I were placed in circumstance C, I would freely choose A" can be true prior to God's creative decree, and whether its truth requires grounding in something other than that decree itself.

The tree must include the following leaves, mapped onto the logical space of answers:
- Classic Molinism (pre-volitional CCFs; God weakly actualises worlds using middle knowledge; transworld depravity is accommodated but not caused)
- Open Theism (no knowledge of future free acts; creation involves genuine risk for God)
- Simple foreknowledge without middle knowledge (all knowledge contained in one eternal intuition; CCFs lack independent pre-volitional truth values)
- Hard determinism or compatibilist providence (freedom is compatible with determination; no independent counterfactual knowledge is required for control)
- Anti-Molinist libertarian risk model (libertarian freedom is real, but CCFs are either false across all worlds or epistemically inaccessible; God creates without middle knowledge and accepts the outcome as open)

For every internal branch, label the split with the claim that separates it from its siblings. For every leaf, give the position's name and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment—what it must say about divine risk, about the grounding of counterfactual truth, or about the compatibility of omniscience with libertarian freedom.

In addition, identify at least two "empty" combinations of answers—logical branches that the tree permits but that no named position occupies—and briefly diagnose each: is the combination incoherent, does it collapse into one of the named leaves, or is it merely unexplored?

Also flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate using only the nodes provided, and explain what further distinction (for instance, about the modal status of essences or about the nature of weak actualisation) would be needed to draw the line.

Present the result as a nested outline. Write in British English. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Avoid invoking obscure formal results or named specialist proofs without explaining them in plain terms within the tree itself. Keep the instrument usable for someone who understands the basic debate but has not memorised the specialist literature.
