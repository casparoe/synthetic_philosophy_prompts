Compare Robert Nozick’s entitlement theory of justice—specifically its claim that any distribution arising from just initial holdings through just voluntary transfers is itself just—with a modified “background-conditional” entitlement view that accepts Nozick’s three principles (justice in acquisition, justice in transfer, and rectification) but holds that the justice of a transfer also depends on whether structural background conditions—such as access to relevant information and the absence of coercive economic dependencies—remain within bounds that preserve the meaningful voluntariness of the exchange. Take the Wilt Chamberlain case, in which fans freely pay Chamberlain to watch him play basketball, as your central test: does requiring such background conditions undermine Nozick’s argument that patterned principles violate liberty by interfering with voluntary choice, or does it merely relocate the distributive judgment to the specification of the baseline? Assess whether this modified view genuinely escapes the charge of being a disguised pattern, and whether Nozick’s original framework can reject the background-condition requirement without begging the question against the patterned theorist.
