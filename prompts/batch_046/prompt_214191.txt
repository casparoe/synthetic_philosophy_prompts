I'm drafting an essay on G.E.M. Anscombe's opposition to Harry Truman receiving an honorary doctorate from Oxford, and I'd value your comments on its structure and accuracy. The standard interpretation treats her argument as a straightforward application of double-effect reasoning: the atomic bombings deliberately killed innocent civilians as a means to ending the war, which constitutes murder regardless of any good outcome. I want to press a different emphasis. On my proposed reading, Anscombe's deeper objection is that in large-scale institutional warfare the "description" of an action is fixed not by private mental states but by bureaucratic and military authorization. For Anscombe, what one does is constituted by the formal description under which the act is performed; in the Manhattan Project, the authorized operational description included destroying populated cities, so the killing of noncombatants was not a foreseen side-effect but part of the action's very form. I then extend this to a modern parallel: autonomous weapons systems distribute agency across algorithms, predictive models, and command hierarchies, so if this institutional-intentionality view is correct, moral evaluation should target the formally authorized description embedded in the system's design rather than the psychological intentions of individual operators. The essay asks whether this reading is textually supported by her philosophy of action, whether extending it to distributed technological agency is coherent or anachronistic, and whether it yields a different moral verdict on Truman than a standard double-effect analysis. I'd especially appreciate feedback on whether the institutional-intentionality claim holds up, whether the parallel with algorithmic warfare illuminates or distorts her view, and which parts of the argument seem weakest or most forced.
