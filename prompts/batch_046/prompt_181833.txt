I am writing the capstone essay for my seminar on comparative moral psychology, in which we have examined the typology of honor, dignity, and victimhood cultures not as static ethnographic labels but as competing normative frameworks that structure how societies assign moral status, distribute blame, and legitimize grievance. The course materials have largely treated these as ideal types, and the literature I have encountered tends to evaluate them by asking which culture best protects individual autonomy or minimizes interpersonal violence. I want to move past that framing. I am particularly struck by a conceptual tension that our discussions have raised but left unresolved: whether victimhood culture should be understood as a sui generis moral form with its own internal logic, or rather as a reactive hybrid that emerges specifically when dignity-based institutions fail to recognize certain forms of structural harm, thereby borrowing the grievance mechanisms characteristic of honor cultures—competitive status claims, public shaming, and demands for redress—while retaining dignity's universalist rhetoric about inherent rights and recognition. If the latter is correct, then the normative question of whether honor, dignity, or victimhood is morally superior may rest on a category mistake, because victimhood culture is not an independent competitor but a symptomatic deformation of dignity culture under conditions of institutional distrust and status anxiety.

Given that I have one semester to develop this into a focused argumentative essay rather than a survey, I need help setting my research agenda. Which open conceptual questions in this space are genuinely upstream of the evaluation problem, and which are taxonomic dead ends that do not affect whether one culture can be judged morally superior? Please address the following lines of inquiry, but rank them—along with any others you judge more important—in order of priority for a student with training in philosophy and social theory but no access to original ethnographic field data.

First, the ontological status of the three categories: is there a defensible, principled distinction between dignity as a non-rivalrous, inherent status and honor as a competitive, zero-sum status, or does this distinction collapse once we account for the ways modern dignity cultures still implicitly rank individuals by perceived moral worth, victim credibility, or proximity to harm? Second, the hybrid thesis itself: what precise conceptual work is required to determine whether victimhood culture operates according to an independent logic of moral recognition, and what form of argument—conceptual, normative, or structural—would show that it genuinely appropriates honor's grievance mechanisms rather than inventing its own? Third, the framework for normative evaluation: assuming the categories hold, by which criteria should we judge moral superiority—minimization of status anxiety, robustness under deep pluralism, protection of the least advantaged, or fidelity to individual agency—and which of these criteria are themselves contested in ways that render the comparison circular without prior resolution?

Please do not provide a reading list, bibliography, or citation guide. Instead, deliver a defended agenda: for each item in your ranking, explain why resolving it is prerequisite to resolving the others, assess its tractability within a single semester of philosophical analysis, and specify what kind of conceptual distinction or argument—rather than new empirical data—would cause you to reorder the list. I am especially interested in whether there is a hidden crux about the relationship between moral luck and structural responsibility that, if clarified, would either render the tripartite comparison meaningful or show it to be moot.
