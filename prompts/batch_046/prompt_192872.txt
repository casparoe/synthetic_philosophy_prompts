Since my father's recent diagnosis with frontotemporal dementia, I have been unable to stop thinking about whether the self that remains is the same consciousness that was always there, or whether individual consciousness is only a temporary modulation of something singular and undivided. This led me to observe a long argument between two colleagues—Dr. Elena Vasquez and Dr. Marcus Chen—about Erwin Schrödinger's Vedanta-inspired philosophy of consciousness, and I have been replaying it in my head ever since.

Here is the exchange as I heard it.

Vasquez argued that Schrödinger's view resolves the hard problem by eliminating the need to explain why one brain produces one consciousness: there is only one consciousness, and brains are like prisms that refract it. The real mistake, she said, is treating personal death as an absolute loss, since the singular consciousness never dies; only the prism breaks. Chen replied that this confuses ontology with phenomenology. Schrödinger, he insisted, was not claiming that Vasquez's consciousness and his are literally the same token mental state, but rather that the boundary between self and other is ultimately conventional. The prism analogy fails, Chen said, because a prism does not experience being broken—yet we experience the death of others as the loss of a specific, irreplaceable perspective. If Vasquez is correct, grief should be conceptually impossible, since no perspective is ever lost, only rearranged.

Vasquez countered that Chen was smuggling in the very ego-boundary the view denies. She proposed what she called "indexical transparency": if consciousness is genuinely singular, then terms like "I" do not denote separate subjects but mark temporary focal points within one field. The grief we feel is real but misinterpreted; it is the field's own reaction to the dissolution of one focal structure, not the loss of an "other." Chen responded that this makes the view unfalsifiable. Any experience—including grief, moral responsibility, or love—can be redescribed as "the field reacting to its own modulation." He said Vasquez owes an account of why indexicals seem irreducibly private, and he offered a specific challenge: if two people simultaneously experience toothache, is there one token toothache or two? If one, then the singular consciousness should be able to report both pains from both locations at once, which never occurs.

Vasquez said Chen's challenge assumes a container model that the view rejects. Spatial location, she argued, is a feature of the prism, not of consciousness itself, so asking whether one consciousness can be in two places is like asking whether a number can be in two rooms. The multiplicity of prisms fully explains the appearance of multiplicity; no further explanation is needed. Chen concluded that Vasquez had turned Schrödinger's cautious philosophical analogy into a metaphysical system immune to evidence. He noted that Schrödinger used Vedantic ideas primarily to illuminate the formal structure of quantum observation, not to construct a complete ontology of mind, and that by elevating the view into a doctrine of universal consciousness, Vasquez risked dissolving the reality of individual suffering merely to preserve the comfort of cosmic unity.
