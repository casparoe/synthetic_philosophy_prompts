I have just come away from a long and somewhat frustrating argument with a close friend about whether weakness of will—akrasia, in the older vocabulary—is genuinely irrational when hyperbolic discounting gives the present self a structurally steeper valuation of near-future goods than of distant ones. My friend insisted that if one’s temporal preferences are genuinely hyperbolic rather than exponential, then yielding to an immediate desire is rational relative to the agent one actually is at that moment; I kept feeling that this collapses the normative distinction between recognizing the good and failing to do it, but I could not articulate exactly where the mistake lay without sounding like a mere moralist. The urgency of this confusion is now practical: tomorrow evening I am delivering the opening address at the retirement symposium for Dr. Marcus Chen, a philosopher of action and behavioral scientist who has spent thirty years studying self-control, intertemporal choice, and the architecture of decision-making. The audience will be split between cognitive scientists and economists who know the hyperbolic discounting literature intimately, and family members, former humanities students, and university administrators who do not. I need a speech of roughly five minutes that uses the tension between akrasia and hyperbolic discounting not as decorative biography but as its organizing frame: specifically, I want to develop the idea—one I think Chen’s own work quietly supports but rarely states explicitly—that treating one’s future self as an opponent to be restrained (the Ulysses model) misdescribes what successful self-control actually involves. Instead, the best strategies redesign the choice environment so that present and future preferences converge, making self-control a form of internal diplomacy rather than internal conquest. The speech must honor Chen’s career without either romanticizing willpower or reducing human agency to a curve on a graph; it should land for the experts without lecturing them and for the family without condescending to them. Please do not include bibliographies, citations, or lists of further reading. Do not quote any specific published text verbatim—if you need to invoke Aristotle on incontinence or the Ulysses story, paraphrase without quotation marks. I want the address to open by acknowledging my argument with my friend, then move into why Chen’s research made that disagreement feel both more technically precise and more humane.
