As a novelist accustomed to nested narratives, I am struck by the Cartesian theater analogy for consciousness: phenomenal experience is often pictured as a private stage upon which sensory scenes are displayed to an inner audience, with the hard problem being why any such audience is needed at all rather than mere information processing. Critics reject this as smuggling in a homunculus, but I want to audit the theatrical framework precisely—especially through the literary device of a play-within-a-play, where characters inside the fiction observe a performance that mirrors the outer plot. Map the correspondences: the stage, the actors, the inner audience, the playwright, and the actual reader onto elements of conscious experience. Then identify where the analogy fractures—particularly regarding spatial containment in the brain, causal efficacy, and misrepresentation without an external director. Finally, assess whether nesting the observer in this way resolves the mystery of subjective presence or merely replicates it at each level, producing an infinite regress that even a novelist would reject as a structural flaw.
