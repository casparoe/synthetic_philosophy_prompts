I've been asked to give the opening remarks at this workshop I'm co-organizing — it's called "What It's Like, If Anything," happening tomorrow in one of those conference rooms with bad fluorescent lights and coffee that tastes like regret. The crowd is split right down the middle: half are philosophy grad students who actually read the illusionism literature, and half are cognitive scientists and ML people who study attention mechanisms and think consciousness is basically an obvious feature of any good report-generation system. I'm only supposed to talk for five minutes, and I'm nervous. I also happen to think illusionism about phenomenal consciousness is basically right — that the "what-it's-likeness" people treat as the heart of the hard problem is more like a theoretical construct our brains generate, not a raw feature sitting out there in the world.

The problem is I can't just open with "we all know what consciousness feels like from the inside," because if illusionism is even in the ballpark, that shared intuition is exactly what's misleading us, and besides the scientists in the back will roll their eyes so hard they'll hurt themselves. I need these remarks to do real structural work: they have to welcome both groups, explain why we're talking past each other without either dismissing the empirical data or conceding there's some magical inner glow, and set up the day's panels so that nobody feels like the deck is stacked. Can you write me opening remarks that sound like me — casual, grad-student energy, I might stumble over pronouncing "phenomenal" — and that land for people deep in the weeds without leaving the engineers wondering why philosophers are arguing about something that feels totally obvious? Please don't include citations or a reading list; I just need the speech itself. And I want to accomodate both sides honestly, even though I think one is ultimately wrong — I don't want to sound like I'm sneering at the scientists.
