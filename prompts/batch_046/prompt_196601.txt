I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has taken up the philosophy of quantum mechanics with some seriousness, though I remain an amateur in its conceptual debates. At a recent reading group, two acquaintances—Dr. Elena Vasquez, who works on foundations of physics, and Marcus Chen, a mathematician with an interest in epistemology—became locked in a disagreement about the Born rule problem in the Everett interpretation. Elena argued that the decision-theoretic derivation succeeds: once branching is accepted, a rational agent should assign future credences proportional to the squared amplitudes of branches, because symmetry constraints on rational belief—specifically, indifference between physically identical outcomes—force the weights to match the Hilbert-space norms. Marcus replied that this argument is circular at its root; the symmetry conditions Elena invokes already presuppose that branches are individuated and weighted by the very norm-squared measure that needs justification. He offered an alternative framing: rather than asking "which world am I in," we should treat the situation as generating indexical uncertainty over token-identical cognitive states distributed across the universal wavefunction, and in the absence of any prior measure, the rational prior ought to be uniform over such cognitive tokens, not weighted by amplitude.

Elena countered that Marcus's uniform prior is unstable, because if one counts cognitive tokens within each macro-branch using the standard quantum measure, the number of tokens in high-amplitude branches already scales with the squared modulus; thus his "uniform" distribution over tokens implicitly reintroduces the Born weights through the counting procedure itself. Marcus then introduced a distinction he believed Elena had overlooked: between "structural weight" (the coefficient of a branch in the global state vector) and "epistemic accessibility" (whether a given cognitive token can access information that distinguishes its branch from others with an identical macroscopic profile). He claimed that decision-theoretic arguments illegitimately conflate these two notions, using conditions about what agents can know to justify structural weights, and that until they are separated, one has not explained why rational credence should track quantum measure rather than any other cardinality assignment over copies. The discussion grew technical, and when they asked me to referee I found myself genuinely unsure which objections were decisive. Please adjudicate their exchange step by step: first, whether Elena's symmetry argument avoids circularity or whether Marcus is correct that it presupposes the Born measure; second, whether Marcus's token-uniform prior is genuinely distinct or collapses under Elena's counting objection; third, whether Marcus's distinction between structural weight and epistemic accessibility does substantive work or is merely verbal; and finally, which party is more correct overall about how deterministic branching should ground probabilistic reasoning.
