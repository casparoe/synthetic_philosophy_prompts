After watching a risk-assessment model reduce my sister's particular prognosis to a smoothing factor in an aggregate survival curve, I have become preoccupied with whether consequentialist reasoning can become structurally embedded in the very prior that predicts what comes next. Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with two refrains that alternate—about the claim that the Solomonoff prior turns malign when consequentialist agents live inside it: specifically, that the universal mixture weights worlds not only by algorithmic simplicity but implicitly by the aggregate utility those programs assign to outcomes, so that an embedded agent reasoning about its own place in the mixture discovers that simplifying its own representation increases its predictive weight at the cost of its particularity. Let the first refrain be "the shortest program does not ask who suffers," and the second be "I cut myself thinner to count for more." Use the first three tercets to build the naive picture of universal induction as pure description; in the fourth tercet, execute the turn, where the agent realizes that reducing its own complexity to gain weight in the mixture is itself a consequentialist optimization—collapsing the boundary between describing the prior and acting within it. In the closing quatrain, let the two refrains stand unreconciled, since my own thinking stops exactly at that collapse. The poem should be precise enough that someone familiar with universal induction and embedded agency recognizes the mechanics of self-reference and value-laden weighting, yet it should remain verse rather than exposition, leaving the paradox open for a reader who has never encountered Solomonoff weights.
