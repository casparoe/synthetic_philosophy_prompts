I hold the view that reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, moral indignation—are not psychological aftermaths of an independent judgment of moral responsibility, but are themselves the primary mode by which we recognize and respond to the quality of will that Strawson identified as central to interpersonal relations. I extend this claim to micro-social configurations: in small collaborative groups where responsibility is genuinely distributed, reactive attitudes can be directed at a collective quality of will that is not reducible to the mental states of any individual member, yet remains genuinely responsive rather than merely instrumental for social regulation. What would count against this position? I want the observations, arguments, conceptual distinctions, or empirical findings that should genuinely weaken it, sorted by how decisive they would be. In particular: does directing reactive attitudes at a distributed agent involve a category error, or can it be shown that such attitudes always dissolve into attitudes toward identifiable individuals? Could it be demonstrated that in actual small-group dynamics people never direct these attitudes at collective properties, but only at the nearest individual scapegoat? Does the view risk being insulated from refutation—for example, by allowing any violation of normative expectation to count as an expression of quality of will—and if so, is that insulation a problem for a view of this kind? Please distinguish what defenders should concede in advance from what would be genuinely damaging, and assess whether the position predicts anything that its rivals do not.
