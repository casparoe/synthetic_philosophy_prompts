In our seminar on Plutarch, hagiography, and moral learning from lives, a student keeps invoking the slogan that "example teaches more than precept," this time to defend assigning only idealized biographies rather than portraits that include vice and failure. Before I respond, I want the point taken apart precisely. Could you lay out the distinct theses this slogan might express—from the trivial pedagogical observation, through psychological claims about narrative transmission, to stronger normative or epistemic claims that moral knowledge is acquired by emulation rather than by principle? In particular, address whether any of these readings actually support privileging hagiography over realistic biography, especially given the distinction between mimetic learning (acquiring dispositions through identification with a model) and diagnostic learning (calibrating judgment through exposure to conflict and failure). For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and note which ones survive the worry that impossible ideals distort character rather than form it. Present the result as a menu I can bring back to the seminar, not as a single verdict.
