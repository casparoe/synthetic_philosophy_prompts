I am drafting a novel about a near-future bureaucracy that adjudicates harms caused by adaptive infrastructure—traffic networks, supply algorithms, predictive policing grids—which learn and act without continuous human authorization. For the narrative, I need a reusable decision procedure my protagonist, a tribunal auditor, can run in a matter of hours to determine whether a responsibility gap genuinely exists, whether responsibility is distributed across a human-machine loop in a way that permits collective accountability, or whether the gap is only apparent because some hidden human decision preserved authorship. The procedure should be ordered as a sequence of tests with explicit stopping conditions; it must distinguish procedural autonomy (the system executes fixed rules faster) from structural autonomy (it generates rules no human endorsed); it must flag which steps are empirical (who set the reward function, when was the last human override) and which are normative judgments about whether distributed responsibility counts as real responsibility; and it must include two worked cases—one in which the procedure assigns blame to a diffuse group in a way the auditor finds morally unjust, and one in which it declares a gap despite a human being nominally "in the loop." Where the procedure cannot settle the matter, it should hand the decision to the auditor with the values conflict named. Keep the steps usable by someone without formal philosophy of technology training, and mark every point where judgment replaces method.
