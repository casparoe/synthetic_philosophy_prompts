Within the Everettian interpretation of quantum mechanics, attempts to recover the Born rule without treating it as an independent postulate have produced a protracted impasse. On one side stand decision-theoretic derivations, in the tradition of Deutsch and Wallace, which seek to show that rational agents embedded in a branching structure ought to assign credences proportional to branch weights; on the other side stand self-locating uncertainty frameworks, which treat the problem as one of indexical reasoning about where a particular observer finds herself among equally real branches. Despite extensive formal development, neither camp persuades the other: decision theorists charge that self-locating accounts smuggle probabilistic assumptions into the premises of indexical reasoning, while the latter reply that decision-theoretic arguments confuse normative constraints on betting behavior with the metaphysical question of how probability attaches to worlds at all. I am trying to determine whether this stalemate is a substantive disagreement about the ontology of branching, a verbal dispute about whether 'derivation' requires eliminating all probabilistic primitives, or a case of two research programs answering different questions—one normative, about rational credence; one metaphysical, about the status of measure—under the single heading 'the Born rule problem.'

Could you diagnose the structure of this disagreement by attending closely to what each side actually says about rational credence, branch counting, decoherence-based preferred bases, and the role of symmetry arguments? In particular, I want to test a specific hypothesis: that much of the disagreement tracks a hidden distinction between 'observer-moment weighting,' which assigns weights to instantaneous experiential slices regardless of historical continuity, and 'world-line weighting,' which assigns weights to continuous histories extending through branching events. If one camp implicitly adopts the former and the other the latter, then formal results satisfying one framework may fail to address the concerns of the other not because of any error, but because they operate with different primitives. Does this distinction capture a real fracture in the debate, or is it a red herring?

Finally, tell me what, if anything, should count as progress here. Would it be a formal result that eliminates the need for either weighting primitive, an empirical distinction internal to the multiverse framework, a conceptual clarification that splits the problem into two independent questions, or an argument that the disagreement is in principle unresolvable because it rests on irreducible differences in pre-theoretic intuition about indexicality? I am not looking for a bibliography or citation list; I want an analysis of the conceptual architecture and the conditions under which the impasse might be broken, or shown to be permanent.
