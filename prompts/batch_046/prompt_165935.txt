I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science, and in our research seminar on scientific discovery we have reached a frustrating impasse regarding simultaneous invention—most prominently Newton and Leibniz on calculus, and Darwin and Wallace on natural selection. All participants accept the standard historical chronology and the socio-intellectual conditions that made these convergences probable: the maturation of analytical techniques in the seventeenth century, and the accumulation of biogeographical and geological data in the nineteenth. Nevertheless, the group divides sharply over the philosophical significance of the coincidence. One faction advances a structuralist interpretation: given those background conditions, some functionally equivalent theory was historically inevitable, and attributing priority to a particular individual is largely a sociological artefact rather than an epistemically meaningful judgement. The opposing faction grants that convergence was likely, yet insists that the specific formalisations—Leibniz's differential notation and metaphysics of infinitesimals versus Newton's method of fluxions and its physical interpretation; Darwin's gradualist, Malthus-inspired mechanism versus Wallace's more abrupt, geographically driven account of speciation—exhibit irreducible conceptual fingerprints. For them, asking who made the discovery remains legitimate, because substitutability fails at the level of theoretical architecture and heuristic potential, even if a 'cousin' theory would eventually have emerged.

What perplexes me is that this disagreement has persisted for over a year without either side conceding ground, despite broad agreement on the empirical history. I suspect we are not merely clashing over bedrock intuitions about individual genius, but are actually talking past one another because we have not distinguished modal inevitability—the claim that in all nearby worlds with the same background conditions, an equivalent theory emerges—from what we might call epistemic substitutability, the claim that for every explanatory and predictive purpose the substitute is fully interchangeable with the actual discovery. The structuralists appear to infer substitutability directly from inevitability, while the individualists seem to reject inevitability altogether whenever they deny substitutability, thereby conflating two logically separable theses. Is the stall therefore a hidden disagreement about the criteria for theoretical equivalence—whether two formalisms count as the same discovery if they share predictive content but diverge in ontological commitment, heuristic fertility, and subsequent research trajectories? Or does the impasse instead reveal a normative dispute about whether scientific credit ought to track causal contribution within a deterministic process, or conceptual innovation that reshapes the space of possible theories? Please offer a committed diagnosis: take a definite position on which of these diagnoses (or another you propose) best explains the deadlock, defend it against the alternative I have sketched, indicate what kind of progress—perhaps a formal model of discovery spaces, a counterfactual analysis of specific formalisations, or a reconceptualisation of the question—would be required to resolve it, and present your answer in British English.
