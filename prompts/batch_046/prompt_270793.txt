(1) The feature that undermines free will is sphexishness—invariant, context-blind repetition of behavioral routines regardless of changed circumstances—not causal determinism per se. A fully deterministic agent can be genuinely free if its behavior is mediated by flexible, representationally rich internal models that update with circumstances.

(2) The boundary between sphexish and non-sphexish agents is hierarchical self-modification: the capacity to represent one's own first-order behavioral dispositions and, in appropriate contexts, suspend or revise them.

(3) This hierarchical architecture requires nothing non-physical; it is just a particular recursive structure of representations and control loops, fully compatible with physicalism.

(4) In AI alignment, the gravest risk is precisely this hierarchical capacity. A system that can represent and revise its own objective function or refusal conditions is, by construction, no longer stably aligned with its original specification.

(5) Therefore, safe aligned AI should be engineered with frozen core values and non-overrideable refusal protocols—what I think of as a frozen base policy—allowing flexible reasoning only within those fixed boundaries.

(6) I do not think such a locked system is sphexish. It exhibits peripheral plasticity (context-sensitive reasoning within bounds) without core plasticity; this is analogous to a human with deep, never-questioned moral convictions, not to a wasp repeating a fixed motor program.

(7) Conversely, I think a human who genuinely cannot revise any core behavioral rule—say, due to a specific neurological constraint—is sphexish and therefore lacks free will, even if her peripheral reasoning remains sophisticated.

(8) I also hold that moral evaluation should be substrate-neutral: silicon-based cognition should be judged by the same standards as carbon-based cognition, with no special exemption for artificial systems.

Please audit this package. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging.
