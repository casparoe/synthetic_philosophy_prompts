In nuclear strategy there's this old argument about brinkmanship that just won't die. You know the setup: Schelling-style escalation ladders, you push the other guy closer to the edge so he'll back down, but if he doesn't your both over the cliff. The rationalists say it's just optimal risk-taking under incomplete information — you don't want to fall, but you want him to think you might. The other camp says that's incoherent: rationality can't include deliberately manufacturing a situation where rational control is lost, so it's not strategy, it's just hoping the other guy blinks. What's weird is both sides agree on the basic payoffs, agree on Cuba and the missiles, and still haven't moved in decades. So is this actually a disagreement about decision theory under catastrophic risk, or is it verbal — are they using 'rational' differently, one for ex-ante optimization and one for ex-post accountability? Or maybe there's a hidden empirical question about whether human institutions can maintain calibrated risk near the brink without accidental slip. I want a diagnosis of the stall, not a survey. And here's a specific twist: if we split brinkmanship into continuous escalation ladders vs discrete single-step commitments, does the disagreement split cleanly, or do both camps just reproduce the same fight inside each version? Also, be honest: is there anything — a formal result, a distinction about agency under risk, maybe a historical case — that could actually break the deadlock, or is this stuck because it hinges on a bedrock intuition about whether gambling with extinction can ever be justified?
