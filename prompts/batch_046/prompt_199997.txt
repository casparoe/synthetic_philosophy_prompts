As a doctoral student working between moral philosophy and cross-cultural psychology, I want to examine the hidden meta-ethical commitments in standard experimental practice. Researchers routinely recruit WEIRD participants, treat their judgments as the default baseline for human moral cognition, and explain away cross-cultural divergence—whether in trolley-type trade-offs or in the salience of purity, loyalty, and authority norms—as either measurement noise, culturally mediated distortion of a single underlying competence, or direct evidence for moral relativism. At the same time, study design almost never treats non-WEIRD patterns as equally authoritative data points about one shared objective moral structure. What implicit theory of moral objectivity does that methodological asymmetry presuppose? Reconstruct the tacit position: does the practice commit the field to a form of normative universalism in which WEIRD intuitions approximate the correct moral framework, despite official meta-ethical neutrality? Can the same practice be coherently reconstructed under anti-realism, under pluralist realism, or under a view that treats cultural variation as tracking genuinely different moral truths? Carefully distinguish what the methodology strictly requires from what merely accommodates it, and specify what would have to change—conceptually and in experimental design—if the implicit commitment were replaced by a robust pluralism that grants every population's judgments equal evidential weight for or against objective moral claims. Please end your response with a line beginning Verdict: followed by a short label for the reconstructed implicit theory (e.g., tacit normative universalism, philosophically underdetermined, etc.).
