Read Ken Kesey’s *One Flew Over the Cuckoo’s Nest* through Foucault’s account of disciplinary power, normalization, and the psychiatric production of madness. Rather than treating Nurse Ratched as a sovereign tyrant who punishes through spectacle, examine the ward as a technology of normalization—the group-therapy sessions, the behavioral logs, the medication schedules, and the therapeutic-community model in which patients are induced to police one another—asking how these practices produce the very categories of “acute” and “chronic” that they claim merely to describe. Focus on three nodes: McMurphy’s entry and his diagnostic labeling despite his physical health; the meetings where mutual “aid” becomes a mechanism of self-regulation; and Chief Bromden’s apparently external narration, which may already have internalized the ward’s epistemic grid. Ask whether McMurphy’s rebellion genuinely disrupts the power-knowledge circuit or whether it functions as the necessary “deviant” case that confirms the system’s truth—especially once he is reclassified, subjected to shock therapy, and finally lobotomized. Incorporate Foucault’s later idea that resistance inside such an institution often takes the form of “counter-conduct”—rejecting specific norms without leaving the field of power—rather than pure liberation, and ask whether Bromden’s final act of smothering the vegetative McMurphy and escaping through the window fits that model or represents a rupture the novel imagines but the framework cannot accommodate. Where does Kesey’s dramatization illuminate the gendered and emotional labor of normalization that Foucault’s more abstract history of the asylum tends to suppress? Where does the novel risk romanticizing individual rebellion in ways that obscure how thoroughly psychiatric power operates through cooperation and self-surveillance rather than through coercion alone? Conclude by stating what this specific reading adds to the philosophical debate about psychiatric power that abstract argument by itself does not.
