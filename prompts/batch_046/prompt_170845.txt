I've been sitting in on a graduate seminar on competence without comprehension, focusing on Darwin's inversion—functional design achieved without a designing mind—and Turing's inversion—intelligent behavior achieved without internal semantic understanding. The professor has given firm verdicts on five cases so far.

First, the evolutionary emergence of the vertebrate eye: she called it competence (functional optical design) without comprehension (no intentional grasp of optics), a paradigmatic instance of the Darwinian inversion.

Second, bacterial chemotaxis, where organisms navigate chemical gradients via internal feedback loops that no cell represents or understands: yes, clearly competence without comprehension.

Third, a child's implicit acquisition of grammatical structure through statistical exposure, with no explicit knowledge of syntactic rules: yes, she said this counts, though the child is conscious; what matters is the absence of representational comprehension of the rules themselves, not the absence of consciousness altogether.

Fourth, an ant colony solving shortest-path problems through pheromone dynamics that no individual ant understands: yes, again competence without comprehension.

Fifth, a human savant who instantly calculates calendar dates with perfect accuracy but cannot articulate any arithmetic principle: no, she insisted this is not the right kind of case, because there is tacit comprehension present in the conscious agent, even if it remains inarticulate.

Now for the new case: an engineered bipedal robot, designed top-down with complete explicit mathematical knowledge by its engineers (no evolutionary algorithms used in its development), that adapts its gait in real-time to uneven terrain using purely dynamical feedback control. The robot has no consciousness, no representational states about the ground, and no semantic understanding of balance or purpose; its creators fully understand every equation governing its movement, but the system itself operates without any grasp of physics.

Predict her verdict: would she classify this robot as exhibiting competence without comprehension? Identify which of her five precedents is the closest analog. Then lay out the two competing rules that best fit her track record—likely one rooted in Darwin's explanatory inversion (functional design explained without an intentional origin or designer) and one rooted in Turing's behavioral inversion (adaptive, apparently intelligent action produced without internal semantic understanding)—and say what each rule predicts about the robot. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, state precisely what principle she would be adopting with each choice. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case that would cleanly distinguish the Darwinian rule from the Turing rule.

End with a line beginning Verdict: followed by your best-guess prediction, e.g., Verdict: Yes—Turing inversion applies, Verdict: No—Darwinian inversion absent, or Verdict: Underdetermined.
