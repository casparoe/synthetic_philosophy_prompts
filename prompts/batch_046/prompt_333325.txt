I work for a small regional tech-heritage trust, and every quarter I have to sign off on which obsolete computing stacks and kit we keep alive through emulation versus letting them go dead. Funding's tight, and if I keep everything the board kills the budget. I want to turn von Neumann's idea — that technology only survives if it can reproduce the conditions it needs to operate, like his self-copying automata but applied to tools and culture — into a proper proceedure my team can actually run. Not a philosophy essay. A checklist: the questions in order, what counts as enough to pass each one, the hard stop where we archive the docs but pull the plug, and the points where it's just a value call and the principle doesn't help. It should cover whether a system describes its own enviroment well enough to be rebuilt, whether anyone still cares if it dies, and what happens when those two answers clash. Apply it to three cases: a 1980s council database tied to one specific hardware box, an open-source compiler that can rebuild itself on modern kit, and a proprietary factory CAD system for a line that's been demolished. Include one where the procedure says kill it but that feels wrong to me. Also say clearly what the proceedure can't do — like deciding who pays, or whether cultural value trumps technical self-sufficiency. Keep it practical, no bibliographies please, I haven't got access to a library right now and neither will the model running this.
