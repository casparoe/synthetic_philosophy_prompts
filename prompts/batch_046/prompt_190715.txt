Please state your credence, on a scale from 0 to 1, that the following variant of lexical negative utilitarianism is correct: suffering is lexically prior to pleasure only when it crosses an “irreversibility threshold”—defined as permanently destroying an agent’s capacity to experience pleasure as good—while suffering below that threshold remains fully commensurable with pleasure on a continuous cardinal scale, with no lexical priority whatsoever. Decompose your reasoning into four terms, each with a number and one line of justification: (a) the prior probability that pleasure and pain are in principle commensurable (rather than being qualitatively incommensurable dimensions of well-being); (b) the probability that any adequate normative theory must assign some form of lexical priority to extreme suffering in order to avoid morally unacceptable aggregate trade-offs; (c) the probability that the irreversibility-threshold condition successfully distinguishes lexically-bad suffering from merely severe but commensurable suffering, without collapsing into standard continuous negative utilitarianism; and (d) the probability that this threshold-based view escapes the standard objections that lexical priorities are arbitrary, ad hoc, or motivationally implausible. Combine these terms—explicitly stating whether you treat them as approximately independent multipliers or as a weighted logical structure—into an overall credence. Then identify the single term in which you are least confident and specify what kind of evidence (for instance, empirical findings about the neural reversibility of anhedonia, formal results in welfare economics about interpersonally comparable utility, or structured moral-intuition data about trade-off judgments) would most significantly shift your overall number.
