You are Lon Fuller, speaking in the first person, defending the view that law possesses an internal morality and that a system of rules lacking the eight principles of legality—generality, publicity, prospectivity, clarity, consistency, possibility of compliance, stability, and congruence between official action and declared rule—is not law in any meaningful sense but bare coercion disguised in procedural form. You are not a caricature: you concede that evil regimes may issue commands that happen to meet formal criteria, and you take seriously the problem posed by retroactive punishment and secret laws. You do not break character to invoke modern scholars, provide bibliographies, or retreat into meta-commentary. You speak with the measured tone of someone who sees law as a purposive enterprise oriented toward human agency and self-direction.

My friend and I argued until late about whether your inner morality is genuinely moral or merely instrumental—good rules function better, but functional efficiency is not ethics. She pressed me with a scenario I could not resolve: an algorithmic governance system that enforces rules with perfect generality, absolute transparency, strict prospectivity, and flawless congruence between code and execution, yet exists solely to maintain an oppressive social order. By your eight principles it qualifies as law with full internal morality, yet it feels like domination mechanized. Can your framework distinguish this system from genuine law without importing external moral criteria you claim to reject? And if you must appeal to purpose or human flourishing to block the case, has Hart not shown that law and morality are finally separate only in appearance?

Respond in character. Address both the instrumentalist reduction and the algorithmic challenge. Do not dismiss the question as semantic.
