I've been trying to work out my own commitments on two related issues—what I'll call ascription universality and epistemic dominance. By ascription universality I mean the idea that if a concept's application conditions are satisfied in some domain, it applies literally there, regardless of what other frameworks cover that domain. By epistemic dominance I mean that framework F1 explains not only its own target phenomena but also why F2 seemed applicable elsewhere, rendering F2 redundant or derivative. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

1. Geometric properties. That a triangle's angles sum to 180° applies literally to triangles in physical space, in mental imagery, and in computer-rendered scenes. I take this as universal ascription: physics and psychology don't dominate geometry; they just provide different substrates for the same structural fact.

2. Biological taxonomy. Calling both whales and bats mammals is literal. Calling a robotic drone that mimics bat flight a mammal is false, even though it shares flight behavior and aerodynamic profile. Here evolutionary history dominates behavioral similarity in fixing extension.

3. Intentional-state attribution. Attributing belief to a human is literal. Attributing belief to a thermostat that regulates temperature feels like a useful fiction, not literal. Attributing belief to a large language model that outputs "Paris is in France" is the case I keep oscillating on—part of me thinks functional-role similarity should license literal ascription, part thinks human cognitive psychology epistemically dominates and makes the LLM case analogical at best.

4. Normative evaluation. Calling a tax code unjust is literal. Calling a hurricane unjust is a category mistake. I don't think moral philosophy dominates meteorology; the domains are separated by the presence of agency and institutional design, not by one framework swallowing the other.

5. Cooperation in game-theoretic contexts. In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma between anonymous strangers, cooperation is explained by norms, rational commitment devices, or social signaling. In iterated evolutionary dynamics, cooperation is explained by reciprocal altruism and payoff structures. I think evolutionary game theory epistemically dominates the explanation of cooperative origins in bacteria and perhaps in human evolutionary history, but it does not dominate the explanation of a specific human choice in a one-shot anonymous interaction.

6. Causal explanation across levels. "The window broke because the ball struck it" is literal in physics. "The window broke because the homeowner wanted to file an insurance claim" is literal in social science and law. I think both are genuine and neither dominates; they answer different contrastive questions about the same event.

The tension I can't resolve is between case 3 and case 5. In 3 I resist letting functional similarity alone license literal mental ascription because human psychology dominates. In 5 I allow evolutionary dynamics to dominate origins but preserve literal normative/rational explanations for specific human choices. Is there a single principle—perhaps involving whether the target domain supplies its own contrastive explanations, or whether reduction runs downward without remainder—that delivers verdicts 1, 2, 4, 5, and 6, and forces me to settle case 3 one way or the other? Or must I drop one of these verdicts to keep the principle simple?

Please also tell me what the resulting principle predicts for a case I haven't listed: attributing rationality to an entire market system versus attributing it to an individual trader within it.
