After retiring from experimental physics and turning to decision theory, I have built an argument that seems to reduce expected-utility maximization to absurdity for almost any real choice. (1) A rational agent ought to maximize expected utility. (2) In the Pasadena game—where successive rounds yield payoffs of ±2^{k-1} with probabilities arranged so that both the positive and negative partial expectations diverge—the total expectation is undefined, not infinite. (3) If expectation is literally undefined, there is no numerical value for the agent to maximize, so expected-utility theory is silent on the choice. (4) Any serious decision—funding an unbounded research program, having children, adopting a technology with open-ended tail risks—if modeled with honest heavy-tailed distributions rather than truncated ones, produces undefined expectation in exactly this mathematical sense. (5) So the theory is silent on nearly every weighty human choice. (6) One might rescue it by imposing an arbitrary cutoff to force convergence. (7) But any such truncation assigns zero weight to outcomes beyond the boundary, which is a substantive claim disguised as a mathematical convenience and lacks epistemic justification. (8) Thus we face a dilemma: abandon expected-utility maximization for ordinary life, or retain it only by smuggling in unjustified assumptions. (9) Both horns are unacceptable. (10) Therefore premise (1) is false.

I would prefer to keep (1) and reject (6) as an ad hoc maneuver, yet I cannot locate the precise slip. Is there an equivocation between mathematical undefinedness and practical indecision? Does (4) illegitimately extend a feature of contrived games to empirically modeled choices? Or is the error elsewhere, perhaps in treating silence as refutation rather than as a call for supplementary decision rules? Tell me which step to reject, explain why it initially seemed secure, and price the cost for rational decision theory—especially whether saving (1) forces us to accept some form of bounded rationality or distributional truncation after all.
