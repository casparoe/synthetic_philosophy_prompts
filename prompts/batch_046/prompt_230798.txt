I have been trying to formalize the procreation asymmetry through a framework I am calling modal-proximity weighting, according to which the moral reason not to create a suffering subject strengthens in proportion to how modally close that subject is to actual existence, while the absence of a corresponding reason to create a flourishing subject remains invariant across modal distance. On this reconstruction, the asymmetry is not a primitive feature of harm and benefit, but rather an artifact of indexical obligations that only bind us with respect to subjects already fixed in the relevant modal neighborhood. This seems to generate a continuous gradient rather than a binary distinction: if an embryo has, say, a sixty percent probability of being carried to term, then the obligation to avoid its suffering should be proportionally weakened, and intermediate probabilities should yield intermediate moral forces. But this appears to dissolve the asymmetry into a general decision-theoretic problem about expected harm, which undermines its standard deployment in reproductive ethics. Is the error in applying modal proximity to potential subjects, in treating the asymmetry as derivable from indexical obligations at all, or in assuming that intermediate probabilities must map onto intermediate obligations? And if the asymmetry survives this reconstruction intact, what does it imply for practices—such as selective reduction or probabilistic preimplantation genetic diagnosis—where modal proximity itself is the variable being deliberately manipulated?
