I serve as the senior ethics reviewer for a foundation that funds experimental moral psychology, and over the past year we have seen a sharp increase in proposals that defend deceptive or mildly harmful methods—hidden observation, induced stress, false performance feedback—by appealing to a mixture of considerations that my non-philosopher board cannot disentangle. I have been trying to apply the philosophical distinction between reasons of the right kind and reasons of the wrong kind, but I need it translated into an actual decision procedure I can run in about twenty minutes per proposal, with ordered questions, clear stopping points, and explicit flags for where judgment cannot be eliminated. To make the procedure usable, I want it built around a three-layer framework I keep returning to: constitutive reasons (what makes the study the kind of inquiry it claims to be), legitimizing reasons (what makes it ethically permissible to run on human participants), and instrumental reasons (what makes it likely to succeed, get published, or advance institutional goals). The particular error I need the procedure to catch is when instrumental reasons are treated as if they were legitimizing reasons, or when the presence of a legitimate legitimizing reason is taken to excuse a justification that is primarily driven by the wrong kind of consideration.

Please construct the procedure as a step-by-step checklist: the questions asked in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, the point at which the procedure issues a hard recommendation not to fund, the points at which it must hand the decision back to the board as a question of values rather than classification, and an explicit note on where two reasonable reviewers could disagree without either being mistaken. Ground the translation in the idea that an action can be supported by reasons of the wrong kind even when reasons of the right kind are also present—so finding a valid legitimizing reason does not cure the error if the proposal’s operative justification is instrumental. Then apply the procedure to three cases: (a) a hidden-observation study mapping moral decision-making in public spaces under time pressure, justified mainly by the claim that informed consent would destroy the phenomenon being studied; (b) a study inducing temporary shame to test resilience theories, justified mainly by the principal investigator’s tenure timeline and the university’s strategic priority in affective science; and (c) a false-feedback study on moral self-correction, where both a robust legitimizing reason (minimal risk, full debriefing, direct scientific value) and a strong instrumental reason (the PI’s promotion case) are genuinely present, but the proposal frames the ethical justification around the promotion timeline rather than the risk-benefit analysis. Include at least one case where the procedure yields a rejection that I, as the reviewer, am inclined to resist, and conclude by stating exactly what the procedure cannot settle—especially situations in which the boundary between constitutive and legitimizing reasons collapses, or in which the right-kind reason is present but so weak that treating it as sufficient becomes itself a value judgment disguised as procedure. The deliverable should assume no philosophical training on the part of the person running it, should require no external sources, and should be honest about where it will misfire.
