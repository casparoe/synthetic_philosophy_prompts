I'm giving the opening remarks next thursday at a small workshop called 'Exceptionalisms' — mostly grad students in history and poli sci, plus my uncle who came cause he finds the idea interesting but doesn't really follow the field. The theme is american exceptionalism treated not as patriotic cheerleading but as a structural problem: how the claim that the US is uniquely founded on universal ideas rather than ethnicity becomes self-reinforcing inside legal and educational institutions.

I need about a 10-minute talk that distinguishes exceptionalism as an empirical thesis (something you could in principle check by comparing countries) from exceptionalism as an ideological mechanism (something that survives evidence against it because the institutions are built to reproduce the belief). To make the second part concrete, please use the hypothetical I sketched out: a state law requiring every public high school to teach a unit called 'The Unprecedented Experiment,' with a mandatory referendum every fifty years on whether to keep the unit or replace it with comparative constitutional history. Don't load me up with citations or a bibliography — I just need the argument to flow clearly for both the specialists and my uncle, and I'd like the closing to acknowledge the historians who think the thesis is dead without actually conceding to them. Keep it conversational but precise, not like a textbook.
