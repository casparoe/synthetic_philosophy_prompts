I've been covering archaeology and heritage for a few years now, and there's this pattern I keep running into that I cant quite place philosophically. Walk into any museum with prehistoric galleries and you'll see wall text confidently reconstructing religious belief—say, a burial orientation facing east gets read as evidence for solar cosmology, or a broken ceramic vessel in a grave becomes proof of ritual offering to ancestors. But apply that same interpretive energy to administrative traces—standardized weights, storage room layouts, uniform brick sizes—and suddenly curators and peer reviewers get super cautious. Without written records they basically refuse to call it a bureaucracy or tax system; they just say possible centralized storage and leave it at that. There's an implicit institutional rule that material culture can bear witness to what people believed, but not to how they governed, unless text backs it up.

I was chatting with a friend about whether this double standard rests on some idea like symbolic excess—that an artifact only carries readable intentional meaning when it violates practical efficiency, so an ornate axe in a grave is interpretable but an effecient granary layout isn't. What philosophical commitments about evidence, representation, or social ontology does that practice actually presuppose? Does it need a view that beliefs are somehow more materially sticky than power structures, or can tractability and risk aversion explain it all? And if we replaced that hidden framework with a flat functionalist epistemology—where every organized material trace is equally available for institutional inference—what exactly would have to change in museum labeling and in how journals review site reports?
