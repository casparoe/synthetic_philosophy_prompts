Write a short workshop proposal (like a 15-minute pitch, or a term-paper prospectus for one semester, no empirical data collection) on whether the moral right to leisure is just a negative liberty—freedom from being forced to labor—or whether it needs a thicker idea of "temporal sovereignty" where you actually control not just how many hours you have off but it's rhythm and predicatability. Use Weber's Protestant work ethic, the French 35-hour framework, and Japanese karoshi culture as your three reference points, but don't try to do full comparative institutional analysis; that's too big for one semester. Instead, frame the open question like this: harm-based arguments can condemn overwork that kills, but they can't explain why unpredictable overtime feels like a rights violation even when total hours stay under forty. Your approach should take Weber's concept of Beruf and then inject a distinction between "empty time" (merely not working) and "recovered time" (time actively protected from work's mental intrusion and anticipatory reach). Say exactly what conclusion you're testing—probably that leisure rights require the thicker, sovereignty-based concept—and spell out what would count as failure, like showing that standard contract theory already handles unpredictability without any new category. Be honest about scope: conceptual analysis only, no fieldwork, and you'll fill in citations yourself later—don't include a bibliography or reading list in the proposal itself. Keep it a bit in the weeds; avoid the standard "work-life balance" framing.
