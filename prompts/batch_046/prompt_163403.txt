I have no academic training, but I work in freight logistics and have been thinking about strikes and union rights on my own. In my field we speak of route protocols, handoff chains, contingency rerouting, shift-level holds, and team-authorized pauses; when an entire shift collectively stops loading until a safety issue is resolved, we log it as a red hold, not as political action. I have also observed what I call a gray hold: workers do not halt the flow but quietly reroute goods through unofficial channels, altering system behavior without declaring a stoppage. I want you to take the philosophical debate about whether a strike is one genuinely shared action bearing collective moral authority, or merely an aggregate of individual refusals that happen to coincide—particularly as that debate concerns collective intentionality, joint agency, and the moral right to organize—and restate it entirely in my vocabulary: routes, protocols, holds, rerouting, handoff responsibility, shift-level authorization, and contingency planning. Be precise: map joint intention onto whatever in logistics comes closest to a coordinated shift hold, map individual withdrawal onto a single driver refusing a load, and map my gray hold onto whatever philosophical category it might belong to, even if you must invent one. Then give me the residue report. Tell me which claims translate cleanly, which survive only with distortion because logistics vocabulary treats every hold as an efficiency problem rather than a justice claim, and whether there are assertions about collective rights or shared moral responsibility that have no counterpart at all in talk of protocols and rerouting. I am especially interested in whether the idea that a strike can possess moral authority exceeding any individual worker’s authority can be expressed using only shift-level decision procedures, or whether it vanishes entirely in translation.
