After a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a friend about free will, determinism, and the old sphex wasp example, I have tried to distill where I actually stand, and I want to know whether the following commitments form a consistent package or whether I am smuggling in an unstated premise.

Here is where I have landed:

(1) Free will requires genuine counterfactual flexibility: in at least some relevant scenarios, an agent with the same prior internal state and environmental conditions must be able to do otherwise.

(2) The sphex wasp’s stereotyped burrow-checking loop—dragging prey to the burrow, inspecting the interior, and repeating the sequence if the prey has been displaced—lacks freedom not because its behavior is deterministic, but because it is representationally blind. It does not represent the prey’s new location as a reason to skip the check; it merely executes a rigid trigger-response mapping.

(3) Human practical reasoning is free precisely because it involves content-sensitive representation: we represent reasons as reasons, and we can revise our representations in light of normative evaluation.

(4) Any content-sensitive representational system can, in principle, be fully implemented by a mechanistic update rule—belief-revision algorithms, utility-maximization procedures, predictive-processing loops—without invoking any non-physical causation.

(5) Consequently, the boundary between free agency and sphexish rigidity must be architectural, not ontological: a free agent possesses a second-order mechanism that can suspend or revise any first-order behavioral loop on the basis of normative evaluation, even when no external cue demands the suspension.

(6) But if that second-order mechanism is itself governed by a mechanistic rule—say, a fixed meta-algorithm governing loop-interruption—then in genuinely identical prior conditions it will always issue the same suspension or non-suspension command. This appears to erase the counterfactual flexibility demanded in (1).

There also seems to be a tension between (2) and (4): (2) denies that determinism is what robs the wasp of freedom, while (4) places humans in the same mechanistic, deterministic framework. I take this tension to be merely apparent: (2) attributes the wasp’s deficiency to representational blindness, and (4) allows that humans share mechanistic implementation while differing in representational richness, so the two do not actually conflict.

The serious problems, if they exist, appear to lie between (3) and (6), and between (1) and (5). In (3) I claim that representation delivers freedom; in (6) I observe that representational mechanisms, even meta-level ones, seem to undermine the very counterfactual openness required by (1). Meanwhile (5) attempts to rescue freedom via architectural self-modification, yet (6) suggests that architecture alone, if fully mechanistic, cannot supply what (1) asks for.

Is this a consistent package? If not, please rank the tensions by severity—especially whether (6) is fatal to (1), or whether (5) can be reconstructed so that it avoids collapse into pure mechanism. For each tension, specify exactly which commitment must be weakened or abandoned, and what that costs (for instance, abandoning (1) might make free will compatible with universal mechanism, but only at the price of reducing freedom to an architectural distinction rather than a modal one). Also, please say whether there is a hidden premise I am relying on—perhaps concerning what counts as “identical prior conditions” for a system with internal stochasticity or with genuinely open-ended self-reference—that resolves the tension without forcing me to drop any of (1) through (6).

Please do not propose bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am working without external sources and want to see whether the position hangs together on its own terms.
