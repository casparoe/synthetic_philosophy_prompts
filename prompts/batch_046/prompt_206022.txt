In debates over Davidson's radical interpretation, a common move is to say that understanding an unknown speaker is like solving a coupled system of differential equations in physics: the unknown functions are the speaker's beliefs and the meanings of her utterances; the boundary conditions are the observable behaviors and shared environmental stimuli; and the principle of charity acts as a global constraint—like an energy-minimization condition—that forces the system toward a single stable equilibrium. The analogy is often used to argue that belief and meaning are not separately determinable, that interpretation has no "dictionary-first" stage, and that the correct assignment is uniquely fixed by rationality constraints rather than discovered.

Please audit this analogy precisely. First, lay out the correspondence table: what corresponds to the unknown functions, the boundary conditions, the coupling terms, the global constraint, and the equilibrium solution? Second, identify the points of breakdown: are belief and meaning genuinely coupled in the way physical variables are, or does charity function as a normative presupposition rather than a physical law? Does the analogy smuggle in an unwarranted determinacy—suggesting a unique solution where Davidson's view actually permits radical underdetermination? Third, examine what features of physical modeling (quantifiability, law-governed interaction, observer-independence) are quietly imported and whether they belong to interpretive practice, especially in cases where the speaker's community employs strategic deception or ritual speech-acts that violate individual rationality. Fourth, determine whether the anti-foundationalist conclusion—that there is no belief-independent access to meaning—survives once these disanalogies are exposed, or whether the analogy was doing the argumentative work all along. Finally, if you find the analogy deficient, propose a better one that preserves Davidson's interdependence thesis without importing the distortions of physical modeling, and briefly defend it.
