I am drafting a novel in which a neurologist treats a patient in a minimally conscious state who produces consistent verbal reports of inner experience, even as the physician privately endorses a view of consciousness as causally redundant. Before I write the scene, I want the underlying philosophy to be rigorous rather than ornamental. My question concerns the clinical and research practice of assessing consciousness through first-person report—both in bedside diagnosis and in laboratory protocols that compare report-based and no-report measures of awareness. What philosophical commitments about mental causation, epistemic authority, and self-reference does this practice implicitly presuppose? Does treating a patient's statement that she is aware as evidential data commit the clinician to interactionism, or can the practice be reconstructed within an epiphenomenalist framework in which the report is caused by the neural substrate but not by the conscious state itself? Furthermore, how does the paradox of phenomenal judgment—if phenomenal consciousness cannot causally produce judgments about itself—bear on the coherence of relying on such reports? Reconstruct the implicit theory of mind embedded in the practice, distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and indicate whether taking epiphenomenalism seriously would force a change in clinical protocol or only in its philosophical interpretation. If my framing is confused—if clinical practice carries no such commitments, or if the paradox is dissolved rather than threatening—please say so plainly instead of complying politely.
