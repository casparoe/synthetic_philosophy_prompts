In a recent seminar on collective memory ethics—specifically on whether societies have an obligation to maintain archives of past injustices and whether individuals have a corresponding right to erasure—someone dismissed a proposal for deleting certain historical records with the phrase “never forget,” as if that alone settled the matter. I take the phrase to be doing serious argumentative work in the kind of debate Avishai Margalit frames around collective remembrance and that David Rieff contests in arguing for the virtues of forgetting, but I cannot tell which precise thesis is being invoked.

Can you lay out, as a precise menu of distinct readings, what “never forget” might actually be asserting? I want more than the obvious literal claim about individual psychology being impossible. Please include at least: an institutional-preservation claim about archives and testimony; a prudential-historical claim about societies that suppress memory repeating violence; an identity-constitutive claim that a community’s self-understanding depends on sustained commemorative practice; a moral claim linking forgiveness to retention rather than erasure; and any further precise formulation you think is live in this literature. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, false, or conceptually confused; and indicate which ones, if any, actually support the conclusion that erasing an archive is wrong.

Then apply the disambiguation to a case I have not resolved: a post-conflict society that adopts a policy of structured forgetting—not destroying evidence, but deliberately ceasing public commemoration—as an explicit condition for political reconciliation. Which readings of “never forget” condemn this policy, and which might permit or even endorse it?

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on memory and ethics, and do not present a contested interpretation—for example, whether collective memory is genuinely identity-constituting, or whether forgiveness requires retention—as if it were the consensus view.
