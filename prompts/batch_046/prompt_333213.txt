Two colleagues of mine—both working in urban policy but with different backgrounds—got into a sustained disagreement at a workshop on housing and collective rights. I was present but stayed out of it. The dispute concerned a proposed upzoning ordinance in a hypothetical mid-sized city that would allow four-to-six-story mixed-use buildings along a corridor currently occupied mostly by two-story structures, some of which house an informal street-market collective that has operated for roughly two decades. The market vendors do not own the buildings; they lease ground-floor spaces at below-market rates secured through an old community-development agreement. The ordinance includes no tenant-protection provisions for commercial leases. Here is how the argument went.

(1) The first colleague argued that the vendors and long-term residents hold a collective right to the city in the sense developed by Henri Lefebvre: not merely a right of access to urban space, but a right to participate in producing and maintaining the specific social and spatial oeuvre they have built. Because the upzoning would almost certainly displace the vendors through rent pressure and demolition, it violates that collective right, regardless of whether the new housing increases overall access.

(2) The second colleague replied that if the right to the city is genuinely collective, it cannot be monopolized by the current occupants of one corridor. The people excluded by high housing costs—potential residents, including the vendors' own children—are equally part of the urban collective. Therefore, upzoning that increases housing supply serves the right to the city, and treating incumbent attachment as a veto transforms a collective right into a private property right in social space.

(3) The first colleague countered that this reply conflates quantitative access with qualitative co-production. The right is not to a unit of housing but to the continuity of the specific practices, relationships, and spatial rhythms that constitute urban life in that place. Replacing the vendors with higher-income residents and chain retail changes not just who lives there, but the kind of collective agent that exists. So the temporal and practical identity of the collective matters.

(4) The second colleague said that without a principled line distinguishing legitimate continuity from illegitimate exclusion, this argument collapses into a defense of the status quo by those who happen to occupy it. She proposed framing the issue through a capabilities lens: the relevant rights are capabilities—to shelter, to livelihood, to mobility—not rights to preserve a particular aesthetic or social configuration. On that view, the vendors' economic vulnerability should be addressed through direct compensation or commercial-lease protections, not by blocking housing supply.

(5) The first colleague responded that the capabilities framing treats urban space as a neutral container for individual functionings, which misses the point of the right to the city entirely. The vendors are not merely individuals who need capabilities; they are co-producers of the space itself. To displace them is not to deny them a capability but to dissolve the collective subject that holds the right. He added that direct compensation is inadequate because it addresses individual loss but cannot restore the collective oeuvre.

Please adjudicate the disagreement move by move: for each of the five moves above, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say which specific points the first colleague is right about, which specific points the second is right about, and—most importantly—what both are missing that would be needed to actually resolve whether the ordinance should go forward.
