Design a 55-minute classroom simulation for 18 political-theory undergraduates that makes participants experience, before the facilitator names it, the structural paradox that strict term limits paired with rotating sortition can displace careerist influence onto permanent advisory staff rather than eliminate it. Use only paper, name cards, and a non-monetary point system. Assign roles by lot each round: Legislators serve at most two rounds then exit permanently; Advisors remain across all rounds, accumulate procedural knowledge, and hold exclusive drafting rights; Citizens vote on final bills. Run three 10-minute legislative rounds in which Legislators must approve a budget but quickly discover they cannot construct compliant legislation without Advisor templates, allowing Advisors to exchange procedural access for policy concessions. Structure the rules so this power drift is inevitable, not accidental. Provide a debrief that maps each round onto the corresponding institutional mechanism, predicts the predictable student misreading that Advisor influence reflects personal corruption rather than structural dependency, and states frankly which feature of real-world legislative continuity the simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Keep the activity self-contained with no outside research required.
