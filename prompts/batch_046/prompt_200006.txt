You are delivering the opening remarks at a retirement symposium for a senior scholar whose work treated money not as a neutral instrument but as an institutional kind constituted by collective intentionality. The audience is split evenly between philosophers of social science and economists, who will catch every ontological distinction, and family members, administrators, and alumni from other fields, who will not. The address should turn structurally on one specific conceptual claim: that money differs from other socially constructed kinds such as gender or race because its collective acceptance is mediated globally through price mechanisms rather than anchored locally in embodied interaction, which produces a distinctive form of ontological fragility—value can collapse without anyone locally reneging on the practice. This claim should do real work in the speech: it should constrain what you can say about economic security and about personal commitment, yielding a single non-obvious piece of advice for the graduates rather than serving as decorative framing. The talk should be about ten minutes, accessible to the non-expert half without condescending to the experts, and it should not include citations, bibliographies, named texts, or appeals to authority; the argument must stand on its own conceptual legs.
