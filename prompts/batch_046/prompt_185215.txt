You are a philosopher of economics and a poet versed in formal verse. Before composing, briefly brainstorm three points: first, Keynes's distinction in A Treatise on Probability between risk, where probabilities are numerically determinate or estimable, and true uncertainty, where they are not; second, the concept of the weight of argument, which Keynes treats as the total amount of relevant evidence relative to the unknown, capable of increasing without altering the probability ratio and explicitly not reducible to a second-order probability; third, Keynes's claim that under true uncertainty rational agents do not maximize expected utility over a unique prior but rely on convention, habit, or animal spirits. Then write a villanelle—nineteen lines arranged in five tercets and a final quatrain, with two refrains alternating in the pattern A1 b A2 a b A1 a b A2 a b A1 a b A2 A1—spoken in the voice of an investor in 1921 confronting a venture for which probabilities are genuinely unknown. The two refrains must be: The weight is high, but no number holds and Probability asks what the evidence tells, but weight asks how much remains untold. The poem's architecture should perform the philosophy: the refrains must remain grammatically separate through all five tercets, meeting only in the final quatrain where they stand side by side without merging, mirroring Keynes's insistence that weight and probability are irreducible dimensions of belief. Around the third tercet, execute a turn—shift the speaker from attempting to assign a numerical likelihood to recognizing that the capital must be committed without one, echoing Keynes's move from calculation to convention. Include one concrete image drawn from the Treatise's logic, such as the idea that evidence is organically interdependent rather than atomically additive, so that the weight grows not by accumulation of independent pieces but by the density of an entangled whole. Render the concept precisely enough that a specialist in Keynes or in imprecise-probability theory recognizes the architecture rather than a name-drop, and write it so that a non-specialist finds it a coherent poem. After the poem, append a brief reflective note—without citations, bibliographies, or reading lists—stating: what specific interpretation of Keynes's weight the poem adopts and where that interpretation takes liberties with the Treatise, especially regarding whether weight could ever be formalized as a second-order measure; what finding about Keynes's actual argument—say, evidence that he later collapsed weight into liquidity preference in the General Theory—would change your mind about the poem's central claim of irreducibility; and which part of the reasoning is most vulnerable to failure, whether the formal analogy between the villanelle's separated refrains and Keynes's separated dimensions is strained, or whether the poem's turn from calculation to convention misrepresents Keynes's view of rational action under uncertainty.
