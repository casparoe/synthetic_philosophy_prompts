Please grade the following undergraduate metaphysics exam answer as if you were a teaching assistant. The exam question was: "Explain Berkeley's master argument against materialism—particularly his claim that conceiving an object existing unperceived necessarily involves conceiving it as perceived—and then evaluate whether this succeeds. Pay special attention to whether the argument confuses a limitation on conception with a proof of metaphysical impossibility. Also consider whether the argument applies analogously to digital objects, such as a tree-model stored in server memory that persists in an unrendered state while no user observes it."

Here is the student's answer:

Berkeley's master argument, presented in the First Dialogue between Hylas and Philonous, aims to demonstrate that matter cannot exist independently of mind. He asks us to conceive of a tree in a garden with no perceiver present. Whenever we attempt this, we find ourselves imagining the tree's color, shape, and texture—thereby including a perceiver in the very idea. Because the conception of an unperceived object necessarily contains a perceiver, the notion is contradictory, which shows that materialism is false.

The argument is not convincing. It depends on misapplying Locke's distinction between primary and secondary qualities: Locke argued that primary qualities like extension and motion inhere in objects themselves, whereas Berkeley wrongly extends mind-dependence to both categories without offering an independent reason to reject Locke's account of primary qualities. Furthermore, Berkeley only proves that we cannot form the idea of an unperceived tree without smuggling in a perceiver; he does not prove that such a tree is impossible in reality. Applied to digital persistence, the point is even clearer: a server can store data about a tree-model without rendering it and without perceiving it, yet the data structure remains. Therefore Berkeley's idealism does not threaten digital objects, and the master argument fails.
