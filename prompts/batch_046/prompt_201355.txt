I'm trying to work out my own commitments about adaptationism versus spandrels, using Gould and Lewontin's critique as the background framework but testing it against cases that go beyond the standard examples. Here are my verdicts. The human chin is a spandrel: a structural byproduct of jaw and dental reduction, not shaped by selection for its current form. The panda's radial sesamoid "thumb" is a clear adaptation, directly modified for bamboo stripping. The broad triangular spaces between the arches of San Marco are spandrels—architectural byproducts that happen to be decorated but were never designed for decoration. Now consider a hypothetical synthetic bacterium engineered for nitrogen fixation: the nitrogenase complex requires a bulky scaffold protein that accidentally creates a sticky outer surface trapping pathogens; infection rates drop, but the stickiness was never selected for. I feel this sticky surface is still a spandrel, not an adaptation, because selection never targeted it, even though it now confers benefit—much like the chin might confer some minor mechanical advantage without being an adaptation. But then consider an obscure deep-sea mollusc whose shell ridges are structurally necessary to house sulfur-oxidizing symbionts; the ridges are not selected for hydrodynamics, yet the symbiosis is the reason they are maintained. Here I want to say the ridges are part of an adaptation—the symbiotic system—rather than pure spandrels, even though the ridges themselves are byproducts of shell growth. That seems to conflict with my synthetic-bacterium verdict, since in both cases a byproduct is tied to a genuinely selected system. Meanwhile, feathers in early theropods: if they evolved first for insulation and were only later co-opted for flight, I treat them as adaptations for insulation that became exaptations, not as spandrels of scale development. So my pattern is: direct selection for current function equals adaptation; structural byproduct with no selection on the trait itself equals spandrel; but if the byproduct is an inseparable part of a selected complex, I'm unsure. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving selective targeting, modularity, or historical sequence—that saves all these verdicts? If not, which verdict should I drop? And what does the best principle predict for a case I haven't listed: a digital-evolution algorithm that accumulates redundant "junk" code because deletion is lethal, and that junk later serves as a mutational buffer?
