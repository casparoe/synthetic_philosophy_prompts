I'm preparing the argument for my final paper in a graduate seminar on Buddhist philosophy of mind and comparative theories of personal identity, and I need a consistency audit of the view I'm defending before I commit to it in print. The position I want to hold together includes the following commitments:

(a) The Buddhist doctrine of anatta denies a permanent, metaphysically simple self-substance (atta), but it does not deny all conventional continuity; the conventional person (puggala) is a dependently originated stream (santāna) of psycho-physical aggregates (khandha) sustained by conditions of contiguity and causal efficacy, and it is real enough for ethical and legal discourse within conventional truth (sammuti-sacca).

(b) Contemporary psychological-continuity theories of personal identity (reductionist accounts in the style of Parfit) capture part of this insight, but they still implicitly treat psychological relations as relations that hold to an underlying subject; the strictly correct view is that there is no underlying subject, only the relational events and conditions themselves.

(c) Narrative-identity theories correctly describe the phenomenological structure of human selfhood—how we experience ourselves as the protagonists of integrating autobiographical stories—but the narrative is a conventional designation (paññatti) imposed upon the stream, not its metaphysical backbone or necessary condition.

(d) For practical ethics and legal accountability, I hold that moral responsibility attaches specifically to the narratively integrated agent: a being is the proper subject of praise or blame only if it can recognize its past actions as its own within a coherent life-story and project that coherence into future deliberation.

(e) I interpret karma and rebirth (punabbhava) non-literally: they describe the causal transmission of mental formations and dispositional tendencies (saṅkhāra) within a single biological life, not the transmigration of a soul across bodies; I reject any non-physical substrate that could carry mental states after brain death.

(f) If a person's entire psychological profile—including memories, dispositions, and narrative structure—were causally transferred with perfect fidelity to a synthetic substrate, the resulting being would be the same conventional person and should inherit all of her moral and legal obligations.

(g) In pathological cases such as severe dissociative identity disorder or split-brain surgery, where two centers of narrative agency emerge within one biological life, neither center should be held fully responsible for actions performed by the other, even though both share an undivided pre-division causal history.

(h) The ultimate soteriological aim of Buddhist practice is the cessation of clinging (upādāna) to the aggregates, which entails dissolving the narrative self-construct; thus the conventional self, while ethically indispensable, is ultimately something to be transcended rather than preserved.

Some of these seem to cohere through a two-truths framework, but I am unsure where the real fractures lie. Specifically: which pairs are genuinely incompatible, which only look incompatible because they operate at different levels of analysis, and what is the least costly revision? I am particularly concerned about three possible fault lines. First, does my reliance on narrative integration as a condition for moral responsibility (d) smuggle in a substantive self that my stream-based ontology (a–b) officially denies? Second, does my acceptance of synthetic uploading as full personal survival (f) conflict with my non-literal reading of karma (e), given that the latter denies any substrate-independent transmission of mental formations? Third, does my verdict about fission and divided responsibility (g) commit me to a criterion of personal identity that my reductionist, stream-based account cannot consistently deliver?

Please audit the set: rank the tensions by severity, show which ones dissolve on inspection and by what reasoning, indicate what each serious tension actually forces me to give up, and do not present all conflicts as equally damaging. I want to know what my position genuinely commits me to, not a survey of Buddhist metaphysics or a reading list.
