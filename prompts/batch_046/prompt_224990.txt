I've been working through Plutarch's Parallel Lives with a small reading group of people who aren't scholars — just interested in what biography is for — and one member keeps insisting that the paired structure is essentially a moral experiment. Her idea is that when Plutarch sets a Greek and a Roman side by side, he is holding the virtue (or vice) constant and varying only the cultural and political environment, so the reader can observe whether character produces the same kind of life regardless of context. She says this explains not only Plutarch's method but also why later hagiographers adopted paired or comparative lives: it turns biography into comparative anatomy for the soul, letting us isolate which traits are universal and which are shaped by circumstance. I can see why the comparison feels right — there are controls, there is comparison, there is an implicit claim about causation — but I also feel it smuggles in a lot. Lives aren't replicable, Plutarch chooses his details for narrative and moral effect, and the "variables" aren't actually independent. Still, I can't precisely say where the correspondence holds and where it collapses, or whether any useful conclusion about moral learning survives once the disanalogies are on the table.

Could you audit the analogy carefully? I'd like an explicit mapping — what corresponds to the hypothesis, the control, the treatment, and the outcome in Plutarch's pairing — followed by the points where biography lacks the features that make an experiment work. Does the analogy import false assumptions about objectivity, repeatability, or the separation of observer and observed? And if it fails as an account of what Plutarch is actually doing, what would be a more accurate analogy for the moral work these paired lives actually perform?
