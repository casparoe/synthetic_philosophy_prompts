I am leading a single ninety-minute seminar for philosophy-of-science graduate students who know Western epistemology—Gettier cases, reliabilism—but have never encountered classical Indian philosophy. I want to teach the Nyāya conception of pramāṇa not as a list of four sources but as an instrumental-causal account of valid cognition (pramāṇa as karaṇa). Do not explain the doctrine; design the explanatory arc. First, what conceptual dependency must be secured before introducing pramāṇa as an instrument rather than a justification-conferring process—should I begin with the Nyāya distinction between pramāṇa and pramā, or with the causal structure of cognition? Second, choose the carrying example: I am weighing the standard inference from smoke to fire on a distant hill against recognizing a familiar face in dim light. Which one better exposes the instrumental nature of inference versus perception for this audience, and what must it include and exclude? Third, map the misconceptions they will import—likely conflating pramāṇa with internalist justification, or with a modern reliability metric—and specify the exact point in the sequence where each should be interrupted. Fourth, provide one diagnostic question for the final ten minutes that reveals whether they have grasped the causal-instrumental framing rather than merely memorized the taxonomy. Finally, identify which portion of the post-Gautama commentary tradition should be explicitly set aside at this level, and state that you are leaving it out.
