In my philosophy course we discussed the digger wasp Sphex ichneumoneus and the idea of sphexishness—its tendency to repeat the burrow-checking ritual mechanically even when the prey has been moved, as though it cannot register the changed situation. The usual framing treats this as a boundary case: the wasp lacks freedom because it is locked in a rigid loop, while a human can notice the change and act for reasons. But I want to test whether that boundary actually holds. Suppose we imagine a hypothetical “recursive sphex” agent that runs the same loop, yet embeds within it a fixed counter: after exactly three identical checks, it automatically breaks the cycle and proceeds to the next step, regardless of what the environment looks like. To an observer, this agent sometimes deviates from the simple loop, yet its deviation is not responsive to the specific reason the prey was moved; it is just another layer of mechanical rule-following. If we grant that this agent is still sphexish—only sphexish at a higher order—then it seems any behavior that looks flexible can be redescribed as a more complex deterministic routine with nested conditions. So my question is this: does the sphexishness framework actually collapse the distinction between mechanical rigidity and free will, because flexibility is merely sphexishness with added internal counters? Or is there a specific structural feature—perhaps being guided by the actual reason for the environmental change rather than by an internal threshold—that survives this reduction and marks a genuine difference? I am especially interested in whether adding representational content about the prey’s location to the loop changes the analysis, or whether that too can be absorbed into a more elaborate but still sphexish program.
