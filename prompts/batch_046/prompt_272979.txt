After a long argument with a friend yesterday about whether David Lewis's framework for conventions can genuinely account for the normative authority of evolved social norms without collapsing into either contractarianism or evolutionary functionalism, I have been reflecting on how precisely students can separate the descriptive conditions of a convention from the normative weight it acquires over time. I am grading responses for a seminar on social ontology, and I want an independent assessment of one particular answer because my friend insisted that the distinction between strategic equilibrium and moral obligation is usually muddled in these discussions, and I want to check whether this response avoids that muddle or falls into it.

The exam question was: "In David Lewis's analysis, what distinguishes a convention from both a habit and a coercively enforced rule, and how might a convention evolve into a social norm that retains binding force even after the coordination problem that originally sustained it has disappeared?" Below is the student's response.

"On Lewis's analysis, a convention is a regularity of conduct R within a population P that resolves a coordination problem. Its core conditions include general conformity to R, a preference for conforming to R conditional on others doing so, the existence of an arbitrary alternative R' that could solve the problem equally well—implying that R is morally arbitrary—and common knowledge among members of P that these conditions hold, which Lewis takes to require an explicit founding agreement or mutual recognition among the original participants. This distinguishes conventions from habits, which involve no such conditional preference or awareness of mutual expectation, and from coercive rules, which depend on external sanctions rather than on the reciprocal stability of expectation. The evolutionary trajectory from convention to norm occurs when repeated interaction causes the population to internalize R: deviation comes to trigger social disapproval rather than mere strategic loss, and eventually the awareness that R' was ever a live option fades entirely. At that point, R functions as a norm with independent moral authority; indeed, because the strategic basis has been transcended, the norm can persist even when the original coordination problem is no longer present, and since its binding force now derives from collective recognition of legitimacy rather than from anticipated reciprocity, it follows that such norms are immune to rational revision."
