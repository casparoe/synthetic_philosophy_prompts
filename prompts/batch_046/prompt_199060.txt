Design a structured 90-minute classroom simulation for 15 upper-level undergraduates that makes participants experience how national antiquity is performed rather than inherited. Work within the frameworks associated with Eric Hobsbawm's analysis of invented traditions and Benedict Anderson's account of imagined communities, but center the exercise on a specific mechanism that standard summaries often skip: retroactive canonization, in which a group not only fabricates a tradition but then actively suppresses awareness of its recent origin, treating contradictory evidence as a threat to collective identity rather than as historical correction.

The deliverable should be a runnable activity with four assigned roles—Founders, Archivists, Skeptics, and Enforcers—clear rules, and small social stakes (for example, collective rewards for maintaining narrative coherence and individual costs for breaking consensus). Structure it in two phases. In Phase One (20 minutes), small groups invent a founding ritual, a symbolic vocabulary, and a brief origin myth for a fictional micro-community, writing these down and sealing them in an envelope. In Phase Two (50 minutes), the sealed documents are hidden; the group must now defend the tradition as ancient. The Founders and Enforcers maintain coherence using only social interpretation and pressure; the Archivists hold the hidden knowledge but face incentives to remain silent; the Skeptics receive planted contradictory evidence and must attempt recovery without direct accusation. Use a minute-by-minute timeline.

Then provide a debrief protocol (20 minutes) that maps each phase onto the mechanics of invented tradition and imagined community, specifically identifying when shared amnesia replaced shared memory. Be explicit about what the simulation reveals regarding the emotional labor required to maintain antiquity, and include an honest limitation: state coercion and multi-generational transmission cannot be reproduced in a single session and must be supplied by the facilitator. Do not ask participants to consult outside texts, and do not include requests for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists in your design.
