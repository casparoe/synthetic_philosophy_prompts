Consider the institutional pattern, widespread in advanced industrial economies, by which automation-driven labor displacement is met not with restrictions on machinery but with policies that treat technology as an exogenous, value-neutral shock: retraining subsidies, lifelong-learning mandates, portable credentialing schemes, and labor-market flexibility programs designed to adapt workers to new technical environments. What philosophical commitments about technology, labor, and value does this practice presuppose? Specifically, does it depend on an instrumentalist view of machinery as a neutral tool whose social effects are determined entirely by the surrounding policy framework, and is that view compatible with Marx's analysis—particularly in the Grundrisse—of capital-integrated machinery as an automatic system that converts living labor into dead labor, extending surplus labor-time rather than reducing necessary labor-time? Reconstruct the implicit theory of technological change that makes worker adaptation seem like an adequate and sufficient response; distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates; and examine whether this institutional logic could survive acceptance of Marx's distinction between formal and real subsumption of labor under capital, or whether that distinction forces a fundamentally different account of what automation does and who controls it. Identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined—whether, for instance, a non-Marxist instrumentalist and a Marxist critic could both endorse the same retraining regime for incompatible reasons—and point out where it quietly rules out the view that machinery, once embedded in capital, operates as an alien power over the worker rather than as an instrument at society's disposal.
