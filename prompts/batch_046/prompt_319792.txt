I am drafting a six-month research proposal for a philosophy-of-science and democracy fellowship. The core question is this: can John Dewey's experimentalist model of democratic inquiry accommodate sustained, structured disagreement over ends—not merely means—without collapsing either into authoritarian expertise or into a thin proceduralism that abandons the normative force of inquiry? Existing interpretations tend to split between those that read Dewey as requiring a pre-existing shared culture of values, which makes his view implausible for pluralist societies, and those that reduce democratic inquiry to instrumental problem-solving stripped of normative commitment. I want the proposal to argue that this dichotomy misses a third possibility reconstructed from Dewey's logic of inquiry, applied specifically to what I will call adversarial deliberative environments—contexts such as climate adaptation governance or algorithmic auditing in which stakeholders hold incommensurable value frameworks yet must still coordinate collective action. The proposal should set out: (1) the precise formulation of the question and why current scholarship on Dewey and democracy leaves it unsettled; (2) the methodological approach—reconstructing Dewey's concept of warranted assertibility through the lens of his later work on valuation and social inquiry rather than through the standard reconstruction via The Public and Its Problems—together with an argument for why this textual and conceptual route is more fruitful than either historical exegesis or direct application to contemporary democratic theory; (3) a description of the deliverable, which should be a conceptual framework identifying the conditions under which adversarial deliberation can still count as democratic inquiry on Deweyan terms, plus a diagnostic tool for distinguishing productive disagreement from mere conflict; (4) explicit success and failure conditions—what result would vindicate the reconstructed view and what finding would show that Dewey's model necessarily presupposes value consensus; and (5) a realistic six-month scope, broken into phases, that does not require empirical fieldwork but does require close conceptual analysis of primary texts and engagement with one contemporary case study. The answer should be written in formal British English, should not include a bibliography or literature review by citation, and should avoid inventing specific secondary sources; references to positions and arguments may be mentioned generally, with the understanding that citations will be added later. Please present the proposal as a polished one-page document with clear headings.
