Question: “Economists often cite the sunk-cost fallacy as a clear case of irrationality: past expenditures are irrecoverable, so only future costs and benefits should matter. But consider contexts of incomplete contracting, in which an agent’s past investment is observable and can serve as a credible signal of future cooperation—so that honoring a sunk cost ex post may have ex ante commitment value. Is honoring sunk costs always irrational? Distinguish descriptive claims about actual human reasoning from normative claims about rational choice, and evaluate whether any ex post honoring of costs can be justified by appeal to commitment or signaling.”

Student answer: “It is not always irrational to honor sunk costs, though in the standard case it is. Descriptively, people do honor sunk costs because of loss aversion and mental accounting, as Kahneman and Tversky showed in their classic demonstration of the fallacy. Normatively, however, rational choice requires that only marginal future utilities count; past costs are by definition irrelevant to the decision calculus. That said, when contracts are incomplete and an agent needs to signal that she will not abandon a joint project, her willingness to bear a sunk cost can function as a credible commitment device. Because entering such a commitment strategy is rational ex ante, the specific act of honoring the sunk cost is also rational ex post: the agent is simply executing the optimal plan she already adopted. Therefore, honoring sunk costs is irrational in isolated one-shot decisions but rational whenever it serves a credible signaling function in an ongoing interaction.”
