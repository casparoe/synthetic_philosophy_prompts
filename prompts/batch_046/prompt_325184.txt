As a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading political philosophy, I have become preoccupied with Isaiah Berlin's well-known distinction between negative and positive liberty and with Philip Pettit's republican argument that freedom is best understood as non-domination rather than as mere non-interference. The literature on this distinction is extensive, yet my own experience of institutional life suggests that both categories may miss something important. Consider a research laboratory in which no statute or formal rule prohibits a scientist from pursuing a particular project—so negative liberty is fully present—and in which no single director or official possesses the formal authority to veto her work—so, on Pettit's account, there may be no identifiable dominator exercising arbitrary power. Nevertheless, the allocation of resources is controlled by an opaque committee whose criteria are neither published nor subject to appeal; the scientist can be deprived of funding without explanation, and she can never predict with confidence whether her next proposal will be rejected for concealed reasons. I wish to call this deficit procedural transparency liberty: a form of unfreedom that consists not in being interfered with, nor in being subject to an agent with arbitrary power, but in being governed by mechanisms whose rules and reasons are epistemically inaccessible.

Write an essay that examines whether this proposed category—procedural transparency liberty—collapses into republican liberty once we properly understand domination, or whether it exposes a genuine gap in the standard taxonomy. Weigh the case for treating institutional opacity as a species of domination against the objection that domination requires an identifiable agent capable of arbitrary interference, not merely an unpredictable bureaucratic process. Reach and justify a definite conclusion about whether negative liberty, republican liberty, or this intermediate notion best captures what is lacking in the laboratory case, and about whether philosophers ought to expand the binary framework.
