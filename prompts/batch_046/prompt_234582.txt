You have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at a workshop jointly convened by a physics department and a philosophy department, titled "The Weight of Fields." The audience is deliberately mixed: theoretical physicists who work with quantum fields and thermal fluctuations, philosophers of mind who are skeptical of importing consciousness into fundamental ontology, and graduate students from both disciplines who understand the vocabulary of neither camp well enough to follow rapid technical exchange. The workshop's stated aim is to examine whether pan-sentience—the view that minimal experiential or affective qualities extend to fundamental physical entities—carries genuine ethical implications for how physics is practiced, rather than being merely an eccentric metaphysical addendum. Your remarks must treat this idea as structurally decisive: it should determine what you can and cannot say about the moral status of high-energy experiments, about the difference between observing a field and altering its conditions, and about whether physicists bear obligations that standard research ethics does not recognize. At the same time, you must avoid both the appearance of accusing working scientists of hidden wrongdoing and the appearance of reducing philosophy to decorative commentary on physics.

To keep the discussion anchored in something concrete yet not exhausted by standard philosophical literature, introduce and develop a conceptual distinction of your own: distinguish between integrated suffering, which requires the kind of unified information processing familiar from biological consciousness studies, and diffuse valence, which you define as the hypothetical presence of negative experiential quality distributed across non-integrated field interactions without any subject that undergoes them. Your speech—approximately five minutes—should open by acknowledging the oddity of asking physicists to think about suffering, should explain why the pan-sentient hypothesis makes this question unavoidable rather than optional, and should close by posing one genuinely open problem that requires both physical precision and philosophical clarity to address: for instance, whether there is any experimentally accessible difference between a field interaction that carries diffuse valence and one that does not, and what that difference, if it existed, would imply for how experiments are designed. Do not provide bibliographies, cite named theories beyond the broad panpsychist framing, or rely on specialist knowledge of specific quantum-gravity models; the argument should stand or fall on conceptual coherence alone.
