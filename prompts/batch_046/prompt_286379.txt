I recently came across a summary of Confucian role ethics—probably from an online essay or a podcast episode—that went something like this. Confucianism rejects the idea of a universal, abstract human nature shared by everyone; instead, modern interpreters describe it as operating on a relational ontology, where a person is literally constituted by the roles they inhabit—parent, child, ruler, subject—with no independent self underneath. The central concept is zhengming, or the rectification of names, which the summary presented as a conservative doctrine: society assigns you a name and a fixed set of hierarchical duties, and morality consists entirely in performing those assigned duties sincerely. Because obligations are role-relative and asymmetrical, there is no basis for universal human rights or for criticizing the structure of roles itself: a father owes his son guidance and discipline, the son owes obedience and care, and neither owes anything meaningful to strangers outside these bonds. The author concluded that this makes Confucian ethics structurally incapable of supporting social reform or egalitarian politics, since challenging an unjust hierarchy would mean rejecting the very relational identity that makes you a moral agent in the first place. I can see why relationships matter more than abstract rules here, but the summary also seemed to miss something about ren—humaneness or benevolence—that apparently extends outward in widening circles, and about the idea that roles can be performed well or badly rather than just followed mechanically. Is this summary fair? Specifically, does it correctly capture the relational view of the self, or does it overstate the absence of individual agency? Is zhengming really about preserving assigned social positions, or is it about ensuring that roles and reality align—including the possibility that a bad ruler fails to be a ruler? And does the conclusion that Confucianism is inherently anti-reform and anti-egalitarian actually follow, or does the tradition contain internal resources—perhaps in the concept of ren or in Mencian ideas about legitimate authority—that the summary ignores?
