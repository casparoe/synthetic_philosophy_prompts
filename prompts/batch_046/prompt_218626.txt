I'm working on an essay for an advanced seminar on philosophy of time that asks students to engage with a literary figure whose work complicates standard metaphysical taxonomies, and I've chosen Borges—but I'm concerned that my framing either collapses him into a familiar analytic position, such as eternalism or a poetic version of McTaggart's B-series, or invents a system he never intended. My central claim is that Borges does not merely illustrate pre-existing theories of time; instead, texts like "The Garden of Forking Paths," "The Library of Babel," and "A New Refutation of Time" together suggest what I call a bibliographic model of temporal location. On this reading, time is neither a linear flow, a branching physical multiverse, nor a static four-dimensional block, but rather a catalog system in which past and future volumes occupy the same ontological shelf, and the present is not a metaphysically privileged instant but the index card currently being accessed by a reader. "The Garden of Forking Paths" then becomes not a narrative about quantum branching but about the topology of textual forking—every decision generates a parallel volume in the same library rather than a separate world—while "A New Refutation of Time" denies succession not by asserting eternal simultaneity in the physical sense, but by treating temporal distinctions as relations of narrative accessibility rather than intrinsic properties.

I am asking for comments on whether this bibliographic model is genuinely extractable from Borges's texts or whether it imposes an alien framework onto literary metaphors. Specifically, I would like feedback on three points: first, whether comparing Borges's denial of succession to analytic B-theory arguments is illuminating or anachronistic, given that Borges was working through Berkeley and Hume rather than through McTaggart; second, whether the essay successfully distinguishes this bibliographic view from both eternalism and presentism, or whether it inadvertently collapses back into one of them; and third, whether the argument that "The Secret Miracle" supports this model—by treating the suspended year of Hladik's execution as a volume that exists in full but is only read in a single mental instant—is convincing or strained. I am not looking for bibliography or citation suggestions, since I will handle references separately; rather, I want to know if the conceptual architecture holds together and whether there are obvious objections I have missed that can be addressed using only the texts themselves and standard philosophical distinctions, without requiring specialist scholarship unavailable without extensive searching.
