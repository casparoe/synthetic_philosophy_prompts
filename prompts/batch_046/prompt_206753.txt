I am designing a graduate seminar module on epistemic logic and self-knowledge, and I want to move past the standard presentation of Williamson's anti-luminosity argument, which usually treats the KK principle—that knowing p entails knowing that one knows p—as either an irrelevant formal curiosity or an obviously false relic of Cartesian transparency. Instead, I want students to see whether any moderated version of KK can be preserved if we restrict luminosity to perceptual states like feeling cold or being in pain. To structure the discussion, I need an evaluation of five conceptual strategies that attempt to save luminosity for these states without abandoning second-order knowledge altogether.

Please rank the following five strategies from 1 (most promising) to 5 (least promising) for jointly preserving both a defensible form of luminosity and a non-trivial version of the KK principle, assigning each a score out of 10 and providing a single-sentence justification. The strategies are: (1) The margin-for-error response, which denies that knowing one is cold requires being able to discriminate every thermally adjacent possible state, thereby weakening luminosity only at the margins; (2) The indexical-contextualist response, which treats "I am cold" as evaluated against context-sensitive epistemic standards that shift as the body warms, so that second-order knowledge fails only because the standard of assessment has moved rather than because the state is hidden; (3) The local-blindness response, which concedes that KK fails at the exact boundary cases where luminosity is threatened, but insists that this is a localized failure that leaves both principles intact for clear cases; (4) The phenomenal-concept response, which argues that the concept deployed in first-order knowledge of being cold is itself epistemically transparent, so that possessing it guarantees second-order access unless the concept is misapplied; and (5) The introspective-simulation response, which claims that what appears to be a failure of luminosity is actually a failure of an offline simulation mechanism used to generate reports, meaning the underlying perceptual knowledge and its second-order counterpart remain intact even when the report is wrong. In your ranking, weight more heavily those strategies that avoid making luminosity depend on contingent contextual shifts or on hidden mechanisms that are inaccessible in principle.

Present your answer in three sections. First, "Ranking and Scores": a numbered list from 1 to 5, each entry giving the strategy name, the score (e.g., 7/10), and one sentence of reasoning. Second, "Synthesis" (approximately 150 words): explain what the top-ranked strategy gets right about the relationship between first-order perceptual knowledge and second-order accessibility, and identify the single feature—whether it is transparency, context-sensitivity, or mechanism-independence—that does the heaviest lifting. Third, "Unavoidable Cost" (approximately 75 words): state one philosophical price that even your best-ranked strategy must pay, specifically a tension it creates with either the universality of KK or the non-graduated nature of perceptual experience, and note why a defender of full Cartesian luminosity would find that price unacceptable.
