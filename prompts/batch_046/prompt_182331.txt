Hey, I'm prepping a seminar on moral status and I've been stuck on a pattern I keep seeing in the insect-sentience literature that I don't have a word for. You know the scale argument -- even if there's only a tiny chance that, say, a cricket feels pain, there are so many of them that the expected suffering is enormous. People invoke this all the time for farmed insects. But here's the thing: the same people usually don't run the argument for wild mosquitoes, or soil nematodes, or the trillions of plankton that might have some minimal neural architecture. And when you push them on why the boundary stops at crickets and doesn't extend to every invertebrate with a nerve net, they don't point to a biological difference -- they say stuff like we have to start somewhere practicaly or those feel different. So the formal argument is uniform -- low probability times huge N -- but in practice the class over which N gets summed is carved out by human sympathy, not taxanomy. I can think of concrete cases: one writer applies the scale reasoning to mealworms in agriculture but explicitly excludes wild beetles because nature is already brutal, even though the numbers are comparable. Is there an established term for this mismatch between the quantitative logic and the affectively guided boundary-drawing? Or is it just an unrecognized failure mode? If there's no name, I'd want one that distinguishes it from plain scope neglect and from speciesism, since it's specifically about scaling reasoning being hijacked by an implicit selection of the reference class.
