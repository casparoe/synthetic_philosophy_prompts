I am leading a single 75-minute session for second-year philosophy undergraduates on why an author's recoverable intentions do not necessarily fix the meaning of a finished text. Do not explain the view to me; design the teaching arc. First, lay out the dependency order: which conceptual separation—between an author's plan and the structural commitments of the finalized work—must be established before the central claim is audible, and what must come only after the students have worked through a concrete case. Second, specify the single carrying example: an invented or hypothetical text in which private authorial notes or drafts point unambiguously in one direction, while the completed form—through its internal syntax, cross-references, or genre conventions—sustains a stable, coherent but different reading; say exactly what this case must include (a genuine conflict, not accidental ambiguity) and exclude (obscurity that makes either reading implausible). Third, map the predictable misconceptions in sequence: that I am endorsing arbitrary reader response; that biographical or historical context becomes irrelevant; and that the text is reducible to physical marks without normative structure. For each, identify the precise point in the arc—before, during, or after the case—where it should be headed off, and the minimal conceptual move that does so without derailing progress. Fourth, provide one diagnostic task or question for the end that distinguishes understanding the separation from merely learning to reject authorial authority. Finally, state explicitly which finer distinction—say, between intended semantic content and intended perlocutionary effect—should be acknowledged as existing but deliberately left unexplored, and explain why omitting it preserves the coherence of the session.
