Contemporary environmental and epidemiological historiography frequently assigns explanatory weight to non-human phenomena—such as the 536 CE volcanic dust-veil event, the 1815 Tambora eruption, or the fourteenth-century emergence of Yersinia pestis—describing them as reshaping labor markets, triggering state fragility, or exposing structural inequalities, language that treats them as nodes in causal networks with efficacy comparable to human policy decisions. At the same time, the same literature routinely disavows environmental determinism and insists that human societies retain autonomous agency. What philosophical theory of causation, explanation, and agency does this combination presuppose? Must it rely on a distributed or relational conception of agency in which non-intentional entities can function as historical actors, or can it be reconstructed within a conventional intentionalist framework that merely permits unusually strong background conditions? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology and ontology of this practice, evaluate whether its rejection of determinism is genuinely consistent with its explanatory rhetoric, and specify what would have to change in narrative structure, event selection, and causal attribution if the field adopted instead a strictly counterfactual-probabilistic model in which climate and pathogens are never described as acting but only as altering the probability space within which human decisions occur.
