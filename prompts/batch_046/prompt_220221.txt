I'm leading a philosophy-of-science session next week for a mixed group of literature and history graduate students with no biology background. The distinction between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function became personally pressing after a recent clinical conversation in which my physician spoke of a tissue's evolutionary function while I was asking only about its present causal contribution to my symptoms. I need a teaching plan rather than the lecture itself. What conceptual groundwork—perhaps the separation between historical explanation and mechanistic explanation—must be in place before either account of function can be introduced? Choose the single carrying example that should run through the session: it must involve a structure with a clear evolutionary origin that now contributes causally to the organism in a different way, and explain why the heart-as-pump example should be avoided because it obscures the divergence. In what sequence will students first reduce "function" to "design purpose," then conflate it with "current utility," and at exactly which points in the arc should each misconception be intercepted? Design one closing question that reveals whether they understand why the two accounts split specifically on exaptation or vestigial structures, rather than whether they can merely recite the definitions. Finally, state explicitly what standard material—such as Wright's selected-effects framework or the full taxonomy of malfunction—should be deliberately left out at this level, and explain why omitting it does not distort the core distinction.
