Take the 1983 National Basketball Association collective bargaining agreement that established the league's first salary cap, and pair it with Wilt Chamberlain's actual 1968 contract with the Los Angeles Lakers—an individual, voluntary agreement that made him the highest-paid player in the sport at that time. Analyze both together through Robert Nozick's entitlement theory of justice in holdings, especially the Wilt Chamberlain argument and the Lockean proviso.

First, map the rights-holders with precision: the individual player, the team that pays him, the players' union that negotiates collectively, the league that sets the rules, and the fans or viewers whose payments ultimately fund the system. Second, apply Nozick's historical principle of justice: is the salary cap a legitimate exercise of collective bargaining, or does it impose a patterned end-state distribution that violates individual entitlements? Third, use Chamberlain's real contract—direct voluntary payment for exceptional labor—to test whether the Wilt Chamberlain argument holds when the voluntary payer is not an individual fan but a corporate entity operating under a collective agreement, and when the surplus is not retained by one star but redistributed across a roster through a cap. Fourth, examine whether Nozick's Lockean proviso applies to labor-market opportunities rather than to physical property: does capping salaries leave anyone worse off in the relevant sense, and does collective representation change how the proviso is satisfied? Fifth, identify where the theory is silent or strained—does it treat the union and league as transparent intermediaries that preserve direct transfers, or as institutional mechanisms that introduce patterning by another name?

Finally, say what this paired case reveals about entitlement theory itself. Does Chamberlain's own career—an unregulated voluntary exchange that produced extreme inequality—function as a confirmation of the argument named after him, or does the subsequent imposition of the salary cap show that real societies reject the theory's conclusions and therefore expose an anomaly? Be disciplined about the mapping: specify exactly whose entitlement is at stake at each step, note where the theory offers no verdict, and do not force it to speak where it is genuinely silent.
