Qualia are usually defined as intrinsic, ineffable, private phenomenal properties — the subjective what-it-is-like character of experience. Dennett's strategy of quining, named after Quine but operating differently, involves denying that qualia possess the four defining properties philosophers usually assign to them. Rather than straightforward eliminativism, he argues that if you strip away ineffability, intrinsicness, privacy, and direct knowability, there is nothing left to eliminate. In fact, he applies Quine's criterion of ontological commitment directly: since qualia don't pull their weight in explaining behavior or neuroscience, we should pare them from our ontology just as Quine pared away possible worlds.

Chase and Sanborn go further with the incoherence thesis. They claim the concept of a private, ineffable quality is not merely unsupported but self-defeating. If a property is genuinely ineffable, then we cannot form a coherent concept of it well enough to either affirm or deny it. So asking whether qualia exist is structurally similar to asking whether square circles exist — the category mistake is built into the question itself. This is stronger than Dennett's view because it targets the concept rather than the entity.

One might worry that if qualia are incoherent, then all first-person reports are unreliable and introspection collapses entirely. That seems like an implausible result. Additionally, the Sanborn mirror experiment, in which subjects report inverted color spectra but behave identically, actually supports Dennett's position over Chase and Sanborn's, because it demonstrates that behavioral data alone can't settle phenomenal questions. Therefore, the incoherence thesis overreaches by undermining introspection in general.
