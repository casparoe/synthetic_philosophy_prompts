I'm trying to figure out when a state apology for historical wrongs is actually warranted, going by my gut on cases.

Germany's formal apology for the Holocaust feels right even though the perpetrators are dead and current citizens weren't alive. Canada apologizing for residential schools decades after they closed: also right. The US apologizing for Japanese internment in the 80s: right.

Now a goverment apologizing for slavery abolished in 1865, but framing it as "our ancestors wronged your ancestors" when half the current population descends from both sides. Still right? I think yes, but it feels different.

Then there's apologizing for breaking a treaty in 1850 with an indigenous nation, but the apology is purely symbolic and explicitly denies any land claim or reparations. Feels hollow, but not wrong exactly.

Here's one I keep going back and forth on: a state issues an apology for a specific massacre ordered by a regime that was overthrown, where every perpetrator was executed and their families exiled, so no descendants of the perpetrators live in the country. The current leaders actually fought that old regime. Is the apology appropriate, or is it like confessing for strangers?

And this pair that bugs me: apologizing for a democratic law that was widely supported at the time but is now seen as horrific (like forced assimilation schools) feels necessary. But apologizing for colonial violence done by an empire that dissolved 70 years ago, where the current state is technically a successor but was also colonized by that same empire, feels performative - like taking blame for something you also suffered.

Is there one principle that gets all of these, maybe about institutional continuity versus inherited culpability? What does it say about a case I haven't listed - say, a government apologizing for a border policy that some historians argue was defensive, not aggressive? And do I have to drop the verdict about the exiled-perpetrator case to keep the principle simple?
