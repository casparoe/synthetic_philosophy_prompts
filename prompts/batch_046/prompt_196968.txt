I am preparing a single seventy-five-minute seminar session on nomic self-reference—specifically, the philosophical paradoxes that arise when legal rules contain provisions governing their own amendment—for a mixed audience of third-year jurisprudence students and philosophy graduate students. They have read Hart’s account of rules of recognition and change, but they have not yet studied Alf Ross’s formulation of the paradox, Peter Suber’s game Nomic, or comparative constitutional case law in any depth. Rather than surveying real-world constitutional crises, I want the session to turn on one carefully constructed hypothetical: the “Meridian Municipal Charter,” which contains Article IX permitting amendment by simple majority vote, Article IX.2 requiring a three-fourths vote to alter Article IX itself, and Article IX.3 allowing the Council, by a two-thirds vote, to suspend IX.2 for ninety days during a formally declared emergency. Please do not deliver the lecture itself; instead, plan the explanatory arc.

First, lay out the exact dependency structure: which conceptual distinction must be in place before the students can see Article IX.3 as generating a paradox of legal validity rather than as a clever drafting technique? I take it that the separation between a rule of change and a rule of recognition must come early, but specify the precise sequence—what each layer enables and why procedural self-reference cannot be introduced until the prior layer is secure. Second, justify the Meridian Charter as the carrying example: identify the features it must possess (nested scope, an explicit temporal limitation, and the absence of any external arbiter or appeal court) and the features it must deliberately lack (no reference to natural law, no historical analog, no constitutional court) in order to isolate the logical structure of suspended self-reference. Third, map the predictable misconceptions: I expect some will treat the problem as one of legislative drafting rather than philosophical jurisprudence; others will conflate self-amendment with an infinite regress and posit a hidden “meta-meta-rule”; and still others may import moral intuitions about promises or contracts. For each misconception, indicate precisely where in the sequence—before presenting the Charter, during its exposition, or after the paradox is revealed—I should intervene to head it off. Fourth, propose one concrete check for understanding at the end of the session that demonstrates the students have grasped the paradox of suspended self-reference rather than merely memorized the Charter’s clauses. Fifth, state explicitly what should be left out at this level—whether Ross’s original formulation, Suber’s game-theoretic framing, or comparative constitutional examples—and explain why that omission serves conceptual clarity rather than ignorance.

Please present your plan with explicit confidence markers: indicate how confident you are in each component—dependency ordering, misconception map, example design, and check for understanding—and single out the specific point about which you are least certain, with a brief explanation of whether that uncertainty stems from audience variability, from the logical structure of self-amendment itself, or from the pedagogical risk of the check you propose.
