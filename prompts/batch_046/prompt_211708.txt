I serve on the advisory council of an international climate-heritage commission charged with issuing a binding institutional recommendation—applicable to museums, environmental tribunals, and treaty bodies—on how to reference the Anthropocene's origin. One faction urges the commission to endorse the International Commission on Stratigraphy's formal geological-epoch framework, anchored to the Crawford Lake global stratotype proposal, as the sole authoritative start marker; they argue that only such stratigraphic formalism can prevent the politicization of climate timelines. The opposing faction maintains that treating the Anthropocene strictly as a geological epoch mischaracterizes it as a historical period shaped by human institutions, and instead advocates a polycentric model in which different regions recognize distinct onset dynamics—such as the early-modern biospheric exchange patterns underlying the Orbis-spike hypothesis for the Americas versus the post-1950 industrial-acceleration markers for highly industrialized zones—without granting any single scientific body final sovereignty over historical meaning. Draft a formal decision memo recommending which framing the commission should adopt, or proposing a workable hybrid, together with explicit criteria that institutional users can apply consistently. The memo must treat the strongest argument on the losing side with full intellectual honesty: specifically, address the danger that plural, regionally variable start dates invite selective narrative construction in climate-liability proceedings. Finally, state clearly what specific argument or piece of evidence would lead you to reverse your recommendation, and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
