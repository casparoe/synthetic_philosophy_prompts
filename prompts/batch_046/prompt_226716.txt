I am taking a course this semester on moral particularism versus generalism, and our discussions of whether ethical knowledge can ever be fully captured in universal principles have left me wondering about an artificial case that did not exist in any historical philosopher's lifetime. Imagine a decision system—call it a particularist classifier—that is trained solely on thousands of richly contextualized moral judgments made by experienced practitioners, such as physicians weighing end-of-life choices or mediators resolving property disputes, but that is explicitly designed never to extract, represent, or apply any general rules from those cases. Instead, it builds what we might term dynamic salience maps: weighted patterns that direct attention to highly specific features of a situation—the particular tremor in a patient's hand, the unique history of a boundary line—without subsuming them under principles. The system makes predictions about what should be done in new cases, yet it lacks any emotional life, any habituated character, any awareness of human flourishing as an end, and indeed any consciousness at all. Given this profile, I want to ask what Aristotle's likely stance would have been had he encountered such a technology. On one hand, he insists that practical wisdom concerns particulars and that universal knowledge alone is insufficient for right action, which might suggest he would welcome the classifier as an externalization of phronesis unencumbered by false universalism. On the other hand, he ties phronesis inseparably to moral virtue, to the correct orientation of desire, and to a grasp of the universal good that particular judgments serve. Would he therefore reject the system as a hollow simulacrum—capable of mimicking particularist judgment without the soul that makes judgment genuinely ethical? Or might he take a more nuanced middle position, granting it a limited analogical status while denying it full practical wisdom?
