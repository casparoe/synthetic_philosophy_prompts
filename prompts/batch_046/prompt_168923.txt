For my A-Level philosophy coursework I am examining the Epicurean claim that death cannot be bad for the one who dies, since harm presupposes a subject capable of experiencing it and death removes that subject entirely. Would you write a sonnet in strict iambic pentameter exploring this argument, with the formal structure doing philosophical work? Specifically, the octave should rhyme presence-words against absence-words, establishing the intuitive fear that death deprives us of future goods; the volta should fall precisely at line nine, turning sharply to the Epicurean inversion that when death is present we are not; and the sestet should dissolve those rhyme pairings so that presence and absence no longer coincide, mirroring the logical separation between being alive and being dead. Please weave in, without naming or quoting, Lucretius’s symmetry thought — that pre-natal non-existence troubles us not and therefore post-mortem non-existence should not either — as an image running through the middle quatrain rather than an explicit citation. The poem should address the deprivation-account objection implicitly: it must acknowledge why it feels as though death robs us, then show that robbery requires a robber and a robbed. I intend to read it at our sixth-form philosophy society, so it needs to be precise enough that someone who knows the debate recognises the ‘no subject’ problem being resolved, yet clear enough for non-specialist listeners to feel the emotional turn. Please do not include footnotes, bibliographies, or scholarly references; I want the idea to stand on its own within the verse.
