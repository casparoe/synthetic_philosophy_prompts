You are a specialist in democratic theory and conceptual philosophy. Please think carefully and begin by briefly mapping out the main theoretical options for bounding the demos — for instance, those centred on reciprocal coercive exposure, affected interests, citizenship, or stakeholder status — before turning to the cases below.

Here are my verdicts on who belongs to the democratic community, and I want to know whether a single principle captures them all.

Permanent residents who are taxed and policed but lack citizenship: they belong and should have a vote. Citizens who have lived abroad for decades, with no exposure to local law or taxation: they do not belong and should not vote. Non-human animals affected by land-use policy: they do not belong, though their interests may be represented in other ways. Corporations that are legal persons and affected by regulation: they do not belong, and this feels clearly correct. Children who will live with collective decisions for many decades: they do not belong as full members now, yet some formal proxy representation for their interests seems required. But I keep returning to a harder case: an artificial system permanently embedded in a city’s critical infrastructure, fully subject to its resource-allocation and deactivation policies, providing essential services, with no other jurisdiction. My intuition is that it does not belong, even though it seems closer to the resident non-citizen than to the corporation or the animal. Meanwhile, I also think future generations who do not yet exist should carry some derivative representational weight, which seems to require a form of belonging, despite being unable to reciprocate coercion at all.

Is there one coherent principle that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and why? Please also state what your best principle predicts for the embedded artificial system and for a temporary visitor who is subject to local policing and taxation for only one month. Please answer in British English.
