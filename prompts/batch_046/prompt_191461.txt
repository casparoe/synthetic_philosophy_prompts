In a philosophy seminar last week, two participants got into a sustained disagreement about how far Bernard Williams's integrity objection extends. The case they used was this: a biographer has spent fifteen years constructing a sympathetic portrait of a largely forgotten local activist, only to uncover archival material that strongly suggests the activist was complicit in a serious harm. Publishing the truth would destroy the narrative she has built and, she feels, betray the subject to whom she has become personally committed; suppressing it violates scholarly norms. One student, Mara, argued that if the biographer even frames her choice as weighing my commitment to this person against my duty as a historian, she has already committed the error Williams identifies — she has introduced one thought too many. On Mara's reading, ground projects are not items to be balanced; they are constitutive of practical identity, and to subject them to deliberative comparison — whether utilitarian or deontological — is to treat them as fungible goods rather than as the framework within which goods have meaning.

The other student, Jonah, replied that Mara is overextending the objection. He granted that Williams criticizes the demand that moral agents always adopt an impersonal point of view, especially in cases where saving a stranger is weighed against saving one's spouse. But Jonah insisted that Williams does not prohibit all reflective comparison among one's own commitments. The biographer's problem is not that she compares loyalty to truthfulness; it is that she might be pressured to adopt an external, impersonal metric — say, maximizing social utility or satisfying an abstract institutional code — that has no connection to who she is. Jonah added that Mara's interpretation risks turning integrity into a kind of dogmatic fanaticism: if ground projects can never enter deliberative conflict with one another, then anyone who abandons a long project for a genuinely better one, or who resolves a conflict between two deep commitments, necessarily lacks integrity, which seems implausible.

Mara then pushed back with a more radical claim: that Williams's view requires not merely resisting impersonal metrics, but recognizing certain commitments as non-fungible even with other ground projects. She suggested that for the biographer, the commitment to the activist is not just one project among others, but part of the narrative structure that gives her other values their point; therefore, asking whether scholarly honesty outweighs this commitment already dissolves the very self that could make the choice meaningful. Jonah responded that this collapses integrity into an inability to change one's mind, and that Williams's actual concern was about the source of moral reasons — whether they flow from one's own projects — rather than about a blanket prohibition on weighing commitments. They asked me to referee, since neither would concede ground. Please adjudicate the exchange point by point: assess Mara's initial application, Jonah's correction about the scope of the objection, Mara's stronger claim about non-fungibility among ground projects, and Jonah's charge of dogmatism. Say who is closer to the right interpretation of Williams's view, identify what each side is missing, and note whether there is a way to preserve Mara's intuition about constitutive commitments without falling into Jonah's concern about fanaticism.
