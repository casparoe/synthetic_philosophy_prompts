I'm taking a philosophy elective in my final year of high school, and last week our instructor introduced Glaucon's challenge from Plato's Republic — the story of the ring of Gyges, which makes its wearer invisible. The idea is that anyone with such a ring would abandon justice if they could act with complete impunity. After class, I kept arguing with two friends about whether I'd actually change my behavior. To test myself, I made a list of concrete scenarios and recorded my immediate verdict: in each case I said whether the action feels permissible or not, and whether my answer changes if I imagine wearing the ring under conditions of what I started calling second-order invisibility — not only am I unseen by others, but I am also permanently shielded from ever observing any downstream harm I cause.

Here are my verdicts. First, if I wore the ring and could physically assault an innocent stranger purely for amusement with no risk of detection and no way for me to ever learn of their suffering, I still think I should not do it; the verdict is firm. Second, if I could anonymously take a small sum from an extremely wealthy corporation — money no individual misses — I think I probably would, and I do not feel deep moral guilt about it. Third, without the ring, I believe I ought to honor a promise made to a now-deceased friend, such as maintaining their garden, even though no living person knows or could blame me; this feels obligatory. Fourth, however, I also think that if being moral consistently meant extreme personal ruin while everyone around me cheated without consequence, there comes a point where self-preservation legitimately overrides moral demands; there is a limit. Fifth, if the ring allowed me to frame an innocent person for a crime they did not commit, thereby saving thousands of lives but ensuring that one person suffers execution, I feel strongly that I should not do it, though my confidence is lower here. The case that keeps flipping for me is this: with second-order invisibility, I could secretly alter a rival's exam scores downward so that I win a scholarship I genuinely believe I deserve more, and neither the rival nor anyone else would ever trace the change to me, nor would I ever witness their disappointment. Some days I call this plainly wrong, like the assault case; other days it feels closer to the corporate theft — a hidden, victimless adjustment in a rigged game.

Is there a single principle — or a small family of principles — that captures all these judgments without forcing me to abandon one? In particular, I want to know whether my distinction between direct bodily harm and indirect competitive harm is doing the real work, or whether something like the presence or absence of a specific victim with a claim against me explains the pattern. If a principle saves my firm verdicts on the assault and the framing cases but forces me to drop my intuition about the scholarship sabotage, I want to know exactly which clause does that and what it costs. Conversely, if the principle accommodates the scholarship case, I want it to predict clearly whether I should also take the corporate money, and whether my belief in a limit to morality in the face of universal cheating is consistent with that principle or has to be rejected as an outlier.
