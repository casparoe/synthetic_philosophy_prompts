Design a 75-minute structured game for an upper-level seminar of about 15 students that makes them feel the tension at the heart of the St. Petersburg paradox: the divergence between an unbounded mathematical expectation and the firm intuition that no rational agent should pay more than a modest sum to play. Divide participants into rotating pairs of "designers" and "players." Designers must negotiate and fix a uniform entry fee (denominated in course-credit points redeemable for a small grade benefit) that they themselves will be required to pay if they switch to the player role in round two. Players then choose whether to pay that fee to enter a coin-flip payout scheme in which the reward doubles with each successive tail, with outcomes revealed via pre-set hidden cards to eliminate real-time variance problems. After round one, roles reverse but the fee remains locked; both groups record reasoning notes before and after seeing the revealed outcomes.

The debrief should map the designers' inability to justify raising the fee despite knowing the expectation is infinite onto the practical-reasoning problem of divergence; map the players' acceptance or refusal onto utility-curvature and risk-aversion accounts; and map the inter-pair negotiation deadlock onto the more radical idea that there may be no normative fact of the matter about the "correct" fee—an analog to the claim that expectation values fail to guide action when verification of the infinite tail is impossible. Provide a minute-by-minute timeline, explicit rules for point conversion, the hidden-card payout mechanism, and an honest admission of what the activity cannot reproduce: the actual phenomenology of facing an infinite prospect, which you as facilitator must supply in a closing framing exercise. Do not ask for bibliographies, named readings, or citations.
