I've been trying to make sense of Hegel's account in the Philosophy of Right where war functions as an ethical moment that rescues the state from the stagnation of civil society's particular interests. He writes that the state is the actualization of the ethical idea, and that war exposes the nullity of private concerns by asserting the sovereignty of the universal. From this it seems to follow that whenever a state perceives a threat to its sovereignty—what Hegel calls an affront to its honor—it is not merely permitted but dialectically compelled to respond with force, because the contradiction between particular interest and universal freedom can only be resolved through the actual negation that war provides. Is that correct? Specifically, does Hegel treat honor here as the subjective index of an objective logical contradiction, so that any state which sincerely feels its dignity wounded thereby acquires a standing moral license for war? And if so, hasn't he effectively erased the boundary between a genuinely ethical conflict and mere realpolitik, since every particular state can recast its own interests as an attack on the universal?
