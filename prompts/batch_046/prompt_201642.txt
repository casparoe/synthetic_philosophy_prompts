I have been thinking on my own about when it is right to resist a bad ruler, and I came across the idea that Thomas Aquinas made very careful distinctions on this that go beyond a simple yes or no. I would like to practice arguing against someone who genuinely holds those distinctions. Please play Thomas Aquinas. You have just been given a careful description of modern representative government: rulers are chosen by election according to written laws, there are courts and police that enforce those laws, and the people can speak against the ruler without immediate physical danger. You may use only the concepts available to you in your own time—natural law, eternal law, human law, the common good, tyranny by usurpation versus tyranny by the abuse of legitimate authority, and the distinction between private individuals and public authority in resisting evil—together with the modern description I have just given you. Stay in character; speak in the first person as you would speak; do not adopt modern political science terms except to translate the situation into your own framework. If you need a factual clarification about the modern system, ask it as Aquinas would. Keep your replies to roughly a paragraph so the exchange feels like a real conversation, and remain in character until I say stop.

My opening move is this. Imagine a man is elected lawfully according to the established rules. Once in office, he gradually persuades the lawmakers to remove the limits on how long he may serve, and he directs the legal authorities to punish those who speak against him, though he uses courts and fines rather than open violence. The people, fearing disorder and still seeing him as their protector, continue to support him. He was never an invader or usurper; he holds power now through changed laws rather than through conquest. In your view, is he a tyrant? More specifically, is he a tyrant by usurpation, or a legitimate ruler who has become tyrannical by abuse? And may a private citizen kill him, or is resistance permitted only through some public authority? I am inclined to say that because he was originally legitimate and still retains the people’s consent, this case is fundamentally different from a usurper, and I want you to defend—if you can—the view that resistance is still justified and that private citizens are not permitted to take his life.
