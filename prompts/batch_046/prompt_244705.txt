You are an expert in metaphysics and in designing philosophy pedagogy for advanced students. Before you draft anything, take a moment to think carefully and briefly brainstorm three distinct physical or procedural mechanisms by which a group of students could be made to experience the tension between treating a modal variant as the very same individual and treating it as a mere counterpart—choose the mechanism that forces the conflict to emerge from rules and stakes rather than from facilitator explanation. Then proceed.

Design a 90-minute seminar activity for 12 advanced philosophy students (graduate or strong upper-level undergraduate) on the disagreement between counterpart theory and transworld identity. The point is not to have students recite Lewis or Kripke, but to make them feel the pull of each view through structured action: specifically, the intuitive appeal of saying “in another world I am a musician” as an identity claim versus a similarity-mediated counterpart claim, and the way origin essentialism and cross-world moral responsibility pull in opposite directions.

The activity must be fully runnable without web access and must not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or page references. If you mention a specific view, paraphrase it without quotation marks; do not attribute verbatim quotes to named philosophers.

Your deliverable should include:

1. Role and setup: assign students concrete roles or subgroups (e.g., “Identity Committee,” “Similarity Board,” “Origin Tribunal”) with distinct voting powers or token budgets, and provide a fictional but richly described agent—call her Agent K—whose life is traced across four modal variants (World A: musician with different parents; World B: musician with same origin but different education; World C: accountant with same origin and education; World D: accountant with different origin and education). Give each variant a card with specified properties.

2. A somewhat niche but philosophically substantive injection: a graded counterpart-spectrum mechanism. Each subgroup must rank the four worlds on a 1–10 scale of “counterpart proximity” to Agent K in the actual world, weighing memory, character, and origin differently according to hidden weighting cards they draw. Then, using those same rankings, they must make binary institutional decisions for each world: does Agent K inherit a debt? Does she bear blame for an action? Does a promise made to her bind her counterpart? The rules should award collective points only when all three subgroups agree on the binary decision, but penalize disagreement. Crucially, the grading mechanism should make sharp cutoffs feel arbitrary, yet the binary decision rules should initially feel natural.

3. Timeline and rules: specify minutes for each phase (e.g., 15 min silent ranking, 25 min cross-group negotiation with token trading, 20 min forced binary vote with revealed scores, 15 min reshuffling where origin weights are revealed and rankings must be revised, 15 min debrief). Include a payoff scheme using points or collective outcomes rather than money, and state exactly what materials are needed (cards, score sheets, weighting envelopes).

4. Emergent problem design: arrange incentives so that students begin by intuitively treating some variants as “me” and others as “not me,” then discover that their graded similarity judgments do not support the binary splits they made—especially when different subgroups weight origin properties differently and find themselves unable to coordinate on a cutoff. The facilitator should not name “counterpart theory,” “transworld identity,” or “origin essentialism” until the debrief.

5. Debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto the metaphysical positions. For counterpart theory, point to the grading exercise and the failure of binary coordination; for transworld identity, point to the intuitive pull to assign blame or inherit debts across worlds and the resistance to treating origin-divergent variants as the same person. Include:
   - An honest prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time (e.g., one student dominates the binary framing, or students confuse epistemic uncertainty about the cutoff with metaphysical vagueness).
   - How the facilitator should handle that failure without lecturing or imposing the framework.
   - A frank admission of which feature of the debate the activity cannot reproduce—specifically, the formal semantics of modal logic or the ontological status of worlds as concrete versus abstract sets—and a brief script for how the facilitator should supply that missing piece in the final five minutes.

6. Constraints check: confirm that the prompt requires no outside research, uses no verbatim quotations, asks for no citations or reading lists, and can be executed by a model without internet access.

Write in a polished, formal tone. Start with your brief brainstorm, then present the full activity design with all rules, roles, materials, timeline, debrief, failure predictions, and honest limitations clearly labeled.
