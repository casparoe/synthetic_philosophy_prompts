In archaeological interpretation and heritage policy there is a stable, widely enforced asymmetry that strikes me as philosophically loaded. When excavating a settlement, archaeologists routinely reconstruct large-scale social institutions—exchange networks, labor hierarchies, property regimes, ritual calendars—from spatial patterning, wear traces, and stratigraphic context alone, and publish these as established features of the culture. Yet the very same site reports treat the attribution of specific propositional beliefs, theological commitments, or individual intentions to members of that culture as methodologically illegitimate unless there is direct textual or iconographic attestation. A particular burial orientation or foundation deposit may license an inference about kinship structure or cosmological geography as an institutional fact, but not about what the people involved actually believed about death or the afterlife.

What implicit philosophy of belief, collective intentionality, and evidentiary warrant does this practice presuppose? Does it commit the discipline to a form of methodological behaviorism about mental states paired with a functionalist or structural-realist view of institutions? Or can the asymmetry be defended on purely epistemic grounds—that material traces underdetermine mental content more severely than they underdetermine social function—without smuggling in an ontology that treats institutions as mind-independent?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory behind the double standard, and carefully distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates. In particular, is it coherent to reconstruct an institution like a ritual calendar without attributing to anyone the minimal belief that the ritual is meaningful, or is some doxastic commitment unavoidable? And if we replaced the current asymmetry with a uniform abductive framework—applying either the same probabilistic confidence to belief-attribution that we apply to institutional reconstruction, or the same evidential skepticism to both—what exactly would have to change in excavation protocol, peer review, and museum labeling?
