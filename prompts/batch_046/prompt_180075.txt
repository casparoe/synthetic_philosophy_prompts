Read Shakespeare’s Othello as a dramatic study in the boundary between manipulation and legitimate persuasion. The philosophical debate often asks whether manipulation is essentially a violation of autonomy—an influence that bypasses or subverts the target’s rational capacities—or whether it can operate through apparently rational channels by selectively framing evidence and exploiting trust. Apply both frameworks to Iago’s conduct: in his scenes with Othello, does he suppress Othello’s reasoning through emotional contagion and deception about the handkerchief, or does he present himself as a loyal advisor offering reasons that Othello evaluates and accepts? Examine Desdemona’s advocacy for Cassio as a contrasting case of open, reason-giving persuasion, and ask whether her failure demonstrates that transparency and respect for autonomy are sufficient conditions for legitimate influence, or whether Othello’s vulnerability shows that these conditions are necessary but not sufficient. Finally, say whether the tragedy ultimately supports the view that manipulation and persuasion are distinguishable by the presence or absence of rational transparency, or whether Shakespeare dramatizes their collapse: that within conditions of radical trust and emotional exposure, even apparently rational dialogue can function as manipulation without the target ever losing the feeling of having reasoned freely. End by stating what Othello adds to the philosophical debate that abstract argument alone does not capture.
