I am working through Cicero's De Senectute (On Old Age) in the Peabody translation for a course on classical ethics, and I have become rather stuck on a passage in which the aged Cato addresses Scipio and Laelius. The text reads:

"I am wise in this respect, that I follow and obey Nature, the surest guide, as if she were a god, and it is utterly improbable that she has well arranged the other parts of life, and yet, like an unskilled poet, slighted the last act of the drama. There must, however, of necessity, be some end, and, as in the case of berries on the trees and the fruits of the earth, there must be that which in its season of full ripeness is, so to speak, ready to wither and fall,—which a wise man ought to bear patiently. For to rebel against Nature is but to repeat the war of the Giants with the Gods."

What I find difficult is the conceptual relation between the two analogies Cato employs. The image of Nature as a poet who might "slight the last act" seems to treat old age as a dramatic conclusion whose value depends upon the coherence of the whole life-narrative — an outcome-based appraisal, as it were — whereas the comparison to berries and fruits that naturally wither once fully ripened treats ageing as an impersonal biological process that requires only patient acceptance, which sounds closer to a process-based evaluation. I am unsure whether Cato intends these metaphors to reinforce one another or whether they pull in different directions: does the worth of old age lie in its being the well-crafted finale of a virtuous life, or does it lie in the stoical endurance of an inevitable organic decline? More specifically, if Nature is the "surest guide" in both respects, how can the same principle be both a dramaturgical arranger of meaningful acts and a simple cyclical force that produces decay? I should be grateful for any help in disentangling this structure, particularly in seeing how Stoic obedience to Nature can accommodate both teleological completeness and non-teleological senescence without contradiction.
