I am drafting a feature on the metaphysics of propositions and want to lay out a precise conceptual knot for readers. The classic Bradley regress threatens propositional unity: if a relation R binds constituents a and b into one fact, then R itself seems to need further relations to connect it to a and to b, producing an infinite regress that leaves only an unordered collection. Most treatments present this as a difficulty for realist views that treat relations as distinct entities. I want to push the problem into less-charted territory by pairing it with an adverbialist framework, on which relational properties are not separate objects but intrinsic modifications—ways in which the relata themselves are modified—so that “a stands in R to b” is just “a is R-ly modified with respect to b,” with no extra relational item in the ontology. Even here, however, one needs an account of what makes the result a single proposition rather than a mere conjunction of two independent modifications. Now add a further twist: consider a negative existential proposition, such as the claim that no R-relation holds between a and b. If relations are not entities, this negation is not the absence of an object but rather a denial that either relatum bears the relevant adverbial modification. Yet the unity of this negative proposition still appears to depend on the very binding mechanism that adverbialism was meant to eliminate. So the conceptual issue is this: does a Bradley-style regress re-emerge at the level of propositional unity even when relations are treated adverbially, specifically in negative existential cases, or does adverbialism block the regress for affirmative propositions while leaving negative ones structurally disunified? In explaining this, distinguish carefully the ontological problem of what binds the world from the propositional problem of what binds the thought or assertion, and assess whether the adverbialist can maintain a consistent account of unity across both polarities without surreptitiously reintroducing the kind of relational entity they sought to avoid.
