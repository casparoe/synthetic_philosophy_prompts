I have been thinking, without any formal background in philosophy or neuroscience, about the distinction sometimes drawn between Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions of history: in the former, a continuous narrative is rewritten so that the past conforms to the present; in the latter, contradictory evidence is simply erased, leaving no trace that anything was ever different. This distinction seems to me to illuminate the colour phi phenomenon, in which a moving dot that changes colour is perceived to have changed at an intermediate point before the second colour is actually shown — as though the brain inserts a corrected frame into the stream of experience only after the fact.

What I find myself wondering is whether a similar process operates not only in perception but in the construction of the self. If our sense of having decided something, or of experiencing an emotion at a particular moment, is not registered live but interpolated into the timeline afterwards — like the intermediate colour that was never physically present — then the "author" of conscious experience may be less a witness and more an editor working with material that has already been altered. One might call this an "interpolated self": a continuous agent that is reconstructed retrospectively rather than observed in real time.

Given this possibility, I would like to understand the most important implications. In particular, does this undermine the reliability of first-person reports about the order of one's own thoughts and actions? Can moral responsibility survive if the "deciding self" is assembled after the fact rather than present at the moment of choice? And is there any phenomenological difference — any way to tell from the inside — between an Orwellian revision, in which the narrative of the self is continuously rewritten, and a Stalinesque one, in which inconvenient mental events are quietly removed, if both leave the subject with the same seamless feeling of continuity?
