I'm taking a seminar on early modern political economy, and we've been working through Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations with particular attention to Book IV and Book V. For my response paper, I want to develop an unconventional reading: that Smith's well-known opposition to the Navigation Acts and to mercantilist restrictions on the North American colonies is not simply an extension of his general free-trade commitments, but rather rests on a specific, largely implicit normative distinction between proprietary colonies—where private interests hold sovereign authority over land and law—and administrative dependencies—where a chartered corporation exercises delegated authority subject to parliamentary oversight. I want to argue that if we take this distinction seriously, it actually undermines Smith's more accommodating stance toward the East India Company's territorial government in Bengal, because that arrangement also fuses legislative, fiscal, and judicial power within a single private body whose primary purpose is commercial profit. Please construct this argument through close reasoning from Smith's own conceptual framework—especially his account of public revenue, the separation of sovereign and commercial functions, and the dangers of allowing government to become an instrument of private gain—without appealing to secondary scholarship or requesting citations. Specifically, defend the claim that Smith's critique of American colonial administration reveals a hidden principle: that sovereignty ought not be exercised by entities whose fundamental aim is private enrichment. Then apply that principle consistently to the East India case, and reach a clear, justified conclusion about whether Smith's colonial theory is therefore internally incoherent or whether a defensible distinction can be recovered that preserves consistency.
