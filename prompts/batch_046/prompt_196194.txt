I'm a third-year student in a philosophy of mind seminar, and I'm drafting a reflection on substrate-independent consciousness that moves between two cases discussed in The Mind's I: the dialogue in which scientists converse with Einstein's preserved brain in a vat, and the later thought experiment about whether a sufficiently complex book—read in sequence with its internal states updated—could be conscious. In my draft I propose a distinction I haven't seen named in the literature, between what I call "causal-path continuity" (the same physical or informational processes linking states over time) and "interpretive-frame continuity" (the ability to be read as sustaining a single narrative perspective regardless of the causal chain). I apply it to both cases: the brain in the vat retains causal-path continuity with Einstein but may lack interpretive-frame continuity if the conversational setting radically alters the context of its responses, while a conscious book might achieve interpretive-frame continuity without any biological causal path. I then ask whether consciousness requires both conditions, whether they genuinely come apart in the ways I suggest, or whether this framing simply collapses into standard functionalism and misses a distinct problem about narrative binding across substrates. Could you comment on whether this comparison is philosophically coherent, whether I am misrepresenting either original thought experiment, and whether there is an established concept near this split between causal and interpretive continuity in personal-identity debates—or whether the distinction needs sharper boundary conditions to avoid being vacuous? I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list; I want conceptual feedback on whether the carving holds up and where its edges lie.
