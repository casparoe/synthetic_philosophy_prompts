I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I am applying for a six-month interdisciplinary fellowship that pairs technologists with humanities researchers. I want to propose a philosophical project rather than an engineering one. The concept I keep returning to is Daniel Dennett's idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity — the self not as a substance but as an abstract point around which narratives organize, analogous to how a center of mass functions in physics.

Here is my half-formed idea. In distributed systems engineering, we design nodes that have no central controller yet achieve coherence through consensus protocols: eventually consistent ledgers, gossip algorithms, conflict-free replicated data types. These systems maintain identity over time without any single authoritative source of truth. This seems structurally parallel to Dennett's narrative self, but the analogy has not, to my knowledge, been developed in philosophical literature.

I need a two-page proposal for this fellowship. The precise research question should be: does treating the self as a center of narrative gravity commit us to viewing it as a kind of distributed consensus mechanism, and if so, does that make the self more or less vulnerable to dissolution when narratives conflict? More specifically, I want to determine whether the analogy reveals that narrative gravity is a causal mechanism — like a protocol actively enforcing coherence — or merely a post-hoc interpretive stance — like an observer attributing center-of-mass to a cloud of particles.

The proposal must explain why existing philosophical answers do not settle this. Dennett's own writings appear ambiguous between the causal and interpretive readings, and standard philosophy-of-mind commentaries have not systematically tested the structural comparison with distributed coherence.

The approach should be conceptual, not empirical. Over six months, I would study Dennett's primary texts and a limited set of philosophy-of-mind discussions, then map the structural features of three distributed consensus mechanisms onto the narrative-self model, identifying exactly where the analogy breaks — for instance, consensus requires explicit agreement rules, whereas narrative coherence may emerge without them. The deliverable should be a framework paper that gives philosophers a precise vocabulary for asking whether narrative identity is maintained by active integration or passive attribution, and gives engineers a philosophical mirror for thinking about identity without central authority.

Please include: a precisely stated question; an explanation of why current positions leave it open; the method broken down by month; a description of what a successful result looks like; what would count as the project failing; and an honest assessment of whether the scope is realistic for six months. The tone should be formal and suitable for a fellowship committee that includes both philosophers and computer scientists.
