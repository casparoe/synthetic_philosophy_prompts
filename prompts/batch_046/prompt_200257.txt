I am a long-form journalist who covers the institutional history of science—particularly cases in which research programs, clinical trials, or environmental studies caused lasting harm and in which the archival record has been partially suppressed or simply left to decay. In feature after feature, I encounter the same structural dilemma: when does a journalist have a positive duty to excavate and publicize a historical scientific wrong, and when does the argument for strategic forgetting prevail? I want to stop resolving this by instinct.

The philosophical resources I have in mind are Avishai Margalit’s distinction between ethical memory—rooted in thick relations of care and loyalty to specific victims—and political or “thin” memory, which serves present collective identity and state-building; and David Rieff’s counter-claim that remembrance can become a form of perpetual punishment, blocking reconciliation and serving the political needs of the living rather than honoring the dead. These frameworks are conceptually rich but not editorial tools. I need them translated.

Please construct for me a reusable decision procedure—a checklist or ordered sequence of tests—that I can run in roughly one hour before pitching or filing a story that turns on a historical scientific harm. The practitioner is me: a journalist with a forty-eight to seventy-two-hour deadline, no research staff, no in-house legal team, and an editor who must sign off. The stakes are publication of material that may retraumatize subjects, damage necessary present-day research, or be instrumentalized by actors with no relation to the original victims.

The procedure must preserve the core distinctions from the underlying theory—especially whether remembrance serves the victims (thick relation) or serves present audiences (thin relation), and whether it blocks reconciliation or enables it—while dropping distinctions that cannot be operationalized without an ethics committee. Mark every step that remains a judgment call, preferably in brackets, and be honest about where the procedure will misfire.

It must handle hard cases: fragmentary documentation paired with severe harm; institutions that have genuinely reformed and now run programs directly opposed to the old wrong; descendants or survivors who explicitly request silence; risks of reducing a complex scientific legacy to a single moral failure; and stories in which the principal harm-doer is dead but the institution or commercial product survives.

Apply the procedure to three hypothetical cases:

(a) A well-documented but largely forgotten university eugenics study conducted in the 1960s; descendants are reachable but have never sought publicity; the same university now houses a prominent disability-studies program and relies on public trust for current clinical trials.

(b) A pharmaceutical study from the 1980s in which informed consent was questionable; the compound remains in widespread use; the principal investigator is deceased but the manufacturer continues active marketing; survivors are divided about publicity, and some fear that exposure will reduce access to the drug for current patients.

(c) One case in which the procedure yields a recommendation that conflicts with my journalistic instinct—perhaps advising against naming a dead perpetrator despite solid documentation, or against framing the story around institutional guilt rather than structural conditions. Explain what the procedure says, why it conflicts with instinct, and what the procedure explicitly cannot adjudicate.

Finally, state clearly what this tool cannot do: it is not a legal-liability assessment, a substitute for direct ethical consultation with affected communities, or a predictive model of institutional response. Specify the conditions under which the procedure should be suspended and replaced by direct engagement with survivors or descendants.

Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote verbatim from any philosophical text; paraphrase all theoretical claims in your own words. Do not invoke niche sub-theories, technical formalisms, or specialized historiography that a generalist model could not be expected to know. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate for an analytical commission.
