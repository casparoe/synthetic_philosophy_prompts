As a journalist covering the intersection of astrophysics and philosophy of science, I am trying to map a recurring argumentative structure in discussions of the Fermi paradox—one that seems to sit just outside the standard taxonomy of logical fallacies and epistemic biases.

Here is the pattern in concrete form. Researchers propose a specific observable signature of technological civilizations—say, large-scale stellar engineering, particular industrial atmospheric pollutants, or anomalous infrared excesses. They then conduct or cite a survey with a well-defined sensitivity threshold that should detect such signatures if they existed at a given prevalence rate across the observable volume. The survey returns null results. Rather than treating this as straightforward disconfirmation of high prevalence, many analysts invoke an auxiliary mechanism: the civilizations that produce these signatures are precisely the ones capable of concealing them, or our observational window is negligibly small relative to the lifespan of detectable phases, or the signatures are transient in ways our survey missed. Crucially, the null result is then interpreted not as weakening the high-prevalence hypothesis, but as confirming that long-lived, detectable civilizations are rare—because if they were common, some should remain visible despite the selection or concealment effects. Conversely, had the survey yielded even a single ambiguous detection, the same analysts would likely treat it as evidence that the concealment mechanism is surmountable and that prevalence might indeed be high. The framework therefore absorbs both presence and absence into support for the same underlying claim about rarity, without specifying in advance what observation would genuinely undermine that claim.

Does this configuration have an established name in philosophy of science or in the Fermi-paradox literature? I am not asking for a bibliography or for citations; I want to know whether philosophers have already carved out this specific move, in which a detection framework is paired with an auxiliary hiding or selection-effect hypothesis in such a way that null results become confirmatory rather than undermining, and in which the lack of a predetermined falsification criterion is part of the structure rather than an accidental omission. If it does have a name, how exactly is it distinguished from generic unfalsifiability, from immunization strategies in the Lakatosian sense, and from the simple heads-I-win-tails-you-lose pattern? If it does not have an established name, can you coin and define one, specifying its boundary conditions—particularly how it differs from legitimate uses of auxiliary hypotheses in observational astronomy—so that the carving is precise about what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie?
