As a journalist who has spent the last decade covering archaeology, climate history, and the politics of the past, I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at a museum-sponsored symposium titled "After Collapse: Reading Societal Change Without Catastrophe." The audience will be divided between specialists—archaeologists working on the Bronze Age Levant, Maya lowland abandonment, and Rapa Nui ecology—and a general audience of patrons and students who arrive with the popular impression that these histories are primarily cautionary tales of ecological suicide or institutional failure. I want the remarks to be substantive enough that the experts do not feel patronized, yet accessible enough that the non-specialists leave with their conceptual framework shifted rather than merely confirmed.

My central concern, which I need the speech to build around structurally, is the ambiguity of "collapse" itself as an analytical category. I want to argue—not as a polemic, but as an opening gesture—that labeling a transformation a "collapse" imports a teleological assumption: that societies are meant to maintain complexity, hierarchy, and monument-building, and that any retreat from those forms is a failure rather than a change in kind. At the same time, I cannot deny that the archaeological record shows real discontinuity: writing systems vanish, populations relocate, and infrastructure is not maintained. The speech should therefore inhabit that tension, using the Bronze Age, the Maya, and Easter Island not as parallel morality plays but as distinct cases where the line between societal failure and societal change is genuinely unsettled. Because of this framing, the remarks should avoid declaring any single interpretation—the environmental, the political, or the deliberately simplifying—the winner, and they should resist the easy invitation to draw direct climate-policy analogies, which would collapse historical specificity into presentist anxiety.

Please write an eight- to ten-minute opening address in my voice: measured, journalistic, without academic jargon, but precise in distinguishing "discontinuity" from "failure." It should welcome the audience, acknowledge the specialists present, set up the symposium's purpose without pretending neutrality is possible, and close with a framing question for the three days ahead rather than resolving them. I am not looking for citations, a bibliography, or references to specific monographs; the speech should stand on conceptual clarity and the weight of the examples themselves.
