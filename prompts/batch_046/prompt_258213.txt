I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. At a recent tech meetup, two other attendees—Maya and Jonah—debated whether moral evaluation should account for effects on the identity of future people. Maya proposed a case: a firm delays a product release by twelve months to repair serious security vulnerabilities. The delay shifts employee timelines, so different people eventually partner and have children. One child born only because of this delay later endures severe suffering from an unrelated genetic disorder. Maya argued that this child cannot claim harm from the delay—without it, they would never have existed—so ethical assessment should disregard such identity-dependent outcomes and consider only impacts on currently living people.

Jonah responded that this reasoning immunizes nearly any large-scale decision—immigration rules, urban development, commute infrastructure—against criticism about future suffering, since all such policies alter who is eventually born. He insisted that the lack of a specific victim does not dissolve the moral issue; we can still compare the overall value of the world with the delay against the world without it.

Maya countered that Jonah was relying on an abstract world-comparison with no practical moral grip. Without an identifiable victim, she said, we lack the relational basis for blame. She offered a programming analogy: if you deploy version 2.0 and a user who only exists because of 2.0 encounters a bug, you do not blame the deployment for harming that particular user relative to version 1.0, since they never would have used 1.0.

Jonah replied that her analogy misses the point. Ethical reasoning, he said, is not about assigning blame to an agent for harming a specific identifiable party; it is about comparing aggregate well-being across outcomes. He added that accepting her view implies that a policy generating billions of lives barely worth living is morally neutral, provided none of those people prefer non-existence—an implication he finds deeply implausible.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: for each of the four positions above, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification; say what both parties are missing; and indicate who is more right overall.
