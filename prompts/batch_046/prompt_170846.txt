I spent the better part of yesterday evening arguing with a close friend — an engineer who insists that if two turbine failures produce identical crack morphologies, they are automatically the same mechanism, full stop — about whether mechanistic identity requires more than functional or morphological equivalence. I am a root-cause analyst in aerospace certification, and I need to settle this not as an academic exercise but as a reusable operational protocol: a step-by-step decision procedure, essentially a flowchart rendered in ordered tests, that I can run in under thirty minutes during a pre-certification review to decide whether an observed anomaly in one subsystem belongs to the same mechanism as a previously catalogued failure mode. The conceptual principle I want translated is what I will call counterfactual organizational stability: two instances share a mechanism not merely when they involve the same parts arranged the same way, but when altering any intermediate step in the causal-organizational chain disrupts the outcome in the same directional manner across both cases, even if the physical components differ or have been replaced.

The procedure must handle high-stakes judgments where liability, redesign costs, and airworthiness depend on the classification, and it must explicitly flag which steps are irreducible judgment calls rather than mechanical checks. Apply it to three scenarios: first, two turbine blades with identical crack patterns where one stems from thermal fatigue and the other from resonance-induced stress; second, a software-controlled fuel-injection anomaly that replicates the functional signature of a mechanical governor failure from an older model; and third, a case where the procedure classifies the anomaly as belonging to an already-known mechanism, but the engineering team will resist that conclusion for liability and contractual reasons, explaining exactly what the procedure can and cannot adjudicate there. Be explicit about time and expertise constraints — I have half an hour, no access to a specialist library, and the sign-off requires me to justify each step to a non-philosopher review board — and do not provide bibliographies or reading lists; I need a practitioner-grade tool, not a literature survey. Make clear where the procedure will misfire and which distinctions must survive the simplification even at the cost of speed.
