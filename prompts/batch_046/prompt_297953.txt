I have been working through some ideas in a philosophy of privacy course and find myself caught between two ways of explaining why privacy is valuable. On one side is the standard informational-control view: privacy matters because individuals can regulate access to personal information, thereby safeguarding autonomy, avoiding harm, and maintaining appropriate social boundaries. On the other side is a framing we developed in a discussion session, which I am tentatively calling structural opacity. On this view, privacy is not chiefly about controlling disclosure but about preserving zones of social interaction that remain partially illegible to institutional or algorithmic observers; the value lies in the fact that this illegibility allows for spontaneous coordination, unmonitored dissent, and forms of trust that depend on not being fully optimized or predicted. Crucially, this account is meant to apply even in public settings where no secret information is hidden, because the concern is not secrecy but the preservation of unquantified interactional space.

Could you compare these two accounts—informational control and structural opacity—specifically with respect to how they explain the value of privacy in public or semi-public contexts where data is technically observable but not systematically collected or analyzed? In particular, address whether structural opacity can genuinely stand as an independent normative foundation or whether it ultimately collapses back into autonomy-based concerns about control. Also, identify where the opacity view seems most vulnerable to objection.
