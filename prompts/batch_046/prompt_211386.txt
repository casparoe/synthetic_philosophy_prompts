At a philosophy-of-mind reading group, Jordan and Riley argued over an extension of Dennett’s wandering two-bitser. They had been discussing whether any artifact lacking biological consciousness could possess original intentionality, and Jordan proposed a modified case: take the familiar quarter-detecting vending machine with two internal states, but fit it with a simple reinforcement-learning loop that adjusts its release patterns based on ambient temperature, humidity, and the temporal clustering of user interactions. Over months it comes to weight one drink more heavily in cold weather and to emit a specific tonal sequence that regular users interpret as a request for change. Jordan maintained that even this adaptive two-bitser has only derived intentionality—its weights are physically realized correlations, and any aboutness is projected by designers and users, exactly as Dennett describes for artifacts. Riley countered that this misses Dennett’s deeper point: original intentionality is itself a myth, and the adaptive coupling introduces real environmental patterns that the system tracks for its own functional ends. Its states are not merely interpreted as requests; they play an integrated causal role in its continued operation, analogous to biological tracking. Jordan replied that this collapses the distinction Searle insists on—if ecological coupling suffices for aboutness, then a thermostat tracking temperature has original intentionality, which is absurd. The line must be drawn at conscious understanding, which the machine lacks regardless of adaptation. Riley answered that Searle’s line is precisely what is at issue, not a neutral criterion: Dennett allows that all intentionality is derived, either from evolutionary selection or from design, so there is no “original” reservoir except in human self-attribution, and denying the adaptive system any non-projected aboutness while granting it to ourselves is unjustified species chauvinism. Jordan then argued that Riley was smuggling original intentionality back in through ecological functionalism—calling the system’s tracking “for its own ends” already assumes a teleology that requires conscious purpose, not mere mechanical feedback. Riley closed by saying teleology can be grounded in selection history, whether evolutionary or algorithmic, without consciousness, and that the real disagreement is whether Searle’s intuition about the Chinese Room applies to dynamically coupled systems or only to static symbol-manipulators.
