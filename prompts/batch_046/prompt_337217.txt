In my philosophy of religion seminar there's this one slogan that keeps getting tossed around like a converstation-ender: "skillful means justify breaking the precepts." Someone used it last tuesday to shut down my argument that even if a bodhisattva kills a would-be murderer to save hundreds, the killer still accumulates negative karma — they said I was just being a "rule-worshipper" and that upaya clearly allows it. I feel like there's like five different claims smuggled into that one line. Can you separate them out for me?

Specifically I'm wondering if the slogan could mean:
- that sila (precepts) are purely instrumental and have no intrinsic value?
- that a sufficiently pure intention literally transforms the karmic quality of the act?
- that we should evaluate actions by total well-being across all beings affected, like a Buddhist version of act-utilitarianism?
- that the precepts are conventional teachings for beginners and don't bind advanced practitioners?
- or maybe something weirder, like that the bodhisattva's realization of emptiness means there's no agent and therefore no karma?

For each of these, tell me if it's a platitude, if it's a substantive claim that actually gets defended somewhere, or if it's just false. And crucially: which of these readings actually supports dismissing my point about the agent's own negative karma? Because it feels like people are sliding between them.

I want to bring the list to office hours, so lay it out as a menu rather than picking one winner.
