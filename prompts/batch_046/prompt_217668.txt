In metaphysical debates over the special composition question, a common argumentative move compares composite material objects to organized sports teams — specifically, the claim is that just as a basketball team is not merely five individuals in proximity but five individuals arranged with assigned roles, shared strategies, institutional rules, and mutual dependencies, so a table or an organism is not merely parts arranged arbitrarily but parts integrated through functional and causal relations. Defenders of moderate or restricted answers invoke this analogy to argue that unrestricted universalism is implausible (there is no "team" formed by strangers in an elevator, hence no object formed by a shoe and a distant star) and that nihilism wrongly denies the reality of genuinely integrated wholes; critics respond that the analogy illegitimately anthropomorphizes physical parts and smuggles in teleology. I ask you to audit this analogy with philosophical precision. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: map the material parts, spatial relations, causal connections, temporal persistence, and identity conditions of the composite object onto the players, positions, rulebook, referee, match dynamics, and victory conditions of the team. Second, isolate the breakdown points — for example, whether parts possess intentions or normative commitments analogous to athletes, whether compositional relations are prescriptive rather than descriptive, whether replacement of parts over time (the biological Ship of Theseus) maps cleanly onto player substitution, and whether the "game" has an external purpose that material objects lack. Third, identify what normative and teleological baggage the analogy imports about the priority or value of the whole over its parts, and assess whether that baggage is available to a metaphysician who denies that nature contains purposes. Fourth, determine whether the moderate conclusion — that composition requires functional or organizational unity — survives once these disanalogies are exposed, or whether the analogy was carrying the argumentative load. Fifth, if you judge the analogy to fail in a decisive respect, propose a more apt comparison and explain exactly what it improves. Write the response in American English, and keep the analysis focused on conceptual structure rather than on empirical science or historical scholarship.
