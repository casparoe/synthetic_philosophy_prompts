Please explain the conceptual problem of reconstructing institutional rules—particularly rules of exclusion or inheritance—when the primary archaeological evidence consists not in what is present in a burial or deposit, but in what is systematically absent. The difficulty is that absence is overdetermined: it may reflect practical constraints (scarcity, perishability, recycling) rather than normative prohibition. Without independent access to the institution being reconstructed, what conceptual criteria could justify preferring a normative reading of absence over a practical one? Furthermore, consider whether framing absence as a form of negative statement—that is, treating deliberate exclusion as an intentional communicative act embedded in material practice—solves the circularity, or merely relocates the inferential burden onto identifying intentionality from material traces. Contrast this with the comparatively straightforward inference of beliefs from positive presence, and clarify whether the asymmetry between presence and absence is epistemic, ontological, or both.
