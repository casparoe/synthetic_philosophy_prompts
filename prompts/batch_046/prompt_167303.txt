I'm preparing an adversarial-collaboration exercise for my epistemology seminar, where we are working through the structure of knowledge and its failure conditions. I want you to draft the joint statement that two opposing interpretive camps on the Gettier problem could sign after a sustained, good-faith collaboration.

Camp One: "Refined-JTB defenders." They grant that the classic Gettier cases—beliefs formed by valid deduction from false premises that happen to be cancelled out by accidental truths—show that an unqualified justified-true-belief analysis is insufficient. But they maintain that the gap can be repaired by refining the justification condition itself, for instance by requiring that the justification not essentially depend on false premises, without introducing a separate fourth condition such as safety, sensitivity, or anti-luck.

Camp Two: "Anti-luck essentialists." They hold that Gettier cases expose a structural defect in any tripartite framework: knowledge requires the absence of a specific kind of epistemic luck that is neither identical to justification nor reducible to it. They claim that every attempt to "repair" JTB by refining justification either fails to cover all Gettier-style scenarios or tacitly smuggles in an independent anti-luck condition, and therefore that knowledge is essentially a four-place (or more) relation.

Please produce the following, in formal prose suitable for a seminar document:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign. These should include: the logical form of Gettier reasoning; what the original false-premise cases establish about the independence of truth and justification; which proposed repairs (no-false-lemma conditions, causal theories, reliabilist amendments) have been shown inadequate and why; and what any adequate analysis of knowledge must preserve about ordinary attributions.

2. A disagreements section in which the residual divergence is stated twice—once in wording Camp One accepts for its own view, once in wording Camp Two accepts for its own view—so that each formulation is endorsed by its side and not a strawman.

3. At least two cruxes: sub-questions such that resolving them should move at least one camp. I suggest focusing on (a) whether there is a coherent notion of "purely doxastic justification" that can be rigorously separated from any truth-conduciveness or reliability requirement; and (b) whether there exist genuine "reverse-Gettier" scenarios—cases in which a belief is safe, anti-lucky, and credit-worthy but lacks traditional internalist justification—in which Camp Two must deny knowledge despite the absence of Gettier-style failure.

4. For each crux, present one concrete case or argument both camps agree is genuinely relevant. For (a), consider testimonial knowledge transmitted by an informant who is generally unreliable but happens to be correct on this specific occasion, and ask whether this should be treated as Gettier-like or as a distinct category. For (b), consider a subject who forms a true belief through a blind but highly reliable cognitive mechanism that the subject cannot articulate or defend. For each case, state in advance what each camp predicts about whether knowledge is present, and identify exactly what empirical or conceptual finding would cause either camp to revise its prediction.

Write precisely and avoid vague generalities. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests—this exercise is designed to be evaluated without web access. Avoid invoking obscure recent technical literature; rely on well-established distinctions but apply them rigorously. If you reference specific texts, paraphrase without quotation marks rather than quoting verbatim. Keep the tone appropriate for a philosophy seminar: analytical, neutral between the camps, and focused on the logical structure of the disagreement rather than rhetorical victory.
