For a postgraduate seminar on the ontology of intellectual property, write a sestina in the voice of an abstract mathematical theorem that is successively enclosed as a trade secret, patented as an algorithmic process, released under a copyleft licence, declared a public-domain axiom, captured within a monopoly of essential patents, and finally returned to an open commons. Use these six end-words, rotating in strict sestina order: claim, proof, commons, exclusion, licence, origin. In each of the six stanzas, the theorem should speak from the ontological position assigned to it by that regime — as hidden knowledge, as economic property with transferable rights, as a shared gift retaining moral rights (droit moral) of paternity and integrity, as an unowned resource open to first appropriation, as a standard locked behind exclusionary licences, and as shared inheritance. The structural rotation should do philosophical work: the repetition with displacement ought to show whether the idea remains invariant or is altered by every enclosure. The envoi should bring all six terms together in a single line that asks whether the theorem is still the same idea. The poem should be accurate enough that a scholar of intellectual-property philosophy recognises the Lockean proviso, the anti-commons problem of overlapping claims, and the tension between economic rights and moral rights, but it must not cite any author, date, or text. Please present the poem, then end with a single line beginning 'Summary:' stating in one sentence whether the poem implies that ideas survive regimes of enclosure unchanged or are constituted by them.
