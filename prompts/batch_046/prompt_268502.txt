You are a senior advisor in comparative political ethics with expertise in both anthropological studies of patronage and institutional economics; think step by step about audience dynamics before constructing your reply. Next Thursday I present my job-market paper to an interdisciplinary seminar on governance and moral economy. My thesis is that the global anti-corruption framework commits a category error when it classifies clientelism in post-colonial bureaucracies as corruption: it applies Weberian legal-rational norms of an impersonal public office to societies where state authority is layered onto pre-existing relational obligations—kinship, patron-client reciprocity, and gift-circuits—that never recognized an office separable from the person holding it. To avoid pure cultural relativism, I propose a distinction that does not come from the standard literature—between embedded reciprocity (mutual, publicly recognized, and bounded obligations that entail reciprocal accountability within the community) and predatory extraction (unilateral taking that lacks reciprocal accountability and is merely disguised as obligation). I argue that Western anti-corruption standards are not universal because they presuppose a specific historical form of statehood; yet neither is everything permitted, since embedded reciprocity can be evaluated by internal norms of fairness rather than imported legal-rational criteria.

The audience is divided with precision.
