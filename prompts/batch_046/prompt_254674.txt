As a journalist who covers science and conceptual reasoning, I am helping design a workshop for an interdisciplinary graduate seminar—twelve students in anthropology and philosophy of science, none with excavation experience, meeting for a single ninety-minute session—on how institutional beliefs are reconstructed from fragmentary material traces. The idea should emerge through action, not lecture: please design a tabletop simulation in which teams of three or four receive identical sets of deliberately ambiguous artifact cards—broken ceramic profiles, stratigraphic diagrams that contradict each other, and carbon-dated layers paired with ethnographic analogues that point in opposite directions—and must collectively vote, round by round, on whether the site represents a hierarchical ritual cult, a decentralized exchange network, or a seasonal domestic cycle. The rules should force participants to lock in an interpretive framework before all cards are revealed, using a token-based scoring system that rewards consistency across rounds but imposes escalating penalties for premature closure, so that the philosophical tension between parsimony and explanatory richness becomes an immediate, felt pressure rather than an abstract thesis.

Crucially, inject a slightly unconventional framing into the mechanics: treat the artifacts not as passive data points but as material arguments, meaning that specific physical properties—fracture patterns, wear marks, and spatial clustering—actively constrain which hypotheses are logically permissible, and build this into the turn structure so that drawing a particular card either opens or closes interpretive pathways regardless of the team’s preference. Provide exact rules, turn order, the payoff or tally scheme (using only paper tokens, never real money), and a debrief script that maps each decision point onto a distinct inference problem—reasoning from negative evidence, analogical projection, and the hermeneutic circle—while being frank about which feature, likely the selection of background analogues, the simulation cannot reproduce and I must supply verbally as facilitator. Also predict the most probable failure mode the first time it is run—whether groups converge too quickly on one narrative, misread the physical-constraint mechanics, or treat absence of evidence as decisive—and suggest a concrete adjustment that preserves immersion. The workshop grew out of a recent conversation with an archaeologist colleague and out of my own reading for a feature on scientific inference; keep the language accessible to non-specialists but precise enough that participants leave feeling the force of archaeological underdetermination in their own judgments.
