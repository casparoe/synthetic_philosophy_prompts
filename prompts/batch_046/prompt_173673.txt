I've been treating Buridan's ass as a limit case for rational preference: if preferences are complete and transitive, then two strictly equivalent options should yield indeterminacy, because no comparative reason selects one over the other. On this reading, Buridan's appeal to the will's power to choose either option looks like an admission that practical reason contains an ineliminable gap—something like incommensurability—rather than a deterministic ordering. What confuses me is whether this gap is meant to be formal, in the sense that it violates the completeness axiom that modern choice theory requires, or metaphysical, in the sense that it concerns the causal efficacy of the will when reasons are balanced. If it's the former, then Buridan seems to anticipate not just the problem of ties but also the kind of aggregation difficulty Arrow's theorem raises—since any rule that breaks ties introduces an arbitrary ordering element. If it's the latter, then treating the paradox as an anticipation of social-choice impossibility results is a category mistake. Which interpretation is defensible, and does the standard resolution—allowing the will to break ties arbitrarily—actually preserve rational completeness by relocating the selection mechanism outside the preference ordering itself?
