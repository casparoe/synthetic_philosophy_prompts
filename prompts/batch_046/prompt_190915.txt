I'm seeking the established term, if there is one, for the following argumentative configuration in debates about space, motion, and inertia. One begins with Newton's rotating bucket: the water climbs the walls, yet the relative positions of water and bucket do not change, so the curvature seems to require absolute rotation or an absolute spatial framework. The relationist reply observes that in an otherwise empty universe the water might remain flat, and that in our universe the effect tracks the distribution of distant matter rather than any invisible substratum. Proponents treat this as rescuing relationism, but critics reply that it merely substitutes one absolute structure — a global field or the total distribution of matter — for another, so that local inertia is determined globally rather than relationally in any robust sense. I am aware that Mach's principle is adjacent to this discussion, yet Mach's principle is typically read as a claim about the causal origin or explanation of inertia in distant masses. The configuration I have in mind is stronger and more metaphysical: it asserts that local rotational phenomena are literally constituted by relations to distant bodies, not merely caused by them. Does this constitutive claim have its own designation? How exactly does it differ from standard Machian relationism, and does it allow the bucket argument to retain its original force against relationism, or does it collapse the distinction between relationism and a disguised form of substantivalism about the cosmic distribution?
