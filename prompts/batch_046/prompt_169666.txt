I was reflecting on the philosophical justification for patents and copyrights after a seminar discussion on whether abstract ideas can genuinely be owned, and I have constructed an argument that seems to demonstrate not merely that intellectual property is inefficient, but that the very concept of owning an idea involves a category mistake. The argument runs as follows. (1) To own X, one must be able to exclude others from X without thereby destroying X. (2) Physical property satisfies this condition: locking a door excludes others while the house remains intact. (3) Ideas, however, are non-rivalrous and non-excludable in themselves; excluding someone from an idea requires either preventing them from knowing it (secrecy) or preventing them from acting on knowledge they already possess (legal prohibition of use). (4) Secrecy is incompatible with the standard patent bargain, in which public disclosure is exchanged for temporary monopoly rights, so disclosure-based ownership collapses if exclusion depends solely on concealment. (5) Preventing a person from using an idea they have independently discovered requires regulating their mental states or internal cognitive operations, rather than merely controlling their interaction with an external object. (6) But regulating another person’s mental states is not a form of property exclusion; it is an exercise of cognitive domination over a subject, not a right over an object. (7) Therefore, applying property logic to ideas transforms the right from a right over a thing into a right over other people’s cognition. (8) Since rights over others’ cognition are not property rights but relations of control over persons, the concept of idea-ownership collapses into a category error. (9) Yet if this reasoning is sound, then not only patents but also copyright, trade secrets, and attribution rights become conceptually incoherent, which seems far too destructive to accept. (10) Hence the argument must go wrong somewhere.

I feel the premises hang together uncomfortably well, and I am particularly reluctant to abandon (1) or (6), because they seem to capture something essential about the nature of property and about the moral difference between controlling an object and controlling a mind. If I must reject a step, I want to understand precisely what I am paying: am I committing myself to the possibility of legitimate cognitive domination, or am I blurring the boundary between owning a thing and governing a person? Could you diagnose which step is weakest, explain why it nevertheless looked plausible, and then set out the costs of rejecting it—particularly for anyone who wishes to preserve both a robust distinction between personal and property rights and some form of legal protection for innovation?
