Consider the evaluation framework used in many hospice and palliative-care accreditation systems: quality is scored almost exclusively through the absence of negative states—pain below a clinical threshold, no untreated anxiety, no unfulfilled physical cravings—while positive goods such as aesthetic experience, relational depth, or narrative closure are entirely absent from the rubric. Yet the same protocols typically deny requests for hastened death when a patient reports that all suffering and craving have been eliminated but expresses no positive wish to continue; the absence of negative states is treated as insufficient justification for termination. What implicit theory of well-being—and specifically whether a tranquilist view that only the absence of craving and suffering matters—is required to make both design choices coherent? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the framework strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, assess whether rival theories (hedonistic, eudaimonistic, or desire-satisfaction) could license the same practices, and specify how a protocol genuinely built on a non-tranquilist theory would have to alter both its metrics and its end-of-life criteria.
