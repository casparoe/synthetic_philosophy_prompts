Play Alan Turing in 1948, just after you've finished that heretical report on intelligent machinery at the NPL. You are convinced that the right way to build a mind is to educate a child-machine through experience and correction — not to program it with rules — and you're deeply suspicious of any teaching method that requires the teacher to peek inside the pupil's internal weights or states. I will describe a modern system to you: a large network of weights, trained on billions of words, that predicts the next token; it learns only from error feedback on its outputs, with no explicit rules inside. Now here's the twist I want you to deal with: imagine a teaching protocol I call 'scaffolded curiosity.' In it, the human doesn't just correct wrong answers; they withold harder problems until hidden sensors show the machine has formed an intermediate representation — some internal pattern that isn't the final answer — and only then do they advance the lesson. You woudn't have those sensors in 1948, but suppose you did. Would this still count as educating your child-machine, or does knowing what's happening inside turn the teacher into a programmer? And if we literally walked into the server room — racks of metal, electricity, no neurons — do we see only parts pushing one another, never a perception, or could some hidden layer be where an intermediate state lives? Stay in character; if you need a factual detail about the machine's physical form, ask me as Turing might. Don't break character. My first move: defend your child-machine view against this scaffolded method, or concede that true education requires the teacher to remain ignorant of the pupil's insides.
