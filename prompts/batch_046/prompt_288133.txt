I'm preparing an essay that reads Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day through the philosophical debate about Orwellian versus Stalinesque revisions to conscious experience, particularly as that debate is framed by the colour phi phenomenon and questions about the timing of experience. In this debate, an Orwellian revision is one in which an initially veridical experience is stored and then altered in memory—like Winston Smith rewriting old newspapers—so that the subject genuinely had one experience and now recalls another. A Stalinesque revision, by contrast, is post-hoc fabrication: the brain never registers the true event at all, but constructs a false experiential record after the fact, much as a Stalinist show trial invented a history that no witness had actually seen. The colour phi illusion—where a dot that changes colour as it moves is experienced as having changed colour at an intermediate location—raises the parallel question for perception: does consciousness first register the correct colour and position and then correct the record in storage, or does it assemble the scene retroactively, so that the experience of the intermediate colour change never occurs in real time?

Read the novel through this lens. Treat Stevens's first-person retrospective narration—his slow, often contradictory reconstruction of his years serving Lord Darlington and his relationship with Miss Kenton—as a kind of autobiographical colour phi. Focus particularly on the drive to Cornwall, the final meeting on the pier, and the moment Stevens confronts the full extent of Lord Darlington's political sympathies. Where does Stevens's account behave like an Orwellian revision, preserving an original emotional or moral truth that he subsequently suppresses in order to maintain his professional self-image? Where does it behave like a Stalinesque fabrication, generating a coherent narrative of dignity and loyalty that may never have corresponded to any lived experience in the first place?

Consider also a more specific framing: treat Stevens's ideal of dignity not merely as a social mask but as a binding mechanism analogous to the perceptual binding that integrates colour and motion in the phi illusion. If dignity operates to weave disparate emotional responses, social obligations, and personal losses into a single, temporally ordered autobiography, does this imply that autobiographical consciousness is inherently postdictive—that the self who experiences and the self who remembers are never the same temporal subject? Or does Ishiguro resist this reading by showing moments—Miss Kenton's tears in the servants' quarters, Stevens's own involuntary reactions during the drive—where the binding fails and an unedited experience breaks through?

Finally, say where the work complicates or advances the philosophical debate itself. Does Stevens's case suggest that the Orwellian-Stalinesque distinction collapses in first-person memory, because there is no original emotional record independent of the narrative that integrates it? Or does the novel imply that the timing of experience carries ethical weight—that to revise one's past is not only a cognitive error but a moral failure of a particular kind?
