Write an eight-minute opening address for the inaugural session of an interdisciplinary workshop on the boundary between legend and history. The audience is mixed: archaeologists specializing in Late Bronze Age collapse and Levantine settlement patterns, medievalists tracking Arthurian manuscript transmission, biblical scholars engaged with the Exodus traditions, and philosophers of history. Your address should frame the workshop's guiding question—at what point does a legend become history?—by introducing and defending a specific conceptual distinction between two modes of historical corroboration: corroboration of the narrated event itself, and corroboration of the explanatory framework that the legend supplies for otherwise anomalous material or textual traces. Apply this distinction systematically to three cases: the Trojan War, the King Arthur traditions, and the Exodus. For each case, indicate how the archaeological, textual, or comparative evidence relates differently to event-corroboration and framework-corroboration, and show why conflating the two has led to recurring disciplinary misunderstandings. The speech must be intelligible to non-specialists or guests who might be present, so any technical term—from philosophy of history or source criticism—must be introduced with an immediate plain-language gloss. At the same time, the conceptual distinction should do genuine structural work: it should determine what kinds of questions are appropriate to ask about each tradition and what kinds of answers would actually resolve them. Do not provide a reading list, cite specific scholars by name, or invoke contested archaeological conclusions as settled fact; rather, treat the current state of dispute in each field as part of the problem you are framing. Avoid decorative references to mythology; the legend-to-history transition should be the organizing principle of the entire address.
