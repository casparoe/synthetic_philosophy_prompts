My friend and I have been arguing about Friedman's F-twist in the context of macroeconomic growth models, and I have ended up defending what I call the structural insulation thesis: unrealistic assumptions about agent rationality and perfect information are not merely tolerable but methodologically indispensable, because rendering them realistic does not just inject noise but introduces confounding variables that lie definitionally outside the model's intended explanatory target. In other words, idealization here is not approximation but abstraction, and attempts to "behavioralize" these models destroy the very isolation of causal mechanisms they are built to achieve. Tell me what would genuinely count against this thesis—not the generic complaint that unrealistic models sometimes fail empirically, but which specific conceptual distinctions, analogies from other modeling practices, or formal results would be decisive. Which objections would a committed defender concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they dismiss as category errors? Rank the defeaters from fatal—say, showing that the insulation thesis collapses into either naive instrumentalism or a rival account of scientific explanation—to merely awkward. I am especially interested in whether there is an internal tension in claiming that abstraction must be protected from realism, while still insisting that the model's predictions must be tested against a reality that includes precisely the behavioral complexity being abstracted away.
