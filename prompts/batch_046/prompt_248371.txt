A philosophy blog summarized Descartes's cogito like this: in the Meditations, Descartes reaches the certainty that he exists not through an inference but through an immediate intuition available to anyone who doubts; nevertheless, the argument presupposes the general principle that whatever thinks exists, which is why Hobbes's counterexample—if one walks, then one exists—fails only if walking lacks the same immediate presence to consciousness that thinking enjoys. The summary adds that the cogito proves only that a thinking thing exists at the instant of doubt, carrying no commitment to temporal persistence, bodily existence, or substantiality; in particular, Descartes allegedly refrains from calling himself a substance in that moment and describes himself as nothing but a thinking thing, leaving all metaphysical weight—immateriality, the soul's nature, even the framework for other minds—to be carried by later proofs involving God and clear and distinct perception. The blogger concludes that reading the cogito as a modest epistemic checkpoint rather than a metaphysical foundation is the only interpretation that respects the text's actual progression.

Something feels off about this account—particularly around whether the general principle is genuinely presupposed, whether Descartes really withholds substantiality in the Second Meditation, and whether the proof's modesty extends as far as the blogger claims. Is this a fair summary? Please distinguish what is accurate, what is an acceptable simplification for a general audience, and what subtly distorts Descartes's position, with particular attention to the debate over inference versus intuition in the Second Replies, the status of res cogitans in Meditation II, and whether the proof itself carries any implicit metaphysical commitments about the self's nature.
