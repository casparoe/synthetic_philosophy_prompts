Defend the position that life should be defined by organizational closure rather than by reproduction, metabolism, or evolutionary adaptation, but argue further that organizational closure itself divides into two kinds: autopoietic closure, in which a system produces and maintains its own boundary conditions, and heteropoietic closure, in which a system maintains stable internal dynamics only through continuous external scaffolding. Apply this distinction to argue that synthetic protocell arrays—membrane-bounded chemical networks that replicate their lipid structures but require an engineered nutrient feed—lack genuine life despite displaying growth and replication, whereas dormant bacterial spores, which suspend internal dynamics but retain the intrinsic capacity to re-establish autopoietic boundaries, do count as alive. Extend the analysis to large-scale coupled ecological-technological systems, such as integrated bioreactor-sensor networks that maintain chemical homeostasis across diffuse spatial boundaries without a discrete organismal membrane, and explain why these systems should be classified as heteropoietic rather than living. Address the objection that this distinction merely encodes an arbitrary preference for carbon-based biochemistry, and show why the capacity for intrinsic boundary-production, rather than substrate composition, is the philosophically relevant criterion. Keep the argument conceptual and self-contained: do not invoke empirical citations or reading lists, but reason directly from the structural properties of the three borderline cases.
