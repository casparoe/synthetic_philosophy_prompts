I am drafting notes for an informal seminar that attempts to apply the structural logic of jumping out of the system—JOOTSing—to normative ethics, using the MU-puzzle as a formal model for critical reflection. A study partner walked me through the following chain of reasoning, which I now find suspicious because its conclusion seems to render moral reasoning either hopelessly provisional or arbitrarily dogmatic. (1) In the formal system associated with the MU-puzzle, the string MU is constructible using only the system’s internal rules, yet its non-derivability can only be recognized from an external vantage point. (2) By strict analogy, any coherent moral framework N contains normative claims that are fully expressible in N’s vocabulary but not justifiable by N’s internal principles—call these normative MU-strings. (3) To identify and correct such blind spots, a rational agent must step outside N into a meta-framework M that supplies the missing justification rules. (4) But M will contain its own normative MU-strings, requiring a further framework M′, and so on, generating an infinite regress of meta-moral evaluation. (5) To block the regress we must posit a terminal framework T that is self-certifying and free of internal blind spots. (6) Yet any rational evaluation of T’s adequacy requires adopting rules that are external to T, which is impossible without abandoning T; hence T is immune to scrutiny and acceptance of it is arbitrary rather than reasoned. (7) Therefore moral reasoning either collapses into an endless hierarchy of provisional frameworks or terminates in unexamined dogma—both outcomes being unacceptable. (8) So the initial analogy between formal undecidability and moral justification must fail at some point. I am especially reluctant to surrender (1) and (3), since they seem to capture something genuine about self-reference and the need for critical distance, but I am unsure whether the real damage is done by (2)’s analogical leap, by (4)’s assumption that every meta-level inherits the same structural defect, or by (6)’s equation of self-certification with arbitrariness. Could you diagnose the weakest step, explain why it initially looked plausible, and price the main alternatives—breaking the analogy, accepting the infinite regress, or embracing a self-certifying terminus—telling me what each costs in terms of moral epistemology?
