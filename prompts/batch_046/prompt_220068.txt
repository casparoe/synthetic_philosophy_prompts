Quine's attack on the analytic-synthetic distinction rested on the claim that attempts to ground analyticity—whether through convention, synonymy, or semantic rules—either presuppose the distinction they aim to explain or collapse into empirical psychology. Quine died in 2000, before the development of modern large language models. Suppose he had lived to examine systems in which logical entailments, mathematical identities, and empirical generalizations are all encoded as continuous statistical weights across a shared neural architecture, with no representational boundary marking necessary truths apart from highly probable ones. Given his holism about belief revision and his skepticism toward any non-circular criterion for analyticity, how do you think Quine would have interpreted this architectural fact? Would he have taken the seamless gradient of model confidence—where a tautology and a well-supported scientific law differ only in weight magnitude and training frequency—as a decisive vindication of his view? Or might he have conceded that, even without a principled philosophical distinction, the functional role of logical truths in inference creates a practical boundary that his earlier framework underestimated?

Furthermore, the rationalist tradition often treats Bayesian updating as a universal mechanism applying equally to logical priors and empirical evidence, implicitly treating the two domains as structurally homogeneous. How would Quine likely have responded to this framing? Would he have seen it as confirming his rejection of two dogmas, or as smuggling back in a disguised version of the analytic-synthetic divide through the back door of probabilistic epistemology?
