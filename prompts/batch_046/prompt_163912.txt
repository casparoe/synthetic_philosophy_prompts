As a journalist covering science and the philosophy of prediction, I have been invited to deliver the fifteen-minute opening address at a workshop called "The Half-Life of Expertise." The audience is split: senior researchers in climate modeling and epidemiology, who have built careers on inside-view judgments about their own fields, and policy staffers and graduate students who know these debates mainly through headline statistics and funding announcements. The organizers want the speech to treat the inside view versus the outside view—not as a slogan but as a structural constraint on what I can honestly say. I must honor the researchers' granular knowledge without pretending that the base rate for ambitious interdisciplinary reform is encouraging; I must also avoid dissolving into generic calls for "humility" that leave the room with nothing to do. There is an additional framing constraint: I want the speech to expose a specific conceptual failure mode—the defensive switch of reference classes when an outside-view base rate threatens an inside-view commitment, a maneuver that mimics reasoning but actually insulates belief. This move should be named, made visible, and shown to undermine genuine epistemic modesty rather than serve it. At the same time, the address must leave both halves of the room with a reason to continue the work that brought them there, not with ironic detachment. The occasion is being held in an institution named after a scientist whose own career illustrates the problem—her inside-view certainty about one predictive framework led her to dismiss an outside-view warning that later proved accurate—though I should not identify her by name or depend on biographical details that require citation. Please write the speech so that the philosophical tension shapes every paragraph: what can be claimed, what must be withheld, and how the speaker positions herself relative to both the experts and the newcomers. Include at least one moment that lands at different depths for the two audiences, and do not include bibliographies, citations, suggested readings, or requests for further sources.
