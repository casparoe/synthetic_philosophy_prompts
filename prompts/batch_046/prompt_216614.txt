I'm drafting a seminar paper on the West German Historikerstreit of 1986–1989—specifically Ernst Nolte's June 1986 Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung essay comparing the Holocaust to Stalinist terror, Jürgen Habermas's defense of Auschwitz's singularity, and the renewed controversy when Nolte was awarded the Konrad Adenauer Prize in 2000—and I want to treat the dispute not as conventional intellectual history but as an institutional episode to be analyzed through Zygmunt Bauman's thesis in Modernity and the Holocaust. Bauman argues that the genocide was not an atavistic breakdown but an outcome of modern bureaucratic rationality, functional division of labor, and instrumental moral distancing; I want to see whether that framework illuminates the structure of the debate itself.

Can you map Bauman's concepts onto the actors and positions in the Streit? Identify who functions as the bureaucratic agent, the technical comparator, and the moral-distance manager: does Nolte's comparative framing—treating Auschwitz and the Gulag as technically commensurable instances of totalitarian violence—exemplify the very rationalization and moral distancing Bauman describes, or does it resist it? Conversely, does Habermas's insistence on uniqueness restore the moral proximity that modernity destroys, or does it operate as another form of containment—using singularity to cordon off the event from normal historical causality and thereby bureaucratize German memory? I want a disciplined account of what Bauman's model predicts about the polarization, who had standing to decide, and what counts as evidence in this framework, followed by an honest assessment of where the model is silent: for instance, whether the intensity of the public conflict contradicts Bauman's picture of modernity's smooth moral anesthesia, and whether the uniqueness claim can be read as a strategic mechanism for managing collective guilt rather than as a genuine ethical refusal of comparison.

Finally, what does the episode in turn show about Bauman's theory? Does the fact that modern West German institutions could generate such a fierce, non-bureaucratic moral conflict over memory confirm that modern rationalization also produces its own disruptions, or does it reveal that Bauman underestimated the capacity of modern public spheres to turn rationalization back upon itself? I'm particularly interested in whether the comparability versus uniqueness debate should be understood not merely as a disagreement about historical facts but as a struggle over the kind of moral distance a modern democratic state requires in order to function.
