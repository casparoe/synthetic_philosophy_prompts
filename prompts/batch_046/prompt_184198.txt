Public agencies and economists routinely use revealed-preference methods—market behavior, willingness-to-pay studies, and choice data—to estimate welfare changes for environmental goods, transportation projects, and ordinary consumer products. At the same time, those same institutions systematically refuse to apply revealed-preference measurement to goods and choices they classify differently: decisions made in deep ignorance of one's future tastes, and goods whose value is said to depend on actually undergoing an experience rather than on anticipating it. Reconstruct the philosophical commitments about utility that this selective practice presupposes. Does it treat utility as a stable mental state that choice behavior tracks, or as something constructed in the experience itself? Does it require that preferences be authoritative only when they are well-informed, and if so, what conception of information and personal identity is built into that condition? Say whether the practice is philosophically consistent—whether it quietly combines a behaviorist theory of utility in one domain with a phenomenological or constructivist theory in another—or whether several rival theories of utility could license the same division. Finally, describe what a uniform policy would have to assume if it either extended revealed preference to all domains or abandoned it everywhere, and assess whether either uniform view is defensible.
