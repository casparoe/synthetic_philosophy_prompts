ok so in my philosophy seminar people keep leaning on this immune-system anology to push back against the situationist critique of virtue ethics. thier mapping is: moral character is like immune competence, extreme situations (milgram-style authority pressure, or the good samaritan rush) are like overwhelming pathogen loads, and the fact that people fail in those experiments is like a healthy person still getting sick in a plague -- it doesn't prove there's no immune system, just that the environment exceeded capacity. they use this to argue that situationists confuse trait not expressed with trait doesn't exist, and that virtue ethics survives if we just understand traits as dispositional but context-sensitive.

i want a full audit. lay out the correspondence table explicitly -- what plays the role of antibodies, of the pathogen, of the clinical test, of the baseline health metric? then tell me where the mapping actually breaks: for instance, immune function has independent biochemical markers, but with virtue we seem to only have the behavioral output itself, so there's no analog to measuring white blood cell counts without infection. also, does the anology quietly import the idea that extreme situations are unnatural testing conditions, and is that fair given that bureaucratic pressure or time urgency are pretty ordinary?

finally, does the anology actually rescue virtue ethics, or does it just redescribe the situationist data in medical language? and if it's doing hidden work, what's a less loaded anology -- maybe comparing character to skill at chess, where board conditions matter but don't erase the player's ability?
