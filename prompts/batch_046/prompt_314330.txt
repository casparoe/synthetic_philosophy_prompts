I'm writing a dissertation chapter on the normative architecture of effective altruism and its internal fault lines, and I want to stress-test three interlocking commitments through a recent episode. The commitments are: the institutional critique, according to which mainstream financial and philanthropic institutions are structurally corrupt or inefficient and should be bypassed in favor of direct, rational allocation; the "earning to give" strategy, which licenses high-risk, high-reward accumulation in conventional sectors precisely because the returns can be redirected to more effective ends; and the measurability bias, which treats quantifiable donation flows and outcome metrics as primary proxies for moral justification.

Please analyze the collapse of FTX in late 2022 and the subsequent revelations about Sam Bankman-Fried's philanthropic commitments through the lens of these three commitments. I want a disciplined conceptual mapping: identify the relevant actors (Bankman-Fried, FTX customers and creditors, the EA-affiliated organizations that received pledged funds, regulators), specify what options and incentives each actor faced under the three commitments, and say which features of the collapse each commitment explains well and which it misdescribes or ignores. In particular, I'm interested in whether the episode reveals a hidden dependency—that earning to give in practice requires the very institutional credibility, regulatory compliance, and banking relationships that the institutional critique disavows—and whether the measurability bias made it easier to treat the quantifiable pipeline of pledged donations as a proxy for moral legitimacy, thereby obscuring the structural fragility of the earning mechanism itself.

Also address where the framework is silent rather than forcing it to speak. For instance, how should the theory assess the moral status of the customers whose funds were misappropriated? Does the fact that some donations actually reached recipients retroactively justify the earning strategy, or does it show that the strategy never had the resources to survive a credibility collapse? And is this episode best read as an anomaly that effective altruism never anticipated, as a direct refutation of one of the three commitments, or as evidence that the three form an unstable triad that was always prone to collapse under weak oversight? Be explicit when the mapping breaks down; I want conceptual precision about the tension between structural disavowal and structural dependence, not a predetermined verdict. I am not asking for references or a bibliography—just a careful case analysis that runs in both directions, with the episode testing the theory as much as the theory illuminates the episode.
