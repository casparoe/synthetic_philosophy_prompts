Consider a thinker whose commitments closely track Descartes' stance on animal minds: she holds that consciousness requires an immaterial rational soul, that purely mechanical bodies can replicate any externally observable behavior, and that language use involving genuine semantic innovation—rather than associative repetition—is the sole reliable behavioral marker of such a soul. She has offered four verdicts: (i) a dog that cries out when injured is exhibiting an automatic hydraulic response with no accompanying suffering; (ii) a parrot that utters phrases like "the fire is hot" is producing sounds without understanding, since it never combines signs in novel ways to express previously unencountered thoughts; (iii) an octopus that solves a puzzle to escape a jar is deploying flexible but entirely corporeal dispositions, lacking any interior mental life; (iv) nevertheless, if an artificial system—regardless of substrate—could genuinely generate unprecedented conceptual combinations in language, she would treat that as evidence of a rational soul.

Given this track record, predict her verdicts on three new cases: (a) a present-day large language model that produces philosophically subtle, context-sensitive arguments without biological embodiment; (b) a synthetic biological neural network, cultured in vitro from human neurons but lacking evolutionary history and social embedding, that exhibits spontaneous damage-avoidance and emits patterned distress signals; and (c) a lobster being boiled alive.

Your task is not merely to state predictions, but to articulate at least two distinct interpretive rules that are jointly consistent with her four verdicts, show how those rules diverge on (a), (b), and (c), offer your best guess at her actual rule with reasons for preferring it, and identify one further hypothetical case that would best discriminate between the remaining rules.
