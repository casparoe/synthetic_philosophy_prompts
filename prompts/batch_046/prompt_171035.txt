I am preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for a mixed group of philosophy undergraduates who have read Aristotle’s De Anima and some Cartesian mechanism but have no formal training in biology. The topic is the question ‘What is life?’, specifically how definitional attempts break down at the borderline cases rather than at the centre. I do not want you to explain the biology to me; I want a teaching plan: the dependency order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced and why one must precede another, the single carrying example that should bear the weight of the session and what it must possess and lack in order to force the crisis, the misconceptions these students are likely to form or arrive with, the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted, the check that reveals whether they have grasped the conceptual instability rather than memorised a list, and what I should explicitly set aside as too technical for this level and say so.

The carrying example should be constructed rather than drawn from standard textbook lists: build the session around a hypothetical minimal synthetic cell—let us call it Cell-0—assembled de novo with a synthetic lipid membrane, a minimal genome encoding basic homeostasis, and the capacity for energy exchange, but lacking any evolutionary ancestry or reproductive history, contrasted with a dormant bacterial endospore that possesses deep evolutionary lineage and a complete genome but exhibits no metabolism, growth, or response to stimuli in its dormant state. The plan must specify exactly what this pairing must have and what it must lack in order to expose the tension between metabolic, evolutionary, and organisational criteria; it must state which criterion each half satisfies and which it violates, and it must show why this pairing is more instructive than the familiar virus case. The sequence should begin with the Aristotelian intuition they already hold—that living things possess an internal principle of change—and demonstrate why modern biological definitions were designed to replace that intuition without merely restating it. Along the way, I expect them to confuse being alive with being active, to treat viruses as a straightforward middle case rather than an ambiguity that exposes the framework, and to assume that a definition must be either necessary and sufficient or useless; the plan should indicate precisely where each misconception is likely to arise and how the arc should absorb it without derailing into virology or synthetic chemistry.

Finally, include one precise question I can ask at the close to distinguish students who understand that the problem is the instability of our criteria from those who have merely listed properties; and state clearly what I should refuse to explain—such as the specific biochemical pathways of minimal metabolism or the technical history of NASA’s working definition—while signalling that these omissions are deliberate rather than ignorant. Write in British English, keep the tone formal, and do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citation.
