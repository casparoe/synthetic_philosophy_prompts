I am currently enrolled in an advanced high school ethics course, and after reading selections from Thomas Aquinas on political authority and resistance, I have become preoccupied with a tension my teacher raised only in passing: Aquinas maintains that private citizens may not kill a tyrant because private judgment usurps public authority and risks disorder, yet he permits resistance by the community or its legitimate representatives when a ruler abuses power that was rightfully his. To test whether this distinction is genuinely stable, my class constructed a carefully bounded hypothetical case. Consider a legitimate monarch who governs justly in every other respect but issues one narrowly targeted edict ordering the execution of all members of a specific religious minority confined to a single province; the provincial governor, who possesses legitimate delegated authority, refuses to enforce the edict and publicly calls for resistance, but the central judiciary and military remain loyal to the crown. With institutional resistance thus paralyzed and the executions imminent, a private citizen within the province assassinates the monarch solely to prevent the atrocity.

Write an essay that examines whether this assassination can be justified on strictly Aquinian principles—specifically, his distinctions between usurpation and the abuse of legitimate authority, his claim that law must serve the common good to bind in conscience, and his prohibition on private violence—without appealing to modern rights-based frameworks he would not recognize. You should weigh the argument that the provincial governor’s refusal activates a legitimate public authority, thereby allowing the citizen to act as its instrument in deposing the ruler, against the counter-argument that the governor’s power is merely executive rather than sovereign, so the citizen’s act remains unauthorized sedition regardless of the crime’s severity. Reach and justify a clear conclusion: either the case falls within Aquinas’s narrow exception for resistance by public authority, or it confirms that no private exception exists even when a legitimate ruler issues an isolated but catastrophic command. In your conclusion, address whether introducing a “threshold of imminent atrocity” alters Aquinas’s framework or merely makes explicit an implicit condition already governing his account of law, authority, and the common good.
