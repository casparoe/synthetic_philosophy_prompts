I'm drafting an essay for a seminar on practical rationality, and I'd appreciate your comments on its central conceptual move. The essay takes up the absent-minded driver problem: a driver intends to take exit 42, knows he is prone to absent-mindedness, and considers tying a string around his finger as a reminder, yet worries that if he is attentive enough to notice the string he will already be attentive enough to take the exit, while if he is too absent-minded he will miss the string as well. Rather than treating this as a straightforward puzzle about the expected utility of reminders, I argue that we should distinguish ordinary prospective forgetting—failing to recall an intended action—from what I call "plan-boundary erosion," a failure in which the driver retains both the exit-plan and the reminder-plan but temporarily misassigns the reminder to a different intentional framework (say, treating the string as part of his grocery-shopping routine rather than his driving route). On my view, the absent-minded driver is not suffering primarily from a deficit in memory storage but from an instability in the hierarchical framing that binds sub-plans to a master intention.

If this distinction holds, then standard decision-theoretic responses—precommitment devices, altering one's environment, or computing the cost of failure—address the wrong target, because they assume the agent will recognize the reminder as belonging to the present plan. I suggest that the real problem is closer to what philosophers of mind call the frame problem: the driver lacks a robust mechanism for maintaining the salience-structure of his current intention-set against competing background routines. I then ask whether any non-cognitive remedy—like a physical string—can solve a framing failure, or whether the solution requires something like an explicit "plan-tag" that re-anchors the reminder to the driving context. The essay concludes by tentatively proposing that practical rationality may need a theory of "intentional framing maintenance" parallel to theories of belief revision, though I am unsure whether this imports issues from philosophy of mind that do not belong in action theory.

Could you comment specifically on whether "plan-boundary erosion" genuinely differs from ordinary absent-mindedness, and whether importing the frame problem into practical reasoning is illuminating or a category mistake? I also want to know if my critique of precommitment strategies rests on a confused premise, or if the task as posed—evaluating a reminder's efficacy when the failure mode is framing rather than recall—cannot be done well without further empirical assumptions about attention. If the essay's framing is misguided, if the distinction collapses under scrutiny, or if I have mischaracterized the standard literature on the problem, please say so plainly rather than offering polite suggestions for revision.
