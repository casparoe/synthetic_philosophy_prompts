In a seminar on cross-cultural philosophy of mind I found myself defending a view that goes beyond the familiar claim that folk-psychological ascription is universal. I hold that the near-ubiquity of attributing beliefs, desires, and intentions across radically different societies doesn't merely show that this framework is widespread, but that it enjoys a kind of epistemic dominance over rival explanatory schemes. By this I mean that frameworks which reject or bypass inner mental causes—say, relational ontologies that distribute agency across kin networks, or ritual-causal models that explain behavior through ceremonial efficacy rather than representational states—are not genuine epistemic competitors on equal footing. Rather, they are either ultimately translatable into folk-psychological descriptions without explanatory loss, or they occupy a subordinate role: they may describe behavior, but they cannot correct, override, or replace the predictions and explanations generated by belief-desire attribution. This is stronger than simple universality; it is a hierarchical claim about the standing of explanatory frameworks, and it implies that any genuinely competing account must eventually answer to the same predictive and corrective standards that folk psychology sets.

I'm trying to be rigorous about what would actually dislodge me. What specific observations, formal arguments, or empirical findings would count against the thesis that universal ascription entails this kind of dominance? I want the defeaters laid out and ranked—from fatal (the kind that would force abandonment) to merely awkward (the kind that just demands qualification)—including what predictions this dominance claim makes that genuinely rival frameworks do not. I also want an honest assessment of whether the view can be insulated by redefining "translation," "dominance," or "explanatory loss" so that no conceivable counterexample could ever reach it, and if so, whether such insulation is a theoretical virtue or a defect for a claim of this kind. Please distinguish between the defeaters that critics would naturally emphasize and those that a committed defender of the view should themselves concede as genuinely counting against it. Where possible, point to the kind of cross-cultural case or conceptual result that would best adjudicate between these. Write your response in American English.
