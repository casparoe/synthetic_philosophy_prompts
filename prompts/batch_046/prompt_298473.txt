I am drafting a proposal for a one-semester philosophy-of-science independent study that sits at the intersection of mechanistic interpretability and the ontology of mechanisms. The half-formed idea is this: in mechanistic anomaly detection, researchers typically flag a neural network as anomalous when its internal causal structure—say, a specific circuit topology or activation pathway—deviates from a baseline “normal” model. This practice seems to presuppose that being the same mechanism requires sharing parts or organizational patterns at a fairly concrete implementation level. But the philosophy-of-science literature on mechanisms has long stressed that mechanisms are constituted by their activities and organizational relations, and that they can be multiply realized across different physical substrates. If that is right, then treating a functionally equivalent but structurally different circuit as an anomalous mechanism might be conceptually confused, analogous to denying that an artificial heart is a heart because it lacks cardiac tissue. On the other hand, in safety-critical contexts we might have independent reasons to care about implementation-level divergence even when functional behavior is preserved, which suggests that mechanistic anomaly detection needs something like a two-tier framework—one tier tracking functional mechanism identity, another tracking implementation deviation—rather than a single criterion of sameness.
