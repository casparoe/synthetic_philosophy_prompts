As a novelist who has lately found himself drawn into philosophical conversations about consciousness, I am preparing a ninety-minute workshop for fourteen graduate students on the meta-problem of consciousness—that peculiar difficulty of explaining why we judge there to be an explanatory gap between neural mechanisms and subjective experience, rather than merely documenting the gap itself. I want them not to receive a lecture on David Chalmers's distinction between the hard problem and the meta-problem, but to inhabit it: please design a participatory simulation, with assigned roles and a modest payoff scheme requiring no real money, in which the group must collectively adjudicate first-person reports of qualitative states across three domains—colour perception, bodily pain, and propositional belief.

The rules should be arranged so that the adjudicators apply what they take to be a consistent criterion for when an explanatory gap is warranted, yet the shifting texture of the reports forces them to revise that criterion repeatedly without being able to articulate it in advance; by the end they should feel that they have manufactured the gap through their own classificatory practice rather than discovered it in the world. Include a precise timeline, the role cards, the debrief that maps each revision onto the structure of the meta-problem, an explicit admission of which feature—most likely the transparency or self-intimacy of phenomenal concepts—the exercise cannot reproduce and I must supply myself, and an honest forecast of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I ask for no bibliography, no citation, and no reading list; the force must come from the activity itself. Please present your design in polished, formal British English, with instructions clear enough that a facilitator without specialist training could run the session.
