I'm preparing notes for a graduate seminar on practical reason and temporal identity, and I've become stuck on an institutional pattern that seems philosophically loaded but is rarely examined as a unified practice. In most jurisdictions, a competent adult may execute an advance directive refusing life-sustaining treatment—mechanical ventilation, artificial nutrition, or resuscitation—should she later enter a severely demented or minimally conscious state. Hospitals and courts generally treat this document as binding authority even when the present patient, if she can express any preference at all, appears content and seems to want continued care; the directive is commonly interpreted as the voice of the authentic or true self speaking across time. Yet the same legal systems are far more reluctant to enforce psychiatric Ulysses contracts, in which a patient with a cyclical mental illness pre-authorizes involuntary medication or commitment during a future manic episode, precisely because the future self's expressed dissent is treated as a genuine withdrawal of consent rather than as an alien intrusion. Meanwhile, contract law will often void a financial agreement signed by someone with early-stage cognitive impairment, treating diminished present capacity as undermining the validity of the act, rather than treating an earlier, more lucid signature as permanently authoritative regardless of later change.

What philosophical commitments about personal identity, autonomy, and diachronic authority does this three-part pattern—honoring medical advance directives, hesitating over psychiatric Ulysses contracts, and voiding contracts signed during cognitive decline—presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it require a psychological-continuity view on which the pre-illness person and the demented or manic patient are not the same moral agent? Does it depend on a distinction between welfare and expressed preference that applies differently in irreversible medical contexts than in reversible psychiatric ones? Or can the pattern be explained without any deep metaphysics of identity, perhaps by appeal to the tractability of prediction, the reversibility of intervention, or the social purpose of each institution? Say whether the practice is genuinely consistent—whether the reasons given for honoring the living will in dementia actually extend to the psychiatric case, and whether contract law's treatment of diminished capacity undermines the authority granted to the earlier medical self—or whether different branches of the system quietly presuppose incompatible views of the earlier self's authority. Finally, if we replaced the implicit view with a strictly presentist account of authority, under which only the currently expressing agent can authorize or refuse treatment, what exactly would have to change in hospital ethics-committee deliberation, psychiatric commitment hearings, and contract-adjudication rules? And is there any version of that presentist view that could preserve some but not all of the current practices, or does the institutional pattern stand or fall as a philosophical unit?
