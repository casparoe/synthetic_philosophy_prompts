I am a doctoral student working on the philosophy of historical memory and comparative moral judgement, and I have found myself holding a cluster of intuitions about when comparing historical atrocities is intellectually illuminating and when it becomes morally obscene. I want to know whether these intuitions cohere around a defensible principle, or whether consistency demands that I abandon some of them.

Here are my verdicts.

First, comparing the Holocaust and the Rwandan genocide along specific structural axes — for instance, the role of bureaucratic administration, the deployment of propaganda, or the dynamics of international bystanding — strikes me as not only permissible but necessary for understanding genocide as a phenomenon. However, declaring that one was the greater atrocity solely on the basis of a higher death toll feels obscene, as though the particular horror of each event could be collapsed into a single metric.

Second, comparing the Holodomor and the Great Leap Forward famine with respect to state intentionality — asking whether the famine was directed at destroying a specific national group or was the by-product of an ideological economic programme — seems entirely appropriate. Yet synthesising these comparisons into an all-things-considered judgement that one was morally worse because more people died strikes me as inappropriate, though I am unsure whether my objection targets the synthesis itself or merely the use of mortality as the decisive criterion.

Third, comparing the transatlantic slave trade and the Belgian Congo Free State as systems of racialised colonial extraction and forced labour feels legitimate; examining comparative mortality figures in this context serves historical understanding rather than competitive victimhood. By contrast, placing the slave trade and the Holocaust on the same scale and asking which took more lives or did more evil crosses a line into obscenity for me, even though the Congo comparison involves death tolls of a comparable magnitude.

Fourth, comparing the Cambodian autogenocide and the violent phase of the Cultural Revolution on the dimension of ideological purification — how each regime used terror to restructure society according to a utopian blueprint — strikes me as analytically valuable and not diminishing to either event. Nevertheless, ranking them ordinally to determine the worst atrocity of the twentieth century feels reductive and wrong.

Fifth, and here is the tension I cannot resolve: comparing Hiroshima and Nagasaki with the Holocaust on the axis of deliberate state killing of non-combatant civilians seems to me analytically productive, particularly in seminars on the ethics of total war, and I want to defend this comparison. At the same time, framing the two as competitors on a single scale of historical evil — for instance, asking students to adjudicate which was worse — feels profoundly obscene. I cannot see why the analytic comparison is acceptable but the competitive ranking is not, given that both appear to rely on the same comparative metrics of scale, intentionality, civilian status, and state agency.

My tentative principle is this: comparative judgement is permissible when it operates within a shared moral kind, such as genocide with genocide or colonial extraction with colonial extraction, or when it isolates a single structural dimension without synthesising a total moral score; it becomes obscene when it produces an all-things-considered ordinal ranking, particularly across moral kinds, because this presupposes a commensurability of suffering that denies the particularity of each atrocity.

This principle seems to accommodate my first four verdicts, but it struggles with the Hiroshima/Holocaust pair: they belong to different moral kinds, yet I want to permit analytic comparison on civilian killing while forbidding ordinal ranking. It also does not clearly explain why comparing the Holodomor and the Great Leap is acceptable dimensionally despite one being classified as genocide and the other not. So I ask: is there a coherent account — perhaps distinguishing what I might call modal comparison, that is, how was this evil structured, from ordinal atrocity ranking, that is, which evil was greater — that saves all five verdicts? If not, which of my intuitions should I overturn, and what does the repaired principle predict for a case I have not mentioned, such as comparing the Armenian genocide with the Herero and Namaqua genocide in German South-West Africa on the dimension of colonial administration and denialist aftermath?

Please provide your answer in British English, with precise formulation of any proposed principle, explicit statement of which verdicts it accommodates and which it forces me to drop, predictions for the unlisted case, and no bibliographies or reading lists, as I am working without access to external sources and need a reasoned analysis I can evaluate on its own terms.
