I run a small grad seminar in epistimic logic, about 8 people, 75 minutes. I want an in-class game that makes Fitch's paradox feel like a social trap rather than a modal logic exercise. I want you to inject a niche twist: the "announcement loop" — when a player publicly states that some sealed envelope contains a true proposition that nobody in the room knows, that very speech act changes the epistemic status of the envelope's contents, so the group has to figure out whether they can consistently claim an unknown exists without destroying it by claiming it. This mirrors the original paradox but runs through collective belief and public declaration instead of formal operators.

Design the full activity. Use sealed envelopes with true statements inside, maybe some false ones as distractors. Give rules for how players can verify, declare ignorance, or challenge each other. Set up a payoff scheme using points or reputation — no real money — so that players are incentivized both to verify truths and to preserve at least one "unknown" to win a bonus round, which should force them into the contradiction themselves. They should hit the wall before anyone names Fitch.

Then write the debrief: map the verification rounds, the public declarations, and the contradiction onto the steps of the knowability principle (all truths are knowable, there is an unknown truth, therefore contradiction). Note where each step can be resisted and what it costs. Be frank about what this game can't reproduce — the precise modal distinction between possibility and knowledge — and say the facilitator has to supply that translation explicitely.

Also tell me what will predictably fail the first time — probably someone just rips open every envelope — and how to handle it. Keep it casual, maybe a typo or two, and don't ask for citations or a reading list since the students won't have web access and I don't want them guessing at sources.
