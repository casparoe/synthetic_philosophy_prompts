The Protestant work ethic, as Weber describes it, transforms labor from a mere economic necessity into a religious calling, or Beruf, especially through Calvinist anxiety about predestination, where relentless work serves as a visible sign of election. This explains why modern capitalism retains an ascetic drive long after its religious origins have faded. The Japanese phenomenon of karoshi, death from overwork, clearly extends this same ethic: Japanese workers labor to extreme hours out of a moral obligation to their corporations, treating work as a total identity rather than a contractual exchange, which is precisely what Weber meant by the iron cage of rationalized labor trapping the individual. Meanwhile, the French 35-hour week, introduced in 1998 primarily to redistribute employment, implicitly treats labor as a burden to be minimized rather than a vocation to be embraced. Consequently, reducing the legal work week to thirty-five hours necessarily undermines the moral dignity of labor, since if work is truly a calling one should not seek to limit it by statute; within Weber's framework, leisure possesses no independent moral status and functions only as recuperation for further productive effort. At the same time, Weber observed that rationalization eventually strips labor of its spiritual meaning, leaving only an empty shell, so karoshi might actually represent this hollowed-out iron cage rather than the authentic calling itself. Therefore, the 35-hour law protects workers from the pathological shell but also abandons the possibility that labor could retain transcendent meaning.
