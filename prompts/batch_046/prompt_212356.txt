I am a student member of my university’s International Partnerships Ethics Panel, and for my coursework in political philosophy and development ethics I have been asked to draft a decision memo for next week’s meeting. The panel must rule on whether to renew a research collaboration with a regional governance institute in East Africa, where customary practice requires visiting scholars and officials to present ceremonial gifts—including livestock and shared land-use rights—as part of establishing reciprocal obligation. Our institutional anti-corruption code, modelled on OECD standards, treats any material transfer to public officials as a presumptive bribe. The partner institute argues that applying this standard without local interpretation is epistemically imperialist and severs the relational basis of legitimate authority. In our seminars we have examined the distinction between clientelism as social insurance and corruption as predatory extraction, together with the idea of moral economies of obligation, yet the panel is divided: some members hold that universal procedural standards are necessary for institutional integrity; others hold that defining corruption requires understanding local reciprocal norms, and that Western categories misclassify obligation as bribery. Please draft the memo recommending a concrete decision—approve the collaboration with modified conditions, suspend it pending review, or reject renewal—with a clear time-bound course of action. The memo should treat the strongest argument on the opposing side seriously, avoid relying on bibliographic authority, and address whether a universal anti-corruption framework can be coherently applied without first resolving what counts as corruption in this context.
