Okay so I've been thinking about this way too much since I started volunteering with this group that tracks how old workplace norms just... vanish? I think I've landed on a view about moral revolutions — like dueling dying out or slavery ending — that borrows from Appiah's honor-world stuff and Williams's interest-based take, but adds something they both miss. I call it the embarassment cascade: a norm collapses not when people are argued into thinking it's wrong, and not just when it stops being profitable, but when high-status people start treating anyone who still does it as genuinely cringe, and that peer-mockery flips the honor calculus overnight. So dueling didn't end because everyone suddenly read Kant; it ended because being challenged to a duel started making you look like a try-hard loser in front of thier peers. Same with slavery, I think — elite embarassment spread faster than abolitionist arguments.

But I want to be honest about where this is exposed. What would actually count against the embarassment-cascade story? I'm not asking for a bibliography — just what historical patterns, psychological studies, or formal models would have to look different for me to be wrong? Which assumptions could fail: maybe elites don't drive norms, maybe ridicule is just a side effect of interest shifts, maybe some norms die with zero elite mockery? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Also, what does my view predict that pure honor or pure interest theories don't — and has it already been qualified into unfalsifiability? If so, is that a problem for a claim like this? And what would Appiah or Williams themselves concede counts against my twist?
