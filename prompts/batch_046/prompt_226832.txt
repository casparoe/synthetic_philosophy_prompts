For a philosophy of mind seminar I am designing, read Christopher Nolan's Inception through the debate between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett on whether dreams are experiences. Malcolm denies that sleeping subjects undergo experiences, treating dream reports as retrospective confabulations assembled upon waking; Dennett allows that something experiential occurs but stresses discontinuity, narrative reconstruction, and the absence of a stable observing self. The film depicts shared, architect-designed dream spaces—nested levels, physical laws altered by design, a sudden "kick" that terminates the episode, and totems used to distinguish reality from dream. Focus especially on the architect Ariadne, who constructs spaces she does not inhabit, and on Cobb's spinning top. Does the film presuppose a genuine experiencing subject inside the dream—someone for whom the collapsing hotel is present—or does its logic align better with Malcolm's view that the "experience" is a causal scaffold producing convergent false memories? Does the architect's absence support a divisionist picture in which the designer and the experiencer are split? Does the totem test for the presence of experience or merely for narrative coherence? And where the film resists both philosophers—particularly in Cobb's unresolved final scene—say so explicitly. I want a reading faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the film to an illustration, nor distort Malcolm or Dennett to fit the plot, and it should end by stating what Inception's logic of nested, collective, and designed consciousness contributes to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not reach.
