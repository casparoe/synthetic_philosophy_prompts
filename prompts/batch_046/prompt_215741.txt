Consider the argument that a “phenomenal zombie”—a being physically and functionally identical to a conscious human but entirely lacking subjective experience—is conceivable, and that this conceivability shows consciousness is not metaphysically necessitated by physical properties. A common reply invokes necessary a posteriori identities (for example, that water is necessarily H₂O) to claim that conceivability can outstrip metaphysical possibility. Evaluate whether this reply undermines the zombie argument. In your assessment, address three things: (i) whether phenomenal concepts are analogous to natural-kind terms like “water”; (ii) whether a complete physical-functional description of the zombie implicitly leaves out something essential to consciousness; and (iii) whether the distinction between prima facie and ideal conceivability affects the strength of the modal intuition.

Student answer:

The zombie argument starts from the intuition that we can clearly imagine a world physically identical to ours in which no one has any inner experience. If that scenario is genuinely conceivable, then it is metaphysically possible, which contradicts physicalism’s claim that physical facts necessarily determine phenomenal facts.

The standard reply appeals to necessary a posteriori truths. Kripke showed that water is necessarily H₂O, yet we can conceive of a clear, thirst-quenching liquid with formula XYZ. This shows that conceivability tracks epistemic possibility rather than metaphysical possibility, because our concept of water is fixed by superficial properties rather than by its hidden chemical essence.

Applying this to consciousness requires asking whether phenomenal concepts work like natural-kind terms. Phenomenal concepts—such as the concept of pain—are not fixed by superficial appearances that mask a deeper nature; rather, they present the quality itself directly. There is no hidden essence of pain that empirical science might discover, analogous to the molecular structure of water. So when we conceive of a physical duplicate lacking consciousness, we are not ignorant of some underlying phenomenal property that the duplicate actually possesses; the absence is real.

Some philosophers, like Chalmers, distinguish prima facie from ideal conceivability, arguing that only the latter reliably guides possibility. Even under ideal reflection, however, the zombie scenario remains coherent: we can specify every physical and functional detail without contradiction, and consciousness is still absent. This suggests the modal gap is not an illusion created by incomplete description but reflects a genuine metaphysical contingency.

The necessary a posteriori reply applies mainly to terms that function as rigid designators for natural kinds—terms whose reference is fixed by an underlying essence in the actual world. Phenomenal concepts, however, are not rigid designators; they refer via direct acquaintance rather than via a causal chain linking them to a hidden nature. Because they lack rigidity, they are not subject to the modal illusions Kripke describes. Therefore, the water analogy fails to dissolve the zombie intuition. The physicalist must either deny that zombies are ideally conceivable or accept that consciousness is not physically necessitated.
