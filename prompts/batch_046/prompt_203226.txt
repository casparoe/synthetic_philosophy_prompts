I hold that supererogation should not be treated as a categorical exception to duty—obligatory, permissible, or forbidden—but should instead be mapped onto a continuous two-dimensional space: one axis measuring the agent's all-things-considered cost (including opportunity cost and sustained psychological burden), the other measuring the moral value produced, with supererogatory acts occupying the region where cost exceeds a socially calibrated baseline yet value exceeds a proportionally scaled threshold, yielding graded degrees of moral surplus rather than a binary status. What would genuinely count against this dimensional account? Sort the defeaters by decisiveness: which conceptual results, empirical findings, or institutional observations would force abandonment of the graded framework; which would merely require recalibrating the axes; and which defenders of the view could legitimately dismiss as misapplying the model? Include an honest assessment of whether the framework has been or could be qualified into unfalsifiability—if every counterexample can be absorbed by shifting parameters—and whether such insulation is a defect for a normative theory of this kind.
