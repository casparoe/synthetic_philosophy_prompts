I am a quantitative ecologist fluent in occupancy-detection modeling, state-space representations, and the statistical separation of ecological state processes from observation processes. I work routinely with false negatives, spatially unsampled grid cells, detection probabilities, and model-selection criteria such as AIC, and I take it as given that true occupancy states in unsurveyed locations are permanently confounded with absence.

Restate, in that vocabulary, the philosophical problem of ontology identification under permanently inaccessible information: specifically, the argument that two ontological schemes—one that posits unobservable structural entities underwriting observable patterns, and one that posits only the observable patterns themselves—are empirically equivalent with respect to every datum we can in principle collect, diverging only in their commitments about the permanently unsampled regions or hidden causal joints. Then translate the standard realist response (that one scheme is genuinely true of those regions despite the inaccessibility) and the anti-realist or pragmatic response (that the divergence is a pseudo-question settled by theoretical virtue rather than fact).

Report the residue precisely: which elements translate cleanly into the state-process versus observation-process distinction, which require distortion to fit within statistical model comparison, and which have no counterpart in occupancy modeling—particularly the claim that there is a fact of the matter about the true ontology of an unsampled cell independent of any model's predictive performance on observed cells. Finally, judge whether this residue indicates that my ecological framework genuinely lacks resources for expressing ontological commitment, or whether it shows that the philosophical disagreement was never more substantive than a disagreement about model-selection conventions that AIC already adjudicates.
