I have been working through Schrödinger's lesser-known philosophical essays and his personal reflections on Vedanta, particularly his claim that consciousness is not genuinely plural but rather a single universal awareness that merely appears fragmented through the multiplicity of biological bodies. This monistic thesis strikes me as simultaneously profound and suspiciously insulated from counterevidence. Write an essay that weighs whether Schrödinger's Vedantic position—that the distinction between self and other is ultimately an illusion sustained only by bodily separation—can survive two specific philosophical pressures: first, the phenomenological datum that conscious experience is always perspectivally anchored, in that there is irreducibly something it is like to be this particular subject situated here rather than everywhere; and second, the clinical and neuropsychological evidence from split-brain patients suggesting that one biological substrate can come to host apparently disjoint, non-communicating streams of awareness. Your essay should reach and defend a clear conclusion about whether these considerations genuinely undermine the unity of consciousness, or whether they can be consistently re-described as further manifestations of the one awareness operating through divided instruments. In particular, address whether Schrödinger's view risks being unfalsifiable—explaining away every counterexample by reinterpreting multiplicity as mere appearance—and whether that unfalsifiability is a fatal philosophical defect or an acceptable feature of a metaphysical framework that aims to dissolve rather than solve the problem of other minds.
