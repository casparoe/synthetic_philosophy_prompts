I've been talking with a close friend who reads a lot of popular philosophy but has no formal training, and we keep circling back to the same disagreement about whether things can genuinely stay the same when everything about them has changed. The other day we applied it to a real-world case we both read about: a small nonprofit that, over about ten years, replaced every staff member, migrated every record to a new digital format, and swapped out all of its physical equipment, yet kept its original charter, tax identification number, and mission statement. When I asked whether it was literally the same organization or just a successor carrying the name, my friend waved it away with the kind of line people always invoke in these situations: "It's just the Ship of Theseus — there's no real fact of the matter, it's only whatever we decide to call the same ship." He seemed to think this settled the question, but I feel the phrase is doing hidden argumentative work that I can't quite isolate. Could you treat that slogan not as a settled answer but as an ambiguous claim that needs disambiguation? Specifically, lay out the different precise theses it could express — from the trivial point that we apply words by choice, through pragmatic and conventionalist readings, to stronger metaphysical claims that organizational identity is constructed or that there are no objective persistence conditions — and for each indicate whether it actually licenses dismissing my nonprofit question, whether it commits my friend to implausible conclusions about other cases of sameness he probably wants to preserve, and whether it depends on smuggling in a hidden picture of what "real" identity would have to involve. I'd like the answer structured as a clear menu of readings I can bring back to our conversation, with verdicts on which are defensible, which are false, and which ones genuinely do the job he thinks they're doing.
