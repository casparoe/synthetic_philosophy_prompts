I have recently found myself reconsidering whether sustained intellectual work can genuinely contribute to happiness, particularly after leaving a career that offered more immediate sensory rewards for one that is largely solitary and abstract. In this context, I encountered a summary of Émilie du Châtelet’s views on happiness—specifically, her argument in the Discourse on Happiness that happiness is essentially the maximization of passionate satisfaction moderated by reason. The summary claimed that du Châtelet holds a strictly hedonistic calculus in which reason plays only an instrumental role, directing us toward the most durable pleasures but never constituting a pleasure itself; that she regards social recognition as a necessary condition for the happiness derived from study, especially for women; and that she explicitly rejects the value of illusion, arguing that any false belief about one’s achievements or relationships necessarily undermines long-term well-being. From this, the summary concluded that du Châtelet’s view is essentially a gender-adapted Epicureanism, and that her defense of women’s education is therefore only a strategic recommendation for securing a particular class of durable pleasures rather than a claim about intellectual dignity. Something about this feels reductive, particularly the idea that reason has no intrinsic value for her and that she sees study purely as a hedonic investment. Could you audit this summary? Please distinguish which claims align with her actual position, which are common simplifications, which misrepresent her arguments—especially regarding the role of reason, the necessity of illusion, and the status of study—and whether the conclusion that her feminism is merely strategic hedonism actually follows from the accurate parts.
