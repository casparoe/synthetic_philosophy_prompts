Following a prolonged argument with a friend about Hofstadter's careenium allegory and whether symbolic configurations—simmballs—can genuinely push around the physical substrate composed of simms, I have turned to an older mechanist account to test my intuitions. In Chapter 5 of Hobbes's Leviathan, Hobbes describes reasoning as a form of reckoning carried out upon names and marks, comparing the mind to an abacus in which beads are moved according to fixed rules. He treats these symbols not as ghostly agents but as physical inscriptions arranged and rearranged by bodily and cerebral motion.

What I need help understanding is exactly how this passage bears on the substrate-push problem within the careenium framework. My friend insisted that Hobbes's view entails strict one-way causation: the substrate, meaning the brain's mechanical motions, always pushes the symbols around, and never the reverse, which would imply that simmballs are purely passive patterns carried by the motion of simms. I am unsure this reading is correct. The passage seems to suggest that once a symbol is inscribed, the rule-governed arrangement of that symbol can determine the next bodily motion—almost as if the symbol's formal position pushes the substrate into a particular configuration. Yet I worry I am importing a modern distinction that Hobbes would reject.

Could you help me parse the logic of Hobbes's argument in this passage? Specifically, does he allow that the formal properties of a symbol—its place in a chain of reckoning—can exert downward causal influence on the physical motions that realize it, or does he reduce all such influence to prior mechanical causes? And if Hobbes does allow downward influence, how should that shape our judgment about whether simmballs in the careenium can genuinely push the substrate around, rather than merely being pushed?
