Here is my opening move. I grant the intuition that permanently entering a full simulation feels like a profound loss, but I think your argument risks proving too much unless you can draw a principled boundary. Consider what I will call localized epistemic isolation: a small community that collectively sustains a harmless false belief—say, that their ancestors actively watch over them—where the belief is causally maintained by social ritual rather than by any actual ancestral presence. The members experience the same phenomenology of reassurance and continuity that they would have if the belief were true, and they never discover the error. If reality-contact is strictly necessary for well-being, then these people's lives are diminished purely by this localized gap between mental content and causal provenance, which seems implausible; we would not judge them worse off in any meaningful sense. The experience machine, by contrast, represents total ontological substitution: every mental state is detached from its usual causal origin. So your view must either accept that all localized false beliefs diminish well-being, which is counterintuitive, or it must identify a structural feature—perhaps modal robustness, or the global scope of the substitution—that distinguishes the machine from these partial cases. Where is that line, and why does crossing it in the global case destroy well-being rather than merely altering its causal history? I want to know whether you have a genuine criterion or whether the intuition tracks only an evolved aversion to wholesale deception.
