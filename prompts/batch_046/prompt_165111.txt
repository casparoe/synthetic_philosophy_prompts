Construct a decision-tree taxonomy for the debate over whether maximal moral goodness—moral sainthood—is desirable, intended for readers who know the issue only informally. Build the tree from logical commitments rather than from named theorists. Begin with two root branches: (1) Can a life be excellent overall if it is maximally moral? (Yes / No / Only if non-moral goods are preserved); and (2) Are non-moral goods—artistic projects, particular attachments, play—legitimately sacrificable for moral demands without moral remainder? (Always sacrificable / Sacrificable but with loss / Never sacrificable if they constitute identity).

Then route through three concrete diagnostic cases, phrased as judgments a respondent can make without knowing philosophical labels:
A. A parent abandons a promising artistic career to care for a disabled child, feels no resentment, and never returns to art. Is this life more admirable, less admirable, or differently admirable than one in which she continues painting part-time?
B. A person achieves perfect impartial benevolence but is personally dull, incapable of deep friendship, and finds joy only in helping strangers. Is this a failure of humanity or its fulfillment?
C. A threshold scenario: someone is as morally good as humanly possible, yet spends Sunday afternoons painting landscapes rather than volunteering. Is this a moral failing, a permissible choice, or proof that sainthood is compatible with non-moral life?

Locate at least five leaves in the resulting space: (i) an integrationist view that sainthood is undesirable because it crowds out non-moral goods; (ii) a rationalist view that non-moral goods are subordinate and sainthood is obligatory; (iii) an ascetic view that sainthood requires eliminating personal projects, which is an admirable loss rather than a cost; (iv) a threshold view that there is no ceiling on goodness but no duty to reach it; and (v) at least one unoccupied combination of answers, with a brief diagnosis of why it is empty (incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored).

Every node must be answerable without knowing labels; do not ask whether the respondent is a Kantian or Wolfian. Include no citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts. Present the tree as a nested outline of no more than 300 words. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate given the three cases.
