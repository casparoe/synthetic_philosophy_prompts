I have been following, mostly through popular science writing and online discussion, a long-running disagreement about whether string theory's multiverse landscape can be confirmed as scientific even though direct empirical tests appear impossible. The proponents seem to argue that the theory's internal mathematical consistency, its uniqueness in reproducing certain gravitational and gauge structures, and the fact that its solution space is vast but not arbitrary together constitute a form of theoretical confirmation: they say that when a framework is structurally closed in this way and no rival framework achieves the same closure, we have rational grounds to accept it even absent direct observation. The critics reply that this is not confirmation at all but a redefinition of science: if a theory permits every possible low-energy physics within its landscape, then no observable outcome could contradict it, and without the possibility of contradiction there is no empirical risk, hence no scientific status. Both sides, as I understand them, agree on the formal results—there is indeed a large landscape of vacuum solutions, and there is indeed no present experiment that selects one—and yet the debate has not moved in years. In my own thinking I have started to distinguish between what I call structural confirmation, where a theory is supported by its internal coherence and its exclusion of rival formal structures, and phenomenal confirmation, where a theory is supported by its exposure to observable outcomes that could have falsified it. My working hypothesis is that the stall is largely verbal: the two sides are using the word "confirmation" for these two different things, and once that distinction is made explicit there is no deeper disagreement about facts or values, only a disagreement about which kind of support counts as scientific. But I am unsure. Is the impasse actually about a hidden empirical question—whether future mathematics or indirect cosmological observation could eventually constrain the landscape—or about bedrock intuitions regarding what science must do, intuitions that neither side can argue for without circularity? Or are the sides answering genuinely different questions, one about the logic of theory choice and the other about the sociology and norms of the scientific community, under the single heading of "Is string theory confirmed?" I want a diagnosis that engages what each side actually says, not a survey of interpretations. Given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a sharper conceptual distinction, a formal result showing that the landscape is or is not empirically constrainable in principle, a change in the framing question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why?
