Write a deadpan parody of an end-user licence agreement, approximately 700 words and formatted as numbered clauses (e.g. 1.1, 2.4, 3.2), for a fictional group-decision platform called “Mutuality” that promises to deliver “verified common knowledge of rationality” among its users. Adopt the dry, legalistic voice of a Silicon Valley terms-of-service document: readable to a layperson, yet precise enough that a specialist in epistemic game theory recognises the real arguments being exaggerated. Quietly embed philosophical positions on the distinction between mere mutual belief and genuine common knowledge; the Bayesian problem of old evidence when all parties already share the prior; the coordination-collapse dynamics illustrated by the email-game model; and whether rationality operates as a public good that can be waived or contracted away by consent. The satire should target the tendency to formalise ordinary social reasoning into algorithmic conditions, and it should stay deadpan—never winking—so that a reader can feel both the absurdity of treating epistemic states as service-level agreements and the genuine difficulty of achieving them in practice. Do not name any living person, do not include a bibliography or citations, and do not invent straw-man positions; every clause should map onto a recognisable feature of the literature. By the end, the document should leave the reader wondering whether common knowledge can ever be “accepted” like a cookie policy. Please write the response in British English.
