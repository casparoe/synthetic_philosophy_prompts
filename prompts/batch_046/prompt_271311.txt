For a long-form feature on the sociology of explanation, I am examining why the debate over Max Weber’s claim that Protestant ascetic values played a causal role in the rise of modern capitalism has persisted for more than a century without resolution. On one side stand materialist historians who treat shared values as epiphenomenal to economic structures and technological change; on the other stand interpretive sociologists who treat meaning-laden motivations as irreducible explanatory variables that cannot be collapsed into material conditions. My working hypothesis is that the deadlock is not primarily about archival gaps or statistical correlations, but about a deeper disagreement over whether culture can be a cause in the same ontological register as resource scarcity or institutional innovation—a problem structurally analogous to the philosophy-of-mind debate over mental causation, yet rarely recognized as such in historical sociology. Is this diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden empirical disagreement disguised as a methodological one? I want an analysis that distinguishes verbal from substantive disagreement: do the opposing camps differ over historical facts, over standards of adequate causal explanation in the human sciences, over whether explanatory pluralism is legitimate, or over the very boundary between rationalizing interpretation and causal mechanism? Please engage concretely with what each side actually asserts—for instance, whether critics reject Weber’s proposed mechanism of worldly asceticism driving capital accumulation, or whether they deny that values can function as structural selectors that constrain economic pathways without serving as direct motivational drivers. Identify whether a formal distinction—treating normative commitments as structural selectors that filter material possibilities rather than as agent-level psychological forces—could dissolve the impasse, or whether it merely relocates the dispute. Also address whether the stalling signals that the question is ill-posed: are the sides answering different questions under the single heading of Weber’s thesis, one asking about the origin of capitalist institutions and the other about the meaning of economic action? Finally, tell me honestly whether any progress is possible: could a new formal model of cultural transmission, a conceptual clarification of causation by omission in social contexts, or a fresh archival discovery about accounting practices and capital timing actually resolve the dispute, or should we expect perpetual disagreement because the standards of explanation themselves are incommensurable? I need this for the conceptual framing of my article, not a survey of interpretations or a reading list.
