The usual debate about animal sacrifice in Maimonides' Guide for the Perplexed pits the accommodationist reading—sacrifice is a divine concession to an Israelite psychological need for concrete ritual—against the symbolic-redirection reading, in which the practice channels idolatrous impulses into monotheistic worship. Both treat the problem as one of belief-formation or affective management. My half-formed idea is to ask whether Maimonides' account actually turns on a claim about practical rationality: that destroying a valuable animal functions as an irreversible, costly signal that restructures how agents weigh immediate gratification against long-term covenantal commitments. In other words, can we reconstruct the relevant chapters of Part III not as a theory of psychological habituation or symbolic substitution, but as an early account of ritual as an institutional technology for preference transformation?
