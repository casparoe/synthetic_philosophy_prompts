As a novelist preparing a narrative that engages Enlightenment moral philosophy, I want to read Voltaire's Candide specifically through the lens of Leibniz's moral doctrine rather than through the familiar caricature of metaphysical optimism alone. I have in mind Leibniz's distinction between metaphysical evil (imperfection in the cosmic order) and moral evil (the fault of rational agents), his conception of justice as charity of the wise—caritas sapientis, the love of God and neighbor informed by knowledge of universal harmony—and the claim that moral perfection consists not in denying particular suffering but in understanding its place in a rational order. Read the novel with these concepts in view: where does Candide's suffering—the Lisbon earthquake, the auto-da-fé, the enslavement in Suriname—function as metaphysical imperfection permitted for a greater good, and where does it become irreducible moral evil that no cosmic harmony can justify? Does Pangloss practice a genuine charity of the wise, or does Voltaire present him as mistaking intellectual complacency for moral love? I am particularly interested in the Eldorado episode and in the novel's closing counsel to cultivate one's garden: do these moments reject Leibnizian moral charity entirely, or do they relocate it from cosmic justification to local, practical care? Where does the novel resist this philosophical framing—where must an honest critic concede that Voltaire is attacking a strawman rather than Leibniz's actual account of moral evil? Finally, what does Candide add to the philosophical debate about whether understanding universal harmony can ever serve as a moral justification for particular cruelty that a treatise on morality, argued in the abstract, could not achieve? I am teaching this to a group of writers, so I want a reading that takes both the novel's narrative architecture and Leibniz's moral system seriously, without reducing either to an illustration of the other.
