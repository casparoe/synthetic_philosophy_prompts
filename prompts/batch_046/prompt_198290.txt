I was recently appointed to an interdisciplinary reading group that is evaluating whether contemporary neuroscience can accommodate genuine mental causation, and I found myself unable to articulate what I would actually count as a successful theory in this space. Before I survey the competing positions, I want the full desiderata: write the specification that any adequate account of mental causation must satisfy if it is to address Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument without merely changing the subject. For each requirement, identify the phenomenon it must explain or the distinction it must preserve—such as the difference between causal efficacy and causal relevance, between token-level and type-level mental causation, or between local supervenience and global closure of the physical domain—together with the specific case or consideration that motivates it, for instance why mental properties seem to drive behavior in a way that is neither reducible to neural firing nor epiphenomenal. State which desiderata are non-negotiable given the structure of the exclusion problem and which are camp-specific preferences, such as compatibility with emergent downward causation, with a strict interventionist account of causation, or with the denial of physical closure. Crucially, examine whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable—particularly the triad of downward mental causation, the closure of physics, and the absence of systematic overdetermination—and explain whether that tension is structural or merely awaits a technical resolution. Keep the emphasis on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring the contenders, though I would welcome a brief assessment of how reductionism, epiphenomenalism, non-reductive physicalism with supervenience, and emergentist dualism stand against the list; I need this framework to guide my own judgment rather than to memorize positions.
