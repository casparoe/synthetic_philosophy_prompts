I've been working through Sartre's ontology of consciousness in Being and Nothingness with a small reading group, and we've reached an impasse on the precise structure of bad faith that I hope you can help me untangle. Most of our group members understand bad faith in the familiar form: the café waiter who plays at being a waiter, treating his social role as an essential nature and thereby fleeing the anxiety of radical freedom. But I'm less clear on Sartre's parallel claim that sincerity—where a person insists on being fully identical with some given essence, psychological or moral—is structurally the same flight, not its opposite.

Could you explain the conceptual dependency between these two modes? I want to understand how the for-itself's necessary non-coincidence with itself—its being defined by a lack or nihilation rather than by any positive content—makes both the waiter's over-identification with facticity and the sincere man's over-identification with transcendence impossible projects. In particular, please clarify why Sartre thinks that even the explicit acknowledgment of one's radical freedom does not dissolve bad faith but often reconstitutes it at a meta-level, and how the attempt to ground one's choices in a pre-given human nature misapprehends the ontological status of consciousness as pure negation. Please avoid broad exhortations about authenticity; I am looking for the internal mechanics of how nihilation relates to self-deception, especially in cases where the subject believes she is being most honest with herself.
