Having spent decades in experimental physics before turning, in retirement, to philosophical questions about belief and rationality, I have been drafting an essay—tentatively titled "Aggregate Confidence and Singular Rejection"—that approaches the lottery paradox and the preface paradox through concepts more familiar in the laboratory than in the seminar room. Rather than treating both puzzles solely as static conflicts within a single agent's propositional attitudes, I propose to examine them as dynamic epistemic processes: the preface paradox, I suggest, structurally resembles the situation in large-scale meta-analysis, where one accepts each individual study with high confidence while knowing that the cumulative false-positive rate makes it virtually certain that some accepted results are erroneous; the lottery paradox, by contrast, resembles the problem of instrument calibration, where one trusts that every unit in a large batch falls within tolerance, yet refuses to identify any particular unit as the defective outlier, despite knowing that the base rate of failure is identical to the false-positive rate in the meta-analytic case.

The essay's central contention is that philosophers have tended to analyze both puzzles as questions about the logic of belief aggregation at a single time, whereas a scientific intuition suggests that the tension—if it can be dissolved—requires modeling belief as temporally distributed and feedback-sensitive, indexed to trajectories of revision rather than to static thresholds. I am particularly concerned with whether this framing genuinely resolves the contradiction or merely relocates it: if rational belief is indexed to a temporal path of updating, does the agent at any given moment still face the same logical conflict between accepting each premise and rejecting the conjunction, or does the temporal dimension provide a principled basis for distinguishing aggregate acceptance from singular rejection?

I would welcome your critical assessment of these notes, focused on coherence rather than bibliography. Specifically, I ask whether the analogies to meta-analytic error accumulation and sensor calibration illuminate the philosophical structure or instead smuggle in empirical assumptions about scientific practice that are irrelevant to the logical form of the classic paradoxes. Does my emphasis on temporal dynamics obscure the specifically epistemic feature of the lottery case—namely, the absence of any evidential basis for singling out one ticket over another? And if one accepts that rational belief should be indexed to revision trajectories, does that constitute a substantive philosophical advance, or does it collapse into a generic skepticism about inductive inference? I seek suggestions for sharpening the distinction between aggregate and singular rejection without invoking formalisms I am unlikely to have mastered.
