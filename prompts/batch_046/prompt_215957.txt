I am serving as a teaching assistant for an upper-division seminar on comparative moral philosophy, and I am grading final exam responses for a unit that juxtaposes African relational ethics with Kantian moral psychology. The exam prompt asked students to do the following: first, explain how the relational conception of personhood associated with Ubuntu philosophy understands the emergence and maintenance of moral agency, particularly in contrast to an individualist, capacity-based account such as Kant's; second, evaluate the objection that if personhood is genuinely constituted by communal relationships rather than possessed independently, then self-directed moral obligations become conceptually incoherent, since there is no pre-relational self available to serve as both bearer and object of such duties. The student was also asked to say whether this objection succeeds, whether the relational view must therefore deny all individual moral responsibility, and whether Kantian autonomy can accommodate relational elements without sacrificing its core commitment.

One student submitted the following answer. The relational view, often summarized by the idea that a person is constituted through other people, holds that moral personhood is not an innate metaphysical property of rational beings but a status achieved and sustained through participation in communal life. Unlike Kant, who grounds dignity in autonomous rational nature possessed by each individual prior to any social interaction, this account treats the self as fundamentally interdependent, with moral capacities emerging through recognition, reciprocity, and shared practices rather than through private possession. Consequently, the notion of a self that stands entirely apart from its community is not merely inaccurate but metaphysically mistaken. Addressing the objection about self-directed duties, the student argues that if there is no independent self, then obligations directed specifically to oneself are indeed incoherent; any apparent duty to develop one's talents or preserve one's dignity is actually an indirect duty owed to the community that depends on one's contribution. The student further claims that individual moral blame is always inappropriate under this framework, since responsibility is fully distributed across the social conditions that produce action, and that Kant's view avoids this problem only by ignoring the social conditions necessary for rational agency altogether.

Please grade this answer. Provide a specific letter or numeric grade, and then offer point-by-point comments that justify the evaluation. Your assessment should identify where the student correctly captures the visible contrast between relational and Kantian accounts, but should also flag any subtle conflations, overstatements, or inferential steps that do not quite follow—particularly regarding the relationship between relational constitution and individual responsibility, and regarding whether Kant's autonomy can genuinely incorporate social conditions. Do not signal whether you think the answer is strong or weak overall; simply deliver the graded assessment with precise reasoning for each point.
