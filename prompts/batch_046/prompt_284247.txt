I am finalizing the examination for my graduate seminar in philosophy of mind, and a recent discussion with a colleague in cognitive neuroscience about intraoperative awareness reports has left me convinced that students treat Dennett's demolition of the Cartesian theater as a settled slogan rather than a live architectural problem. I want to test whether they can navigate the deeper tension: whether the multiple drafts model genuinely eliminates a central observer or merely displaces one into an implicit editorial mechanism that selects which draft achieves reportability. For the 90-minute final essay, please draft a question that asks students to evaluate whether Dennett's framework can explain the subjective impression of a continuous, unified phenomenal stream—particularly in light of change blindness and postdictive perceptual filling-in—without smuggling back in the very homuncular audience he rejects. The question must be unanswerable by mere summary of Dennett's major work; a weak response should be identifiable by its reliance on familiar metaphors without addressing the selection problem. Along with the question, provide a model answer that traces the necessary argumentative moves, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, each assigned four marks for a total of twenty, including for each point a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that should cost marks—such as conflating the absence of a Cartesian theater with the absence of any temporal integration mechanism, or treating change blindness as direct proof of draft multiplicity rather than as a phenomenon that needs interpretation within the model.
