I've been working through a problem in my philosophy of social science course that seems to sit at the intersection of social ontology and metaphysics. We often treat money as the paradigm case of an institutional kind: a piece of paper is a twenty-dollar bill not because of its physical properties but because of collective recognition and assigned function, and nobody seriously thinks that recognizing this undermines the causal efficacy of monetary exchange or makes "dollar" an unreal category. By contrast, debates about gender essentialism often proceed as though showing that gender is socially constructed automatically threatens its metaphysical status—either reducing it to an illusion or stripping it of explanatory power over social outcomes. This makes me wonder whether there is a hidden false equivalence in how we apply the "social construction" label across domains. Specifically, I want to ask: if we model gender categories on monetary denominations—purely institutional, functionally assigned, carrying normative weight without requiring a hidden biological substrate—would that framework actually dissolve the standard essentialism debates, or does gender construction involve a structurally different kind of collective intentionality (perhaps embodied, intersubjective, or tied to material practices in ways that currency is not) that makes the monetary analogy misleading? In other words, is the persistence of gender essentialism a failure to recognize that institutional kinds can be fully real, or is gender not an institutional kind in the same sense as money at all?

Please answer by diagnosing the confusion in the comparison rather than simply endorsing one side of the essentialism debate. I also want you to be explicit about what would change your assessment: which feature of gender (or of institutional kinds generally) would have to be shown to work differently for the monetary model to fail, and where do you think your reasoning is most vulnerable to a counterexample?
