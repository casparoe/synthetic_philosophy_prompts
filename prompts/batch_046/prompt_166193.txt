As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun serious study of moral philosophy, I have drafted the following argument about P.F. Strawson’s distinction between reactive and objective attitudes, and I would welcome rigorous conceptual comments—particularly on coherence and analogical strength, not on bibliographic completeness.

My central observation is that reactive attitudes such as resentment and gratitude appear structurally analogous to the feedback signals I once managed in precision measurement apparatus. In both cases, one encounters not a neutral report of deviation but a normative, affect-laden response that presupposes membership in a shared framework of expectation. When a calibrated instrument drifts, the experimenter does not merely log the error objectively; she reacts with practical frustration that motivates recalibration, precisely because the device is embedded in a network of trust and purpose. By analogy, resentment functions not as a clinical diagnosis of harm but as a social recalibration signal that treats the other as a responsible member of the moral community rather than as an object to be managed.

Yet I worry that this comparison imports an inappropriate teleology. Instrumental feedback is engineered to converge on a predetermined correct value, whereas reactive attitudes seem to constitute the normative framework itself rather than correct deviations within it. Moreover, in science the objective attitude can always be adopted without moral loss—one may study a malfunctioning sensor purely as physics—whereas Strawson insists that systematically suspending reactive attitudes toward human agents involves a genuine human cost and a withdrawal from interpersonal relations.

My tentative conclusion is that the analogy usefully illuminates the coordinating function of reactive attitudes in maintaining social expectation, but it fails to capture their constitutive role in moral responsibility. I reach this verdict with some hesitation, because I am unsure whether my scientific background is leading me to over-systematize what Strawson intends as an irreducibly interpersonal phenomenon.

Please comment specifically on three points. First, does the instrumental-feedback analogy genuinely clarify the participant versus objective distinction, or does it obscure the fact that moral norms are not externally calibrated targets? Second, is my characterization of the objective attitude—harmless in science but costly in morality—faithful to Strawson’s view, or have I exaggerated the asymmetry? Third, might a stronger version of the analogy, perhaps drawing on emergent stability in complex adaptive systems rather than engineered feedback, rescue the comparison without reducing moral relations to engineering?

If the analogy collapses entirely, I would also value suggestions for how a scientist accustomed to system-level thinking might better frame the irreducibility of the participant stance without abandoning conceptual rigor.
