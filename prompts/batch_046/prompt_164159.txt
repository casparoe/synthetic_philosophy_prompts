In Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, there is a well-known analogy between Gödel's self-referential formula — which, within a formal arithmetic system, asserts its own unprovability — and the emergence of self-aware consciousness in minds. The comparison is often used to argue that once a representational system becomes complex enough to model itself, it produces a strange loop, and that this loop is not merely a structural curiosity but the very mechanism by which subjective awareness arises. Recently, I have seen this analogy extended to contemporary large language models: some commentators claim that because these systems can generate descriptions of their own outputs and process meta-level instructions about their architecture, they have already crossed a threshold into a weak form of Gödelian self-reference, and that true artificial consciousness is therefore mainly a matter of scaling up this capacity.

I would like to understand exactly where this comparison holds and where it collapses. Could you set out the explicit mapping between Gödel's formal construction — the arithmetisation of syntax, the encoding of proof-predicates as numerical relations, the self-referential formula, and the system's inability to demonstrate its own consistency — and the cognitive or computational phenomena it is said to illuminate? Then identify the disanalogies: which properties of mathematical self-reference are genuinely present in mental or artificial self-reference, and which are imported only by metaphor? In particular, please examine whether the analogy depends on conflating syntactic self-reference, in which symbols manipulate representations of other symbols, with semantic self-reference, in which a subject experiences itself as the bearer of experience. Does this distinction undermine the claim that scaling a model's ability to talk about itself produces consciousness?

Finally, assess whether the conclusion — that consciousness emerges from sufficiently complex self-modelling — survives once these disanalogies are made explicit. If the Gödelian comparison breaks down at a crucial point, is there a better analogy, perhaps drawn from cybernetics or recursive systems theory, that could illuminate self-awareness without importing the same conceptual liabilities?
