I have recently been appointed chair of the acquisitions committee for a mid-size public museum of modern and contemporary art, and I must now sign off on every proposed purchase. The board requires me to certify that our cited reasons for acquiring a work are of the right kind—genuinely justifying—rather than of the wrong kind, such as anticipated donor gratitude, projected revenue, or alignment with political mandates. I need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the philosophical literature on the wrong-kind-reasons problem, that a committee of curators and trustees (none trained in philosophy) can apply in a single two-hour meeting. The deliverable should be an ordered checklist with explicit tests that distinguish justifying reasons from merely motivating or incentivizing ones; it must handle collective and institutional motives, where different members may hold different reasons, and it must address whether predictive reasons—such as expected audience growth—can ever count as right-kind for a public institution. Include worked applications to three proposed acquisitions: first, a major gift contingent on prominent display of the donated work; second, a politically charged installation whose artistic merit is disputed but whose acquisition would satisfy a municipal diversity mandate; and third, a case where the procedure produces a recommendation the committee will intuitively reject. At each stage, flag where judgment cannot be eliminated and where the procedure is likely to misfire, especially regarding the transmission of wrong-kind reasons across group decision-making and the bootstrapping problem for institutional value. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need only the operationalized framework with its limits clearly marked.
