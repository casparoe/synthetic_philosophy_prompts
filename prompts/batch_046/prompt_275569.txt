Okay so there's this pattern I keep seeing in climate ethics debates and I don't know if it has it's own name. Imagine two possible futures: in Future A we keep burning coal, in Future B we switch to renewables. The people who live in Future A are seperate individuals from the ones in Future B—different parents meet, different kids are born, etc. So if someone says "Future A harms people," defenders reply that nobody in Future A is worse off than they would've been in Future B, because those specific people wouldn't exist in B at all. But here's the part that bugs me: the harm being discussed isn't really "this specific person is worse off," it's that the enviroment they inherit is more polluted, and yet the identity shift gets used to shut down the harm claim entirely. I know this is in the neighborhood of the non-identity problem, but that usually gets framed as "you can't harm a specific future person by causing them to exist in bad conditions." This feels different—it's using the same logic at an institutional scale to dodge responsibility for collective damage. Does this specific move have a name? How is it different from the standard non-identity problem, and if there's no term, what would you call the boundary where identity-dependency stops being about personal harm and starts being a shield for collective damage?
