As a doctoral student in philosophy of science working on the ontology of spacetime, I have been trying to map the traditional substantivalism-relationism debate onto the technical machinery of modern differential geometry, and I keep hitting a conceptual snag with Newton’s bucket that my supervisors have not resolved to my satisfaction. The standard narrative is familiar: in an approximately empty universe, the concavity of the water surface in a rotating bucket indicates rotation relative to absolute space, since there are no other bodies relative to which the rotation could be defined. Substantivalists take this as evidence for an independent spacetime manifold whose points can occupy different states of motion; Machian relationists reject absolute space and ground rotational inertia instead in relations to distant matter. What I want to understand, however, is how this argument interacts with the distinction—central to the formulation of general relativity—between active and passive diffeomorphisms. The substantivalist typically interprets active diffeomorphisms as mapping spacetime points onto physically distinct configurations, which requires that those points possess a primitive identity that survives transformation. The relationist, by denying such point-identity, treats all diffeomorphisms as passive coordinate redescriptions, leaving only material and geometric relations as physically real.

My specific conceptual difficulty is this: if the relationist denies that spacetime points exist independently, then rotational motion must be defined purely in terms of relational structures—either material relations or the intrinsic geometry of the metric field itself. Yet Newton’s bucket appears designed precisely to show that rotational inertia persists in the absence of material relations, which suggests that the water’s concavity is not reducible to relations among bodies. Some contemporary relationists respond by treating the metric field as a relational entity capable of carrying rotational structure without underlying points, but this raises further puzzles about asymptotic boundary conditions and whether local geometric relations can ground inertia without Mach’s principle. So please explain the conceptual issue at stake here: does interpreting the bucket through the active-passive distinction genuinely presuppose substantivalism about point-identity, or can a relationist consistently maintain that the concavity reflects a real physical difference—grounded in relational geometry rather than motion through an absolute container—without invoking distant matter? Reach a clear conceptual resolution about whether the bucket argument retains its anti-relationist force once primitive point-identity is rejected, or whether that distinction dissolves the argument’s evidential weight by reconceiving rotation as a feature of relational structure alone.
