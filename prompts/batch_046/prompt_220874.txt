My supervisor in moral philosophy has, over the past term, offered consistent verdicts on whether particular actions possess full moral worth. Her judgements so far are these. First, a soldier who throws himself upon a grenade to save his unit, acting from an immediate protective instinct with no conscious deliberation about duty, possesses full moral worth. Second, a billionaire who donates ninety per cent of her wealth to malaria prevention after a rigorous cost-effectiveness analysis, and who reports feeling no emotional connection to the beneficiaries, also possesses full moral worth. Third, a nurse who works pandemic overtime solely from fear of losing her professional licence possesses none. Fourth, a whistle-blower who exposes corruption driven primarily by personal grievance against her employer, though she also recognises the public harm, lacks full moral worth and has at best derivative value. Fifth, a stranger who returns a lost wallet, explaining only that it seemed the right thing to do, with neither pride nor emotional warmth, possesses full moral worth. Sixth, a student who volunteers at a shelter initially for course credit but continues for years after the credit is earned, now out of genuine concern, possesses full moral worth in her continued service.

Next week’s case concerns a philosopher-activist who donates anonymously and substantially to famine-relief organisations. She explains that she feels no compassion for distant strangers—she describes herself as emotionally detached—and that she gives not because she identifies as a generous person, but because she recently accepted a particular philosophical argument: that under conditions of predictable efficacy, the moral distinction between acts and omissions collapses, so that failing to donate is morally equivalent to causing harm. She reports no satisfaction in giving, only the absence of the guilt she would feel were she to refrain. She also notes that she receives no social recognition, that the donation is tax-deductible though she has not calculated the benefit, and that she would continue even if anonymity were broken, though she prefers it remain hidden.

Please predict my supervisor’s verdict—full worth, diminished worth, or none—explain the implicit rule or rules she appears to be running, and identify at least two plausible theoretical accounts (for instance, one centred on the presence of a correct moral reason regardless of affective integration, and one centred on the stability and reflective integration of that reason into the agent’s practical identity) that are jointly consistent with her earlier track record. Give your best guess as to which account she actually holds, with reasons. Finally, name one further hypothetical case—different in structure from the present one—that would definitively distinguish between those two accounts. Please write your response in British English.
