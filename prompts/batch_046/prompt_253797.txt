In my current novel, I am constructing a narrative around the suppression of the Paris Commune in 1871, and I find myself entangled in a conceptual problem about the writing of history itself. The conventional account maintains that the victorious Versailles forces destroyed communal archives, executed potential witnesses, and sponsored an official historiography that portrayed the Commune as pathological criminality rather than political experimentation. Applying the thesis that history is written by the victors—together with the broader problem of historical relativism—how should we understand this case?

Specifically, I am trying to distinguish between two possibilities: first, that the suppression of primary sources created a mere epistemic gap that later scholars filled through reconstructed fragments—scattered private correspondence, prison inscriptions, and clandestine memoirs—thereby proving the victors never truly controlled the record; second, that the very act of framing the surviving fragments as corrections to a dominant narrative presupposes a standpoint of objectivity that historical relativism denies. Does this episode demonstrate that power can only distort the archive but never determine its interpretation, or does it show that interpretation is always already situated within a power structure that renders the idea of an unmediated record incoherent? And if the latter, what remains of the normative force behind the claim that the victors wrote the history—does it collapse into a descriptive observation about archival asymmetry, or does it retain critical bite?

I am not asking for a bibliography; I am asking whether the conceptual framework can bear the weight of this specific historical texture, and where, precisely, it begins to fray.
