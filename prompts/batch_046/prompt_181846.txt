Write an essay evaluating whether cross-cultural variation in folk moral judgments—particularly the divergence between WEIRD and non-WEIRD populations on individualizing versus binding moral foundations, and on the relative weight given to intention versus outcome in blame—undermines the claim that morality is objectively true. Structure the argument around a distinction that is rarely made explicit in popular treatments: the gap between descriptive divergence (the empirical fact that different cultures report different intuitions) and normative divergence (the claim that these differences persist even after idealized reflection, correction of factual error, and exposure to the full range of human reasons). Defend one of two conclusions: either that moral objectivity survives because normative divergence collapses once we filter out bias and ignorance, leaving a convergent core; or that it does not survive, because the residual divergence tracks genuinely incommensurable frameworks of value that no amount of idealization can adjudicate without begging the question. Whichever verdict you reach, justify it by directly confronting the strongest objection from the opposing side—specifically, the charge that your preferred idealization conditions covertly encode the moral commitments they are meant to test. Do not conclude with a non-committal summary; the essay must take a stand.
