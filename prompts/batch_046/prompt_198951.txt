For an upcoming feature on how mid-century moral philosophy might be re-read through the lens of institutional information design, I want to test a deliberately unconventional thesis. Please argue that Hannah Arendt's account of Adolf Eichmann—specifically her claim that his wrongdoing stemmed less from ideological conviction than from a kind of moral-cognitive vacancy—should be reconceptualized not as an individual psychological deficit but as an emergent structural feature of bureaucratic organizations that filter ethical information to reduce cognitive load. Defend the view that the phenomenon Arendt identified as banal evil is best understood through a model of hierarchical information compression, functionally analogous to lossy data transmission, in which contextual moral details are systematically discarded as signals pass upward through administrative layers. Your argument should explain how this structural interpretation responds to historical scholarship that stresses Eichmann's genuine ideological commitment, clarify what the compression metaphor adds that Arendt's original psychological framing cannot capture, and pinpoint exactly where the model fails—particularly whether it can apply to harmful acts that occur outside large-scale bureaucratic mediation. Keep the reasoning conceptual rather than technical; avoid naming particular archives, historians, or primary texts, and do not retreat to generic observations about organizational complexity, but show precisely why translating Arendt's thesis from individual psychology into distributed institutional cognition changes its philosophical stakes.
