I'm currently taking a seminar on Nietzsche's philosophy of history centered on his essay from the Untimely Meditations that distinguishes monumental, antiquarian, and critical modes of relating to the past. Our instructor has taken clear positions on several cases that I am trying to systematize. She judged the Renaissance revival of republican civic virtue as a healthy monumental use: the past served as a model for future action without becoming mere imitation. She treated medieval monastic manuscript preservation as a necessary but dangerous antiquarian practice, one that maintained continuity yet risked burying present life under the weight of accumulated dead facts. She endorsed the French Revolution's destruction of feudal archives and symbols as justified critical history, accepting the loss of inheritance because it liberated present life from oppressive bonds. By contrast, she condemned a nineteenth-century nationalist movement's reconstruction of a mythic ethnic origin story as a harmful, life-denying monumental history, arguing that it served present vanity and political expansion rather than genuine future creation. Finally, she criticized an individual's obsessive genealogical tracing of every ancestor for pure identity as a harmful antiquarian practice, since it accumulated past data with no orientation toward present action or growth.

Based on this track record, I want to predict how she would evaluate a proposed institutional program—let's call it the Critical Autobiography Archive—in which every university student is required to identify and publicly reject one inherited cultural tradition they deem life-denying, with these rejections compiled into a searchable, algorithmically curated database intended to inspire collective liberation. The case is constructed to sit at the boundary between critical liberation and institutional abstraction.

I'm less interested in a simple verdict than in the reasoning structure behind it. What rule or rules seem to govern her five earlier judgments? I can see at least two consistent interpretations: one that assigns practices to Nietzsche's three categories primarily by their temporal orientation—future-modeling, continuity-preserving, or liberating-destruction—and then filters them by whether they genuinely strengthen present life; and another that weighs the scale and mode of mediation, treating collective, technologically archived, or representational practices as prone to life-denial regardless of intended category, while allowing embodied, action-oriented practices to remain healthy in any mode. Under each rule, what does she say about the Archive—approve it as critical history serving life, or reject it as an abstract collective representation that substitutes database preservation for lived liberation? Which rule do you think she is actually running, and what is the closest analog among her earlier verdicts? Most importantly, what single further case—perhaps an individual, non-archived act of tradition-rejection, or a collective but purely destructive and non-recorded ritual—would best distinguish whether she applies the temporal-orientation rule or the mediation-abstraction rule?
