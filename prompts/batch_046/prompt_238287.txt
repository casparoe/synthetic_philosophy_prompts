I'm drafting a six-month research proposal for a small interdisciplinary fellowship that supports conceptual work at the intersection of philosophy of life and existential phenomenology, and I need help sharpening the framing so that it is both tractable and genuinely open. The precise question is this: if we take Camus-style absurdism not as the denial of all value but as the claim that human projects of justification are structurally mismatched with a silent universe, does this undermine the subjective-conditions component of contemporary hybrid theories of meaning in life—such as the view that meaning requires both objective value and subjective fulfillment—rather than, as is usually assumed, the objective-conditions component alone?

Existing treatments tend to read absurdism as a threat primarily to objective-list theories, on the grounds that cosmic pointlessness strips objectively valuable activities of their normative authority; hybrid theorists then respond by locating meaning in the fitting alignment between subjective attraction and objective value, apparently insulating the subjective side from metaphysical anxiety. I think this settlement is too quick. The proposal would investigate whether the subjective condition—what I will call the experience of normative grip—depends on a kind of strategic opacity about the ultimate groundlessness of one's commitments. If making the absurd explicit introduces what I term epistemic friction into first-person practical reasoning, then the subjective fulfillment required for meaning may either collapse into mere psychological contentment or be forced to borrow authority from the objective conditions in a way that renders the hybrid structure redundant. This is philosophical rather than empirical work: it would analyze the conceptual architecture of hybrid accounts, trace the phenomenology of justification-seeking that absurdism exposes, and construct a model of how normative commitments maintain inertia under conditions of explicit groundlessness.

A useful deliverable would not be a definitive proof that absurdism defeats hybridism, but a precisely articulated dilemma for hybrid theorists: either they must show how subjective fulfillment survives full transparency about cosmic meaninglessness without becoming merely adaptive, or they must concede that the subjective component implicitly relies on a suppressed metaphysics of value, undermining their claim to have reconciled objectivity and subjectivity. A failed outcome would be one that merely rehearses the familiar debate between objective and subjective theories without demonstrating why the absurdist challenge targets the hybrid synthesis specifically. Given the six-month scope, the project should be restricted to analyzing two representative hybrid frameworks—the fitting-fulfillment model and the narrative-identity model—through the lens of epistemic friction, yielding a thirty-page conceptual paper suitable for a specialized journal or workshop, together with a brief briefing for well-being policy researchers on whether existential anxiety is a conceptual threat to meaning-in-life or only a psychological obstacle.
