I am drafting a dissertation chapter on relational personhood and have formulated the following reductio against Ubuntu-inspired accounts that I cannot dissolve. (1) If personhood is constituted by reciprocal recognitional relationships rather than by intrinsic properties of biological individuals, then there is no pre-social substrate that bears moral status independently of communal acknowledgment. (2) Consequently, moral standing is not possessed a priori but is achieved and maintained through ongoing social recognition. (3) From this it follows that the withdrawal of recognition—through exile, systematic ostracism, or conditions of social death—does not merely inflict psychological harm but literally diminishes or suspends the moral status of the excluded party. (4) Yet if diminished moral status is the direct result of exclusion, then a community that enforces segregation or disenfranchisement does not violate the rights of the excluded in the same manner it violates the rights of fully recognized members; rather, it merely updates their relational standing to match the reduced recognition they actually receive. (5) Therefore, relational personhood appears unable to condemn structural exclusion as a direct rights-violation without contradiction, since on its own terms the excluded possess diminished standing precisely because recognition has been withdrawn. (6) To avoid this result, the relationalist must either accept that severe social exclusion is not a moral wrong of the first order, or reintroduce an intrinsic, non-relational core of dignity that the view officially rejects. I am inclined to defend (1) and (3), and I suspect the error lies in the inference from (3) to (4), but I cannot articulate the precise equivocation or scope shift involved. Which step should be rejected, and what does that rejection commit the relational account to? I would prefer not to surrender (1) or (3) if a less costly revision is available.
