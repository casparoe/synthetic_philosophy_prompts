Consider three coexisting regulatory frameworks for commercial sex: the Swedish Sex Purchase Act, which criminalizes clients but not providers and frames the practice as gender-based violence incompatible with equality; the German legalization model, which treats prostitution as lawful labor subject to mandatory registration, health counseling, and brothel licensing requirements; and New Zealand's decriminalization framework, which removes criminal penalties for providers and clients alike and regulates the industry through standard occupational health and workplace safety law.

These regimes appear to share one premise—that the state should not punish the seller—while diverging radically on what that premise means. What implicit theories of autonomy, harm, and gendered moral agency do these frameworks collectively presuppose? Does the Nordic model's refusal to criminalize the seller while criminalizing the buyer presuppose a particular theory of victimhood or structurally compromised consent that is inconsistent with the labor-rights framing in New Zealand? Does the German registration and health-counseling regime presuppose that sexual labor is uniquely dangerous or morally suspect in ways that justify state verification of the worker's body and premises, or can those requirements be justified on purely liberal-paternalist grounds applicable to other risky professions? Reconstruct the philosophical commitments—about whether consent can be fully autonomous under economic duress, whether the state owes neutrality among conceptions of sexual morality, and whether harm is primarily individual or structural—that render each regime internally coherent. Then distinguish what each policy strictly requires from what is merely rhetorical ornament, and describe what a purely contractualist framework—one that treats sexual services as morally equivalent to other personal services—would have to change in institutional design if it replaced the underlying theory of autonomy shared, in different forms, by all three regimes.
