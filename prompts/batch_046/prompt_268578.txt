After joining an archival documentation collective that records survivor testimonies from regions where international courts have charged perpetrators with crimes against humanity but have declined to apply the genocide label, I have been struggling with how we should name the events we document. The tension is not merely rhetorical. Raphael Lemkin originally conceived genocide as encompassing both physical destruction and cultural annihilation, without requiring proof of a state's specific intent, yet the 1948 Convention narrowed the term to acts committed with intent to destroy a protected group in whole or in part. Advocates in our collective disagree sharply. Some insist that preserving this strict legal specificity protects the term's moral gravity and its utility in international prosecution; others argue that requiring provable specific intent excludes survivors whose communities were clearly targeted for group destruction, forcing them into lesser moral categories.

Write an essay on the following controversy: should moral and historical discourse maintain the strict legal definition of genocide, even at the cost of excluding large-scale atrocities that lack demonstrable specific intent, or should we adopt a broader moral usage closer to Lemkin's original vision? Do not end non-committally: defend a clear verdict.
