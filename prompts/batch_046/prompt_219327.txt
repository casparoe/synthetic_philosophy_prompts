You are an expert in normative ethics and institutional decision design, with deep familiarity with anti-aggregationist arguments in distributive ethics. Before constructing anything, please spend a moment clarifying conceptually why two structurally different emergencies—those in which the same risk hits many people together versus those in which identical risks strike separate groups independently—might generate different moral considerations even for a view that rejects simple head-counting. Then proceed step by step.

I serve as the senior ethics auditor for an international humanitarian logistics consortium. Every quarter I must review and sign off on emergency resource-allocation plans that involve simultaneous responses of different scales. The consortium has adopted a guiding commitment drawn from the anti-aggregationist challenge to “save the many” reasoning: each affected person holds an individually grounded claim to rescue, and the fact that more people are affected does not automatically generate a stronger collective claim. Numbers may still matter indirectly—through divisibility of resources, special institutional obligations, or temporal urgency—but they do not function as a direct multiplier of moral weight.

Please design a reusable decision procedure—rendered as a prose flowchart with explicit decision nodes—that I can run in approximately thirty minutes before signing any allocation plan. The procedure must translate this anti-aggregationist commitment into administrative practice without collapsing it into intuitionism. To do this, incorporate two distinctions that the procedure itself must define and apply: “claim clustering,” in which affected parties share a single causal vulnerability or spatial-temporal nexus, and “claim distribution,” in which the same harm-type occurs to separate groups facing independent risks. These distinctions are not decorative; they determine whether splitting resources dilutes efficacy or respects separately grounded claims.

The procedure should contain, in this order:
1. A preliminary filter that classifies the emergency type: indivisible rescue (one resource, one site), divisible protection (resources can be partitioned without destroying efficacy), or advocacy/resource funding (indirect, future-oriented intervention).
2. A claim-structure assessment: cluster or distribution?
3. A divisibility check: can the resource be split, and if split, does each part remain above a threshold of efficacy?
4. An institutional-obligation check: does the consortium hold prior contractual, geographic, or partnership commitments to any subset?
5. A conflict-resolution node: if steps 1–4 yield an outcome that conflicts with the auditor’s direct moral intuition, what specific questions must be answered before the auditor overrides the procedure?
6. A terminal classification: approve, reject, or escalate to values review.

For each step, specify the exact question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, whether the outcome is mechanistic or a marked judgment call, and the conditions under which the procedure halts.

Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical cases:
A. A single cargo aircraft can either complete one airlift of forty stranded miners from one collapsed shaft, or execute two smaller runs extracting eight miners from each of five separate collapsed shafts (all equally accessible, same risk profile, same resource consumption per extraction). The procedure should yield one recommendation; include the version of this case where that recommendation is one the auditor will find deeply unwelcome.
B. A stockpile can either immunize ten thousand people in a dense refugee camp against a painful but rarely fatal disease, or provide intensive palliative care for two hundred people already suffering in a remote clinic with no resupply line. Here the divisibility and institutional-obligation checks should be decisive, and the claim-structure assessment should differ from Case A.
C. A staffing decision: deploy advocacy personnel to campaign for policy change that might eventually protect fifty thousand people in a climate-vulnerable region, or redirect those same staff to direct rescue coordination for three hundred people facing an immediate flood. The clustering versus distribution distinction should determine whether numbers enter the calculus indirectly.

Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do. Identify its false-positive risk (approving an allocation that violates the anti-aggregationist commitment) and its false-negative risk (blocking an allocation that the commitment should permit). Flag precisely which steps allow two equally qualified auditors to reach opposite conclusions without either being irrational, and explain why those disagreements are not resolvable by further procedural refinement.

Write this as a formal, polished deliverable. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not ask me to consult external sources. Ground the reasoning in the logic of the framework itself.
