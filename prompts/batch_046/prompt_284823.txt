A recent textbook gloss summarizes the naturalism versus normativism debate in philosophy of medicine as follows. Christopher Boorse holds that disease is a statistically abnormal reduction in functional ability measured against a species-typical evolutionary design, and that this statistical baseline applies uniformly regardless of local environment or culture. Jerome Wakefield, on his harmful dysfunction analysis, is said to treat social harm as the primary criterion and evolutionary dysfunction merely as supporting evidence, so that cultural stigma can by itself make a condition diseased even when no internal mechanism has failed. From this, the text concludes that naturalism collapses for mental disorders because evolutionary design for psychology is too vague, whereas normativism succeeds precisely by letting social values draw the boundary. How fair is this summary? Separate what correctly captures Boorse's reference-class relativism and Wakefield's two-part structure, what reverses or overstates their priorities, and whether the conclusion about mental disorders actually follows once those corrections are made. In particular, assess whether either account can accommodate conditions that arise only through specific socio-ecological scaffolding—sometimes called niche construction—without importing normative judgments through the back door.
