For an assignment that asks me to render a philosophical problem in verse, please write a villanelle—nineteen lines arranged as five tercets followed by a quatrain, with two refrains that alternate according to the traditional pattern—about the question Plato raises in the Meno: if true belief and knowledge both guide action correctly, why is knowledge said to be more valuable? Let refrain A1 express the idea that true belief is sufficient but unstable, echoing the image of the Daedalus statues that run away unless tied down, and let refrain A2 express the idea that knowledge is belief secured by working out the reason why—what the dialogue describes as an account of the cause. The poem should use the form structurally: the two refrains should take turns through the tercets without resolving, and in the final quatrain they should stand side by side, showing how the two positions keep passing each other. In addition to Plato’s analogy, incorporate the modern swamping problem—the worry that if justification is valuable only as an instrument for reaching truth, then its value is swamped by the value of truth itself, leaving knowledge with no advantage over true belief. The poem should be accurate enough that a student of epistemology would recognize the argument—including the practical equivalence of belief and knowledge, the need for stability, and the instrumental-value objection—rather than merely naming the dialogue. Do not include direct quotations from any source, and do not provide citations, footnotes, or a reading list. I need the verse to work as a poem in its own right while also doing the philosophical labor I have described.
