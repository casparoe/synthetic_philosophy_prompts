My teacher in our government seminar has ruled on six institutional-design cases. First, she endorsed replacing our municipal council entirely with citizens selected by lot for a single non-renewable four-year term, arguing that career politicians accumulate distorting incentives. Second, she rejected a proposal for strict two-term limits on elected councilors, saying turnover without selection-method change merely reshuffles the same pool of ambitious candidates. Third, she approved a hybrid council in which one-third of seats are filled by lot for one term and two-thirds remain elected without term limits, describing it as an acceptable compromise if pure sortition is too abrupt. Fourth, she rejected pure sortition for our school board, reasoning that educational governance requires institutional memory and stakeholder continuity. Fifth, she supported mandatory retirement at age seventy for appointed commissioners but opposed it for elected officials, insisting that the legitimacy of selection—not age—should govern tenure rules. Sixth, she permitted career politicians to sit on advisory boards with deliberative but no binding authority, framing this as using expertise without concentrating decision-making power.

Our assignment presents a new proposal for the city legislature: a bicameral body in which Chamber A is filled entirely by lot with citizens serving staggered five-year terms that are non-renewable and exclude re-selection, while Chamber B is elected with no term limits; legislation requires passage in both chambers, and Chamber A may veto a bill twice, triggering an automatic popular referendum if the veto is sustained.

Predict her verdict, identifying whether she is implicitly running a rule about selection-method purity, about term-length and non-renewability, about the permissible proximity of career politicians to binding power, or about hybrid pragmatism. Which of her six earlier rulings is the closest analog for each interpretation? If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, specify the principle she would be adopting with approval versus with rejection. Finally, propose one further institutional design—perhaps a unicameral body with two-year sortition terms and unlimited eligibility for re-selection by lot—that would best distinguish her true underlying rule.
