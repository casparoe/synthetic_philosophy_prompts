Analyse the three canonical failures of Athenian direct democracy—the assembly’s reversal over the fate of Mytilene in 427 BCE, the vote to launch the Sicilian Expedition in 415 BCE, and the trial of Socrates in 399 BCE—through the conceptual lens of deliberative path-dependence: the thesis that the procedural architecture of a collective decision-making body does not merely aggregate pre-existing preferences but actively constructs the cognitive pathways available to its members, thereby locking in particular framings of risk, justice, and authority while systematically excluding alternatives. For each case, identify the specific institutional mechanism at work—the compressed temporal structure of the Mytilenean debate, the absence of strategic review bodies in the expedition’s planning, and the combination of non-anonymous jury voting with unrestricted accusatorial rhetoric in the trial—and show precisely how that mechanism shaped the epistemic options open to the citizens. Then evaluate whether this framework genuinely explains why a highly participatory polity repeatedly produced catastrophic or unjust outcomes, or whether it neglects the decisive role of individual demagogy, contingency, and external strategic misjudgement. Finally, state whether these failures should be read as intrinsic pathologies of direct democratic procedure correctable only by institutional redesign, or as anomalies compatible with an essentially sound collective epistemology, and specify what philosophical conception of democratic knowledge—procedural, aggregative, or deference-based—each interpretation presupposes.
