I am currently working through Dennett's critique of the Cartesian theatre and his multiple drafts model of consciousness, particularly in relation to empirical findings such as change blindness and masked priming. In discussions with fellow students, I keep encountering a specific pattern that I do not have a name for. People will explicitly deny that there is a central observer or a single screen where experience comes together, and they will accept that the brain produces many parallel drafts of sensory processing. Yet when we turn to a concrete case—say, a masked word that influences a subject's behaviour without being reported, or a Libet-style experiment measuring readiness potentials—those same people ask questions that presuppose a singular, authoritative version of the experience. They want to know the precise moment when consciousness registered the stimulus, or they speak of one particular draft being the one that was actually experienced, as if there were a definitive selection event that promotes it from pre-conscious to conscious status. They reject the spatial idea of a theatre, but they seem to retain its functional architecture: one canonical draft, a specific point of entry into awareness, and a clear boundary between what is in and what is out. I have noticed this not only in classroom debate but also in popular explanations of neuroscience that endorse distributed processing yet still talk about the brain becoming aware at a particular millisecond.

Does this configuration have an established name in the philosophy of mind? Is it recognised as a specific failure mode of applying Dennett's model—perhaps a kind of residual Cartesian materialism without the spatial commitment—or is it closer to a misinterpretation of his notion of drafts achieving cerebral celebrity? If it is not a named phenomenon, how ought one to characterise it precisely, and what distinguishes it from ordinary inconsistency or from legitimate empirical questions about the temporal dynamics of reportability? I would be grateful for an answer in British English that lays out the nearest established concepts, explains where this pattern fits relative to each, and, should no adequate term exist, proposes a definition with clear boundary conditions.
