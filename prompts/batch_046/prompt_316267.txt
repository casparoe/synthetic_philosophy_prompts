I've been thinking about Franklin's project of moral perfection from the Autobiography—specifically his 13 virtues and the idea that you tackle them in order because temperance helps with silence, etc. I tried to formalize why his ordering might actually trap him, and now I think the argument proves too much because it seems to show you can never start self-improvement at all.

Here's the argument:
1. Franklin's method requires tracking faults in each virtue using a little notebook, which means you need enough humility and self-knowledge to record errors honestly.
2. But Franklin puts Humility last, arguing you can't truly be humble until you've already made progress on the other twelve.
3. So to begin tracking faults in Temperance (the first virtue), you already need the Humility that only comes after mastering all twelve.
4. Therefore the entry conditions for Franklin's system require having already completed the system.
5. The same loop applies to any linear virtue-training program: recognizing a deficit in stage N requires the meta-virtue that belongs to stage N+1, so there's always a hidden prerequisit that hasn't been met.
6. So either Franklin never actually started (which is false—he did keep the book), or linear moral-development models are incoherent.
7. Therefore Franklin's ordering must be arbitrary, and with it collapses any idea that moral virtues can be cultivated sequencially.

I want to keep (1) and (4) if I can, because I like the idea that self-monitoring has hidden prerequisites. But (6) feels wrong—Franklin clearly started somewhere. Where's the slip? Is it an equivocation between 'having some humility' and 'having perfected humility'? Or is there a quantifier shift between 'for each virtue' and 'for all virtues together'? If I reject (3), what do I have to pay—do I have to say humility isn't a virtue that depends on prior ones, or that Franklin's ordering was just practical not logical? I'd rather not recieve the loop entirely if I can avoid it.
