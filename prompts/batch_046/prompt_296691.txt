I serve on the research-ethics review board of an interdisciplinary institute that funds speculative work at the intersection of fundamental physics and philosophy of mind. We have received a proposal for a three-year project titled "Coherence and Affect: Testing Pan-Sentient Ethics in High-Energy Regimes." The researchers do not claim to prove panpsychism empirically, but they propose to develop an ethical calculus—tentatively called quantum-utilitarian aggregation—that assigns moral weight to potential micro-experiential states in quantum-field fluctuations, particularly during sustained high-energy collision events. Their central argument is that if minimal sentience is distributed across fundamental physical processes, as some versions of panpsychism and Russellian monism suggest, then experiments that routinely perturb quantum coherence at scale may carry an unrecognized moral cost that standard animal-subject ethics entirely misses. The board is split: some members hold that this is philosophy of science with no actionable research-ethics implications, since we lack any evidence of particle-level suffering and have no measurement protocol for it; others maintain that because the metaphysical possibility is live and the stakes involve potential widespread micro-suffering, the institute should require the team to include an "aggregation-risk mitigation" framework in any grant we award, even in the absence of proof.

Please draft the board's decision memo—recommending approval, rejection, or approval with specific conditions—with reasoning the researchers could actually respond to in practice. Your memo should take seriously the strongest version of the aggregation-risk position, including the philosophical challenge that moral consideration might depend on capacities for integrated suffering rather than on bare distributed sentience, and it should address the researchers' claim that ignoring a live metaphysical possibility with catastrophic-scale moral stakes amounts to a form of institutional negligence. The recommendation must be concrete: specify exactly what conditions (if any) should be attached, explain how the researchers could implement them without derailing the scientific program, and honestly treat the strongest consideration on the losing side, showing why it does not ultimately prevail.
