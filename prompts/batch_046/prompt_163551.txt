At a recent graduate seminar on philosophical probability, two fellow students got into an extended disagreement about whether Bertrand's paradox undermines the principle of indifference in continuous parameter spaces. I was present and did not take sides. Their exchange went as follows.

The first student argued that the paradox demonstrates the principle's incoherence for continuous spaces: selecting a random chord in a circle yields probabilities of 1/4, 1/3, or 1/2 depending solely on parameterization—by endpoint positions on the circumference, by radial distance from the center, or by midpoint position inside the circle—and since nothing internal to the problem privileges one parameterization, the principle licenses contradictory probabilities for the same event.

The second student replied that this misdiagnoses the error: the principle requires application over the correct symmetry class. Endpoint selection respects the circle's rotational and translational symmetry, whereas radial-distance selection breaks rotational invariance by treating directions differently. Hence 1/3 is the uniquely rational assignment, and the other values are artifacts of smuggling in arbitrary coordinate choices.

The first student countered that symmetry-respecting is itself contested: the circle also possesses reflection and scaling symmetries that different parameterizations preserve to different degrees. Selecting endpoint-position as privileged already assumes a prior about which symmetries are mathematically relevant rather than physically accidental. Without an independent criterion for weighting symmetries, the appeal to symmetry merely relocates arbitrariness.

The second student then introduced a physical-selection argument: if long sticks are thrown randomly onto the circle from a distant source with uniformly distributed orientation and position, the chord-length distribution converges to the radial-distance parameterization, yielding 1/2. This shows the parameterization tracks a real physical process, and probabilistic intuitions should align with empirically realizable selection mechanisms.

The first student responded that this conflates mathematics with contingent physics. Changing the physical process—throwing darts inside the circle and extending perpendicular lines—produces 1/4, and altering angular momentum distributions can approximate 1/3. Since any parameterization can be matched to some physically possible mechanism, the physical argument doesn't privilege one mathematical result; it only shows different causal stories generate different distributions.

The second student concluded by proposing a meta-indifference resolution: apply indifference not to chord-selection methods directly, but over the space of admissible parameterizations weighted by the descriptive simplicity of the required coordinate system. The simplest parameterization—endpoint selection on a one-dimensional boundary—should carry the highest prior weight, making 1/3 the rational default.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the five arguments above, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Identify what, if anything, both are missing about whether there is a genuinely non-arbitrary application of the principle of indifference in geometric probability, or whether the paradox reveals a deeper structural limitation. In your answer, clearly separate what is settled from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers and mathematicians working on indifference and geometric probability; do not present any single resolution of Bertrand's paradox as the consensus view where one does not exist.
