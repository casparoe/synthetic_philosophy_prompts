I am a doctoral student in philosophy and medical humanities, currently writing a dissertation chapter on the institutional operationalization of care ethics. I have reconstructed the decision logic of a hospital Care Ethics Consultation Service (CECS) that applies a care-ethical framework to conflicts over withdrawal, delegation, and obligation. The service uses an internal distinction—rarely articulated in standard literature—between status-based obligations, which derive from legal designation, kinship, or professional role, and practice-based obligations, which derive from the sustained performance of care labor within an interdependent relationship. From its anonymized case summaries between 2018 and 2023, I have extracted the following four precedents on withdrawal and delegation.

Case One: A daughter is the legal next-of-kin for her father, who has advanced dementia in a nursing facility. She refuses all contact and care-planning participation, citing severe emotional and physical abuse during her childhood. The service ruled that the history of harm extinguishes any practice-based obligation to provide direct care or emotional presence; her status-based obligation is limited to non-obstruction—she must not block institutional decisions or withhold medically relevant history, but she may withdraw entirely.

Case Two: A privately hired live-in caregiver requests immediate termination with a patient who has become repeatedly verbally abusive and racially hostile. The service ruled that her professional status generates a status-based obligation of orderly transition—reasonable notice and assistance in arranging replacement—but does not generate an indefinite practice-based obligation to endure abuse. Once transition is secured, withdrawal is permissible.

Case Three: A neighbor who, for three years, voluntarily assisted an elderly person with groceries, transportation, and companionship seeks to end all involvement after the elder moves to assisted living. The service ruled that, absent kinship, legal designation, or sustained intimate interdependence, neither a status-based nor a practice-based obligation survives institutionalization. The neighbor may withdraw completely, subject only to a minimal transitional duty of alerting staff to immediate concerns.

Case Four: A spouse is the designated healthcare proxy for a partner with advanced dementia. Citing severe emotional exhaustion and declining health, she requests to resign the proxy role so that a court-appointed guardian may assume it. The service ruled that the formal designation creates a status-based procedural obligation that can be relinquished only if a qualified replacement is available. Because no replacement existed, she must retain decision-making authority, though she may withdraw from all direct care labor and receive institutional support (e.g., social-work mediation) to fulfill her procedural role.

The new case before the service is as follows. M and J formed a close chosen-family bond over eight years, living together and providing mutual care, though they never married or formalized the relationship legally. Three years ago, when J began experiencing cognitive decline, J executed a durable power of attorney for healthcare that revoked any prior informal designations and named J's estranged adult child—who had been absent for fifteen years—as proxy. J then entered a long-term care facility. Despite the revocation, M continued weekly visits, staff advocacy, and coordination of J's non-medical needs for the next two and a half years. Six months ago, M relocated for employment and ceased all contact. Now J has lost all decision-making capacity, and the facility requires immediate consent for a time-sensitive but non-emergency surgical repair of a hip fracture that affects mobility. The designated proxy has not responded to repeated contact attempts over eight weeks. M, now living in the same city again, has returned and offers to resume advocacy and to assume proxy decision-making, arguing that J's revocation was executed during a period of paranoia associated with early dementia, and that M's sustained practice-based care creates an obligation to prevent abandonment. The facility asks the CECS whether M should be encouraged or permitted to resume the proxy role, whether M bears a moral obligation to do so, and how the service should advise if M declines.

Assuming the CECS aims to be consistent with its own precedents, how should it rule on M's position? Please identify the closest analog among Cases One through Four, articulate the implicit rule that analog supports—whether it privileges formal revocation as a relational rupture, treats the absence of a functioning proxy as sustaining procedural duty, allows practice-based obligations to survive institutionalization and revocation, or applies some other pattern—and state what the service should recommend regarding M's involvement and the facility's course of action. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, specify the principle the service would be adopting with each possible ruling (for example, privileging status-based designation over practice-based history, or treating sustained care labor as generating a delegable but not extinguishable obligation), and name the further hypothetical case that would distinguish between those competing principles.
