I am drafting a novel about the architecture of national memory, and I find myself unable to articulate the pattern behind my own moral intuitions regarding what I have come to call defeat narratives—the stories societies tell when they have lost militarily or politically but must preserve dignity. I need a principle, if one exists, that captures the distinctions I keep drawing, because my characters are modeled on these very structures of remembrance.

Here are my verdicts on concrete historical instances, which I ask you to treat as data points.

First, the American Lost Cause mythology that crystallized after 1865: it transforms a clear military defeat into a narrative of cultural purity betrayed by external aggression. My judgment is that this is not an authentic elegy but a revisionist denial; the defeat is not mourned, it is displaced onto an enemy. The myth is toxic precisely because it refuses to acknowledge the loss as a loss.

Second, the story of Masada in 73 CE, where the Zealots chose collective death over Roman submission. Here I think we have a genuine lost cause: the defeat is fully acknowledged, and the death is embraced as an active refusal rather than a passive failure. The myth is ethically ambiguous—it glorifies suicide—but it does not lie about the outcome.

Third, the Kosovo Field narrative of 1389, in which a Serbian defeat by Ottoman forces becomes a centuries-long martyrdom engine defining national identity. I classify this as a necessary illusion: the defeat is real, yet the myth converts it into a spiritual victory that licenses future violence. It is authentic in its grief but dangerous in its temporal orientation, because it turns a past loss into a permanent claim on the future.

Fourth, Dunkirk in 1940. I am firm that this is not a lost cause narrative at all. The evacuation is framed as a triumph of communal endurance, and any elegiac content is almost entirely absent. It is strategic withdrawal narrated as moral victory.

Fifth, the fall of Constantinople in 1453 and the associated legend of the sleeping emperor who will one day return. On some days I read this as a pure elegy for an empire honestly mourned; on others, I see it as a deferred triumph myth structurally akin to Kosovo, in which defeat is only provisional. I keep reversing my judgment, and this is the case that most disturbs my taxonomy.

To test any principle you propose, I also ask you to apply it to a fictional case drawn from my novel. In the Republic of Luria, the founding story is not a battle lost but a ritualized silent retreat: soldiers abandon a fortress not in panic but in perfect, disciplined order, turning tactical failure into an aesthetic of absence. There is no enemy vilified, no future victory promised, and no collective death embraced—only a formalized departure that the culture remembers as its purest expression of self-possession.

Is there a coherent account—perhaps concerning the relationship between acknowledged defeat and the temporal orientation of the myth, or between the presence of an external enemy and the internalization of loss—that delivers all five historical verdicts without collapsing them into one another? Which of my judgments, if any, must be sacrificed for simplicity? And what does your principle predict for Luria: is it a lost cause, a non-elegiac withdrawal like Dunkirk, or something that breaks the taxonomy entirely?

Please state your principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, note where it forces me to overturn one of my intuitions, and predict the status of the Lurian retreat.
