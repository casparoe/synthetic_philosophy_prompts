Please argue that the bootstrapping problem for reliabilism dissolves once we separate calibration debt from epistemic amortization. Respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. First, define calibration debt as the circular justification initially required to endorse a cognitive faculty's reliability, and distinguish it sharply from the justification of particular outputs produced by that faculty. Second, explain epistemic amortization as the distribution of a single non-circular calibration event's justificatory weight across many subsequent beliefs, so that later outputs do not repeat the circular step. Third, show that so-called easy knowledge—such as knowing that one has hands—does not generate new calibration debt once amortization is underway, because the belief's warrant derives from the prior calibration rather than an independent faculty-check. Fourth, reply to the objection that amortization still presupposes a prior belief in the faculty's reliability by treating that prior commitment as a practical postulate rather than a piece of propositional knowledge. Fifth, conclude that bootstrapping is therefore an economic problem about the allocation of justificatory resources across time, not a logical circularity that undermines reliabilism, but insist that this resolution commits the reliabilist to a dynamic, resource-sensitive model of justification.
