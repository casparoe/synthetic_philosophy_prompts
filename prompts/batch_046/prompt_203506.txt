In my seminar on moral psychology and ethics, we have been working through Bernard Williams's integrity objection and the problem he identified as involving one thought too many—specifically, the corruption that occurs when consequentialist calculation intrudes upon relationships and ground projects that ought to be governed by direct attachment. Our professor has evaluated five student case analyses with a consistent but subtle pattern.

Her verdicts so far:
(1) A husband saves his wife from drowning rather than two strangers, acting from direct emotional attachment without weighing lives; she judged this as integrity fully intact.
(2) The same rescue choice, but made after an explicit calculation that the emotional cost to him of losing his spouse exceeds the moral value of saving two strangers; she judged this as reasoning corrupted—not wrong in outcome, but wrong in mental route, with integrity undermined by the weighing.
(3) A journalist refuses to reveal a confidential source despite legal pressure and potential harm to others, explaining that protecting sources is constitutive of who she is; she approved, noting that ground projects can justify refusal without any consequentialist balancing.
(4) A physician declines to perform unnecessary surgery on a demanding wealthy patient, stating that being someone who does not cause harm is central to her identity; she approved, treating this as a direct expression of a ground project rather than a calculated trade-off.
(5) A parent refuses to donate a kidney to save a stranger's child, appealing only to bodily autonomy and family loyalty without linking these to any broader, life-defining commitment; she was skeptical, writing that without a genuine ground project the refusal risks being mere preference, which does not earn the protection of integrity.

The case for next week's discussion is this: A composer has spent thirty years developing a musical form devoted exclusively to expressing grief without resolution or consolation. She is offered an enormous commission to adapt this form into an evidence-based therapeutic protocol for trauma recovery; clinical trials suggest it would substantially reduce suffering. She refuses, explaining that if she turns grief into a healing instrument, she ceases to be someone who bears witness to it, and that this refusal is itself part of her ground project. She reaches this judgment with full awareness of the suffering her refusal allows to continue, and she explicitly rejects framing the choice in terms of costs and benefits.

Please predict her verdict—would she see this as integrity intact, or as some reverse corruption of reasoning? Identify the precise rule she seems to be running: is it about the absence of consequentialist weighing in justification, about consistency with a self-defining commitment regardless of external costs, or about the non-instrumentalizability of certain practices? Which of her five prior evaluations best distinguishes these competing interpretations? Finally, describe the single hypothetical case that would settle whether her view requires ground projects to be genuinely identity-constituting or merely requires that they not be defended through explicit cost-benefit analysis.
