I sit on the Cabinet Office's National Metrics Advisory Panel, and following an extended argument with a close friend who advises another ministry, I must circulate a formal opinion before Thursday's meeting. The Department for Levelling Up has proposed that the government adopt the World Happiness Report country rankings as a binding strategic target—specifically, committing resources to reach the top five within a decade and tying departmental performance reviews partly to year-on-year rank improvement.

My friend contends that these rankings primarily capture life-evaluation responses that are culturally calibrated and therefore not comparable across societies; that they conflate experienced well-being with reported satisfaction; and that making them a policy target will incentivise cosmetic interventions (short-term mood management) over structural ones (security, autonomy, social trust). She also argues that even if the metric were perfect, a state's legitimate aim is not to maximise aggregate happiness but to secure the conditions for citizens to pursue their own conceptions of flourishing, and that treating a rank as an end in itself inverts that relationship.

Please draft the panel's decision memo—addressed to the Permanent Secretary, recommending either adoption, rejection, or adoption with strict conditions—including a concrete course of action with an explicit time frame or trigger. The memo should treat my friend's position with intellectual honesty: identify the single strongest consideration she raises and explain why it does or does not defeat the proposal. It should also clarify whether there is a defensible interpretation of 'happiness' that the report actually tracks, and whether a government ought to treat that interpretation as a legitimate object of policy. Write the memo in British English, with a formal, polished register appropriate to Whitehall.
