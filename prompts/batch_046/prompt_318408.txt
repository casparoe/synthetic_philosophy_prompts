I spent thirty years as a field ecologist studying population dynamics and community assembly before retiring and turning, somewhat unexpectedly, to moral philosophy. I have arrived at a position that feels almost certainly wrong, yet I cannot locate its precise point of failure. The view is this: widespread habitat destruction and ecosystem fragmentation, while undeniably catastrophic for biodiversity, evolutionary potential, and ecological function, are not straightforwardly harmful from the standpoint of aggregate wild-animal welfare, and in many documented scenarios they actually reduce total suffering. The reasoning turns on trophic dynamics and density-dependent regulation. When apex predators are extirpated and primary productivity collapses, the reproductive surpluses that typically generate boom-and-bust cycles—followed by mass starvation, parasitic overload, and intense predation—are sharply curtailed. Surviving populations stabilize at lower densities with truncated life histories, reduced juvenile recruitment, and diminished per-capita exposure to chronic stressors. One is then multiplying a lower average suffering-intensity by a far smaller number of lives, which may yield a net reduction in total welfare burden compared to an intact ecosystem teeming with prey that briefly flourish and then die in large numbers. This strikes me as empirically compatible with the literature on trophic cascades and demographic bottlenecks, though I recognize that conservation biology almost never measures welfare, only persistence or abundance.

What I want is an honest map of what would genuinely count against this claim—not rhetorical appeals to the intrinsic value of wilderness, but the specific conceptual distinctions, empirical patterns, or formal results that would undermine it. For instance, does the argument illicitly aggregate subjective states across taxa with radically different nervous architectures and temporal profiles of experience? Would long-term evolutionary trajectories in degraded habitats eventually recreate suffering-intensive dynamics through mesopredator release or novel pathogen regimes? Are there well-documented cases in which fragmentation elevates chronic physiological stress, immunosuppression, or prolonged injury in survivors in ways that outweigh the welfare gains from reduced population size? I am also concerned that the view may have been quietly insulated: if every observed reduction in suffering is dismissed as a temporary transitional state, while every observed increase is treated as the true equilibrium, then the claim risks unfalsifiability in precisely the domain—welfare—where falsifiability should matter most. I want to know whether that insulation is a genuine philosophical defect for a thesis of this kind, and whether a defender ought to specify in advance what demographic or behavioral signature would count as decisive refutation.

Please distinguish fatal defeaters from merely awkward ones, and say explicitly which objections a committed proponent of this welfare-calculus view would concede as damaging and which they would dismiss as category mistakes. I am particularly interested in whether my background as a natural scientist leads me to over-weight population-level metrics and under-weight the moral significance of individual lives that are truncated or never realized, and whether that bias is correctable without abandoning the empirical core of the argument. I am not asking for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists—only for the structure of the view's actual exposure, and for an assessment of how well-insulated it truly is.
