I have recently found myself caught between a longstanding institutional obligation and what feels like an incommensurable personal calling, which led me to join a small reading group working through Kierkegaard’s Fear and Trembling. Last week, two members—Maya and Jonas—got into an extended argument about the teleological suspension of the ethical that I have not been able to settle. I will lay out their exchange as precisely as I can recall; I am genuinely unsure where the error, if any, lies.

Maya opened by arguing that Abraham’s suspension of the ethical is not an arbitrary exemption but a structural feature of faith’s singular relation to the absolute. Because the ethical is the universal and communicable, any attempt to justify the binding of Isaac within ethical language—say, by appealing to a general duty to obey God—already collapses the singular call back into universality. Abraham’s silence before Sarah, Isaac, and the community is therefore not a failure of reason but the necessary correlate of an act that transcends justification. The knight of faith, she added, returns to the finite with joy precisely because the ethical is not destroyed but preserved in paradoxical suspension, like a melody held in abeyance and then resumed in an altered key.

Jonas replied that this makes silence a symptom of moral failure rather than transcendence. If an act genuinely suspends the ethical, it must still be rationally assessable from within the suspended sphere; otherwise suspension is merely a euphemism for breaking rules without reasons. Abraham’s inability to explain himself shows he lacks justification altogether, not that he possesses a higher one. Furthermore, Jonas argued, if the suspension is truly incommunicable, then the community has no criterion to distinguish a knight of faith from a delusional father who hears voices. That renders the concept practically useless: anyone facing a conflict between duty and personal vocation—including, he noted, situations like mine—would have no safeguard against self-deception.

Maya countered that Jonas is importing a Kantian demand for public justification that Kierkegaard’s pseudonym Johannes de Silentio explicitly rejects. The ethical sphere demands communicability; faith operates in the sphere of the singular and incommunicable. The risk of self-deception is not an objection but the very condition of faith: Johannes admires Abraham precisely because he cannot understand him. The suspension does not provide a decision procedure; it demonstrates the limits of ethical universalism and shows that if one breaks the universal for the absolute, one does so without comfort or communal validation.

Jonas then pressed a further point. Even if we grant this structure, Maya has smuggled in an unstated normative premise: that singularity trumps universality only when the singular is directed toward God. But Kierkegaard offers no independent criterion for identifying such a direction. Without one, Abraham’s act is indistinguishable from irrational violence justified by private conviction. Jonas concluded that for anyone personally facing a vocational rupture—again, he gestured toward my situation—the suspension offers not guidance but a dangerous invitation to confuse psychological intensity with divine command.

Maya’s final response was that Jonas conflates descriptive structure with prescriptive license. The teleological suspension describes what happens when the universal is relativized by the absolute relation; it does not license every subjective impulse. The burden of the knight is precisely that he receives back the finite by virtue of the absurd, not by virtue of his own will. If I am torn between professional duty and a singular calling, the lesson is not that I should suspend the ethical, but that if I do, I must bear the incommunicable burden alone, without claiming justification.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of Maya’s and Jonas’s contributions, rate whether it is clearly mistaken, largely correct, or somewhere in between, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing—whether a distinction between Johannes de Silentio’s voice and Kierkegaard’s own position, whether a problem with the analogy between Abraham’s trial and modern vocational conflict, or something else entirely. I am not asking which side I should take in my own situation; I am asking where the arguments actually stand.
