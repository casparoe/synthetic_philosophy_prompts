The proposal concerns Ian Hacking’s idea of looping effects—the process by which psychiatric classifications can alter the behaviour and self-understanding of the people classified, which in turn modifies the classification itself. Most philosophical discussion applies this framework to canonical historical cases, such as multiple personality disorder or autism spectrum conditions. I want to propose a more narrowly focused investigation: whether Hacking’s model requires revision when applied to recently codified diagnostic categories—specifically prolonged grief disorder and gaming disorder—where patient communities are not passive recipients of a label but actively generate counter-diagnostic narratives through digital platforms and peer-led forums. The precise research question is whether these contemporary cases exhibit a different causal structure of looping, which I tentatively call contested looping, in which classification triggers organised resistance rather than assimilation, and whether this undermines the predictive or explanatory utility of Hacking’s original framework for modern psychiatric taxonomy.

Please set out in the proposal exactly why current answers leave this open: standard applications of looping effects treat the classified population as largely reactive, and empirical mental-health research tends to measure prevalence or symptom change rather than conceptual structure. A philosophical project is therefore better placed to make progress, because it can analyse the logical and causal architecture of the loop without requiring clinical trials or epidemiological datasets. Recommend a specific conceptual approach—comparing the strong looping model, in which classification reshapes identity, against the proposed contested looping model, in which classification provokes counter-narrative formation—and explain why this comparison is preferable to either a purely historical case-study method or a normative ethics of diagnosis. Define what a useful result would look like: a clear typology with criteria that allow researchers or policy makers to determine which mode is operating in a given domain, together with an honest account of failure, namely if the distinction collapses into a mere description of social conflict with no distinctive philosophical purchase. Finally, outline a realistic six-month scope: conceptual mapping and familiarisation in months one to two, framework development in months three to four, and drafting of the deliverable—a conceptual paper of approximately twenty pages, suitable for a philosophy-of-science journal or a policy briefing—in months five to six.
