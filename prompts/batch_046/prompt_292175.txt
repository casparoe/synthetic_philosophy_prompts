I am drafting a scene in my novel in which two acquaintances—Elias, an engineer inclined toward determinism, and Sera, a philosopher of action—debate the sphex wasp’s rigid burrow-checking loop and what it reveals about mechanical rigidity, flexibility, and free will. Their reconstructed exchange ran as follows.

Elias opened by observing that the wasp’s behavior—dragging prey to the burrow, entering to check, and repeating the check endlessly if the prey is moved—exemplifies sphexishness: a deterministic subroutine that never updates. He argued, however, that this demonstrates determinism and freedom are not opposed. If one imagines a slightly more complex wasp with a feedback mechanism, it breaks the loop without invoking any non-physical cause. Rigidity, he said, is merely low representational complexity, not proof that determinism precludes free will.

Sera replied that this misses the essential point. The wasp is not pursuing a goal; it is executing a trigger-response pair. Its endless repetition reveals the absence of goal-directed autonomy—the capacity to suspend a subroutine in light of a broader aim. Free will, she insisted, requires exactly that suspension. So mechanical rigidity is not just simplicity; it is the absence of autonomous goal-direction.

Elias countered that Sera draws a false binary between “subroutines” and “goals.” Human habits—locking doors, brushing teeth—are subroutines too. The difference between us and the wasp is quantitative: more nested representational layers, not a magical qualitative break. If flexibility is only added complexity, then free will remains compatible with determinism; it is determinism with more variables.

Sera conceded the nesting but argued it solves nothing. If every “correction” is itself triggered by a prior state, one has only built more elaborate clockwork. True flexibility, she proposed, requires normative self-correction—the ability to evaluate not merely whether a behavior works, but whether one ought to continue it. The wasp cannot ask “should I check again?”; it simply checks. Humans can ask that, but only because we internalize linguistic norms, which are themselves deterministic cultural programs. Thus even our “autonomy” may be high-complexity sphexishness masked by language.

Elias responded that Sera had smuggled in an unexplained faculty. “Normative self-correction” sounds like a separate ingredient, yet if real it must operate through some mechanism—neural, computational, or social. He offered an alternative: understand flexibility as counterfactual robustness, the capacity to maintain functional coherence across perturbations. By this measure, a human who breaks a harmful habit after reflection runs a richer counterfactual model rather than escaping mechanism. The wasp fails not because it lacks a metaphysical ingredient, but because its architecture lacks robustness.

Sera granted that counterfactual robustness captures adaptability, but insisted it misses reason-responsiveness. The wasp’s failure is not merely that it breaks under perturbation; it is that it possesses no representational model of the burrow’s contents at all. A flexible agent adjusts in light of reasons, not merely in reaction to disturbance. If reason-responsiveness reduces to robustness in a richer architecture, then free will becomes a matter of engineering complexity. But that flattens the moral distinction we care about: we do not blame the wasp for looping, yet we blame a human who repeats harm despite possessing the architecture to correct herself. That asymmetry suggests something more than complexity—perhaps ownership of one’s normative framework.

Elias closed by saying that “ownership” is just robustness applied to self-referential representations. The moral asymmetry Sera identifies is practical, not metaphysical: we blame humans because we can predict that blame will trigger corrective mechanisms in them. With the wasp, blame is causally futile because its architecture lacks the relevant feedback loops. That is a difference in causal efficacy, not in kind.
