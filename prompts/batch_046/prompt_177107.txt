Write a final-examination essay question for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on nineteenth-century philosophy, culture, and religion, together with a model answer and an integrated marking scheme, all composed as a single continuous prose text in British English with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as though written in a formal letter. The question should address Søren Kierkegaard's analysis of the press and the public, particularly as developed in his 1846 review En literair Anmeldelse, published as Two Ages: A Literary Review, and in related journal reflections on the Corsair affair, but it must not be answerable by mere summary of those texts. Instead, introduce a hypothetical institutional form — a deliberative platform on which contributions are made without any named author, are ordered and made visible solely by an aggregated quantitative score that updates in real time, and are never attributed to an individual — and ask the student whether this institution represents, for Kierkegaard, an intensification of the public he already diagnosed or a structurally different phenomenon altogether. The student should be required to defend their answer by carefully distinguishing the levelling effect of numerical abstraction from simple anonymity, by explaining why the destruction of qualitative decision through quantitative comparison is central to Kierkegaard's critique, and by assessing whether the addition of instantaneous reflexive feedback introduces a new form of the reflexive communication he associates with modern publicity. The model answer should set out the specific conceptual moves that earn full marks — for instance, recognising that the public for Kierkegaard is not merely a large audience but an abstract power that makes everyone responsible for nothing; noting that anonymity in the platform removes the individual even as an object of ridicule, which differs from the press's creation of a phantom collective; and evaluating whether real-time ranking produces a more extreme form of levelling or transforms the temporal structure of reflection — and should also specify, woven into the same continuous prose, the errors that should cost marks, such as conflating Kierkegaard's concept of the public with any anonymous crowd, treating the press as a purely technological cause rather than a structural condition of modern reflective culture, or ignoring the distinction between the passionate decision of the single individual and the passive approval of the aggregated score. State clearly that the examination allows ninety minutes, that answers should be approximately 1,200 words, that thirty marks are available, and that students must not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; if specific passages are referenced they should be paraphrased without quotation marks rather than quoted from memory. Keep the entire response in continuous prose.
