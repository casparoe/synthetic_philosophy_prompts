Question: Bernard Williams distinguishes internal reasons, which depend on an agent's subjective motivational set (S), from external reasons, which are said to apply regardless of S provided they can be reached by a sound deliberative route. Imagine an agent A who has no present or derivable concern for her own health and no motivation that could rationally generate such a concern. Does A have an external reason to stop smoking? In answering, explain Williams's position on whether genuinely external reasons exist, clarify what the sound deliberative route requires and what it excludes, and apply the framework to institutional obligations—specifically, whether a soldier who values loyalty but has no independent desire to obey this particular lawful order can have an internal reason to obey.

Student's answer:

Williams maintains that all reasons for action are internal: an agent has a reason to φ only if there is a sound deliberative route connecting φ to something in her subjective motivational set S. If agent A lacks any motivation related to health, and if no chain of reasoning, imagination, or practical reflection could rationally produce such a motivation, then A has no reason—internal or external—to stop smoking. Williams rejects genuinely external reasons because to say A ought to quit despite having no motivational connection is either false or expresses a reason that belongs to someone else's S.

The sound deliberative route allows motivations to evolve through rational reflection. For instance, if A wants social acceptance and learns that smoking causes stigma, she acquires a reason to quit. The same structure applies to institutional roles: a soldier who values loyalty can derive a specific reason to obey a lawful order through practical reasoning that connects loyalty to obedience in this context. However, Williams's framework is unstable because the deliberative route includes not only rational reflection but also causal psychological influence—manipulation, conditioning, and social pressure—so long as these processes eventually alter the contents of S. Once conditioning is admitted, the boundary between an internal reason and mere causal determination collapses. Moreover, Williams implicitly holds that moral reasons are necessarily internal, since every rational agent includes within S a general desire for moral consistency; consequently, there can be no genuinely moral external reasons, only ones that observers mistakenly project onto agents.
