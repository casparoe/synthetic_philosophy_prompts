I need a two-page proposal for a six-month philosophy fellowship on wild animal welfare and ecology. The half-formed idea: everyone in this debate treats welfare like a scalar—count suffering, count flourishing, see if habitat destruction raises or lowers the total. But I think there's a hidden structural issue. Some ecosystems run on high-turnover reproductive strategies that generate enormous numbers of short-lived individuals with intense suffering; destroying the habitat might reduce harm not just by lowering headcount, but by shifting the temporal pattern of welfare from sharp, repeated peaks to diffuse, low-amplitude states. So the moral question might depend on the shape of suffering over time, not only its sum.

The precise question: can we develop a non-scalar, structurally-sensitive framework for evaluating how habitat destruction affects wild animal welfare, and does that framework yield obligations that differ from both standard animal-welfare aggregation and ecocentric preservation? I want to know whether this is a real moral difference or just a redescription of lower numbers.

Why it's open: the wild-animal-suffering literature mostly aggregates individual experiences; ecologists talk about biodiversity or ecosystem services. Neither asks wether the distribution of suffering across generations and time-scales is itself morally relevant in a way that isn't captured by totals.

My approach: conceptual analysis of "welfare structure" borrowing from philosophy of time and distributive ethics; build simplified formal models comparing high-turnover vs low-turnover ecosystem dynamics; then test whether standard utilitarian or rights-based frameworks can absorb the structural distinction or if it genuinely breaks them. No empirical fieldwork—this is conceptual, but it needs to be sharp enough that a policy person could see what moral difference it makes.

What success looks like: clear criteria for when a structural change counts as a morally significant reduction of suffering, plus an argument that this can't be collapsed into numerical reduction. Failure: if the framework just restates aggregation in fancier language, or if I can't specify when structure matters without smuggling in controversial empirical claims.

Scope: month 1-2 framing welfare structure and mapping existing positions; months 3-4 building the formal comparison; months 5-6 writing and applying it to concrete cases like insect populations versus large-mammal ecosystems. No citations in the proposal itself, I'll fill those in later.
