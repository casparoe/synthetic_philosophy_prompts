I am preparing for a graduate seminar in political philosophy on liberalism's internal limits, and I keep encountering a specific formulation of what is usually called Popper's paradox of tolerance: the idea that unlimited tolerance, if extended even to those who are intolerant, must eventually result in the destruction of tolerance itself. In our discussions, this formulation is repeatedly invoked—not always with the same emphasis—to justify conclusions about whether liberal states ought to restrict extremist political speech, whether tolerance is a self-undermining virtue, or whether liberalism contains an unavoidable strategic vulnerability. The formulation functions almost like a slogan that ends debate rather than clarifying it, and I want to understand what precise claims are actually hiding inside it.

Could you lay out the distinct theses that this one-liner could be expressing, arranged roughly from the most trivial to the most substantive? For each reading, I want to know: (1) whether it is a purely empirical prediction about political dynamics, a conceptual claim about the meaning of tolerance, or a normative argument about what liberal institutions are permitted to do; (2) whether it actually supports restricting speech or suppressing intolerant groups, or whether it is compatible with a strict libertarian or rights-based prohibition on such restrictions; and (3) whether it depends on treating tolerance as a kind of cooperative equilibrium vulnerable to defection—an idea borrowed more from strategic interaction than from moral philosophy—rather than as a moral stance that becomes contradictory when applied universally. In particular, please identify which readings are platitudes that no one would deny, which are contentious but defensible empirical hypotheses, and which involve a hidden normative premise about the priority of preserving liberal institutions over extending procedural fairness to their enemies. Finally, tell me which of these readings is actually doing the work when someone uses the paradox to claim that liberal democracies must, on pain of self-destruction, draw hard boundaries around acceptable political participation.
