There's this evaluation framework used both in an evolutionary bio grad program and its partner wildlife center — they call it the trait-function evaluation, or TFE — and it sorts every morphological or genetic feature into three bins: adaptation, exaptation (a spandrel that got co-opted for something useful later), or pure spandrel with no current utility. In the grad program, students have to defend a classification for any trait they study, but the rubric is lopsided: adaptive stories get full credit with just a plausible selective narrative and some correllation data, exaptation stories need to document both the original spandrel origin and the later co-option event, and spandrel explanations are marked incomplte unless the student first rules out every adaptive and exaptive alternative they can think of. Over at the conservation center, they use the same three bins to decide which genetic variants to keep in a captive breeding pool: adaptations and exaptations are preserved, pure spandrels are actively bred out, even when the spandrel is neutral or linked to something important.

What philosophical commitments about biological explanation and about the value of evolutionary origins does that paired practice presuppose? Is it assuming that natural selection is the only legitimate default in science, or is there a hidden view about what makes a trait "real" or worth saving? Reconstruct the implicit theory, check if the grading rubric and the breeding protocol actually presuppose the same thing or if they're philosophically inconsistent, and say what'd have to change in both if we dropped the idea that origin-story determines both explanatory burdon and conservation priority — like if spandrels and exaptations were treated symetrically and a neutral spandrel could stay in the pool just because it's harmless.
