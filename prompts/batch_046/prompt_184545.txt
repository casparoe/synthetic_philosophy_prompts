I have been trying to understand what happens inside a brain when a person makes a genuine decision, and I keep running into the same wall in every discussion I encounter. One view describes the brain as a careenium — a space filled with symbols such as "self," "intention," or "justice" that interact according to their own structural rules. From this angle, when I choose to raise my hand, the symbol-level event is not merely a summary of neuron firing but the actual cause of it; in other words, the symbols genuinely push the substrate around. The opposing view insists that this is a category mistake: there are only neurons, ions, and electrical fields, and calling any cluster of them a "symbol" is nothing more than a convenient fiction we apply afterward. I have also tried to think about these clusters as simmballs — discrete bundles of symbolic content that seem to possess their own inertia and direction — but I am unsure whether that idea clarifies the mechanism or merely renames the mystery. The disagreement never advances. Those who defend symbol-causation accuse the reductionists of missing the explanatory level at which behavior actually makes sense; the reductionists reply that adding a symbolic layer violates the physical closure of the system. Is this stall a substantive empirical disagreement about brain mechanics, a verbal dispute about what counts as a cause, or are the two sides answering entirely different questions — one about how to explain action, one about what physical law permits? I want a diagnosis that engages what each side actually says rather than surveying the positions, together with an honest answer about whether a precise distinction, a conceivable experiment, or a change in framing could break the impasse, or whether it is rooted in bedrock intuitions that no argument can touch.
