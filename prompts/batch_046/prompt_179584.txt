I have recently been working through the metaphysics of causation for a graduate seminar, and after confronting Hume’s problem of induction I turned to Al-Ghazālī’s occasionalism in Tahāfut al-Falāsifa as a possible theological resolution. I find myself convinced that his doctrine of continuous recreation (khalq jadīd) and the habit of God (ʿādat Allāh) is not merely an anti-philosophical denial of natural powers, but rather an unwitting anticipation of modern interventionist accounts of causation—such as those associated with Woodward—which treat causal relations not as necessary metaphysical connections but as invariant patterns sustained by an external stabilizing framework. If fire does not burn cotton by virtue of any inherent power in the fire, but only through God’s direct volition at each instant, then causal knowledge becomes knowledge of stable counterfactual dependencies maintained by a controller; substituting divine will with the controlled conditions of an experiment seems to yield precisely the same epistemic structure. Given this, is Al-Ghazālī’s occasionalism best understood not as a rejection of scientific reasoning but as its correct metaphysical foundation—namely, that all genuine causation is intervention-dependent and that so-called secondary causes are merely heuristic placeholders for invariant regularities imposed from outside the system?

I want a committed answer: take a definite position on whether this interventionist reading of Al-Ghazālī is philosophically defensible or instead rests on a fundamental misreading of his occasionalism. In particular, address whether the denial of secondary causation in Tahāfut can be reconciled with the manipulability and modularity conditions that define modern interventionist causation, or whether the two frameworks are separated by an irreducible gap—perhaps in Al-Ghazālī’s insistence on direct divine agency at every temporal atom versus the interventionist’s acceptance of autonomous causal mechanisms that can be externally perturbed. Do not merely survey the options; argue for one view, explain precisely where the analogy between divine habit and experimental control succeeds and where it collapses, and indicate whether occasionalism, properly understood, offers any genuine resources for contemporary philosophy of science or should be kept strictly within theological metaphysics.
