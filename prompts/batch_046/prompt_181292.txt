One student answered as follows:

The special composition question asks when some parts make up one object. Van Inwagen's extreme answer says there are only elementary particles arranged in various ways and living organisms; artifacts like tables and clouds do not exist, because no life-process unifies them. Universalism denies this restriction, holding that for any plurality there is an object composed of exactly those parts—so there is an object made of my left shoe and the Eiffel Tower.

The moderate view, defended by David Lewis, holds that parts compose a whole whenever they are causally integrated into a functionally unified system. A car or computer therefore counts as one object, but scattered dust does not.

Applying this to the neural mesh: because the ten brains are linked by interfaces and function as one conscious controller, they meet the integration condition. So the moderate view should count them as composing not merely one object, but specifically a single conscious subject—perhaps even an organism, if sustained functional self-regulation is enough for life. Van Inwagen would deny the mesh is one organism, since biological life requires cellular organization that the wired-together brains do not jointly possess; he would see only ten organisms arranged mesh-wise, plus hardware. A weakness in the moderate criterion is that integration seems to come in degrees: if ten brains form one object, do nine? Without a sharp boundary, the view risks arbitrariness.
