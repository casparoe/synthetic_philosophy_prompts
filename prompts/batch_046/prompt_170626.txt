You are an expert in intellectual history and conceptual analysis, acting as a philosophical consultant for a university seminar. I am currently enrolled in a course titled "The Enlightenment on Trial: Progress, Domination, and Their Afterlives," and I have been trying to organize my gut reactions to whether specific Enlightenment episodes and their modern legacies count as genuinely emancipatory or as mechanisms of domination. Rather than asking for a historical narrative, I want you to help me discover whether there is a coherent principle behind my judgments. Please think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the dimensions along which an Enlightenment project might be evaluated—such as whose agency it expands, whose knowledge it validates, whether its universalism is inclusive or extractive, and whether affected parties are treated as co-authors of the norm—before formulating a precise principle and testing it.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. Beccaria’s campaign against judicial torture and capital punishment strikes me as clear progress: it uses rational argument accessible to any reader to restrict state violence. The Encyclopédie’s cross-referencing system, which treated knowledge as an interconnected network rather than a hierarchical chain of being, also seems progressive to me, despite its Eurocentric taxonomies. Kant’s essay on enlightenment feels progressive in its call for public reason, yet I am genuinely torn about its public-private distinction, which permits free debate in public while demanding obedience in one’s institutional role; I lean toward progress, but I can see the domination reading. The French Revolution’s imposition of the metric system and decimal time strikes me as domination: a rationalist standardization that erased local customary measures without democratic consultation. The Scottish stadial theories of history—classifying societies into stages of savagery, barbarism, and civilization—strike me as domination, specifically a form of cognitive extractivism in which non-European societies were observed only to confirm European theoretical frameworks; yet this is structurally similar to the Encyclopédie’s taxonomic ambitions, which I rated as progress. The Haitian Revolution’s explicit invocation of universal rights to overthrow slavery is, for me, unambiguous progress. Finally, a case I keep returning to: a contemporary development agency applies Enlightenment-derived universal human rights metrics to evaluate indigenous land management, judges the practices irrational, and restructures them; I feel this is domination, but I struggle to explain why it differs from the Haitian Revolution’s use of the same universalist language.

Is there one principle—perhaps centered on whether universalism operates as inclusive empowerment versus extractive normalization, or on whether the norm’s subjects are also its co-authors—that delivers all seven verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If the verdicts conflict irreducibly, state which one I should overturn for the sake of coherence, and explain what the best available principle predicts for an unlisted case: the eighteenth-century Republic of Letters correspondence networks, which fostered cosmopolitan debate among European elites while systematically excluding vernacular-language scholars and colonial subjects from full participation.
