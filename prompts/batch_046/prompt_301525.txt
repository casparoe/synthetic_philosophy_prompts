I was arguing with some friends about Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma and that old Scientific American luring lottery from the 80s, and I noticed something weird that I can't find a word for. In Platonia you've got 20 people who can each send a telegram with some probablity so exactly one gets the billion, and people defend the superrational strategy—randomizing with 1/20—by saying "we'd all reason identically, so our choices are correlated." Fine. But then the same people talk about the lottery, where you write a number on a postcard and the prize gets divided by total entries, and suddenly they're saying "I have to send a huge number because everyone else will," which is pure best-response logic that treats other people as completely independant from me.

What bugs me is that nobody notices they're using opposite assumptions about how minds relate. In Platonia they need perfect correlation to justify the randomization, but in the lottery they assume zero correlation—everyone's just a separate expected-value calculator—to justify sending in a googol of entries. I saw this exact flip happen in like three different online threads, and it's not just hypocrisy; it's a specific kind of reasoning mismatch that lets people defend cooperation in one game and massive defection in the other without ever reconciling the two frameworks. Does this configuration have an established name in the decision-theory or philosophy literature?

I'm not just looking for "inconsistency" or "motte-and-bailey"—I want to know exactly what the pattern is, how it differs from plain equivocation, and if there's no term, what you'd call it and where its boundaries are. Please answer in American English, and don't worry about giving me a bibliography.
