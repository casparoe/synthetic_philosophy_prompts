Two colleagues of mine, both regular readers of the rationalist blogosphere and with some philosophical training, got stuck in an extended argument after a panel on democratic ethics. They kept returning to the same tightly specified hypothetical: a democratically elected leader receives credible, time-sensitive intelligence that a small, precisely targeted strike on an authoritarian regime's communications hub will prevent an imminent genocide, but will certainly kill eight non-combatant technicians and will likely trigger cascading infrastructure failures that harm additional civilians. The leader authorizes the strike; the genocide is averted. Both agreed that, ex post, this was the least-bad available option, but they disagreed sharply on whether the leader's hands were "dirty" in any meaningful sense.

Maya opened with what she called the classic dirty-hands structure: the leader deliberately brought about the deaths of innocents, and that wrong does not evaporate just because it was proportionate or necessary. The moral stain remains personally attached. She should feel genuine guilt—not merely sorrow—seek some form of public acknowledgment, and understand that her authorization was a real moral cost rather than a neutral administrative trade-off. The fact that she had to do it makes her hands dirty, not clean.

Raj countered that Maya was conflating a tragic choice with an actual moral failure. In standard deontic terms, the action is permissible, perhaps obligatory, given the proportionality and necessity conditions. If no absolute constraint was violated—because the prohibition on killing innocents is weighted, not categorical—then there is no residue that demands guilt. The leader should feel profound sorrow for the deaths, but sorrow is an appropriate affective response to tragedy, whereas guilt presupposes wrongdoing. Treating justified hard choices as dirty hands, he said, pathologizes correct moral reasoning and empties the concept of its bite.

Maya responded that Raj was implicitly smuggling in a consequentialist calculus and labeling it deontology. Even when a constraint is outweighed, she argued, it can leave what philosophers call a moral remainder: the wrong done is not canceled by the greater good achieved. She then introduced a further distinction about institutional decision-making. In bureaucratic or collective contexts, people sometimes try to "distribute" dirty hands across committees, legal reviews, and shared authorizations, hoping that dividing the decision dilutes individual culpability. She argued this is a conceptual mistake—moral residue is not like physical labor that can be split among workers. The person who gives the final authorization still bears the primary stain, even if others share the burden of justification.

Raj said that Maya's appeal to moral remainder sounds compelling but risks being unfalsifiable: any justified hard choice can be retroactively described as leaving residue, which makes the claim tautological rather than explanatory. He proposed an alternative framing. Rather than asking whether hands are dirty, we should ask whether the decision-maker has incurred a forward-looking moral debt—say, an obligation to dedicate subsequent political capital to preventing similar crises, or to provide reparative support to affected families. If there is such a debt, then there is something to repair; but the correct attitude is not backward-looking guilt but forward-looking repair. He added that if there is no such debt, then there is no dirty hands, only difficult work properly executed.

Maya replied that Raj's moral-debt framing was actually compatible with her view, not opposed to it. The debt exists precisely because the hands are dirty; you do not incur a reparative obligation for actions that were purely clean. She then pressed a point about democratic structure: if citizens elect leaders knowing they may face such choices, do the citizens share in the dirt? She proposed that in a democracy, dirty hands might be "delegated" but not "outsourced"—voters authorize the institutional possibility of such acts, but they do not execute them, so the leader retains primary moral responsibility while the polity bears a secondary, diffuse responsibility.

Raj ended by arguing that Maya was over-moralizing politics. Politics operates within a role morality defined by institutional purpose—protecting citizens—not by the norms that govern private life. A leader who kills eight to save fifty thousand is not violating a private moral norm in a public context; she is fulfilling her institutional duty. To call her hands dirty is to apply private morality to public roles in a category error. He conceded that the deaths should be publicly mourned, but insisted that mourning is categorically different from confessing wrongdoing.

They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: rate each of Maya's and Raj's central moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify specifically what both are missing—particularly regarding whether democratic delegation actually changes the moral structure of dirty hands, and whether there is a principled distinction between "moral residue" that demands guilt versus "moral debt" that demands repair, rather than the two being merely different descriptions of the same phenomenon.
