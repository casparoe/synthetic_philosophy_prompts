I've been arguing with a friend about whether patriotism is a virtue or a vice, and I think I've pinned my view down to something pretty specific, but I want to stress-test it before I commit. I hold that patriotism is virtuous only in a form I'd call "instiutional loyalty" — the object isn't the nation as an ethnic or cultural unit, but the concrete practices of democratic reciprocity, like fair procedures and mutual accountability, and this loyalty necessarily includes a duty to dissent when those practices turn corrupt or exclusionary. I also think the moral weight is asymmetric: it creates strong negative duties (don't undermine the cooperative framework) but only weak positive duties, roughly comparable to what we owe distant relatives rather than strangers or our kids, so it can't license sacrificing outsiders for national glory but might justify mild distributive preference for co-citizens. What would actually count against this? I'm not after the generic "nationalism is bad" line — I want the real defeaters: which intuitions about specific cases (say, a citizen hiding corruption out of loyalty), which empirical findings about how people form patriotic attachments, and which conceptual arguments (maybe about whether institutions can even be proper objects of loyalty) would damage the view? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a defender should concede versus dismiss as misreadings. Especialy, has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, is that actually a problem for a virtue claim of this kind?
