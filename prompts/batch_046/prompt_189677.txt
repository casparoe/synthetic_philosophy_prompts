A philosophy blogger summarised the discursive dilemma like this: "When a committee aggregates judgments on logically connected propositions—for instance, whether a defendant breached a duty of care, whether that breach caused the claimant’s injury, and whether the defendant is therefore liable—majority voting on each item separately can produce an inconsistent collective position: majorities may accept both premises yet reject the conclusion. This is essentially Arrow’s impossibility theorem transferred from preference aggregation to belief aggregation; it demonstrates that no democratic rule can simultaneously respect every voter’s input, apply uniform treatment across all propositions, and guarantee logical consistency. Among social-choice theorists, the standard remedy is premise-based aggregation: the group votes only on the premises, derives the conclusion mechanically by entailment, and never votes on the conclusion itself. This is widely regarded as uncontroversial, since logical coherence should plainly outweigh a direct majority opinion on the outcome, even if critics object that it forces members to accept a conclusion they explicitly rejected."
