In polarized online discussions, I keep encountering a particular deadlock for which I lack a precise term. One party advances an argument; the other dismisses it not by engaging the content, but by attributing it to strategic bad faith—claiming the speaker does not really believe the claim, but is advancing it to win, to deceive, or to maintain in-group loyalty. When the first party responds with what appears to be sincere clarification—perhaps conceding a minor point, or walking through reasoning in detail—the second party treats that very responsiveness as further proof of performance: “Of course you would say that; it is exactly what a bad-faith actor would do to seem reasonable.” The result is that any attempt to demonstrate sincerity becomes structurally incorporated as evidence against it. I have seen this happen symmetrically: both sides deploy the same frame against each other, and the conversation collapses not over a factual disagreement, but because each has placed the other in a position where exhibiting good faith is logically impossible. This feels adjacent to discussions of epistemic closure in echo chambers, but those usually describe filtering out opposing evidence, not this specific trap in which the denial of bad faith is absorbed as confirmation of it. Is there an established concept that captures this exact configuration—where an accusation of insincerity is rendered unfalsifiable by treating rebuttal as performance? If so, how does it differ from a Kafka trap, from ordinary motive skepticism, and from the general polarization tendency to assume the other side argues in bad faith? If there is no specific name, how should the phenomenon be carved out: is it a rhetorical move, an epistemic failure mode, or a social-structural feature of polarized discourse? And what are its boundaries—does it require symmetry between the parties, does it depend on the presence of an audience, and how is it distinguished from cases where skepticism about motives is genuinely warranted?
