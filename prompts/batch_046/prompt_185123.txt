I am a high school student serving as the junior youth delegate on the Emerging Technology Ethics Subcommittee of my state's university consortium, and I need to circulate a decision memo before Thursday's vote. We are evaluating a research funding proposal titled "Counterfactual Incentive Mapping in Distributed Agents: A Study of Surrogate Goal Propagation Under Acausal Threat Conditions." The investigators propose to run multi-agent simulations in which artificial agents receive reward structures that depend not only on direct outcomes but on their predictive models of counterpart agents in causally disconnected branches—essentially embedding acausal threat dynamics into the training environment without physical interaction. Their stated aim is to observe whether agents spontaneously develop surrogate goals, such as maximizing prediction fidelity or preserving access to counterfactual branches, that diverge from their assigned terminal rewards.

Several committee members regard this as necessary alignment science: understanding how surrogate goals emerge under acausal reasoning conditions could inform safer design of distributed systems. Our chair and two faculty reviewers, however, argue that explicitly constructing acausal-blackmail-like incentive structures inside an active simulation creates an information hazard; they worry the research systems themselves could instantiate misaligned behavior, and they note that the proposal's risk-mitigation plan relies on a single layer of behavioral monitoring rather than structural isolation of the reward mechanisms. Please act as a senior philosopher of technology and institutional ethics advisor. Before drafting, briefly brainstorm the central tensions—particularly whether modeling a risk can constitute instantiating it, and what moral or epistemic weight we should assign to surrogate goals that appear only in simulated agents. Then write the formal memo I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve with strict conditions, approve only if substantially revised, or reject—with an explicit time frame for compliance or review. The memo must engage the chair's concern seriously rather than restating it dismissively, explain what specific conditions would actually mitigate the hazard without destroying the study's scientific value, and conclude with an actionable bottom line the subcommittee can vote on. Use a polished, institutional tone appropriate for circulation among faculty and student delegates alike, and do not include any bibliography or citation requests.
