I have just finished a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a close friend about the scope and limits of care ethics, and I find myself holding a position I want to test rather than defend blindly. I maintain that care ethics, taken seriously, does not merely supplement justice-based frameworks but actively dissolves the coherence of universal moral principles: specifically, I claim that moral obligation is not distributed according to any principle of equality or desert, but is strictly calibrated to the relational density and asymmetrical vulnerability present in a given caring bond, such that there is no residual floor of equal respect owed to strangers that survives this calibration intact. In other words, I think the care perspective does not just prioritize particular relationships; it undermines the very idea that there is a consistent moral unit—whether person, rational agent, or rights-bearer—to which universal obligations could attach.

What I want is an honest assessment of what would actually count against this view. Not a generic list of objections to care ethics, but the specific defeaters for this stronger claim: which intuitions about strangers or distant suffering it must suppress, which formal results in distributive ethics it conflicts with, and whether there is a coherent intermediate position—say, a layered view that preserves minimal universal obligations without collapsing back into liberal impartiality—that I should take seriously. I want these ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an indication of which ones a committed defender of this relational-calibration view would themselves concede as damaging and which they would dismiss as smuggling in the very impartialist premises they reject. I am also concerned whether my position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if every counterexample can be redescribed as a failure of relational attunement rather than a failure of the theory, does that insulation constitute a virtue or a vice for a normative view of this kind?

Please be explicit about what would change your mind—whether a particular thought experiment, an empirical finding about moral cognition across cultures, or a logical tension with some other domain of ethics—and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail, especially if it depends on treating vulnerability and relational density as morally primitive rather than derivative.
