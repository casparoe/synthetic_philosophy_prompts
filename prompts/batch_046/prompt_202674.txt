I've been working through an anti-luminosity argument that imports the KK principle together with a cognitive saturation constraint, and I'm convinced it proves too much — it ends up denying that anyone actually introspects. The steps are: (1) A mental condition C is luminous if and only if, necessarily, whenever C obtains in subject S, S is in a position to know that C obtains. (2) The KK principle states that, necessarily, if S knows p, then S knows that S knows p. (3) Suppose feeling cold is luminous. (4) Then whenever S feels cold, S knows she feels cold; applying (2) iteratively yields that S knows K^n(feels cold) for every n. (5) Actual human working memory, however, can only explicitly represent about seven nested propositional attitudes — call this the saturation point. (6) So for any actual S there is some finite n beyond which S cannot hold K^n(feels cold) in explicit working memory. (7) Therefore, in no actual case can S be in a position to know all the iterations that luminosity plus KK jointly require. (8) Hence feeling cold is not luminous for finite minds, and generalizing removes luminosity for every mental state. (9) Without luminosity there is no direct, non-inferential introspective knowledge. (10) So introspection is impossible. I want to preserve both the saturation point and the reality of introspection; I'm willing to weaken either luminosity or KK, but I need the costs priced precisely. Answer in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: identify the faulty step, explain why it seemed plausible, and compare the three escape routes — giving up luminosity, restricting KK away from mental states, or denying that knowledge iterations require explicit working-memory representation — in terms of what each side must pay.
