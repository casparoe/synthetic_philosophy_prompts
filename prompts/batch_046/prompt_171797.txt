Student answer:
Hofstadter's strange-loop account treats the self as an emergent pattern sustained by self-reference, analogous to the Gödel sentence in formal arithmetic. Because patterns are substrate-independent, the loop need not be confined to one skull: when a bereaved partner internalizes my speech patterns and emotional reactions, part of my loop is literally running in their neural substrate. This is not mere psychological influence but a distributed continuation, since the functional organization that constituted my I is now partly realized elsewhere. The view therefore avoids Derek Parfit's reductionism. Parfit analyzes identity as a one-dimensional chain of mental states linked by psychological continuity, but Hofstadter requires a two-level reflexive architecture in which the pattern must represent itself. That reflexivity makes the loop non-decomposable: you cannot split it like a computer program across processors without destroying the self-reference. Still, the view faces a difficulty. If my loop is genuinely distributed, I ought to regard harm to those who carry it as harm to me, yet egoistic concern clearly does not extend that far. Hofstadter might respond that loop-fidelity is graded—high-fidelity copies in intimate others, weak ones in acquaintances—but this introduces an arbitrary cutoff. In the end, distributed persistence is coherent only if we reject all-or-nothing identity and accept a continuum of selfhood across social networks.
