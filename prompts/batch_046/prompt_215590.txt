Write a short research proposal—suitable for a philosophy dissertation prospectus or a one-semester workshop fellowship—on the following question. The proposal should be about 2500 to 3000 words in scope and must include: a precisely stated open question; an explanation of why standard answers in personal identity and philosophy of mind leave it unresolved; the specific conceptual approach you will take and why it is preferable to the obvious alternatives; a clear description of what a successful result would look like and what would count as failure; and an honest assessment of scope and risk for a single semester.

The domain is Dennett's conception of the self as a center of narrative gravity. Your task is to frame and defend a project that asks whether this conception can serve as a determinate criterion for personal identity under conditions of distributed, non-unified autobiographical reconstruction. Specifically: if autobiographical memory is not stored in a single biological substrate but is instead reconstructed from disparate, sometimes conflicting internal and external records—without any hierarchical integrator—does narrative gravity still converge on a single center, fragment into multiple competing centers, or dissolve entirely?

In framing the question, avoid treating narrative gravity as a purely metaphorical description. Instead, propose to operationalize it through a functionalist model of attractor dynamics in reconstructed autobiography: define the conditions (coherence, temporal continuity, conflict resolution) under which competing narrative streams either merge into one gravitational center or remain fragmented. Apply this framework to two conceptual test cases: severe dissociative conditions with co-conscious but non-integrated narrative streams, and synthetic agents whose memories are reconstructed from distributed databases lacking unified storage.

Explain why existing accounts—psychological continuity theories that require unified mental life, and biological continuity theories that ignore narrative structure—fail to address this boundary case. Argue that your attractor-based approach is preferable to standard metaphysical analysis because it makes the metaphor of gravity conceptually precise, yielding determinate conditions for identity preservation rather than intuitive judgments.

State what success would look like: a set of boundary conditions specifying exactly when distributed reconstruction preserves one self versus generating many. State what failure would look like: a demonstration that the dynamics are merely descriptive and cannot distinguish shared data across one self from shared data across many selves.

Finally, assess realistic scope: commit to conceptual formalization and the two test cases only, explicitly bracketing empirical neuroscience, consciousness studies, and engineering implementation. Identify the main risk of overreach—attempting quantitative mathematical modeling rather than philosophical conceptual analysis—and explain how the proposal avoids it.

Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or literature review by reference. Refer to positions and arguments by name or description as needed, but leave specific works and page references for later filling-in.
