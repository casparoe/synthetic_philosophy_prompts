my study group got into populism and "the will of the people" and one guy walked us into this. (1) populists say a government is legitimate only if it expresses the will of the people, understood not as summed preferences but as a single collective agent with one coherent mind. (2) a collective agent can only genuinely will what fits its shared intentions, so disagreement on ends means there is no pre-existing unified will waiting to be discovered. (3) therefore any democratic procedure—elections, deliberation, even a leader "reading the room"—doesn't reveal an already-there will but constructs one by resolving contradictions. (4) but if the will is constructed rather than expressed, then by the populist's own lights the outcome lacks authentic legitimacy. (5) this applies to populist regimes too, since once they take power they also synthesize disagreement into one voice. (6) so populism implies no institutional form, including itself, can ever be legitimate. (7) yet populists clearly think their own movements are legitimate. (8) therefore populism is self-defeating.

i want to keep (1) and (2) if possible, because they explain why populists reject liberal pluralism, but i'm suspicious of the slide between (3) and (4). is the mistake treating constructed unity as automatically "fake"? or is there a hidden quantifier shift—maybe "the people" can have a will in a distributed or representative sense without being a literal collective agent? which premise should i reject, and what does it cost? does giving up (2) just reduce "the people" to a statistical aggregate, and is that fatal to the populist critique of liberalism?
