I'm in an early modern philosophy seminar and have adopted what I call an episodic reading of Descartes's cogito: the argument establishes only that an occurrent thinking event is occurring, not that there is a persisting substantial self. On this view, the thinker indexed by the first-person pronoun exists exactly as long as that present mental act lasts, and any claim to diachronic identity or to being a res cogitans must be imported later via the causal argument for God and the subsequent guarantee of clear and distinct perceptions. This seems to avoid the standard charge that Descartes begs the question about substance in Meditations II, since I deny he establishes substance there at all. But I want a committed diagnosis of what would genuinely defeat this reading. Which specific intuitions, formal features of the argument—perhaps the indexical structure of self-reference, or the conditions necessary for doubting—would show that the cogito necessarily carries more than episodic existence? What possible cases, or results in philosophy of mind about diachronic unity, would count against it, and which of these would an episodic reader concede versus dismiss? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Finally, is the episodic reading conceptually stable, or does it collapse into either full substance dualism or an eliminativist view that Descartes never intended? Take a definite position, defend it with reasons, and do not merely survey the options.
