I hold the view that in transitional justice, amnesty granted in exchange for full disclosure—the model adopted by South Africa’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission—is not a morally regrettable compromise but the only conceptually coherent response when perpetrators monopolize the factual record of systemic violence. The argument is not merely instrumental. There is, I suggest, an epistemic contract between the post-conflict state and survivors: the state’s primary obligation is not the retributive distribution of suffering but the restoration of a shared, authoritative record, and when perpetrators alone possess knowledge of burial sites, chains of command, and institutional mechanisms, legal immunity becomes the only legitimate currency of exchange. Critics who invoke Argentina’s prosecutorial trajectory or Rwanda’s gacaca hybrid typically treat justice as purely distributive—who is punished—while neglecting its relational and knowledge-centered dimension, and they thereby misunderstand the moral structure of the state’s duty. I want an honest map of what would count against this position. I am not asking for the familiar objection that amnesty insults victims; I want the deeper defeaters. Which actual or hypothetical cases show that full disclosure can be secured without trading away punishment? Are there conceptual arguments that truth bought with immunity is necessarily epistemically corrupted—performatively insincere, strategically curated, or structurally unreliable—such that the epistemic contract yields not knowledge but a manufactured narrative? Is there empirical evidence that amnesty-for-truth suppresses deeper truths by allowing perpetrators to control the framing and scope of revelation? And is there a normative argument that the very idea of an epistemic contract is a category error: that survivors are owed punishment and recognition as ends, not information extraction, and that framing the state’s duty around disclosure instrumentalizes suffering for collective cognitive utility? Finally, has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability—defended by claiming that any failure of disclosure is merely imperfect implementation rather than a flaw in the model—and if so, is that insulation a conceptual virtue or a moral vice that renders the view impervious to the lived reality of victim experience?
