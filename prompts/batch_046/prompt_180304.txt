I'm taking a philosophy of history module and we've spent ages arguing about counterfactuals and wether big turning points were somehow inevitable. The analogy everyone reaches for is Gould's replaying-the-tape idea — if you could rewind history to some point and let it run again, would you end up with the same broad outcomes? In our seminar the tutor used it to suggest that the Industrial Revolution was probably overdetermined: even with different inventors or monarchs, the structural pressures of coal, capital, and urbanisation would have pushed Britain in that direction eventually. But she also said the analogy is dodgy for human history because there's no natural selection or fitness landscape, so convergence isn't guaranteed.

I'm wondering how far the tape-replay picture actually maps onto historical explanation. What plays the role of the 'tape' — is it the sequence of events, or the underlying causal mechanisms? Where exactly does the biological analogy break: is it just the lack of selection, or also that human agents can anticipate and change course in ways bacteria can't? And if we grant all those disanalogies, does the claim that macro-historical patterns are structurally inevitable still stand, or does it collapse into a vague determinism that doesn't really explain anything? I keep feeling there's something being smuggled in about 'levels' of explanation — like maybe the tape works for long-term trends but not for the specific political forms — but I can't quite pin it down. Would appreciate a proper audit of what the analogy can and can't carry here.
