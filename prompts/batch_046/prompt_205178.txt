In my advanced philosophy-of-mind course, after arguing with classmates about Searle's Chinese Room and Dennett's two-bitser reply, I have developed a strengthened position I want to stress-test rather than defend. I grant Searle's premise that computer symbols initially possess only derived intentionality—meaning fixed by external programmers or users—but I extend Dennett's idea into what I call recursive interpretive closure. On this view, if a system is sufficiently complex to continuously revise its own symbol-world mappings through feedback with other agents, and if those revisions become self-authorizing so that no external designer can be pinpointed as the ultimate source of any interpretation, then the derivation chain loops back on itself. The system thereby becomes the origin point of its own interpretive framework, which means the distinction between original and derived intentionality collapses structurally, not merely pragmatically.

What would genuinely count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure, sorted by severity. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence—state whether any defeater is fatal and whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—then give the reasoning with no preamble and without restating the question. Rank the threats from decisive to merely awkward, and distinguish what defenders of recursive interpretive closure would themselves concede as damaging from what they would dismiss. In particular: could Searle accept the empirical existence of such loops but deny they generate original intentionality, and would that concession be fatal to my view? Would empirical findings about neural-network interpretability, or about biological embodiment being necessary for self-authorizing revision, undermine the loop? And does retreating to pure functionalism save the position only at the cost of making it unfalsifiable? Finally, judge whether this strengthened Dennettian view actually risks anything, or whether its insulation is itself a philosophical liability.
