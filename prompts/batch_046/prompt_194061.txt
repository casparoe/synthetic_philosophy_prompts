I am preparing a discussion for a philosophy and literature seminar, and I want to read Edward Albee’s Who’s Afraid of Virginia Woolf? through Plato’s account of wine and disclosure in the Laws, particularly the distinction between drinking for pleasure and drinking as a supervised civic examination—what the Athenian Stranger describes as a kind of basanos of the soul, conducted in the presence of a sober symposiarch who reads the drinker’s revealed character. The play’s long night of heavy drinking strips away social masks: George and Martha’s toxic games with Nick and Honey escalate until the imaginary child is exposed and “killed.” Where does this align with Plato’s idea that intoxication removes inhibitions and makes the true disposition visible, and where does it depart from his conviction that such visibility should serve an ordering, pedagogical purpose? I would like you to examine specific dynamics—the party games, the gradual revelation of the couple’s private mythology, and George’s ambiguous role as both participant and orchestrator—and ask whether he functions at all like a Platonic symposiarch, or whether his own drunkenness collapses the observer-observed structure that Plato’s model requires. Please also say where the play resists being read as a simple illustration: Albee seems to present alcohol-fuelled truth-telling as destructive rather than civic, and I want to know whether that resistance adds something to the philosophical debate about revelation and virtue that Plato’s argument alone cannot supply. I am not looking for a bibliography, just a faithful two-way reading that neither reduces the drama to philosophy nor distorts Plato to fit the plot.
