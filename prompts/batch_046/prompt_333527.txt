I'm taking a graduate seminar on democratic theory and my study group has been trying to formalize the populist claim that legitimate government must track "the will of the people." We constructed what seemed like a tight argument, but it lands in an absurdity: either populism is self-contradictory, or it lets governments claim popular mandates for policies while disclaiming responsibility for their unavoidable consequences. We can't locate the exact slip. Here is our reasoning:

(1) Populism holds that a law is democratically legitimate if and only if it expresses the will of the people.
(2) If a collective agent wills policy P, and P necessarily entails consequence Q, then—by the transitivity of rational agency—willing P just is willing the package (P-and-Q).
(3) In practice, democratic publics often will policies (for example, expansive welfare provision together with low taxation) whose necessary entailments (fiscal deficit or service cuts) they simultaneously reject.
(4) By (2), if the people will the policy, they must also will its entailment; but by (3) they reject that entailment. So they both will and do not will Q.
(5) To avoid contradiction, the populist must deny that legitimacy requires tracking entailments—only the explicit policy content counts.
(6) But then a government can enact P under a popular mandate and disclaim democratic responsibility for Q, even though Q was unavoidable. This severs democratic choice from democratic accountability.
(7) Therefore, either populism is contradictory (if it retains (2)), or it licenses governments to hide behind partial mandates (if it drops (2)). Both outcomes seem unacceptable.

We suspect the error lies somewhere between (2) and (5)—perhaps an equivocation between logical entailment and political responsibility, or an illicit move from individual rational agency to collective will. But we are unsure which step is least costly to reject, and what abandoning it does to the broader populist framework. Which premise or inference should be given up? If the argument is valid and the fault lies in a false premise, identify it and explain what the populist loses by abandoning it. I would prefer to preserve (1) if that is defensible.
