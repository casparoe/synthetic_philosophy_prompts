Draft the joint statement that a committed proponent of strict forward-looking instrumental rationality and a committed proponent of diachronic commitment-rationality about sunk costs could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The instrumentalist holds that unrecoverable past expenditures should never influence present choice; rational decision-making is exclusively future-oriented, and any weight given to sunk costs is a cognitive bias—whether in economic, moral, or creative domains—regardless of context.

The commitment-rationalist holds that sunk costs can generate genuine practical reasons when they are embedded in long-term projects or identity-constituting commitments; abandoning them solely because they are unrecoverable can violate diachronic coherence, and honoring them is not always reducible to a prediction error, loss aversion, or disguised risk preference. This side treats "commitment-embedded unrecoverability" as a live theoretical category: a past investment can become structurally integrated into an agent's practical identity such that its abandonment imposes a "diachronic integrity cost" that is not captured by standard expected-utility frameworks.

Please produce:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can accept—about the formal structure of decision trees containing unrecoverable nodes, the empirical psychology of loss aversion and escalation of commitment, the distinction between predictive and non-predictive practical reasons, and the conditions under which an agent can genuinely distinguish "continuing because of the past investment" from "continuing for independent forward-looking reasons"—written in neutral wording each party would endorse.

2. The precise disagreements, each formulated so that the side holding it would accept the wording: specifically, (a) whether rationality permits non-instrumental, identity-constituting reasons to weigh against expected utility without collapsing into mere preference satisfaction; (b) whether "honoring a sunk cost" can ever be descriptively and normatively distinct from "choosing the option one would have chosen for independent forward-looking reasons"; and (c) whether observed cases of persisting with failing projects out of loyalty to prior investment are always instances of reasoning failure, or whether some track the preservation of diachronic integrity.

3. The cruxes—sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one party. Expected cruxes include: whether there exists a coherent, non-redundant notion of diachronic integrity cost; whether moral and creative domains provide clear instances in which honoring sunk costs produces outcomes that both sides agree are valuable but that the instrumentalist must explain away as accidental; and whether psychological studies of escalation can be interpreted in ways that distinguish bias from commitment-tracking.

4. For each crux, present one concrete scenario or structured thought experiment both sides agree is relevant—such as a scientist who has spent a decade on a research program that now appears empirically dead, or an activist who has sacrificed personal welfare for a cause now judged ineffective—together with each side's explicit prediction about how a rational agent should behave, what emotional or deliberative profile that behavior should display, and what empirical or normative finding would count for or against that side's view.

5. A brief concluding assessment identifying whether any portion of the dispute is verbal—e.g., if defining "rational" in a purely instrumental fashion makes the commitment-rationalist's position necessarily false by stipulation rather than by argument—and, if so, which portion and why.

Write in formal, precise prose. Do not include citations, bibliographies, references to specific modern authors or texts, or requests for further reading. Treat "diachronic integrity cost" and "commitment-embedded unrecoverability" as theoretical notions to be analyzed and tested within the collaboration, not as established technical terms with canonical definitions.
