For a philosophy-of-mind reading group I have been asked to set out my current profile on agency and self-deception, so that others can probe the joints. Here is roughly where I stand, and I want to know whether the package is jointly tenable.

(1) I accept a broadly Sartrean picture of radical freedom: however constrained my situation, I am never fully determined by it, because I always retain the freedom to choose my attitude toward it, and in that sense I constitute myself through my choices rather than being constituted by prior conditions.

(2) I understand bad faith not merely as lying to oneself, but as the specific project of identifying with one's facticity—treating one's social roles, past actions, or biological nature as if they fixed one's possibilities—thereby fleeing the anxiety that accompanies the recognition of radical freedom.

(3) I find the predictive-processing (free-energy principle) account of cognition compelling: perception, action, and inference are unified under hierarchical Bayesian updating that minimises prediction error, and this applies to the self-model as much as to the external world.

(4) I hold that moral responsibility requires that the agent genuinely could have done otherwise at the moment of decision in a counterfactual sense; compatibilist reconstructions that preserve responsibility without alternative possibilities strike me as missing the phenomenon they claim to save.

(5) I think authenticity is not an achieved state but an ongoing practice: it consists in continually refusing to let any description—psychological, sociological, or neuroscientific—exhaust my self-conception, precisely because such descriptions treat me as an object with fixed properties.

(6) I am inclined to one-box in Newcomb-style problems, on the grounds that if a perfect predictor correlates with my choice, the correlation runs from my decision to the forecast rather than from the forecast to my decision, so treating the prediction as an external constraint is a mistake.

My question is whether these six commitments hang together, or whether I am helping myself to incompatible motivations. In particular: does (3) sit comfortably with (1), or does a hierarchical-Bayesian self-model collapse radical freedom into inference from strong priors? Is (5) consistent with (3), or is adopting predictive processing as my own self-model itself an instance of the bad faith described in (2)—treating myself as a prediction machine and thus fleeing my freedom into facticity? Does (4) conflict with (6), given that perfect predictability seems hard to reconcile with the genuine availability of alternatives? And is there an apparent tension between (1) and (5) that dissolves on closer inspection?

Please audit the set. Rank the tensions by severity, not treating them all as equally damaging. For each genuine tension, say which commitment is least costly to revise and what that revision costs. Identify any tension that is only apparent. Then tell me which single revision—giving up one commitment or modifying one—would clean up the most conflicts. Finally, flag any point where the consistency check itself depends on a judgment call that the procedure cannot settle.

Please answer in British English, and keep the analysis formal and precise. I am not looking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want the conceptual work done directly on the commitments as stated.
