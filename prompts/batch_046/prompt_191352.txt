I serve on a constitutional review panel that has ruled on whether appeals to the will of the people can justify overriding ordinary legislative procedure. Our track record: we upheld a national referendum with 73 percent turnout and a clear majority, describing it as direct popular expression; we struck down an executive decree justified by private polling that claimed to reveal an unexpressed majority preference; we upheld a parliamentary statute passed after a general election with 65 percent turnout; we struck down a citizen petition bearing signatures from 57 percent of eligible voters demanding immediate policy change, on the ground that self-selected aggregation lacks representative legitimacy; and we upheld a randomly selected citizens' jury recommendation that parliament adopted following months of structured deliberation, treating it as a legitimate fusion of representative sampling and reasoned judgment.

We now face a proposal defended by reference to MandateNet, an encrypted protocol that gathers private preference signals from a randomly sampled 83 percent of adult citizens, applies an algorithm designed to correct for strategic misrepresentation, and outputs an auditable policy recommendation. There is no public debate, no vote, and no review by an elected body, but proponents claim the result tracks authentic popular preference more accurately than elections do.

If we are to remain consistent with ourselves, what should we decide? Lay out the two most plausible interpretive rules that fit our five precedents—one centered on institutional authorization and deliberative form, the other on verifiable, representative preference aggregation—state what each predicts for MandateNet, identify the closest analog in our record, and explain what principle we would be committing to with either decision. Finally, specify the one further case that would best discriminate between the two rules.
