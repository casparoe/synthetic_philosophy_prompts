First, I think the Dutch book thing is real — if your beliefs about probabilities don't add up right, like if you think A is more likely than B and B more than C but then C more than A, then someone can set up bets where you loose money no matter what happens. That feels like a real flaw in how you're thinking, not just bad luck.

Second, I also think it's totally okay in the Ellsberg urn situation to pick the urn with the known 50/50 split over the one where you have no idea, even if that means you're not acting like all unknown probabilities are just 50/50 inside your head. To me that feels rational, not like a mistake.

Third, I think rationality is mostly about not being a sucker for other people, so being Dutch-bookable is only bad if there's actually an opponent who knows your beliefs and can price bets against you. If it's just "nature" running the outcomes and nobody is setting prices, then losing money isn't proof your beliefs were wrong — it's just bad luck.

Fourth, I think if a belief is genuinely ambiguous — like you honestly don't think there's a hidden fact about the exact odds — then you can't be pinned down to specific betting rates, which means you're automatically protected from Dutch books anyway because there's nothing for the bookie to grab onto.

Fifth, I think decision theory shouldn't care about whether you figured out the problem on Tuesday or Wednesday, so if being Dutch-bookable somehow depends on the order you think about the bets, that's not a real rationality failure.

So do these hang together? I feel like my first point and my second point might be fighting, and maybe my third point is just me weaseling out of the first one.
