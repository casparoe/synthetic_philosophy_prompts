I'm prepping interview questions for my podcast next week. The guest is a philosopher — lets call her the author of a recent monograph on Nietzsches Untimely Meditations — who defends a tweaked version of the three-mode taxonomy. She keeps monumental, antiquarian, and critical history intact, but adds a fourth mode she calls 'projective history': you take a past figure or event, treat it as a counterfactual branching point, and use the simulated trajectory to guide present action. She claims this avoids both the veneration of monumental history and the demolition of critical history, and that it solves the rootlessness problem of critical mode without falling into antiquarian nostalgia.

I want eight to nine questions that go well beyond having her restate the four boxes. Specifically:

- At least one that presses whether projective history is just monumental history with extra steps — if youre still using past exemplars to motivate action, how is that not veneration? Include a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that the counterfactual framing breaks the emotional identification.

- One about Nietzsches warning that too much history paralyzes life. Does adding a fourth mode make the burden heavier, or does she think instrumentalizing the past actually lightens it? Follow-up for the answer shes most likely to give.

- One on the antiquarian need for rootedness and origin. If projective history generates synthetic roots — counterfactual lineages that never actually happened — can those satisfy the antiquarian drive, or do they just highlight that we're rootless? Follow-up if she argues synthetic roots function identically.

- One that asks whether projective history can do the destructive, life-affirming work of critical history — tearing down harmful traditions — without itself being a form of critique. Whats the follow-up when she says the demolition is built into the counterfactual comparison?

- And at least one on whether this taxonomy even applies to individual lives, since Nietzsche was mainly worried about personal vitality, but her examples all seem collective or political.

Make them sound like actual spoken interview questions — not exam prompts — with some casual phrasing. I dont want generic stuff like "what drew you to philosophy?" and please dont include any bibliography or reading list requests.
