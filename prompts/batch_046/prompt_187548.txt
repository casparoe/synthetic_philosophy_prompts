Here's an argument I cooked up for a forum thread and now I think it proves way too much, but I can't tell where the slight of hand is.

(1) Teleosemantics naturalizes content by tying representation to proper function: R represents X iff R's evolutionary job is to indicate X.

(2) Proper functions don't care about substrate or location - they're just stable causal roles shaped by selection history.

(3) So if a bacterium's chemoreceptors have the proper function of tracking glucose gradients, then they genuinely represent glucose, not just causally respond to it.

(4) But decoupled cognition - being able to represent X without currently behaving toward X - is just a more complex proper function (say, maintaining an internal model for future use). There's no principled functional difference between coupled and decoupled tracking; it's all on the same continuum.

(5) Therefore teleosemantics either assigns mental content to bacteria, root systems, and immune cells (pan-intentionalism), or it has to import some non-functional boundary condition like 'this only counts inside a nervous system.'

(6) Any non-functional boundary is adhoc and breaks the naturalizing promise.

(7) So teleosemantics fails to distinguish mental representation from mere biological tracking.

I really want to hold onto (1) and (4) - they seem right - but maybe (2) is smuggling in something? Or is the error in treating decoupling as just more function? Give me a committed take: which premise is the weak link, and what does giving it up actually cost? Don't just list options - tell me which one you'd bite and why.
