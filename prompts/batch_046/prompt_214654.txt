I'm a high school senior working through philosophy of science for an independent study, and I've gotten stuck in what feels like a reductio of Milton Friedman's F-twist. I take the core idea to be that in positive science the literal realism of a model's assumptions doesn't matter—only predictive accuracy validates it. But when I try to formalize this consistently and push it across domains, I end up in a position I can't defend. Here is the chain:

(1) Scientific evaluation should be instrumental: we judge a model primarily by predictive success, not by whether every one of its assumptions literally describes reality.

(2) "Unrealistic" assumptions come in two kinds: idealizations that simplify reality (frictionless planes, point masses), and ontological claims that directly contradict other well-established theories (for instance, that inanimate particles possess intentional states).

(3) The F-twist licenses both kinds, provided predictions hold, because there is no separate scientific test for "realism of assumptions" independent of predictive power.

(4) So if two models M and N yield identical predictions across all relevant domains, science has no internal basis to prefer M over N, no matter how bizarre N's ontology is.

(5) Apply this to physics. Model P is standard quantum mechanics with no hidden variables. Model Q uses the exact same Schrödinger equation but interprets the wavefunction as describing microscopic agents literally negotiating outcomes. Q reproduces every prediction of P.

(6) By (4), physics has no scientific reason to reject Q in favor of P.

(7) By (3), the fact that Q's ontology contradicts chemistry and biology is not itself a scientific objection, because those fields are evaluated by their own predictive models, not by cross-domain ontological consistency checks.

(8) Therefore Friedmanism implies that science is ontologically silent: it can validate equations and forecast outcomes, but it can never justify claims about what entities actually exist.

(9) But this proves too much. If science is ontologically silent, then evolutionary biology's predictive success gives us no reason to think common descent is real rather than a useful fiction, provided some "separately-created-with-pre-placed-fossils" model reproduced the same biological predictions. Yet we do treat evolutionary success as supporting the reality of the process, not merely its equations.

I feel like one step slips—perhaps an equivocation between "idealized" and "contradictory," or a hidden assumption that predictive equivalence must be global rather than local. Is the error in (3), that Friedman only licensed simplifications and not direct contradictions? Or in (7), that science actually does employ cross-domain consistency as a constraint? Or in (4), that predictive equivalence doesn't entail scientific equivalence because explanation and unification carry independent weight?

If I have to reject (1) entirely, that feels costly: I'd be committing to the view that science demands literal truth of every assumption, which seems like an impossible standard for any tractable model. I'd prefer to preserve some form of instrumentalism while blocking the slide into full ontological silence. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost me?
