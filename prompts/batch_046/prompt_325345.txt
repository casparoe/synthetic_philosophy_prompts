Exam question: Moore holds in Principia Ethica that appreciation of beautiful objects and personal friendship are among the greatest intrinsic goods, and he appeals to organic unities to explain why their value exceeds the sum of parts. (1) Explain Moore’s distinction between intrinsic and instrumental value, and show how organic unity applies to friendship and to aesthetic experience without reducing either to an aggregate of pleasant moments. (2) Address the “composition problem”: if the individual mental states composing a friendship or an aesthetic experience are not themselves bearers of the full value, what makes the unified whole valuable? Note whether the problem applies differently when the unity involves two subjects rather than one. (3) Evaluate whether Moore can consistently treat friendship as valuable for its own sake if part of its value depends on the moral character it cultivates in the friends, or whether that dependence introduces an instrumental element he must reject.

Student answer: Moore’s central claim is that only states of consciousness have intrinsic value, and among them personal affection and aesthetic enjoyment rank highest. He introduces organic unities to show that the value of a whole need not equal the sum of its parts. Applying this to friendship, Moore should say that its value is not merely the total pleasure each friend takes in the other, but a unified state of mutual affection and shared awareness that possesses intrinsic worth in its own right. Aesthetic experience works analogously: appreciating a painting is not a sequence of discrete sensory pleasures but an integrated conscious state in which perceptual and emotional elements form a single unity.

This resolves the composition problem: the parts are instrumentally valuable insofar as they contribute to the unity, while the unity itself is the bearer of intrinsic value. So individual moments of affection are valuable only as parts of the friendship, not independently. By contrast, an aesthetic experience involves only one subject, so there is no parallel difficulty about distributing value across persons; the unity is contained within one consciousness.

Regarding the object of appreciation, Moore is clear that the painting itself is part of the organic unity and therefore shares in the intrinsic value of the experience. The object is not a mere cause of the mental state; because the experience includes the object as presented, the object participates in the value of the whole rather than standing outside it.

Finally, on moral development: if friendship improves the moral character of the friends, this might seem to make friendship instrumentally valuable for producing virtue. But Moore should reject this contribution, because on his view virtue itself is only instrumentally valuable for producing good states of consciousness. So if friendship produces virtue, and virtue produces further good states, friendship is ultimately valuable only as a means. Therefore Moore must deny that moral development adds anything to friendship’s intrinsic worth.
