Construct a thought experiment engineered to test whether a single first-person perspective—an "I"—can be distributed across two physically separated brains without collapsing into either two subjects or one subject with an illusion of division. The case should be built around an asymmetric functional division: Brain A houses the continuous autobiographical memory stream and long-term narrative identity, while Brain B houses present-tense agency, volitional control, and affective self-monitoring, with the two coupled via a high-bandwidth, bidirectional neural interface that operates only during waking hours. Constraints: avoid science-fictional mechanisms (no perfect uploading, no quantum coherence, no disembodied AI); keep the scenario realizable in principle with existing or near-future neuroscience (e.g., split-brain architectures, brain-computer interfaces, or targeted neurostimulation); present the core scenario in a single paragraph. Then show what Hofstadter's pattern-based account of the self—according to which the "I" is a high-level loop of symbols rather than a substance localized in one skull—would say about the unity and persistence of the subject in this arrangement, and contrast it with the verdict of a substance-based or brain-bound theory of personal identity. Identify the precise structural feature that does the work—whether it is the directional asymmetry of contribution, the temporal discontinuity of coupling, or the absence of a single locus of integration—and explain what happens to the disagreement if that feature is neutralized (for instance, if both brains contribute symmetrically to memory and agency). Finally, note whether removing the feature collapses the case back into a standard split-brain debate or leaves a genuinely novel problem about distributed selves. The point of the exercise is not to settle the metaphysics of identity, but to isolate the exact condition under which a pattern-based view must either accept a genuinely distributed "I" or retreat to a localized one.
