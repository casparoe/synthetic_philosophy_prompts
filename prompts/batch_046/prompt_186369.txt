I'm taking a graduate seminar on psychiatric ontology and we've been working through the idea that psychiatric categories might be "interactive kinds" in Hacking's sense—classifications that, once institutionalized, change the people classified in ways that alter the very properties that ground the classification. I tried to turn this into a precise argument about why psychiatric taxonomy is uniquely threatened, but now I'm stuck with a conclusion that seems to swallow general medicine too. Here is the chain I built: (1) If a category C is an interactive kind, then applying C to an individual causally influences the properties that are used to determine membership in C. (2) In psychiatric classification—say, for a disorder like borderline personality disorder—being diagnosed changes self-concept, help-seeking patterns, and interpersonal dynamics in ways that reshape the symptom cluster. (3) So for psychiatric categories, the diagnostic criteria and the phenomenon they track are not causally independent. (4) A necessary condition for a scientific taxonomy is that the properties used for classification be causally independent of the act of classification—otherwise the taxonomy measures its own effects rather than an underlying reality. (5) Therefore psychiatric categories fail to meet a necessary condition for scientific taxonomy. (6) Now consider that the same looping occurs in non-psychiatric medicine: labeling someone "pre-hypertensive" alters diet and stress, which changes blood-pressure trajectory; labeling a pregnancy "high-risk" changes monitoring intensity and behavior. (7) So either the causal-independence condition fails everywhere, undermining medicine generally, or psychiatric taxonomy is not uniquely threatened. (8) But if the condition fails everywhere, then no applied science can have stable taxonomies, which is absurd. (9) Hence the argument must contain an error if psychiatric taxonomy is genuinely more vulnerable than other medical taxonomies.

I want to preserve (1), (2), and the intuition behind (5), because I think looping is real and psychiatrists should worry about it. But I need to know whether (4) is the false premise—perhaps taxonomy doesn't require causal independence, only predictive stability? Or is there a hidden equivocation between "causally independent of the act of classification" and "causally independent of the institutional context"? If I drop (4), what does that cost me—do I have to concede that self-fulfilling taxonomies are still scientific? Alternatively, maybe the error is in (6): perhaps psychiatric looping is quantitatively or qualitatively different from pre-hypertensive looping, and I need a principle to distinguish them without begging the question. Where exactly should I cut, and what am I paying at that point?
