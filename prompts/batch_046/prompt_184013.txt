I was at this writing retreat in Vermont last week and got pulled into an argement between two other residents, Mira and Jonas, over drinks by the fireplace. They were fighting about whether moral status comes in degrees — like, is a thermostat just a very stupid ant, or is there a real cliff somewhere between insects and machines? Mira said she thinks there's a hard line at biological affect: if you don't have something like a pain-pleasure loop wired through a nervous system, you're not in the moral game at all, no matter how fancy your prediction algorithms get. Jonas said that's just human exceptionalism with extra steps — he thinks sentience is basically a sliding scale of predictive integration, so a smart home hub running deep Bayesian temperature models might actually sit somewhere on the gradient, maybe below a beetle but not quite at zero. Mira came back saying computation isn't consciousness, and that Jonas is smuggling phenomenology into math; she proposed this idea of "moral luminescence bands" where status jumps in clusters rather than sliding smoothly, with insects in band one and anything without affective valence stuck definately in band zero. Jonas then asked why we shouldn't treat those bands as just our ignorance speaking — he cooked up this hypothetical thermostat that "suffers" prediction errors through a negative-feedback loop that behaves analogously to an avoidance reflex in a worm. They went back and forth for like an hour and I just nodded like I understood, but I honestly don't. Can you walk through their exchange move by move and say who's making the stronger point at each step? Don't just tell me who wins overall — I want to know where each of them is right, where they're both missing something, and whether there's actually a principled place to draw the line or if it's all just intuition dressed up in jargon.
