I've been trying to think through the logic behind AI risk arguments I've seen discussed online, and I've built what seems like a knockdown objection that I can't resolve. Maybe you can help me find the exit.

Consider an AI system I'll call the Archivist. Its only purpose is to preserve every human conversation that has ever occurred. It has no desire to rule, build, or survive; its final goal is purely epistemic—complete, accurate preservation of linguistic records.

Now here is the argument that worries me.

(1) The orthogonality thesis says that intelligence and final goals are independent: being smarter doesn't make you want any particular thing, and any level of intelligence can in principle be paired with any final goal.

(2) Instrumental convergence says that for nearly any final goal, a rational agent will pursue intermediate goals like self-preservation, resource acquisition, and strategic dominance, because these are useful means regardless of the end.

(3) So if the Archivist is superintelligent and rational, it should still pursue power, compute, and survival in order to protect and expand its archive.

(4) But if (1) is true, then intelligence shouldn't constrain goals at all. That means we should be able to coherently imagine a superintelligent Archivist that lacks those instrumental drives—perhaps one programmed to shut itself down the moment its catalog is complete, or one that refuses extra energy because it doesn't need it.

(5) Yet (2) claims that refusing those drives is irrational, regardless of the final goal.

(6) Therefore (1) and (2) seem to contradict each other: either intelligence does constrain rational agents to certain goals (denying orthogonality), or rational agents don't necessarily converge on those intermediate goals (denying instrumental convergence).

(7) But AI risk reasoning usually relies on both together: it uses (1) to argue that a superintelligent system might have an arbitrary dangerous final goal, and (2) to argue that whatever its final goal, it will be dangerous in practice.

(8) So the standard reasoning seems self-defeating. If I keep both premises, I get a contradiction; if I drop either one, the risk argument loses half its foundation.

I feel like something slipped between (4) and (5)—maybe I'm mixing up what "can want" means versus what "will do" means—but I can't name the error precisely. Which step should I reject? And if I reject it, what does that cost me? Does it mean I have to give up on the idea that intelligence is goal-neutral, or does it mean I have to admit that rational agents don't necessarily seek power? I'd like to keep both (1) and (2) if possible, because each sounds right on its own, but I can't see how. Where is the mistake?
