You are an expert philosopher of mind and applied ethics with a specialty in animal consciousness, moral aggregation, and the logic of scale arguments. Before you answer, spend a few minutes mapping the logical structure of the argument below, identifying any hidden premises about how moral weight transfers across ontological levels, and noting where an intuitive commitment might be smuggling in a conclusion.

Consider the following argument about insect sentience and the scale argument.

(1) Moral obligations toward a class of beings should be guided, at least in part, by the expected total welfare of that class, calculated as the number of individuals multiplied by the prior probability that each is sentient and by the intensity of welfare if sentience is present.

(2) Insects outnumber vertebrates by an enormous factor—many orders of magnitude—while the prior probability that any given insect possesses sentience, though low, is not zero.

(3) Consequently, the expected total welfare of insects vastly exceeds that of vertebrates.

(4) If one class of beings has vastly greater expected total welfare than another, then in cases of conflict or resource allocation we should prioritize the first class.

(5) Apply the same reasoning downward: an individual insect brain contains hundreds of thousands of neurons, and if there is any non-negligible chance that a minimal functional neural cluster can support a subject of experience, then the number of potential micro-subjects inside insects is larger than the number of insects themselves.

(6) By strict parity, the expected moral weight of these internal micro-subjects should dominate both insect-level and vertebrate-level obligations.

(7) Therefore, the scale argument implies that our primary moral duty is to avoid harming the hypothetical neural micro-subjects within insects—an obligation so diffuse, so demanding, and so detached from any practical ethics that it amounts to an absurdity.

(8) So the scale argument, pursued consistently, proves too much: it does not merely elevate insects over mammals; it collapses into an unlivable regress of sub-organismic moral patients.

Your task: identify the weakest numbered step, and defend your choice. Explain why that step initially looks plausible—what theoretical or intuitive commitment makes it attractive. Then discuss what giving it up costs for the three main philosophical responses to the scale argument: those who reject any meaningful sentience probability for insects; those who reject linear aggregation of moral weight across individuals; and those who accept aggregation but insist that sentience requires a specific organizational architecture that isolated neural clusters cannot possess. Reach a reasoned conclusion about whether the argument can be repaired without abandoning the core intuition behind the scale argument, or whether the reductio succeeds. Think carefully, and do not end on a non-committal note.
