I am a retired physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, have been working through the basic texts of political philosophy; my instinct is still to think in terms of control systems, feedback loops, and the hazard of removing a regulating parameter before one understands what disturbance it was suppressing. In that spirit, I have been reflecting on Edmund Burke's critique of revolutionary rationalism and on G. K. Chesterton's related warning — paraphrased, that a reformer ought not demolish a barrier until he knows why it was erected — which together suggest that inherited institutions often encode tacit, distributed practical knowledge that no single mind possesses. I would like you to apply this Burkean framework, with particular attention to the idea of institutional inheritance as epistemic scaffolding, to a familiar historical episode: the rapid economic liberalization carried out in Russia in the early 1990s, in which price controls, state distribution channels, and industrial subsidies were abolished within months, producing hyperinflation, the emergence of a small class of wealthy oligarchs through voucher privatization, and the collapse of existing social safety nets.

Please treat the Soviet economic apparatus not merely as a metaphorical fence, but as an instance of what I will call path-dependent institutional calibration — a structure that, however coercive and inefficient, had been adjusted over decades to local conditions and therefore carried information that central planners themselves could not fully articulate. Analyze the episode through the Burkean lens: identify the principal actors and the actual options they faced, explain what the theory predicts they should have done and why, and then map precisely which features of the outcome — the speed of price liberalization, the manner of privatization, the social dislocation — the theory genuinely illuminates and which it misdescribes or leaves entirely silent. Then run the analysis in the opposite direction: does the Russian case confirm Burkean conservatism, expose an anomaly it never anticipated, or reveal a hidden boundary condition? Be disciplined about the mapping; do not force the theory to speak where it has nothing to say.

If my framing rests on a confused premise — for instance, if describing the Soviet system as a calibrated repository of practical knowledge is historically naive, or if the two-directional case analysis asks you to do something that cannot be done well without access to specialized archival sources — please say so plainly rather than complying politely. I am not asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want a conceptual analysis that could be followed from common knowledge alone, and I invite you to push back if the task as posed is incoherent.
