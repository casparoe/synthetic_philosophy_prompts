You are grading a final exam in a philosophy of formal epistemology course. The exam presented the following prompt to all students:

"Explain the Dutch book argument for probabilism. Distinguish its synchronic and diachronic formulations. Then evaluate one prominent objection that the argument demonstrates only pragmatic vulnerability, not epistemic irrationality. In your evaluation, address whether shifting to a scoring-rule framework (e.g., proper scoring rules for partial belief) changes the force of the objection, and comment briefly on whether agents with imprecise credences—represented by convex sets of probability functions—are immune to Dutch-book vulnerability."

Below is one complete student answer, reproduced verbatim for your assessment.

---

Student answer:

The Dutch book argument, developed by Ramsey and de Finetti, demonstrates that if an agent’s degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, then a bookie can construct a portfolio of bets that the agent will accept and that guarantee the agent a net loss regardless of outcome. In the synchronic case, if an agent assigns credence 0.6 to p and 0.6 to not-p, the bookie can sell the agent both bets at stakes calibrated to those credences; the agent pays out 1.2 in total and receives back only 0.4, so the loss is certain.

The diachronic formulation extends this across time. If an agent learns new evidence but updates by Jeffrey conditioning rather than by Bayes’ rule, her future credences will stand in the wrong relation to her present ones, and a bookie can arrange a sequence of bets—one placed now, one placed after the update—that together guarantee loss. This shows that rational updating is not merely prudent but required.

A standard objection, raised by van Fraassen, is that Dutch-book vulnerability shows only pragmatic irrationality: the agent is a sucker, but her beliefs might still be epistemically sound. This objection fails, however, because the diachronic argument invokes the reflection principle. The reflection principle states that your present beliefs about your future credences should align with those future credences themselves. Failing to conditionalize violates this principle, which is a purely epistemic norm about intrapersonal coherence over time. So the Dutch book argument succeeds in establishing epistemic rationality for both static coherence and dynamic updating.

As for the scoring-rule framework: replacing bets with proper scoring rules, such as the Brier score, removes the explicit betting scenario, but it does not remove the underlying problem. An incoherent agent is still dominated—there exists a rival credence assignment that yields a better score in every world—so the pragmatic/epistemic distinction collapses here as well.

Finally, agents who hold imprecise credences, represented by convex sets of probabilities rather than single point values, are immune to Dutch books. Because there is no unique credence to bet against, the bookie cannot calibrate stakes that exploit a specific violation of the probability axioms. Thus imprecision provides a genuine escape route from Dutch-book arguments altogether.
