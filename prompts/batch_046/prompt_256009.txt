I am preparing to lead a single ninety-minute seminar for advanced undergraduates in philosophy who have just completed a unit on Cartesian skepticism but have never studied G.E. Moore's proof of an external world. The students arrive with two preconceptions: they tend to view Moore either as a naive empiricist who misses the philosophical point entirely, or as a rhetorician who begs the question against the idealist by assuming what needs to be shown. I want the session to show them why the proof is neither naive nor question-begging, but I need a teaching plan rather than the lecture text itself.

Please design the explanatory arc in detail. First, identify the conceptual distinction that must be established before Moore's move can even appear intelligible—something that separates a proof of existence from an empirical report or a causal inference—and explain why it needs to precede any discussion of hands, trees, or physical objects. Second, propose one concrete carrying example or scenario to build the argument around; it should not be the familiar hand-raising demonstration, and you should specify what features the example must possess and what pitfalls it must avoid in order to carry the philosophical weight. Third, map out the misconceptions students will form in sequence: that Moore is making an ordinary empirical observation, that he derives the existence of an object from a mental state, that he confuses subjective certainty with logical proof, and that the argument only works if one has already rejected skepticism on independent grounds. For each misconception, indicate precisely where in the sequence it should be anticipated and defused. Fourth, provide one targeted question I can ask at the end of the session to test whether they understand the logical structure of the proof rather than having simply memorized its conclusion. Finally, state clearly what should be deliberately omitted at this level—such as comparisons to later Wittgensteinian remarks on certainty, or the full historical context of British idealism—and explain how leaving these out protects the clarity of the explanation. Please write your answer in American English.
