Write a decision tree that sorts philosophical responses to Goodman’s problem of projectibility — the grue riddle — into distinct positions, designed for a graduate philosophy-of-science seminar. Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from philosopher-names: first pose the diagnostic questions, then map the combinations of answers onto named or unnamed positions, marking any unoccupied combinations with a brief diagnosis (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). Each internal node must be a concrete claim or case that a student could evaluate without knowing which philosophical label it leads to. Root node: does the riddle show that inductive reasoning requires a prior distinction between projectible and non-projectible predicates? From there, split by whether that distinction is (1) an objective feature of natural kinds, (2) fixed by linguistic entrenchment and conventional use, (3) a revisable pragmatic choice, or (4) an illusion dissolved by rejecting confirmation holism. To prevent the tree from being answered by rote recall, inject the following niche analog: a hypothetical inductive agent that categorizes stimuli by discontinuous spectral-phase bands rather than by color, using a predicate frem that applies to objects below time T if they fall in frequency band A, and after T if they fall in non-contiguous band B. Include a branch asking whether this analog makes the riddle about (a) representational architecture, (b) objective physical kind-structure, or (c) nothing substantive — and require that this branch interact with the four root branches so that some combinations yield leaves. Every leaf must carry either a named position (for example, Goodman’s irrealist pragmatism, a Carnap-style linguistic conventionalism, a Quinean naturalized epistemology stance, or a Humean skeptical view about necessary connections) or the label unnamed plus one sentence describing its most awkward commitment, such as being forced to treat frem-induction as rationally equivalent to green-induction. You must produce at least eight leaves, cover all four root branches combined with the spectral-phase branch, and explicitly flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot cleanly separate, diagnosing whether the failure is substantive or verbal. Also list any logical combination of answers that has no occupant and briefly say why. Present the tree as nested bullet points with each split labeled by the claim or case that defines it, followed by a scoring key that maps answer-patterns onto leaves using only the language of the nodes, never asking the respondent to name a philosopher. Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, required readings, or requests for textual quotations; avoid depending on knowledge of any single obscure publication. Audience: students familiar with the standard grue formulation and basic confirmation theory but not being tested on doctrinal history.
