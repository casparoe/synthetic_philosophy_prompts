Write a taxonomy of positions on origin essentialism — specifically, whether you could have come from a different sperm and egg — designed as a handout for an upper-level metaphysics seminar. The deliverable should be a branching tree in which every internal node is labeled by a concrete diagnostic claim or case that splits the logical space, and every leaf is either a named position or marked “unnamed,” followed by one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Requirements for the tree:
- Root node: Is there any fact about your actual biological origin that is essential to your being the very person you are?
- If the answer is yes, the first branch must split on whether the essential fact concerns (i) the particular sperm and egg cells themselves, (ii) only the specific nuclear genome that resulted, (iii) the full cytoplasmic and epigenetic developmental pathway, or (iv) some other combination.
- Use the following concrete scenarios as the actual branch-splitting questions, not as mere illustrations: (a) you are the product of mitochondrial replacement therapy that preserves your nuclear DNA but replaces all cytoplasmic contribution; (b) you are one monozygotic twin who shares nuclear DNA with a sibling but originated from the same zygote split; (c) you were produced via in vitro gametogenesis derived from your parents’ somatic cells rather than from the actual sperm and egg that existed; (d) a somatic-cell clone carries your genome but develops in an artificial environment with radically different epigenetic programming.
- The tree must contain at least these leaves: (1) strict causal-origin essentialism (the particular gametes are indispensable); (2) nuclear-genome essentialism; (3) full developmental-pathway essentialism (including cytoplasm and environment); (4) an anti-essentialist view that permits different gametes with a different genome; and (5) at least one logically available combination that appears unoccupied, with a brief diagnosis of whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
- Every branch must be phrased as a claim or case verdict that a student can answer without already knowing the position-label it corresponds to. Do not ask “Are you a Kripkean?” or “Do you accept psychological continuity?” The sorting must operate through verdicts on the cases.
- Note any pair of leaves that your tree genuinely cannot separate, and specify which further case would distinguish them.
- Keep the scope tight: roughly six to eight leaves and four to five branch nodes, suitable for one handout page. Do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. You may inject one niche but intelligible twist — for example, treating artificial-womb epigenetics or mitochondrial DNA as part of the essential origin package — but avoid obscure technical formalisms or highly specialized theories that a well-read philosophy student would not recognize. Ensure the taxonomy is built from the logical space of answers first, with named positions mapped onto that space afterward.
