I am writing an independent essay for my philosophy coursework on the concept of actual infinity and its bearing on modern puzzles about infinite processes. I have been reading Georg Cantor's Grundlagen einer allgemeinen Mannigfaltigkeitslehre from 1883, in translation, and I am struggling with a particular passage in which he distinguishes the actual infinite from the merely potential infinite. He argues, if I understand correctly, that an infinite sequence such as 1, 2, 3, ... should not be regarded only as a process that never reaches completion, but rather as a fully determined, existing totality of all its members taken together; he suggests that treating it otherwise confuses the genuinely infinite with the merely indefinite. I should like help in understanding precisely what Cantor means by saying that such a collection exists together as a completed whole. Does he mean that properties can be ascribed to the totality itself—such as its cardinality—without requiring that we have traversed each element in turn?

To make my essay more precise, I want to apply this to James F. Thomson's well-known lamp puzzle, but with a modification that I have not seen discussed in standard introductions. Rather than toggling the lamp once at each natural-numbered minute and then asking about its state after all of them, imagine that the toggling continues through a second, identical infinite cycle: the lamp is toggled at every ordinal stage less than ω·2, with the rule that at each limit stage (such as ω itself) the lamp retains whatever state results from the limit of the preceding toggles, if that limit is defined, and otherwise it flips. Given Cantor's insistence that actual infinities are definite totalities, does his position commit him to saying that there is a determinate fact about whether the lamp is on or off at stage ω·2? Or does his distinction between potential and actual infinity instead show why the question is illegitimate, because no final stage is ever reached even within the transfinite sequence?

Please could you help me unpack the passage's meaning, and then advise whether Cantor's framework genuinely resolves the modified lamp case, or whether the resolution depends on introducing an assumption—such as the existence of a well-defined limit state at limit ordinals—that Cantor himself does not justify?
