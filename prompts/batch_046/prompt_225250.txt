I am preparing a feature for a science-and-ideas column on the architecture of agency, and in discussions with my editor we have kept returning to Douglas Hofstadter’s concept of sphexishness—the rigid, loop-bound behavior of the digger wasp that bears the term’s name, which can be deceived into repeating the same mechanical sequence without deviation. The conventional philosophical treatment uses this as the baseline absence of free will: pure rigidity against which genuine deliberation is defined. For my piece, however, I want to develop the opposite claim with full rigor, not as a pop-science gloss. Please argue that sphexishness is not merely the null point of agency but a necessary structural precondition for it. Specifically, defend the view that any system capable of flexible, reason-responsive deliberation must embed certain behavioral domains in precisely the kind of unbreakable, loop-like rigidity Hofstadter describes, and that without these “frozen” subroutines, cognition collapses into unstructured randomness rather than expanded choice. In developing this, distinguish between first-order sphexishness—mindless repetition—and what might be called second-order sphexishness: the deliberate, self-imposed preservation of rigid behavioral loops that free cognitive resources for genuine deliberation elsewhere. You should also address the obvious objection that this sounds like compatibilism in disguise, showing instead why rigidity and flexibility are functionally interdependent rather than deterministically reduced to one another. I am writing for an educated general audience, so keep the reasoning precise but avoid unnecessary technical jargon; and please do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or suggestions for further reading. Approximately five hundred to seven hundred words, in a formal register appropriate for long-form journalism.
