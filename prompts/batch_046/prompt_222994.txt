I'm preparing a ninety-minute seminar for graduate students in philosophy of law with no prior ethics training. The idea is what makes consent valid, but I want to avoid the standard checklist framing—no deception, no coercion, sufficient competence—because it obscures the interdependence of the three conditions. Specifically, I want them to see that deception, coercion, and competence form a system in which correcting one failure can generate another, and that competence is domain-relative rather than a global threshold. Plan the teaching sequence: the order in which the three conditions must be introduced and why each depends on the prior one; the single carrying example that should run through the session—specify what it must contain (institutional rather than interpersonal pressure, a technically true but materially misleading omission, and a partial rather than total competence gap) and why standard clinical-trial or contractual examples fail for this purpose; the predictable misconceptions—likely "coercion requires an explicit threat," "any deception voids consent regardless of materiality," and "competence is binary"—and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; one end-of-session question that tests whether students grasp the interdependence rather than reciting conditions in isolation; and what to deliberately leave out—probably relational-autonomy theory and the full legal doctrine of informed consent—along with why omitting it preserves the conceptual point. Keep the plan focused on the dependency structure and the misconception map, not on historical cases, bibliographies, or citations.
