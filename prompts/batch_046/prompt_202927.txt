The exam presented students with a textual description of a Bongard problem: in the left-hand panels, two circles of different sizes are arranged so that the smaller is entirely enclosed within the larger; in the right-hand panels, the same two sizes appear, but the smaller extends partially outside the larger circle's boundary. The question asked students to (a) state the rule distinguishing the two classes, and then (b) apply all three frameworks to explain how a learner might acquire this distinction, specifically addressing whether EPH's graded-prototype mechanism can accommodate the sharp categorical boundary observed, and concluding with an argument for which framework offers the most adequate explanation.

The student wrote the following. "Part (a): The left class exhibits complete spatial containment of the smaller circle within the larger, whereas the right class exhibits only partial overlap with exterior extension of the smaller circle. Part (b): Feature-Isolation fails here because both classes contain the same observable properties—two circles, a size difference, and overlap—so FI can only succeed by smuggling in a relational predicate such as 'fully-enclosed,' which is not a primitive observable feature but a derived structural description; hence FI is inadequate in principle. Relational-Binding succeeds naturally, since it encodes the containment relation directly against the partial-overlap relation, preserving categorical sharpness without auxiliary machinery. Under Exemplar-Prototype Hybridization, the learner stores specific left and right instances and abstracts graded prototypes—'small-inside-large' versus 'small-adjacent-to-large'—then classifies novel cases by similarity distance. Although prototypes are graded, the decision rule based on relative similarity dominance produces a sharp boundary whenever one prototype is closer, so EPH can indeed account for the categorical split; it merely introduces unnecessary storage costs. Consequently, both RB and EPH are viable accounts, though RB is more parsimonious. Furthermore, Bongard's original empirical studies demonstrated that human solvers rely exclusively on Relational-Binding and never employ feature lists or exemplar storage, which means the correct explanation is RB alone."
