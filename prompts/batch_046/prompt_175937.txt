You are an expert philosopher of value theory and comparative axiology, with particular familiarity with negative-state theories of well-being, Buddhist philosophical psychology, and the formal structure of population ethics. I want you to think carefully and systematically about a specific position I am calling tranquilism: the view that an individual's well-being is constituted entirely by the absence of craving—understood not merely as desire but as the affective-cognitive grip of thirst for things to be otherwise—and the absence of suffering in all its registers (physical, mental, and what might be called existential disquiet). On this view, positive goods—pleasure, achievement, deep relationship, aesthetic experience, or the exercise of complex capacities—carry no intrinsic weight; at best they are instrumentally valuable insofar as they reduce craving or prevent suffering, and at worst they are sources of hidden dependency that renew craving in subtler forms. I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of historical antecedents, but I want you to treat this as a live philosophical thesis rather than a strawman.

My request is to map the exposure of this position: what exactly would have to be true for tranquilism to be false, or at least seriously undermined? Please begin by brainstorming the conceptual space—distinguishing conceptual incoherence, empirical psychological implausibility, normative counterintuition, and structural problems that arise when the view is embedded in broader ethical theories (for instance, in decisions about creating beings, ending lives, or distributing resources). Then, sorted from fatal to merely awkward, present the defeaters: specific arguments, thought experiments, or results in other philosophical domains (say, about personal identity, the nature of motivation, or the logic of comparative evaluation) that genuinely threaten the view. I want an honest assessment of which of these a committed tranquilist would themselves concede as counting against them—perhaps the difficulty of explaining non-instrumental motivation, or the asymmetry between creating a being with no cravings and destroying one—versus which they would dismiss as relying on contested intuitions about the goodness of achievement or attachment. In particular, address whether tranquilism risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—say, by expanding the definition of craving to cover any state that is not pure absence—and whether that insulation, if present, is a theoretical virtue or a fatal methodological flaw. Please avoid appeals to obscure technical literature I could not evaluate without access to sources; ground your analysis in concepts and distinctions that are philosophically robust and widely intelligible, but push into the weeds far enough that the answer is not something that could be pulled from a standard introductory summary of negative utilitarianism or Buddhist ethics.
