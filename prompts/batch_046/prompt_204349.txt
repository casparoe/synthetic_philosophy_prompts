I am drafting the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate module on Church, State and Secularism, and I need one essay question that cannot be satisfied by merely rehearsing the standard comparative survey of England, Germany, France and the United States. Our students have already encountered the familiar typologies—constitutional establishment, cooperative separation, and strict separation—but I want to force them to navigate a sharper conceptual tension that demands they apply, rather than recite, the framework.

Please construct a 90-minute essay question, with a suggested word limit of 1000 words and a total of 25 marks, that asks candidates to evaluate this specific claim: that a state which collects religious membership dues through its public fiscal apparatus (as with the German Kirchensteuer) should not be classified as religious establishment, but rather as an instance of 'contractual delegation', and that this arrangement is therefore more genuinely secular—because it respects the equality of citizens—than the French model of laïcité, which employs state authority to suppress conspicuous religious expression in public institutions. The question should require students to distinguish constitutional recognition from fiscal cooperation, to assess whether neutrality of justification is violated when the state acts as a collection agent for religious bodies, to compare the equality objections raised against both fiscal cooperation and against bans on religious symbols, and to reach a reasoned verdict on whether the German model achieves secular equality in a way the French model undermines it. It must not ask for bibliographies, case citations, or specific court judgments.

Please provide: (1) the exam question itself, clearly formulated for the candidates; (2) a model answer of approximately 700 words, written in British English with British spelling and punctuation; and (3) a marking scheme built around four must-hit argumentative moves, with marks assigned that total 25, and for each move a one-line note on the typical misconception or omission that should cost marks.
