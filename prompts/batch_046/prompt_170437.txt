I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, though I read around the subject in my spare time, and I've been trying to work out the ethics of dramatizing real dead people in historical fiction, biopics, and—particularly given my own field—in new interactive media such as AI-driven museum installations or narrative games that let users steer the actions of figures drawn from the historical record. I don't have a framework for deciding when artistic license becomes a wrong done to someone who can no longer correct the record, and I'd like to know whether my gut reactions line up behind a single principle or whether they fracture in ways that force me to abandon one.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, mostly firm but with one I keep revisiting. First, a biopic invents a private marital argument for a well-documented scientist, using only her published letters and known temperament to shape the dialogue: this feels permissible, even though the exchange never happened. Second, a novel puts a specific, anachronistic political slogan into the mouth of a dead labor organizer—one that flatly contradicts his documented speeches—in order to recruit him for a modern cause: this feels clearly wrong. Third, an interactive installation synthesizes a dead writer's syntax and vocabulary from her complete published works, allowing visitors to ask her questions she never answered in life, but without attributing to her claims she never held: this unsettles me, yet I can't classify it with the second case; I lean toward permissible but with serious reservations. Fourth, a film stages a dramatic meeting between two dead painters who never actually encountered each other, letting them debate a theme both wrote about separately: this seems like ordinary artistic compression and is acceptable. Fifth, a documentary uses reconstructed voice synthesis to have a dead poet read aloud a poem she never performed, presenting the result as "her voice" without clarifying that the performance is fabricated: this strikes me as deceptive and wrong, though perhaps less morally grave than the political misattribution. The case I keep going back and forth on is a narrative game that lets players make moral choices for an avatar of a real civil-rights leader—choices that diverge sharply from history—framed explicitly as counterfactual exploration. Sometimes it feels like harmless speculative fiction; other times it feels like letting strangers play dress-up with someone's moral legacy, and I can't settle which intuition to trust.

Is there a principle—perhaps about fidelity to documented character, deception regarding the historical record, a kind of posthumous autonomy over one's public identity, or a distinction between private and public personhood—that captures the pattern? I'd want it to explain precisely why the invented marital scene is okay but the synthesized poem-reading is not, why the interactive writer-installation differs from the political misattribution, and whether my uncertainty about the counterfactual game should be resolved by tightening the principle or by overturning one of my firmer verdicts. If no single principle saves them all without excessive complexity, which verdict should I drop, and what does the cleaner account predict for a case I haven't listed—say, an AI chatbot trained exclusively on a dead philosopher's texts that answers student questions in the first person without ever disclosing that it is not the philosopher?
