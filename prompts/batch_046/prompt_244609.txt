Exam question:
Consider Douglas Hofstadter's analysis of M.C. Escher's Drawing Hands, in which two hands appear to draw each other into existence upon a sheet of paper. Hofstadter distinguishes a tangled hierarchy—where levels that ought to remain separate become improperly intertwined—from a strange loop, in which a hierarchical chain loops back upon its own origin. He also holds that any strange loop requires an inviolate level: a ground or framework that is not itself part of the loop, without which the system collapses into pure contradiction rather than structured paradox.

First, explain why Drawing Hands is standardly interpreted as a strange loop rather than merely a tangled hierarchy, precisely identifying the loop between the representational levels of the two hands. Second, identify what functions as the inviolate level in this image, carefully distinguishing elements that lie inside the representational loop from those that lie outside it. Third, evaluate the following claim: "If an artificial intelligence system were engineered to continuously rewrite its own source code in direct response to its outputs, it would constitute a technological strange loop with no inviolate level whatsoever, since every layer—including its hardware substrate and programming environment—would eventually be altered by the loop itself. Therefore, Hofstadter's claim that strange loops require an inviolate level is falsified by this possibility."

Student answer:
The image shows two hands, each holding a pencil and drawing the other hand's wrist and cuff onto the page. This establishes a hierarchy in which one hand belongs to the level of reality—the artist's hand—while the other belongs to the level of representation—the drawn hand. Because the drawn hand then draws the "real" hand back into existence, the levels become intertwined. Specifically, this is a strange loop rather than just a tangled hierarchy because in a tangled hierarchy the levels remain linear even when they cross, whereas here the chain returns to its starting point: Hand A creates Hand B, and Hand B creates Hand A, closing the circle. Moreover, a true strange loop requires at least three hierarchical levels to loop back upon themselves, and since Drawing Hands involves only two levels—the real hand and the drawn hand—it technically approximates a loop without fully achieving one, placing it at the boundary between the two categories.

As for the inviolate level, the sheet of paper serves this function. Although both hands are depicted upon it, neither hand draws the paper itself; the paper remains untouched by the loop, providing the stable background against which the paradox occurs. Even though the paper is visible within the frame, it is not generated by either hand, so it stands outside the loop and prevents the paradox from becoming self-undermining.

Turning to the AI case: if a system rewrites its own code based on its outputs, then indeed every layer is inside the loop. The software modifies the software, and since the hardware is merely running that software, the hardware is also eventually modified—through firmware updates, physical reconfiguration, or functional alteration—so nothing remains outside. Consequently, there is no inviolate level in such a system. This demonstrates that Hofstadter's claim is too strong: strange loops can exist in fully self-contained systems without any external ground. The AI example therefore falsifies the necessity of an inviolate level, showing that the requirement applies only to representational images like Escher's, not to dynamic technological systems.
