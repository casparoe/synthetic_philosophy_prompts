I am a graduate student in a seminar on moral change and political philosophy, and I am struck by what looks like a decades-long impasse over how moral revolutions actually happen. On one side, Kwame Anthony Appiah argues that practices like dueling and Atlantic slavery ended not because people suddenly recognized them as immoral, but because shifts in honor codes made defending them socially costly and shameful; moral reasoning followed rather than led the change. On the other side, the materialist thesis associated with Eric Williams holds that slavery collapsed because it became economically irrational—industrial capitalism made plantation labor unprofitable—while dueling faded as aristocratic status structures dissolved under bourgeois economic pressures. Both camps repeatedly invoke the same historical episodes—British abolition, the decline of dueling in England, footbinding in China—and both claim the other is either mistaking correlation for causation or reading normative justification into material necessity.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a substantive empirical disagreement that a newly discovered archive could settle, but a conceptual deadlock about what counts as an explanation of normative change. Specifically, I suspect Appiah and the Williams tradition are answering different questions—Appiah about the mechanism of public justification and social recognition, the Williams side about the structural conditions that make certain justifications available—while treating the same historical narratives as if they were competing causal accounts of a single event. But I am unsure whether there is a hidden empirical disagreement about counterfactuals (for instance, whether abolition would have occurred without the economic pressures), or whether the dispute is partly verbal, turning on whether honor is reducible to status-interest by another name.
