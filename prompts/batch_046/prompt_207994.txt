As a novelist drafting a narrative about synthetic cognition, I have found myself returning repeatedly to Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian—not as an evolutionary timeline but as a set of coexisting architectures of agency. In conversation with friends over the past month, I proposed a hypothetical system that acquires behavioral flexibility not through individual trial and error, nor through the internal simulation that defines Popperian creatures, but through the rapid assimilation of culturally transmitted heuristics via language-like protocols; yet this system lacks the full semiotic autonomy and reflexive meta-representation that Dennett assigns to Gregorian creatures. The result is a hybrid that seems to occupy a space the taxonomy does not cleanly map, and it has convinced me that treating these four categories as a linear ladder obscures more than it reveals.

If we reconceive them instead as dimensions of a conceptual matrix—selection without learning, conditioning without modeling, modeling without culture, and culture with reflexive autonomy—then several open questions become urgent for anyone trying to classify or design minds. Among them: whether Gregorian cultural scaffolding necessarily requires a prior Popperian substrate, or whether sufficiently dense social networks could allow scaled Skinnerian conditioning to generate Gregorian-like outcomes directly from Darwinian selection; whether the boundary between Skinnerian and Gregorian minds is categorical or merely a point on a continuum of semiotic dependency; and what normative or metaphysical weight we should assign to intermediate architectures that possess cultural transmission without full reflexive autonomy. I am less interested in an empirical survey than in a defended conceptual agenda: which two or three of these questions are most worth sustained philosophical attention, in what order should they be pursued, and why?
