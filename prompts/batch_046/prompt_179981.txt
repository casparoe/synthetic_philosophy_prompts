I am a novelist midway through a manuscript in which the central figure gradually realizes that what he calls "myself" is not a private inner stage but a center of narrative gravity—an invisible focal point around which the other characters' recollections, motives, and dialogue orbit like debris around a mass with no substance of its own. In workshop discussions with other fiction writers, we have been arguing over whether such a Dennettian conception of the self actually undermines moral responsibility or merely relocates it, and I want to sharpen my own intuition through a piece of sustained satire rather than straight argument.

Please write a deadpan parody of an academic tenure-review dossier, roughly 600 words, composed in the bureaucratic voice of a literary-studies committee evaluating my protagonist for promotion from "minor supporting figure" to "fully realized autonomous self." The dossier should treat narrative gravity as an empirical, measurable property: cite the candidate's gravitational coefficient (how many subsidiary characters have revised their personal histories to accommodate his presence), audit his self-model for robustness against contradictory backstory injections, and penalize any residual Cartesian essentialism as a failure of theoretical hygiene. The satire must exaggerate a real feature of the view—namely, that if the self is literally a story, then moral praise and blame become editorial judgments about narrative coherence rather than responses to an underlying agent—without inventing properties foreign to narrative-gravity theories. Keep the irony sustained and deadpan; a specialist should recognize the parody as fair, and a reader should finish feeling the genuine tension over whether this framework leaves any standing room for authentic responsibility. Please begin the response with the dossier itself, with no preface or commentary.
