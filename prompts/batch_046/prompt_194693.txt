In my graduate seminar on value theory, we have been circling the heterogeneity problem for hedonism for weeks without making any real progress, and I am beginning to suspect that the stall is structural rather than evidential. The familiar dispute runs as follows. Hedonists maintain that every pleasure—whether arising from a warm bath, a solved mathematical proof, or a recalled melody—exhibits some uniform feature, often described as a bare hedonic tone or a distinctive pro-attitude of liking, which justifies treating them as members of a single value-category. Critics, reviving classic objections that run back through the twentieth-century literature on well-being and more recent phenomenological challenges, reply that careful introspection reveals no shared qualitative thread across these states; at best, they share only the fact that we happen to apply the same word to them. What is frustrating is that both sides now concede the descriptive landscape: hedonists acknowledge that pleasures vary wildly in intentional content, sensory modality, and cognitive embedding, while critics grant that there may be some minimal evaluative orientation present in each case. Nevertheless, nobody shifts position. So I want to ask: is this disagreement genuinely about the phenomenology of affect, or is it disguised? Might one side be asking what pleasures share phenomenally, while the other asks what justifies grouping them together normatively or as a natural kind?

My own working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because it silently conflates three separate demands for commonality—phenomenal resemblance, evaluative interchangeability, and natural-kind unity—without recognizing that meeting one does not entail meeting the others. For example, if pleasure is a determinable rather than a determinate quality—more like color than like scarlet—then the absence of a single introspectible feel is not a failure of hedonism but a category mistake on the part of the critics. Conversely, if hedonism requires that all pleasures contribute identically to well-being, then even a uniform pro-attitude may not salvage the view. Given this possible diagnosis, is there anything—perhaps an empirical finding in affective neuroscience, a formal distinction drawn from the philosophy of science, or a conceptual clarification about determinables—that could actually move the dispute? Or is the question ill-posed in a way that means genuine progress requires abandoning the search for the common feature altogether? I am asking for an honest diagnosis of the kind of disagreement this is—verbal, substantive, about standards of evidence, or about bedrock intuitions—supported by what each side actually says, together with a clear statement of what, if anything, would count as progress.
