I'm writing a paper for my philosophy of economics course on Keynes's account of uncertainty, and I've arrived at what feels like an internal tension in his work. In his Treatise on Probability, Keynes treats probability as a logical relation between propositions, rejects both frequency interpretations and purely subjective ones, and introduces the idea that the weight of an argument can increase with evidence without changing the underlying probability. From this, it seems to me that the uncertainty Keynes describes in the General Theory—where investors face genuinely unknown future returns—must amount to arguments with low weight that could, in principle, be strengthened by gathering more information. If so, then the distinction between risk and uncertainty collapses into a temporary epistemic limitation rather than a structural feature of economic life, and animal spirits are just a label for rational agents updating on weak priors. Where exactly does this reading go wrong? Specifically, what feature of Keynes's logical-relationist framework prevents economic uncertainty from being dissolved into calibrated subjective probability once sufficient data is available?
