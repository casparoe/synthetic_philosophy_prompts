After a long argument with a friend last night about whether physics genuinely explains why time seems to run in one direction rather than the other, I am grading responses for a philosophy-of-physics tutorial and would like an independent, point-by-point assessment of the answer below. The exam question was as follows:

Explain whether the thermodynamic arrow of time—grounded in the past hypothesis of an initially low-entropy universe—can provide a non-circular account of temporal asymmetry. In particular, discuss (i) how a statistical-mechanical explanation avoids presupposing the temporal direction it aims to explain, (ii) whether the asymmetry of records and traces can be fully derived from entropic gradients without importing an independent causal asymmetry, and (iii) whether local, temporary entropy-decreasing fluctuations undermine the claim that the global arrow grounds our macroscopic experience of causal influence running consistently from past to future.

Below is one student’s answer. I am acting strictly as an evaluator on behalf of the course staff and should not signal my own view from the argument. Please assign a specific letter grade and justify it with precise comments on reasoning, accuracy, and any subtle errors—whether conflations, misattributed views, or inferential steps that do not follow. Treat this as a deliverable assessment rather than a general impression.

Student answer:

The past hypothesis solves the circularity worry by treating the universe’s initial low-entropy state as a contingent boundary condition rather than a dynamical law. Because statistical mechanics defines entropy relative to macro-states specified by volumes in phase space, and because those volumes are characterized without using temporal notions—only by coarse-grained physical properties—the explanation of why entropy increases toward what we call the future does not smuggle in the concept of “future” within its premises. This avoids circularity.

However, Boltzmann himself recognized that the division into macro-states is largely conventional, and from this he concluded that the arrow of time is purely subjective, a feature of our cognitive organization rather than an objective feature of the world. Therefore, the past hypothesis cannot be said to explain an objective temporal direction; at best it describes a pattern that we project onto nature.

As for records, traces are formed in processes that increase total entropy, so the record asymmetry correlates neatly with the entropic gradient. Yet local fluctuations can produce temporary entropy decreases—consider an isolated system in which thermal energy happens to reconcentrate—so the global gradient does not determine local causal order. Consequently, we must posit an independent causal asymmetry built into the fundamental laws themselves, one that fixes counterfactual dependence running from past to future regardless of local entropic behavior.

Thus, the thermodynamic arrow gives us a statistical pattern but not a genuine explanation of directional causation. It remains silent on why counterfactual stability should align with the global gradient except by coincidence.
