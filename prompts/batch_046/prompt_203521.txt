I've been teaching Hofstadter's ant fugue material again — Aunt Hillary, whether the colony counts as a mind, all that — and I keep seeing the same argumentative loop in essays and seminar papers. A student says an ant colony can't be conscious because there's no central place where everything comes together. I reply that human brains are distributed too — no single neuron sees the whole picture. They then say brains have fuctional integration, maybe global workspace dynamics, that binds things together. So I ask: if we built an artificial system with exactly that architecture but made of ant-like agents, would it be conscious? They say no, the substrate is wrong — biological neurons matter. But then they agree that a silicon brain simulation could be conscious, so substrate independence is back on the table. The criterian keeps sliding: first centralisation, then integration, then substrate, then back to function. The conclusion — ants don't have minds — never budges. Is there an established name for this kind of retrofitted boundary-drawing, where the conditions for attributing a mind are adjusted case-by-case to exclude distributed systems without also excluding brains? I know there's motte-and-bailey, and there's emergence literature, but I'm after the specific failure mode where the ontological standard shifts to protect an intuition. If there's no name, coin one and draw its edges — how it differs from ordinary ad hoc reasoning, and where the boundary cases sit. And please say what would change your mind: which finding — say, evidence of global workspace dynamics in actual ant colonies, or a proof that substrate independence fails — would convince you this sliding is rigorous philosophy rather than a defensive pattern?
