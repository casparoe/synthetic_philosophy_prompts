Construct a formal decision-tree taxonomy for classifying positions on whether moral status is gradable and how far downward it extends—from adult humans through insects to simple regulatory devices such as thermostats. The tree must be navigable without prior knowledge of the philosophical labels, so phrase each internal node as a concrete commitment question rather than as jargon. Inject the following conceptual distinctions into the branch logic: (a) whether the relevant mental property is phenomenal consciousness, access consciousness, or a functional analog of homeostatic self-regulation; (b) whether gradation is continuous, clustered into discrete tiers, or discontinuous due to the absence of a unified experiential perspective; (c) whether artificial feedback systems that correct deviations (e.g., thermostat circuits) instantiate the structurally relevant capacity in a degenerate form, or whether biological implementation is necessary; and (d) whether what grounds moral status is the intensity of valenced experience, the structural complexity of the experiencing subject, or a relational property of being wrongable. Each leaf should be named (for example: continuous-gradient sentientism, tiered biocentrism, binary neural-thresholdism, relational-functionalism, or unnamed if the combination is unoccupied) and accompanied by a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment. Identify any pair of leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate, and for every unoccupied branch combination—such as a view that accepts continuous gradation but places insects at zero—provide a brief diagnosis of whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. The taxonomy should contain no more than six branch points and should be suitable as a seminar handout for philosophy of mind or ethics.
