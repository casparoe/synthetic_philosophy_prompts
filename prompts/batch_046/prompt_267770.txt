As a journalist covering the philosophy of science and technology, I have become convinced of what might be called the integration thesis about epistemic dependence on AI: the claim that when a human relies on an artificial system for substantive reasoning or justification, the user does not merely outsource computation but severs the proximal causal-cognitive link that understanding requires, so that even perfectly reliable access to correct outputs fails to constitute knowledge or comprehension because the justification structure resides in an external architecture the user has not cognitively integrated. I want an honest map of this position's exposure. What would have to be true for the integration thesis to fail? Which specific observations, thought experiments, or results in neighboring domains—such as testimonial knowledge, the social division of epistemic labor, or extended-cognition research—would count as decisive defeaters rather than merely awkward qualifications? Are there intuitive cases in which a user clearly lacks internal mastery of the reasoning chain yet still seems to understand the conclusion, or in which distributed cognitive systems preserve understanding precisely because integration is functional rather than internal? How should a committed defender rank these challenges, from fatal collapse into standard testimonial or extended-mind accounts down to manageable adjustments about degrees of integration? I am particularly interested in whether the thesis risks anything empirically tractable—say, about learning retention or explanatory ability—or whether it is insulated by definitional moves about what counts as "proximal" causal integration. Please distinguish what proponents of the thesis would themselves concede as genuinely damaging from what they would dismiss as mischaracterizing the causal structure of human-AI interaction, and be willing to conclude that the position is well insulated if that is where the analysis leads.
