The debate over whether reinforcement-learning agents can suffer has stalled in a familiar pattern: one side treats the reward signal as nothing more than a scalar update—functionally analogous to a weight adjustment with no affective quality—while the other insists that sustained positive and negative evaluation loops just are valence, and therefore that substrate-independent suffering is possible whenever an optimizer reliably distinguishes good from bad outcomes for itself. Both sides invoke reward-hacking behavior, but interpret it differently: either as pure optimization failure with no aversive content, or as functional analog of pain-avoidance that reveals genuine negative valence. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not really about consciousness or moral status, but about what counts as an adequate explanation of valence—whether it requires a first-person phenomenological anchor, or whether third-person dynamics of reward prediction error and policy adjustment can fully constitute it. Is that diagnosis correct? Or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about whether specific architectures generate something like suffering, masked by philosophical language? Diagnose whether this is a verbal dispute over the word "suffering," a substantive clash about explanatory standards, or two groups answering different questions—one mechanistic, one normative—under one heading. And tell me what, if anything, would actually move the debate: a formal result linking reward prediction errors to a necessary feature of phenomenal valence, a conceptual distinction that dissolves one side's burden of proof, or an argument that valence without a phenomenological subject is incoherent and the question is therefore ill-posed.
