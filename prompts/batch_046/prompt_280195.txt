Design a 75-minute classroom activity for 14 upper-level philosophy undergraduates in a single seminar session that makes them feel the structural force of the leveling-down objection against prioritarianism—not as an intuitive oddity, but as a consequence of applying a rank-sensitive social welfare rule. Frame the exercise specifically around the tension between the Pigou-Dalton principle and the separability axiom: participants should physically apply a weighted aggregation rule (weights 4, 3, 2, 1 assigned to ranks from worst-off to best-off) to concrete welfare profiles represented on cards, then confront a “leveling-down” profile that lowers only the top rank’s welfare without altering anyone else’s, leaving total and average welfare unchanged. The rules and scoring incentives should be arranged so that, by the formula they are using, this destructive move strictly improves the social ordering and earns the group collective points—forcing them to approve it or reject the rule they have just been applying.

Include: assigned roles (four “society” groups of three students each holding welfare-level cards, plus one rotating “social planner” per round); physical materials (numbered cards, weight tokens, a visible scoreboard); a timeline broken into setup (10 min), three ranking rounds (30 min), the critical leveling-down decision round with real small stakes (e.g., the group gains or loses shared snack-credit points based on whether they apply the rule consistently, 15 min), and a structured debrief (20 min). The debrief must map each round onto a formal feature—rank-weighting, Pigou-Dalton transfers, separability violation—say exactly which student choice in the leveling-down round corresponds to accepting or denying the prioritarian evaluation, predict what will go wrong the first time you run it (e.g., students will try to renegotiate weights mid-round or conflate leveling-down with envy), and be explicit about what this simulation cannot reproduce—namely, the normative claim that welfare itself, not just rank-order, carries independent weight—so the facilitator must supply that in closing.

The idea should emerge from the incentives and card mechanics before you name it; do not lecture on prioritarianism until after the leveling-down round.
