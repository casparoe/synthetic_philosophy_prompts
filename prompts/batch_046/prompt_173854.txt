I'm advising a small interdisciplinary research group—philosophers, institutional economists, and design researchers—with three years of funding to extend Adam Smith's framework of sympathy and the impartial spectator into domains where moral judgment is mediated by large-scale statistical representations rather than face-to-face interaction. The group is specifically interested in what I will call the "scaling problem" for sympathy: whether the imaginative projection Smith describes can operate when we know others only through aggregated data, algorithmic profiles, or demographic abstractions, and whether the impartial spectator can retain normative authority under such conditions. Several open conceptual lines have emerged from our preliminary discussions. First, whether sympathy requires actual affective resonance or can function through purely informational representations of others' situations; second, whether the impartial spectator should be reconstructed as a statistical construct—something like an aggregate of informed, unbiased perspectives—or whether such reconstruction dissolves the spectator's critical distance; third, whether Smith's critique of mercantile systems, which relied on proximity and visible partiality, applies analogously to modern attention economies where sympathy is constantly elicited but never satisfied; and fourth, whether resolving the scaling problem is even upstream of deeper questions about the normative authority of the spectator itself, or whether it is a distracting application problem that should be deferred until the foundational architecture is secure.

Given these competing directions, I want a defended prioritization rather than a survey. For the purpose of this agenda, assume the group's goal is not empirical measurement but conceptual clarification that could eventually reshape how institutions design feedback mechanisms, transparency protocols, or participatory frameworks. Rank the open questions I have mentioned—or substitute others you think are more central—according to which deserve the group's primary attention, which should be treated as downstream dependencies, and which should be deprioritized because resolving them would not substantially alter institutional design practice even if answered clearly. For each priority, specify the criteria you are using—whether tractability for philosophers rather than empirical researchers, how many other questions depend on its answer, the risk of pursuing a red herring, or the potential to change practice if resolved—and identify what kind of conceptual result over the next two to three years would cause you to reorder the list. In particular, I want you to address whether the scaling problem is the central crux that unlocks the others, or whether focusing on it prematurely risks building an elaborate institutional architecture on an unstable account of sympathy's basic mechanics. The deliverable should be an ordering with reasoning, not a bibliography or a list of scholars to consult.
