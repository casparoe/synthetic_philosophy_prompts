I am preparing remarks for an interdisciplinary workshop on formal methods in practical philosophy, and I want a consistency check before I present. My commitments are these:

(1) Gödel's first incompleteness theorem applies strictly to consistent formal systems that can represent primitive recursive arithmetic; without substantial analogical argument, it does not apply to informal, heuristic, or non-recursive reasoning processes.

(2) The Penrose-Lucas argument that human mathematicians transcend any formal system—because they can recognize the Gödel sentence of that system—is a non-sequitur, since recognizing the sentence for system S requires a meta-system that is itself Gödel-vulnerable, and humans routinely shift frameworks rather than occupying one fixed system.

(3) Despite (1) and (2), I hold that Gödelian considerations carry genuine philosophical weight outside mathematics—specifically, they show that any sufficiently powerful representational framework (including a fully formalized normative or decision-theoretic system) contains truths internal to it that outstrip its derivational capacity.

(4) I apply this specifically to ethics: a consistent formal ethical theory with enough expressive power to encode basic arithmetic—for instance, one that aggregates preferences or weights outcomes numerically—will contain normative truths that the theory itself cannot prove.

(5) At the same time, I maintain that actual human moral reasoning is not, and cannot be, captured by any single such formal system: it is non-monotonic, deeply context-sensitive, embedded in social practices, and not recursively axiomatized.

(6) I therefore reject any metaphysical dualism or anti-mechanist conclusion drawn from Gödel—there is no "Gödelian proof" that the mind is non-computational.

(7) My intended synthesis is that the incompleteness of formal ethics is structurally analogous to mathematical incompleteness (not merely metaphorical), but because humans never actually operate inside one fixed formalization of ethics, the limitation functions mainly as a constraint on normative theory-building rather than as a description of cognitive limitation.

Which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, which pairs only appear incompatible but dissolve on closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision to restore full consistency? Please rank the tensions by severity, identifying for each what would have to be given up and at what philosophical cost. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
