Write an essay addressing the following controversy in the logic of self-amending normative systems—think of Peter Suber's Nomic, but abstracted into institutional design. The question is whether a system in which every rule, including the meta-rules that govern amendment, is in principle revisable must eventually collapse into either arbitrary instability or a hidden, de facto unamendable kernel.

On one side, argue that without at least one unamendable procedural anchor—whether explicit or tacit—there is no principled way to distinguish legitimate amendment from revolutionary overthrow; the system therefore either secretly preserves such a kernel or loses its identity across revisions. On the other side, argue that this demand for an anchor mistakes contingent social equilibrium for logical necessity; a genuinely open system can stabilize through recursive expectation-tracking and reflective coherence among agents, without ever bottoming out in a fixed point.

Weigh both positions carefully, but do not end non-committally. Reach and defend a specific verdict: is the absence of an unamendable kernel a fatal flaw, a desirable feature, or a category error? Your argument should turn on the dynamics of self-reference and institutional stability, not on appeals to particular constitutions, court cases, or legal histories. If you invoke examples, treat them as illustrative thought experiments rather than historical evidence requiring citation.
