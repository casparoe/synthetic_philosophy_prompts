I'm working through what an adequate philosophical theory of introspective reliability actually has to deliver, because most debates seem to presuppose incompatible standards of adequacy without making them explicit. I want a full specification — a requirements list — for a theory that explains when first-person access to one's own mental states is genuinely trustworthy, as opposed to a merely descriptive account of how people report on themselves. The theory must accommodate specific phenomena: the well-documented asymmetry between relatively accurate affective self-report and systematic confabulation in high-level causal explanation; cases of "blind insight," where behavioral adjustment precedes conscious recognition of the mental change driving it; and the persistent calibration tension between first-person authority and legitimate third-person correction. It must also preserve distinctions that cannot be collapsed, including the difference between possessing a mental state and knowing that one possesses it, between error in the content of an introspective judgment and error in its mode of access, and between transparency for qualitative sensory states versus opacity for complex propositional attitudes. In addition, the theory must remain consistent with general empirical patterns regarding the limits of verbal self-report in causal reasoning and the fact that first-person privilege does not vanish even when external correction is available. For each requirement, state the motivating case or consideration that makes it indispensable, and indicate whether it represents a genuinely non-negotiable constraint or a contingent commitment of one theoretical camp. Finally, identify the single requirement that does the most damage to the leading existing accounts — whether rationalist transparency models, constructivist interpretive frameworks, or empiricist calibration theories — and briefly score those contenders against the full list; a table is acceptable.

Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. The evaluation should proceed entirely from conceptual analysis and generally established empirical patterns. I also want you to address whether some subset of these desiderata might be jointly unsatisfiable — that is, whether the list itself reveals a structural impossibility in reconciling first-person authority with fallibilist correction — and if so, what that implies for the very project of a unified theory of introspective reliability. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on exhaustive historical taxonomy, since the conceptual work lies in determining which demands are legitimate and which are prejudices imported from one side of the debate.
