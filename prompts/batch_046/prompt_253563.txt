I'm trying to sort out whether some of my moral intuitions reflect genuine normative distinctions or are merely stable biases and taste-like preferences, and I'm finding the boundary surprisingly slippery. In a seminar on moral psychology we discussed how preferences can be rationalized into principles after the fact, but I want to test my own judgments before settling on anything.

Here are my gut verdicts on concrete cases. If my emotionally fragile friend asks whether I like their failed painting, lying gently feels not merely permissible but required; telling the same lie to a resilient colleague feels condescending and wrong. I judge a CEO who lays off hundreds to prevent bankruptcy as less blameworthy than a manager who does so to inflate their bonus, though both cause identical harm and both acted deliberately; yet I worry this distinction is just a post-hoc preference for collective narratives. I think it is far worse for a physician to withhold a risky but valid treatment because of the physician's private religious conviction than because of a conservative statistical risk model; that one feels principled, not preferential. I consistently hold that causing harm through direct action is worse than allowing an identical harm through inaction, but the strength of that conviction fades when the inaction involves willfully ignoring a drowning neighbor rather than failing to rescue a distant stranger. The case that keeps reversing on me is promise-breaking: I feel that breaking a written contract is morally worse than breaking an identical verbal agreement, but on reflection that seems like pure legalistic bias, and then I swing back because formalization seems to signal a stronger commitment mechanism.

Is there a principle, or a small family of principles, that captures most of these patterns without becoming ad hoc? I want something that explains why emotional fragility licenses deception but resilience doesn't, why collective-justified harm receives different moral weight than self-interested harm, and why direct agency amplifies blame—while also telling me whether my written-versus-verbal promise intuition should be retained or discarded as a bias. Please state the principle precisely, identify which clause is motivated by which case, apply it to a scenario I haven't considered—say, a government official who quietly delays a life-saving regulation because of bureaucratic inertia rather than active repeal—and be explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Keep your response within about 400 words, with the principle in numbered clauses and a concluding paragraph listing the sacrificed verdicts.
