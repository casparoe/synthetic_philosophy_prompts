I work as a senior QA reviewer at a translation firm that deploys neural machine translation for legal contracts and literary texts. I need a reusable evaluation protocol—something my team can run in about thirty minutes per document, without requiring a linguistics or philosophy PhD—to decide whether a given MT output reflects genuine linguistic understanding (grasping authorial intent, resolving ambiguity via world knowledge, preserving tonal and conceptual coherence) or merely shallow statistical pattern-matching of the kind Hofstadter argues characterizes statistical translation systems. The protocol must yield a signed recommendation (approve, revise, or reject) with an audit trail, and it must explicitly flag every point where the decision remains an irreducible judgment call rather than a checkable fact.

Please construct the procedure as an ordered set of tests or checkpoints—closer to a decision flowchart in prose than a vague rubric—specifying for each step what counts as passing, what counts as failing, and where the procedure halts with a definitive answer versus where it hands off to human judgment. The hard cases it must handle include: syntactic ambiguities in legal language that happen to be resolved correctly by frequency bias rather than by grasping contractual logic; literary metaphors or irony translated with locally correct word choices but with the underlying conceptual mapping flattened; and outputs that are grammatically flawless and terminologically accurate yet miss pragmatic force (for example, a warning rendered as a neutral statement).

Then apply the procedure to three concrete scenarios:

1. A neural MT rendering of a contract clause containing a scope ambiguity that the system resolves in line with standard legal interpretation—determine whether the protocol approves, revises, or rejects, and identify which step drives the decision.
2. A literary passage relying on sustained irony, where every sentence is translated with lexical accuracy but the cumulative tone becomes earnest—run the protocol and show where it detects the failure or where it risks false approval.
3. A technically perfect, fluent translation of a short poem in which every image is preserved literally but the emotional register is uniformly flattened—this should be the case where the protocol gives an answer you think I will dislike (likely approve or weak revise), and you must say exactly which judgment call is being masked as procedural success and what the protocol cannot do about it.

Conclude with an explicit statement of the protocol's limits: what kinds of failure it will miss, what false positives it will generate, and under what conditions it should be overridden regardless of its output.
