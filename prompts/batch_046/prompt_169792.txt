Exam question: “Demandingness objections claim that act-utilitarianism asks too much of agents. Distinguish between resource demandingness and attention demandingness, and explain how Bernard Williams’s concern about ground projects relates to this distinction. Then evaluate whether adopting a scalar conception of moral reasons—where moral considerations provide weighty but not necessarily overriding reasons—successfully dissolves the demandingness objection. In your evaluation, consider how a consequentialist might respond.”

Student answer: “Act-utilitarianism demands that agents maximize aggregate well-being, which generates the demandingness objection: ordinary people must constantly sacrifice personal projects, relationships, and comfort for the greater good. This can be split into resource demandingness—the material and time costs of constant aid—and attention demandingness—the cognitive burden of always monitoring whether one’s actions are optimal. Bernard Williams criticized this by arguing that utilitarianism undermines an agent’s ground projects—the long-term commitments that constitute her identity. When moral calculation always takes precedence, the agent suffers psychological burnout and loses the sense of self that makes action meaningful. Peter Singer’s drowning child analogy reinforces the objection’s reach: if physical distance does not diminish obligation, then our everyday spending on luxuries is morally equivalent to letting children drown. Some philosophers propose a scalar view of moral reasons, where moral weight is strong but not absolute. On this view, agents can legitimately balance moral demands against personal projects according to context, rather than being required to always prioritize utility. I believe this dissolves the demandingness objection entirely, because it removes the all-or-nothing structure that makes utilitarianism so extreme. Once moral reasons are treated as one weighted factor among others, there is no uniquely correct balance point—so the theory no longer issues precise, overriding demands. A consequentialist might reply that scalar weights for well-being are still extremely high, but this misses the point: proportional weighing introduces necessary flexibility, and without a fixed threshold, agents cannot be accused of failing to meet an exact standard.”
