I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but after a long discussion with a medically informed colleague about whether pain is automatically awful or only awful because it drives us to act, I realised we were talking past each other and needed a structured framework to locate our actual disagreement. Please draft the joint statement that two opposing camps would produce in an adversarial collaboration on pain asymbolia and the badness of pain.

The first camp comprises intrinsic-aversiveness theorists, who maintain that the phenomenal quality of hurt is itself sufficient for badness: if a subject genuinely feels pain, then something bad is occurring to them, regardless of whether they show avoidance behaviour or report distress. The second camp comprises functional-role theorists, who maintain that pain is bad solely in virtue of its motivational and evaluative function; they take clinical cases of pain asymbolia—where patients retain full sensory-discriminative capacities, being able to localise stimuli, describe intensity, and distinguish sharp from dull, yet entirely lack unpleasantness, suffering, and the urge to withdraw—to show that the phenomenal state can exist without any harm.

Please structure the document as follows. Second, provide a disagreements table in which each disagreement is phrased in neutral wording that each camp would accept as an accurate statement of its own view. The central disagreement should concern whether phenomenal hurt is sufficient for badness, or whether badness necessarily requires the functional role of motivating avoidance and being evaluated as unwelcome. Third, identify the cruxes—the precise sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. I anticipate one crux concerns whether there is a robust distinction between merely detecting nociceptive damage and experiencing that detection as bad, and another concerns a hypothetical complete decoupling scenario in which all affective and motivational pathways are selectively interrupted while sensory discrimination remains fully intact. Fourth, for each crux, specify a concrete case, argument, or evidence that both sides agree bears upon it, recording each side's prediction in advance. Fifth, because of my engineering background, please include an explicit crux addressing whether analogies to software error-handling are illuminating: in distributed computing, an error signal can propagate through a system without being evaluated as bad by any supervisory or evaluative module. Both camps should state whether this analogy supports their position, undermines it, or is irrelevant, with brief reasoning. Sixth, conclude with an honest assessment of whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal—for instance, if both camps agree on all clinical and behavioural facts but define pain differently—identifying which part and explaining why.

Please write the response in formal, polished British English, using British spellings throughout (for example, behaviour, analyse, and neutralise). Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific papers I should look up. The document should be suitable for someone without philosophical training to understand, yet conceptually precise enough that neither camp could claim it misrepresents their view.
