A friend walked me into this argument and I cannot find the exit. She claims that the classic problem of dirty hands in politics is not merely solved but dissolved, and that accepting her reasoning forces us to eliminate all moral tragedy from practical life. Here is her chain, laid out precisely:

(1) Define a dirty-hands case as one in which a political actor performs act A, A is morally wrong, and A is genuinely necessary to prevent grave harm H.
(2) If A is genuinely necessary for preventing H, then in that situation the agent's alternatives are A or allowing H.
(3) If allowing H is far worse than A, then A is the best available option, and practical reason directs the agent to choose A.
(4) But if practical reason directs the agent to choose A, then A is not a moral wrong in that context; it is justified, perhaps even obligatory.
(5) Therefore, if condition (1) holds — if A is genuinely necessary — then the claim that A is morally wrong fails. The category "dirty hands" is empty.
(6) The remaining possibility is that A was not necessary; but then the actor is an ordinary wrongdoer, not a tragic figure stained by unavoidable political necessity.
(7) So the Walzerian picture of a good person compelled by office to do wrong for the public good collapses: either they acted rightly, or they acted wrongly without excuse.
(8) She extends this: the same reasoning applies everywhere — to the surgeon who deceives a patient, to the parent who breaks a promise. If valid, it removes not only political dirty hands but the very idea of a justified act that still leaves moral residue or scar.
(9) She thinks this proves too much, because it makes moral life implausibly clean and erases the intuition that some correct choices still damage the agent. She suspects a hidden slide between practical reason and moral evaluation, but she cannot name the step.

Which premise or inference should I reject, and what does the rejection cost? I am especially reluctant to give up the thought that politics is structurally distinctive — that the scale, coercion, and accountability of state power generate a kind of moral risk that private life does not replicate. Does preserving that distinction require abandoning (3), (4), or something deeper about how "ought" and "wrong" interact?
