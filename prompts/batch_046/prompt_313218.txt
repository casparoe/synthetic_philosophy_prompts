I'm taking a course on democratic theory and we started arguing about this local policy in my hometown, Millbrook, and I can't tell if its hypocritical or actually consistent. So Millbrook's planning board requires anyone asking for a zoning variance to file two forms: Form A is your personal justification, where you can say literally anything—religious duty, family legacy, whatever. Form B has to be a reason any resident could accept regardless of their worldview. The board officially only looks at Form B, but they archive Form A and sometimes cite it in their minutes when they deny requests. Meanwhile, the school board uses the same two-form set-up for curriculum appeals, except on Form B parents are allowed to invoke religious objections if they frame them as concerns about 'public moral character' rather than doctrine. So religious reasons are banned from Form B in zoning but permitted in Form B for schools, as long as you use the right vocabulary. I feel like there's a hidden theory of what counts as a 'public' reason hiding in this split, but I don't know enough philosophy to name it. What view of political justification does this pair of practices actually presuppose? Is it cohearent to wall off religious arguments in land-use decisions but let them in through the side door in education policy? And if the town actually believes that only secular reasons can justify coercive laws, why do they bother collecting Form A at all—doesn't that imply they think personal convictions have some kind of political weight even when they're officially ignored?
