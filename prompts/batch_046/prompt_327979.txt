After a long argument with a friend about why originals command prices that perfect forgeries never match, she kept returning to one comparison: an original painting is like a handwritten letter from someone you love, whereas a forgery is like a flawlessly printed replica of that same letter. She used it to license the claim that value resides in the direct causal-historical trace of the creator’s hand and intention, not in the visual properties alone. I want a rigorous audit of how far the mapping holds. Give me the correspondence table—what plays the role of the writer, the ink and paper, the recipient, the emotional weight of physical contact—then the disanalogies that actually matter: letters are usually valued for propositional content that survives copying intact, while paintings are often valued for non-reproducible surface qualities; also, in practices like Japanese woodblock printing, cast bronze, or conceptual art, the “original” is either destroyed in production or is merely an instruction, so the causal chain works differently. Does the analogy quietly smuggle in a Romantic theory of singular authorship that collapses for photography, digital files, or works produced in editions? And if the breaks are serious, does the conclusion—that originals carry an irreplaceable value—survive on its own, or was the analogy carrying the entire argument? Please separate what is settled in the philosophy of art from what remains genuinely contested among aestheticians and art ontologists, and do not present any contested view—such as that causal history always determines aesthetic value—as if it were the consensus.
