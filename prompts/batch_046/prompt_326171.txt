Here are my verdicts on cases involving very small probabilities of astronomically large moral outcomes, and I want to know whether a coherent principle lies behind them or whether I must abandon one.

Case 1: A well-established charity can save 100 lives with near-certainty for £1 million. A speculative research programme has a 99.99 per cent chance of achieving nothing and a 0.01 per cent chance of developing a technology that saves 10^20 lives. I think the charity is the better use of the funds.

Case 2: A public-health regulation will certainly prevent 200 deaths per year. An alternative framework has a 99.9 per cent chance of preventing 200 deaths per year and a 0.1 per cent chance of triggering an unforeseen cascade that saves 10^15 lives. I think we should adopt the alternative.

Case 3: A personal medical treatment offers me a 99.99 per cent chance of full recovery from a serious but non-fatal condition, and a 0.01 per cent chance of leaving me in a state from which I could eventually contribute to a project saving 10^18 lives. I think I should refuse the treatment and take the standard option with lower upside.

Case 4: A legal reform will certainly prevent 30 wrongful executions per decade, but carries a 0.001 per cent risk of being misinterpreted in a crisis in a way that causes a nuclear exchange. I think the reform should still pass.

The tension I see is between Case 1 and Case 2: in the charitable context I reject the extreme tail, yet in the regulatory context I embrace a structurally similar one. Meanwhile, Case 3 suggests I am reluctant to bear personal risk for an astronomical collective upside, while Case 4 shows I will tolerate a catastrophic tail risk for a modest but certain institutional gain. Is there a single principle—perhaps involving agency, epistemic position, or the distinction between commission and omission—that captures all four verdicts? If not, which should I overturn? And what does your preferred principle predict for the case I keep returning to: a democratic electorate choosing between a status-quo candidate and a challenger whose platform carries a 0.2 per cent probability of initiating either a durable global utopia or a protracted civilisational collapse?
