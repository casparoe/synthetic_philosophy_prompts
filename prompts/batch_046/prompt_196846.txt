I'm taking this phil language course and we're deep in Russell's 1905 Mind paper On Denoting. There's a passage right at the start where he sorts denoting phrases into three cases, and my study group is totally split on what case (3) is actually doing. Here's the bit:

"Thus a phrase is denoting solely in virtue of its form. We may distinguish three cases:
(1) A phrase may be denoting, and yet not denote anything; e.g., 'the present King of France'.
(2) A phrase may denote one definite object; e.g., 'the present King of England' denotes a certain man.
(3) A phrase may denote ambiguously; e.g. 'a man' denotes not many men, but an ambiguous man."

I get (1) — empty descriptions like the King of France — and (2) is straightforward. But case (3) confuses me. Is Russell saying 'a man' denotes one unspecified guy, or is he describing a logical ambiguity about quantifier scope? And here's where I'm probably overreaching: I'm writing a paper on descriptions in ancient historiography, and I keep wondering whether Russell's framework can handle something like 'the founder of Rome' when different sources name Romulus, or some other figure, or leave it vague. That feels like it should fall under ambiguous denotation, but since the phrase is definite ('the founder'), maybe Russell forces it into case (1) — no denotation — if uniqueness fails. Or does case (3) cover definite descriptions with contested referents? Could someone walk me through exactly what Russell means by 'ambiguous man' here, and whether his tripartite scheme can accomodate descriptions that are formally definite but historically indeterminate? I don't need a full bibliography, just help parsing the logic so I don't misapply it.
