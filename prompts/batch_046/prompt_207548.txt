Design a 75-minute classroom game for a philosophy seminar of about 14 graduate students that makes them feel the force of updateless decision theory. Before any assignment, every participant writes one conditional decision protocol—mapping a visible state (red, blue, or green) to an action (Cooperate or Defect)—and seals it in an envelope. Once all protocols are collected and duplicated, each student is randomly assigned to one of three physically separated “world-rooms,” each displaying one of the three colors. The protocol in the envelope is executed in all three rooms, but only the room matching the student’s assignment is physically inhabited by them; the other two are evaluated counterfactually. The collective payoff is determined by a printed correlation matrix that rewards logical consistency across all three executions, not by local outcomes alone. Introduce a “binding clause”: one of the counterfactual rooms displays a state that the occupant can prove they do not inhabit (e.g., a sealed verification card), yet the protocol’s behavior in that very room still shifts the real room’s payoff. Use only paper, envelopes, color cards, and the payoff matrix—no real money, no digital tools. The rules must prevent post-assignment communication and block any attempt to revise the protocol after learning one’s assigned color, so that participants experience choosing an algorithm rather than an action, and caring about outcomes in worlds they know they do not occupy. Provide a debrief that maps the sealed-protocol phase to selecting a decision function, the random assignment to refraining from world-index updating, and the binding clause to logical correlation without causal linkage. Include an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first run—likely attempts to renegotiate protocols after assignment or refusal to accept payoffs tied to uninhabited rooms—and how to handle it. Finally, state clearly which formal feature of updateless decision theory the activity can only gesture at—specifically, the modal or logical structure that justifies treating counterfactual instances as genuinely correlated—rather than reproduce.
