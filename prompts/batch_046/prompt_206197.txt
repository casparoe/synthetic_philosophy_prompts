I am taking an advanced course on agency and moral metaphysics, and in a recent seminar on Strawson's regress argument a fellow student proposed an extension that now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to prove far too much. The argument runs as follows. (1) If an agent S is genuinely morally responsible for performing action A, then S must also be responsible for being the kind of agent who is disposed to perform A. (2) Being responsible for one's dispositional character requires being responsible for the psychological and causal history that formed it. (3) That responsibility can only be grounded if S is ultimately responsible for the conditions of S's own origin — that is, only if S is causa sui in some robust sense. (4) No human agent is causa sui. (5) Therefore no human agent is genuinely morally responsible for any action. So far this is familiar, but my classmate added these further steps. (6) Accepting or rejecting an argument is itself an exercise of agency for which one must be the appropriate kind of agent; hence epistemic assent is not exempt from the regress. (7) Consequently, if (5) is true, then no one is responsible for believing (5), which entails that no one can be rationally credited or blamed for accepting it. (8) But an argument that cannot be rationally assessed — that is, for which no one can bear epistemic responsibility — cannot be genuinely known or justifiably asserted. (9) Hence (5) undermines the conditions required for its own justification and must be false. The chain looks valid, yet the conclusion that Strawson's regress collapses into self-refutation feels like a reductio that lands too hard. I want to know exactly where the slip occurs. Is (6) a legitimate extension of the Basic Argument into the epistemic domain, or does it conflate moral responsibility with rational accountability in a way that breaks the analogy? If I reject (6) or (8) to save the core regress, what do I owe by way of explanation, and does that concession weaken the original argument's force? Please separate what is widely accepted about Strawson's original regress from what remains genuinely contested about its extension to belief-formation and rational evaluation.
