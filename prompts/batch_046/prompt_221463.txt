I hold a specific form of constitutive panpsychism: macro-phenomenal properties are constituted, not summed, by micro-phenomenal properties arranged through a "phenomenal bonding" relation structurally analogous to chemical valence—individual micro-subjects do not merge into one subject, but form a composite subject whose experience is irreducibly relational, generating unified, gestalt-like macro-qualities that no micro-level subject possesses. On this view, the combination problem only threatens panpsychism if one assumes macro-consciousness must literally be an aggregate of discrete micro-consciousnesses; reject that aggregation model and the problem becomes a predictive feature rather than a fatal bug.

What would count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure: which empirical findings—say, about split-brain dissociation, distributed neural correlates, or failures of phenomenal unity—would undermine the chemical-bonding analogy; which philosophical arguments show that relational constitution still leaves subjective unity unexplained or collapses the view into a disguised emergentism; and whether specifying the bonding conditions with enough precision risks insulating the view, so that any absence of macro-unity can always be explained away by saying the conditions weren't met. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what defenders of this view should themselves concede from what critics alone allege, and assess whether such insulation, if present, is a genuine problem for a metaphysical claim of this kind.
