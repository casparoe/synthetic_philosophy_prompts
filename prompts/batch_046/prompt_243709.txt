A recent essay in political ethics summarizes Michael Walzer's problem of dirty hands in the following terms: "Democratic leaders occasionally must commit acts that are morally wrong—authorizing deception, suppressing opposition, or using coercive force—in order to preserve the state or secure a greater good. Walzer argues that such actions are justified only if the leader personally bears the guilt afterward; the presence of remorse is what distinguishes a necessary evil from mere corruption. Consequently, dirty hands is essentially a psychological phenomenon about the temperament of rulers, not a structural feature of democratic institutions. Furthermore, the essay claims that when subordinate officials carry out these policies without experiencing comparable anguish, they become the primary moral wrongdoers, which in turn exonerates the elected leader who issued the order, since responsibility has been displaced downward."
