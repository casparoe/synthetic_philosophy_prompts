For an independent philosophy-of-mind project, I am trying to determine what a theory of conscious unity actually has to deliver if it is to account for split-brain (commissurotomy) phenomena without collapsing into either mysticism or reductionism. Rather than surveying existing theories, I want the specification first.

Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of the unity of consciousness must meet in light of split-brain cases. For each requirement, state the phenomenon or conceptual distinction it protects, whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp, and the reasoning behind it. In particular, include the following:

1. The core clinical profile: each hemisphere can guide behavior and process information that the other hemisphere cannot verbally report or act upon, yet bimanual coordination sometimes succeeds. The theory must explain both the dissociation and the residual integration without treating one as noise.

2. A clear separation between phenomenal unity (a single field of subjective experience), access unity (information available for global reasoning and report), and narrative unity (a single autobiographical self-model). The theory must keep these distinct and specify, for a split-brain subject, which obtain and which do not.

3. Consistency with neuroanatomy: the corpus callosum is not the sole interhemispheric bridge, since the anterior commissure and various subcortical pathways remain intact after callosotomy. Therefore, the absence of callosal transfer cannot by itself entail the absence of all integration. The theory must specify exactly what kind of neural connectivity is necessary for each form of unity.

4. Accommodation of graded or partial dissociation. Consider a hypothetical patient in whom verbal report of left-visual-field stimuli remains intact, but voluntary left-hand action guided specifically by those stimuli is abolished despite intact motor pathways. The theory must handle such modality-specific splits rather than assuming an all-or-nothing division between hemispheres.

5. A verdict on whether conscious unity is binary—either one consciousness or two discrete ones—or whether it can come in degrees, together with what empirical pattern would settle the question.

6. Non-contradiction with the broader neuroscience result that consciousness requires some form of cortical integration, without committing to a specific mechanism (for example, global workspace dynamics versus integrated-information metrics) unless that commitment is independently justified by the unity problem itself.

Next, identify whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable. For instance, can one consistently maintain full phenomenal unity across hemispheres, accept complete callosal disconnection, and preserve a single narrative self-model? If not, state precisely which combination creates the contradiction and why.

Then indicate which single desideratum does the most work in excluding popular positions—for example, whether the graded partial-dissociation case eliminates theories that posit exactly two discrete streams of consciousness.

Finally, score three broad approaches briefly against the list: (a) the view that split-brain patients literally harbor two distinct conscious subjects; (b) the view that there remains one subject with partitioned access but unified phenomenal experience; and (c) the view that split-brain phenomena reveal no genuine division in consciousness at all, only dissociations in report and action mechanisms. Keep the scoring brief; the criteria themselves are the main deliverable.

Please avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. I need a framework I can evaluate from first principles and general knowledge alone.
