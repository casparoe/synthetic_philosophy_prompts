Assume the role of a senior philosopher of language. Before you answer, take a moment to think carefully and outline the core difficulty: that any finite record of past behavior and corrections never uniquely fixes the rule that was meant to be followed.

Consider this concrete situation. For three years, a group of friends has played a self-designed negotiation game every month with no written rulebook—only evolving verbal agreements and occasional corrections of mistakes. A founding member recently returned with a new participant who insists that moves the group has consistently rejected are actually permitted by the game's 'original logic.' The old session notes are ambiguous. Three positions have formed: (A) the correct rule is whatever best coheres with the group's accumulated practice; (B) there is no prior fact—only whatever consensus the group builds now; (C) the practice has always been guided by an implicit normative structure that can be discovered but never fully articulated, and the new interpretation violates that structure.

Without naming specific philosophers, analyze whether any of these positions genuinely escapes the skeptical problem about rule-determinacy, or whether they all collapse into the same difficulty. In particular: is there a principled distinction between genuinely continuing the practice and retrofitting a convenient interpretation? Does collective correction over time provide a non-circular grounding for the rule? And which view, if any, respects the difference between following a rule and merely conforming to a behavioral pattern?
