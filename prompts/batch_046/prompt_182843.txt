In a graduate seminar on classical Indian epistemology, we have spent the semester circling a dispute that now feels less like a disagreement about texts and more like a disagreement about what kind of question epistemology is supposed to answer. The focal issue is the Nyāya account of the pramāṇas—perception, inference, comparison, and testimony—but the deadlock concerns whether these four are genuinely irreducible cognitive capacities or whether comparison and testimony are ultimately reducible to complex forms of inference. Both sides cite the Nyāyasūtra and its Bhāṣya with equal fluency; both concede that the commentarial tradition contains passages supporting either reduction or irreducibility. Yet neither camp has moved, because the reductionists treat a pramāṇa as any causally reliable process that produces true cognition, while the irreducibilists treat it as a normatively autonomous source of justification that cannot be reconstructed without circularity. I am inclined to think that the debate has stalled not because the textual evidence is ambiguous—though it is—but because the two sides are operating with different conceptions of what an epistemological taxonomy should do: one aims at a causal-psychological map of cognition, the other at a normative classification of entitlements.

Is this diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden substantive disagreement about, say, whether testimony requires an independent source of authority that inference cannot supply, or about whether comparison involves a unique relational cognition absent from inferential reasoning? More generally, if the impasse really stems from conflating two distinct projects—describing how knowledge arises and prescribing when belief is warranted—would the appropriate progress be a conceptual distinction that separates these cleanly within the Nyāya framework, perhaps by distinguishing the origin of cognition from its epistemic validation? Or is this a case where two legitimate questions have been collapsed under the single heading "pramāṇa theory," such that no internal resolution is possible and the debate should be expected to remain a bedrock clash of intuitions about whether epistemology must be primarily descriptive, primarily normative, or irreducibly both? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says about reduction and authority, together with an honest assessment of whether anything—a formal distinction, a closer reading of the sūtras, or a change of framing—could actually move it.
