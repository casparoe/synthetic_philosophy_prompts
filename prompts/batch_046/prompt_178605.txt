I am a doctoral student in political theory working on the reception of Burkean conservatism in contemporary institutional design, and I have become stuck on a diagnostic problem rather than a substantive one. In my seminars and in recent literature on institutional reform, the debate over how to apply Chesterton's fence principle to modern administrative and epistemic structures—peer review protocols, adversarial legal procedures, credentialing bodies—has reached an impasse that feels structurally familiar, yet no one has clearly named its form. One camp treats the fence as an epistemic humility heuristic: because institutional knowledge is distributed and often tacit, we ought not dismantle inherited structures until we can articulate the coordination problem they solve, on pain of discarding solutions we do not understand. The opposing camp treats this as a category error when applied to institutions that emerged from contingency, power consolidation, or technological conditions now obsolete; they argue that the burden of proof should lie with defenders to show that the institution's current form tracks a present functional need, not merely historical persistence. Both sides accept that some institutions encode practical wisdom; both reject crude status-quo bias; and both can invoke Burkean themes about prescription and the tacit wisdom of custom. Yet when the discussion turns to concrete reform proposals—say, decentralizing a centralized accreditation system or preserving an adversarial court structure—the disagreement does not resolve into an empirical dispute about outcomes, nor does it appear to be purely axiological. I am inclined to think the stall arises because the two sides operate with different implicit ontologies of institutions: one assumes institutions are functional responses to persistent coordination problems, so that absence of visible dysfunction is weak but genuine evidence of hidden function, while the other assumes institutions are path-dependent accumulations that may have drifted far from any original purpose, so that persistence is weak evidence of current value. Is this the right diagnosis—that we are facing not a disagreement about evidence or normative weight, but a metaphysical divergence about what kind of thing an institution is, and therefore about what counts as a reason to preserve or remove it? Or is there a concealed formal or empirical question—perhaps about the relative rates of institutional drift versus explicit reasoning, or about the conditions under which functional explanations can be empirically distinguished from spurious post-hoc rationalizations—that could in principle break the deadlock? More generally, is this a verbal dispute about the meaning of function, a substantive disagreement in social ontology, or a bedrock divergence in methodological intuition about the default stance toward inherited structures? And given your diagnosis, what would actually move the debate: a conceptual clarification of functional explanation in social ontology, an empirical comparative study of reform outcomes across institutional types, or an acceptance that the fence principle and its critique answer different questions and should not be expected to converge?
