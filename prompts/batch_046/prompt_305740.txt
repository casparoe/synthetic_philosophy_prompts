I am a retired physical chemist who, since leaving active research, has devoted considerable attention to philosophy of science and formal decision theory. In recent discussions with colleagues at a local philosophical society, I encountered the strategy-stealing argument that demonstrates the first player in Hex possesses a non-losing strategy. Reflecting on this, I have developed what I call the first-mover derivation thesis: in any symmetric interactive system—whether a combinatorial game such as chess or checkers, or a normative framework like the social contract—the agent who initiates interaction can invariably appropriate the optimal counter-strategy available to the respondent, thereby rendering the respondent's position structurally derivative.

This leads me to ask whether the strategy-stealing assumption, properly understood, proves that symmetric procedural arrangements are inherently unstable, since the second party can never achieve an independent strategic optimum. More specifically, if the argument applies generally, why do chess theorists still regard the opening as genuinely contested rather than solved in White's favor? And ought philosophers of politics to reject symmetric democratic procedures on the grounds that they implicitly privilege the initiating party? Please provide your response in American English, and I would be grateful if you could explain precisely where my reasoning misapplies the underlying concept.
