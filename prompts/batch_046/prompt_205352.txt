I am currently enrolled in an upper-level seminar on metaphysics and the persistence of objects through change, and a family dispute has begun to look like a concrete case of the theoretical problem we have been discussing: whether something that undergoes total qualitative change over time remains one and the same thing, or whether it is better understood as a succession of distinct stages linked together.

Here is the situation. In 1995, my grandfather created a trust that designates as beneficiary "the person who completes the family apprenticeship program." The program is rigorous and long—typically fifteen years—and is designed to produce a comprehensive transformation of skills, values, and social ties. Participants enter with one set of commitments and relationships and exit with a different career, different beliefs, and almost no overlap in memories or personal connections with their earlier stage. My cousin entered in 2010 and completed the program this past year. Now, however, several relatives argue that she should not receive the distribution, because she is not "the same person" who entered the agreement. They emphasize that she cannot recall her life before 2012, holds political and religious commitments diametrically opposed to those she held at entry, and has replaced every significant personal relationship from that period. My cousin, by contrast, insists that she is obviously continuous with the entrant—same legal identity, same bodily continuity, an unbroken stream of consciousness—regardless of how radically the contents have changed.

What complicates the philosophical interest is a claim I encountered in our course materials: if one adopts the view that persisting things are four-dimensional entities composed of distinct temporal parts, then the question of whether she counts as the beneficiary may not have a unique answer; instead, it might depend on which temporal stage we choose to privilege in interpreting the trust. Another position we discussed holds that there is a single, enduring subject that is fully present at each moment and remains strictly identical despite every change.

I want to understand whether our family disagreement is tracking a genuine metaphysical dispute or whether it is largely verbal—whether we are arguing about the nature of reality or about how to apply a word. Specifically, I am asking: does one of these two frameworks—the view that the person is wholly present at each time versus the view that she is an extended series of temporal stages—have stronger philosophical support? And if the second framework is correct, does that undermine the idea that there is a fact of the matter about who should inherit?

Please distinguish, in your response, between points that are broadly accepted among philosophers working on these issues and points that remain actively contested. I am working without library access and do not need citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts; I want the reasoning evaluated on its own merits.
