As a retired crystallographer who has recently turned to the philosophy of physics, I am preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for first-year philosophy graduate students who have completed an introductory course in general relativity but have not yet studied philosophy of science. I want to teach Einstein’s hole argument—not as a historical curiosity, but as a live problem about the ontological status of spacetime points. Please do not explain the argument to me; instead, plan the explanatory arc for my session. Specifically: (1) What must come first—must I begin with the distinction between active and passive diffeomorphisms, or with the failure of determinism in the field equations? Justify the ordering with reference to conceptual dependencies. (2) Choose one concrete carrying example: I am inclined to adapt an analogy from my own field, namely the labeling of atomic positions in a crystal lattice containing a dislocation defect, but I want you to assess whether this analogy succeeds and to explain precisely why the more common “city map with renamed streets” analogy should be avoided or modified in this context. (3) Identify the three misconceptions these students are most likely to form—my preliminary guesses are (a) that the argument proves spacetime points are unreal, (b) that it is structurally identical to gauge redundancy in classical electromagnetism, and (c) that it undermines determinism in a manner unique to general relativity—and specify exactly where in the sequence I should intervene to block each one. (4) Provide one final diagnostic question I can ask that tests whether they understand the difference between representational redundancy and ontological underdetermination, rather than testing mere recall of the argument’s steps. (5) State explicitly what you would leave out—whether the fiber-bundle formulation, the modern relationalist response, or the debate between substantivalists and relationalists about manifold points—and explain why omitting it is pedagogically necessary rather than merely convenient. Please keep the plan formal, precise, and structured; I am accustomed to rigorous exposition from the natural sciences, and I want this session to meet that standard without becoming a history lesson or requiring bibliographic apparatus.
