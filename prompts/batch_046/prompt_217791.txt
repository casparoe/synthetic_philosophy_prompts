I am grading responses for a graduate seminar problem set on updating with uncertain evidence. The problem asks students to apply Jeffrey conditionalization to a clinical diagnosis scenario and then to address a standard conceptual objection. The setup is this. A physician assigns prior probabilities P(A) = 0.08 to a rare autoimmune disorder, P(V) = 0.55 to a common viral infection, and P(O) = 0.37 to other conditions. The test characteristics are P(+|A) = 0.90, P(+|V) = 0.30, and P(+|O) = 0.15. Rather than treating the observed positive result as certain, the physician partitions her experience into three cells: E1, that the result is genuinely positive, with direct probability 0.60; E2, that contamination produced a false positive, with direct probability 0.25; and E3, that a false negative was misread as positive, with direct probability 0.15. The student is asked, first, to compute the Jeffrey-updated posteriors for A and V; second, to explain precisely why strict Bayesian conditioning on the proposition that the test is positive is inadequate here and which feature of Jeffrey's rule addresses the inadequacy; and third, to evaluate the charge that Jeffrey conditionalization violates the principle of total evidence, being careful to separate Jeffrey's own justification—that experience is graded rather than propositional—from later derivational defenses that embed the rule within standard Bayesianism by positing hidden variables governing the observation process.

One student submitted the following. For the computation she writes that P(A|E1) is approximately 0.25 via Bayes's theorem, that P(A|E2) equals the prior 0.08, and that P(A|E3) also equals the prior 0.08 because misreading does not change the base rate; hence P'(A) ≈ (0.60)(0.25) + (0.25)(0.08) + (0.15)(0.08) ≈ 0.18. For V she uses P(V|E1) ≈ 0.56, P(V|E2) ≈ 0.55, and P(V|E3) ≈ 0.60, giving P'(V) ≈ (0.60)(0.56) + (0.25)(0.55) + (0.15)(0.60) ≈ 0.56. In the conceptual section she argues that standard conditioning fails because the physician's credence in the result is only partial, and Jeffrey's rule remedies this by allowing probabilities to be assigned directly to experiences rather than to propositions. She then addresses the total-evidence objection by stating that the critic is correct: Jeffrey permits arbitrary partitioning of experience, which means the agent can neglect parts of her mental state. She adds that Jeffrey himself avoids this problem by deriving his rule from strict conditioning on a hidden-variable model of the observation apparatus, so that the partition is not arbitrary but determined by the physical causal structure of the test.

Please grade this answer out of 20, with specific point-by-point comments. In particular, check whether her computation for A and V correctly applies the Jeffrey formula given her own partition values; whether her explanation of standard conditioning's failure captures the right distinction; and whether her treatment of the total-evidence objection correctly distinguishes Jeffrey's primitive, non-propositional justification from the hidden-variable reconstructions advanced by later authors. Focus on technical and conceptual accuracy rather than writing style.
