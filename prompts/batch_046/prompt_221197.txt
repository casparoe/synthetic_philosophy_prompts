I'm taking this course on philosophy of history and there's this weird split in how we treat contingency that I keep noticing. On one side, basically every history museum near me now has these interactive branch-point exhibits—like the one where you can turn a dial and see teh 20th century diverge because an archduke's driver takes a wrong turn, or because one telegram gets delayed. The plaques always invoke that old rhyme about a lost nail and a lost kingdom, and the gift shops sell board games built around what-if moments. But then my cousin's high school history department uses a grading rubric that explicitly docks points for essays that focus on individual mistakes or accidental events and rewards students for identifying long-term structural causes instead. The same school district funds both the museum partnership and the curriculum. So we literally build monuments to chaos and then teach kids to write it out of their explanations.

What philosophical view of historical causation—or of what counts as a legitimate explanation—does that institutional split presuppose? Does it need the idea that micro-contingency is real but only fit for public entertainment, while serious knowledge must deal in structural necessity? Or can the split be explained purely as a genre distinction without any deeper ontological commitment about whether history is actually chaotic? Reconstruct the implicit theory behind the practice, say whether it's internally consistent or whether it smuggles in two incompatible pictures of teh past, and tell me what would have to change in either museum design or exam rubrics if we replaced that implicit view with one that treats small contingent triggers as fully legitimate explanatory factors everywhere.
