You are a philosopher of rational choice with expertise in decision theory, causal reasoning, and the evidential-causal debate. I want you to think carefully and systematically about the following cluster of scenarios, which sit near the classic Newcomb-style dilemma but are modified in ways that blur the usual one-box versus two-box taxonomy. Before giving your final assessment, please briefly map out the structural variables—predictor accuracy, temporal proximity of the scan, presence of correlation-breaking interventions, and agent-sample size—that differentiate the cases.

Here are my intuitive verdicts. Case A: a predictor with 90% accuracy scans your brain 24 hours in advance and fills an opaque box with $1M only if she predicts you will one-box; a transparent box holds $1,000. I feel one-boxing is clearly rational. Case B: the predictor is 99.9% accurate, but the scan occurs one millisecond before your choice, and there is a physically possible feedback loop through which two-boxing could corrupt the scan signal. I still lean one-box, but the causal argument for two-boxing feels much stronger here—my confidence drops noticeably. Case C: two independent predictors, each 80% accurate, both fill the box only if they agree you will one-box. I feel this should push me further toward one-boxing, though I am unsure whether the reason is the conjunction of predictions or the effective increase in reliability. Case D: you can purchase a "causal shield" for $100 that makes your decision physically independent of the scan, but the shield's activation is itself visible to the predictor before she fills the box. I think buying the shield and then two-boxing is rational only if the shield genuinely breaks the correlation, not merely symbolically; otherwise it feels like a costly way of one-boxing in disguise. Case E: you are one of a million identical agents, the predictor can scan only 1% of them due to resource limits, and you do not know whether you are in the scanned subset. Here my intuition collapses entirely—I do not feel either strategy is clearly superior.

My question is this: is there a single coherent principle—perhaps about the normative weight of evidential reasons relative to causal reasons under conditions of predictive transparency, or about the conditions under which an intervention severs the correlation that grounds the prediction—that captures Cases A through D? If so, state it precisely and show which feature of each case activates which clause. If not, offer the smallest family of principles that does. In either case, explicitly discuss Case E: does your principle deliver a verdict there, and if so, does that verdict align with my intuition of collapse, or does it suggest that my intuition in Case E should be revised rather than accommodated? Please reach a definite conclusion about whether my pattern of judgments is best explained by evidential decision theory with a causal-intervention exception, by some hybrid account, or by the failure of any unitary principle.
