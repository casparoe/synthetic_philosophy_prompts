I am presenting my paper at a decision-theory workshop next Thursday. The thesis I defend is that standard evidential-cooperation arguments for large-world longtermism fail because of what I call correlation decay: as the population of evidential twins grows, the evidential link between my local choice and the global distribution of twin choices weakens non-linearly. Local self-knowledge about my own reasoning process becomes statistically unrepresentative of the global twin distribution, so linear scaling of cooperative payoff is unjustified. I do not reject evidential reasoning altogether; I claim that its scaling law is sub-linear to the point that many canonical longtermist cooperation scenarios collapse.

The audience is precisely this: two decision theorists who are committed evidential-decision-theory proponents and have worked through the formal literature on multiverse-wide cooperation and the unilateralist's curse; one causal-decision-theory advocate who regards evidential reasoning about distant worlds as causally incoherent; one updateless-decision-theory proponent who believes reflection principles already dissolve the problem; two philosophers of science skeptical of the modal-realist ontology required to talk about evidential twins across worlds; and a graduate student working on reflective consistency and the reflection principle. They care about formal rigor, not about applied ethics or policy.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet. I need the eight objections I will actually face, ordered by how damaging they are and by which subgroup will raise them. For each objection, phrase it exactly as that audience member would phrase it — using the technical vocabulary they prefer, not generic philosophy objections. For each, specify: (1) what I should concede immediately to preserve credibility; (2) what I must hold firm on; (3) the weakest point in my reply; and (4) a concise reply suitable for a two-minute exchange.

Specifically include the following targets:
