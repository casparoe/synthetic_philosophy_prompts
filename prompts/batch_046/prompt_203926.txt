Since I began formalizing my own artistic practice—specifically, a recent series of algorithmic compositions that interpolate between harmonic spaces and a concurrent attempt to articulate my philosophy of creation for a forthcoming monograph—I have found myself committed to the following package of views about creativity. They are largely inspired by Hofstadter’s model of conceptual variation, but modified with distinctions that now feel unstable, and I need to know whether they form a coherent profile.

Here are my commitments:

(a) Creativity is fundamentally knob-twiddling within a conceptual space: every genuinely creative product is a parameterized variation on an underlying theme or invariant structural core.

(b) There are two ontologically distinct classes of knobs. Surface knobs adjust thematic content—melodic contour, chromatic palette, narrative arc—while leaving the space’s ontology intact. Substrate knobs alter what entities and relations are permitted within the space itself; turning them does not vary a theme but reconstructs the framework in which themes can exist.

(c) Despite recognizing substrate knobs, I hold that genuinely creative acts are exclusively surface-level adjustments. Substrate alteration is not creativity but ontological reconstruction—analogous to replacing the chessboard rather than moving a piece.

(d) The phenomenology of “radical originality” is best explained by high-dimensional surface-knob adjustments occurring below the threshold of conscious tracking, combined with temporary substrate stability that makes the variation feel like a rupture rather than a slide.

(e) The aesthetic value of a creative work tracks its connective density: the number and strength of inter-knob dependencies it activates within the recipient’s conceptual network, not the magnitude of parameter change.

(f) Conscious access to which specific knobs are being turned is neither necessary nor, typically, present during peak creative performance; the best work arises from associative processes that hold the substrate constant unconsciously.

(g) Nevertheless, I judge that certain works—such as some atonal compositions or non-figurative paintings—are valuable precisely because they compel substrate-level reconstruction in the audience, forcing listeners or viewers to build a new conceptual space rather than navigate an existing one.

Please audit this set for joint tenability. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear incompatible and dissolve upon closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision in each case? Rank the tensions by severity, specifying for each what would have to give and at what cost to the overall view. Identify the single revision—abandoning or modifying one commitment—that would clean up the greatest number of conflicts with minimal damage elsewhere. I am not asking for a lecture on Hofstadter’s texts, for bibliographic references, or for a summary of his canonical examples; I want a consistency check on this specific profile.
