I've been thinking about procreative beneficence and liberal eugenics lately — mostly because a friend sent me that Savulescu paper and I got into an argument about whether parents are actually required to pick the "best" embryo, or just allowed to. I have a bunch of gut reactions to specific cases, and I'm trying to see if there's one principle that captures them, or if I'm just being inconsistent.

Here are my verdicts: (1) If you can do PGD to avoid passing on Huntington's, you should — it's not just permissable, there's a real moral push there. (2) Selecting for eye color or height for purely cosmetic reasons feels frivolous and tacky, but I don't think it's wrong exactly; it's not morally required, but I don't want to ban it. (3) If there's a gene edit that boosts IQ by a lot but also makes the kid emotionally flat — net welfare might still be up — I think parents can do it if they want, but they shouldn't be obligated to maximize welfare if it means trading away personality like that. (4) Choosing an embryo predicted to be more cooperative and empathic, even though that might make them less competitive, feels like it should be encouraged, but liberal eugenics seems to only care about the kid's own expected wellbeing, not social spillovers. (5) Now here's the tension: I think it's wrong for parents to select for extreme risk-taking or aggression just because the kid might genuinely enjoy the adrenaline — it creates bad externalities. But if the principle is just individual welfare, and that kid's welfare is high, I don't see how the view blocks it. Meanwhile, I also think parents shouldn't reject the "best" embryo just because they prefer a kid with a specific aesthetic trait (like being very artistic in a way that correlates with lower income). So I want the principle to allow cosmetic choice in case 2 but block it in case 5? Or maybe I need to drop one. And what about selecting against deafness when Deaf parents want a deaf child? I think they shouldn't be forced to have a hearing child, but I also feel like systematically selecting for deafness is wrong — though I'm not sure that's consistent with my other views.

So: is there a coherent principle here? Maybe something like "maximize expected welfare within a constraint of not treating the child as a means to social goals"? Or does that fail to explain why cosmetic selection is okay but risk-taking selection isn't? Which of my verdicts has to go if I want a clean account? And how does it apply to cases I haven't mentioned — like selecting for traits that are good for the child but bad for society, or selecting for diversity rather than welfare? Please push back hard if I'm mixing up procreative beneficence with liberal eugenics in a confused way, or if my cases depend on some hidden assumption that doesn't hold. I want the actual principle, or a small set of candidates, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it, and an honest list of which verdicts each one can't save. Don't just tell me I'm consistent if I'm not — I'd rather know which intuition to drop.
