I was chatting with some friends about how academic opinion can lock in even when people privately disagree, and now I'm running a philosophy workshop — about 12 grad students, one hour, in a seminar room with movable chairs. I want a classroom game that makes participants actually feel rational herding, not just understand it from the outside; they should run into the problem before anyone names it.

Specifically, I need rules that create both an information cascade (you ignore your private signal because you think everyone else knows better) and a reputational cascade (you go with the crowd to avoid being the visible outlier), so that in the debrief we can map which round was which. The setup should use sequential public commitments — people sit in a circle and announce choices one by one — with hidden private clue cards that point toward a correct answer about some abstract conceptual pattern (not the usual red-and-blue urn, please; I've seen that version a hundred times). Build in two seperate score tracks: points for being accurate, and social-cost points for being the lone dissenter. Include a payoff scheme with small real stakes (maybe candy or a shared group penalty), rules that block private signaling like eye contact or whispering, and a timeline that fits 60 minutes. After they play, the debrief should connect specific moments to Bayesian updating versus social pressure, predict what will go wrong the first run — like the first speaker anchoring the whole cascade or people cheating — and say exactly how to prevent it. Also be frank: what feature of real-world herding in philosophy or politics can't this reproduce because the stakes are artificial? No lecture up front, and don't ask them to read anything beforehand.
