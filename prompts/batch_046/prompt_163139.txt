I am delivering the opening address at a one-day interdisciplinary colloquium I have convened under the title “The Archive and the Subaltern: Epistemology from Below.” The audience is deliberately mixed: half are professional historians and philosophers of history—several of whom work on microhistory and on the recovery of subaltern lives through state, ecclesiastical, and legal records—while the other half are graduate students from other disciplines, practicing archivists, and a small number of family members and donors who lack technical background. I need an approximately eight-minute speech that takes the conceptual tension between history from below and the history of elites and states as its organizing principle, not as decorative framing. Specifically, the speech must do real structural work: it should establish precisely what can and cannot be said about “giving voice” to the dead when every trace of those lives survives only through the bureaucratic, coercive, and classificatory instruments of the very powers that silenced them.

To avoid well-worn formulations, please frame the ethical obligation involved through a specific but accessible conceptual device: the idea of an “epistemic debt” incurred when a historian translates traces of resistance—recorded in the master’s vocabulary, within files designed for control—into present-day narrative. This debt should function in the speech as a constraint rather than a slogan: it demands fidelity to the dead without licensing ventriloquism, and it should shape the conclusion that recovery is always mediated representation rather than transparent restitution. The remarks must navigate between naive celebratory recovery, which treats the archive as a window onto pure subaltern agency, and a paralyzing skepticism that would render all historical representation impossible. They should be rigorous enough that specialists recognize the stakes—particularly the problem of mediated recovery and the asymmetry between state-centered and bottom-up historiography—without alienating listeners who have never encountered microhistoriography.
