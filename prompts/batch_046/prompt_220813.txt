Consider a senior epistemologist—call her Professor V—who has published explicit verdicts on whether a belief counts as knowledge in four structurally different scenarios. Her track record is as follows.

First, in the classic Gettier case in which a subject validly infers from a false justified belief that either Jones owns a Ford or Brown is in Barcelona, and the conclusion is true only because Brown happens to be in Barcelona: Professor V rules that this is not knowledge, on the ground that the truth of the conclusion is not properly connected to the subject’s justification.

Second, in a scenario in which an agent reads a broken thermometer that happens to display the correct temperature because an unrelated environmental factor compensates for the malfunction, but the agent has performed all standard checks and has no evidence of malfunction: Professor V rules that this IS knowledge, on the ground that the agent exercised appropriate epistemic diligence and the instrument’s general reliability supports the belief, even though the specific causal path was aberrant.

Third, in the well-known fake-barn scenario, in which an agent correctly identifies a real barn in an environment filled with convincing facades: Professor V rules that this is not knowledge, because the environment renders the belief too easily false.

Fourth, in a case in which a researcher relies on a peer-reviewed study containing an undetected statistical error that does not affect the specific conclusion she draws, and her own reasoning is sound: Professor V rules that this IS knowledge, because the justification resides in the researcher’s reasoning and the study’s overall reliability, not in the hidden error.

Now consider a new case—the “calibrated oracle.” A scientist forms the belief that compound X is chemically stable. She bases this on predictions from a machine-learning model that has been extensively validated and has high overall accuracy. Unbeknownst to her, the model’s architecture contains two independent hidden biases: one that systematically overestimates stability for compound X’s class, and another that systematically underestimates it by exactly the same magnitude. The biases cancel, yielding the correct prediction. The scientist understands the model’s general methodology, has verified its statistical performance, and has no reason to suspect internal error. However, the specific reasoning path within the model for this compound is causally flawed.

What should Professor V rule if she wishes to remain consistent with her four earlier verdicts? Identify the closest analog among them, and state what principle she would be adopting with each possible choice. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, articulate the two or more candidate rules that jointly explain her earlier rulings, say what each predicts here, offer a best-guess recommendation with the reason for preferring it, and specify the further hypothetical case that would force a decision between the rules.
