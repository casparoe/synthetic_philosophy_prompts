I'm working through judgments about intentional action in cases that resemble the Knobe effect but add an explicit probabilistic framing. In the standard harm case, a CEO who launches a program knowing it will damage the environment is judged to have harmed it intentionally; in the standard help case, a CEO who launches a program knowing it will improve air quality is judged not to have improved it intentionally, only as a side effect. Now consider two variants. First, a CEO approves a project knowing there is a 30 percent chance of severe ecological damage and a 70 percent chance of no effect, and he proceeds solely for profit; my intuition is that he intentionally risked environmental harm. Second, a CEO approves a project knowing there is a 30 percent chance of ecological improvement and a 70 percent chance of no effect, and he proceeds solely for profit; here I do not say he intentionally sought improvement, but neither do I say he intentionally risked improvement—I treat him as merely reckless with probabilities. So the harm-versus-help asymmetry survives, yet the probabilistic framing seems to alter what gets attributed in a way the standard binary cases do not capture. Is there an established term for this specific interaction—the Knobe asymmetry operating under explicit probabilistic framing—or does it fall under existing discussions of risk attribution, moral luck, or epistemic conditions on intentionality? If it is unnamed, can you define the phenomenon precisely, distinguish it from the standard Knobe effect and from general judgments about risk-taking, and say what it implies for the usual explanations of the asymmetry?
