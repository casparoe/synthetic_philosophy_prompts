I'm preparing the final meeting of my graduate seminar on Kant's 1795 essay *Toward Perpetual Peace*, and I want to close with a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with two refrains that alternate and finally converge in the last two lines. The philosophical burden should fall on the tension that is too often glossed over: the distinction between the moral duty to establish republican constitutions, a federation of free states, and cosmopolitan right (the three definitive articles), and the "guarantee" that nature herself provides in the Second Supplement, where the mechanism of human self-interest, international commerce, and the spread of republican government mechanically produces peace without requiring moral improvement. I want one refrain to speak from the side of nature's mechanism—something about merchants, ledgers, or the hidden hand of egoism that writes treaties without virtue—while the other refrain should speak from the side of right—something about the abolition of standing armies, the prohibition on contracting national debt for war, or the categorical refusal of a world-state in favor of a voluntary federation. The imagery should weave in the preliminary articles (the refusal of standing armies, the prohibition on national debt for external affairs, the rejection of interference in other constitutions) without turning them into a list. The villanelle's compulsive repetition should do structural work: each return of the refrains should feel like Kant's two arguments—mechanical and moral—passing each other without resolution, until the final quatrain forces them into adjacency. The poem should be accurate enough that a specialist recognizes the 1795 text's architecture, but it should not be a paraphrase; someone who does not know Kant should still find it a coherent poem about the uneasy alliance between self-interest and justice.
