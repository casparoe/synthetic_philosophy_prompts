I'm delivering the opening remarks at a one-day interdisciplinary workshop called "Loops and Selves," hosted by a small philosophy-of-mind institute. The room is split: on one side are researchers in cognitive science and philosophy who are familiar with Douglas Hofstadter's account of the self as a strange loop—the symbol 'I' understood not as a label for a fixed soul but as a self-referential pattern in the brain's representational machinery that loops back to represent the very system producing it, and more specifically as a pattern that modifies itself each time it is activated, so that the 'I' referring to itself today is not quite the same loop as the one that referred yesterday; on the other side are graduate students in literature and family members of the institute's late founder, an engineer who insisted that consciousness had to be explainable without invoking non-physical substances, but who never read philosophy professionally. The founder's family has asked that the welcome not feel like an academic lecture, while the researchers expect the framing to be conceptually serious. Your remarks should be about six minutes, set the tone for discussions on artificial minds and personal identity, and make the strange-loop view do real structural work: specifically, you must show why this view makes it difficult to say that the self is an unchanging substance that survives intact, or that an artificial system could never possess an 'I' simply because it lacks biological substrate, yet you should not simply assert those prohibitions—you should derive them from the loop idea itself and acknowledge the discomfort that follows. Avoid any bibliography, reading list, or citation requests; do not quote any author verbatim (if you invoke a specific claim, paraphrase it without quotation marks); and do not rely on highly technical formal logic or obscure neuroscience that a non-specialist could not follow. The speech should feel like a genuine welcome, not a disguised essay, but the philosophical idea must shape what can and cannot be said.
