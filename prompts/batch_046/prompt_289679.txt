I'm taking a seminar on the metaphysics of action and we spent several sessions on the killing versus letting die distinction, but I felt the usual ventilator-and-trolley examples had become too schematic to reveal where the boundary really breaks. I would like you to write a dialogue between two philosophers—Dr. Elena Vasquez and Dr. Marcus Chen—who are equally sharp and genuinely engage with each other's arguments rather than talking past one another.

Set the scene concretely: they are observing a demonstration of an autonomous hospital logistics system that manages scarce life-support capacity. When demand exceeds supply, the system never physically disconnects anyone; instead, it recalculates priority scores and stops delivering replacement filter cartridges to certain patients, knowing that without these cartridges those patients will die within hours. Vasquez defends a structurally sensitive version of the doctrine of doing and allowing: she claims this is allowing to die, because the system merely withholds a sustaining intervention that was never guaranteed, and the deaths flow from the patients' underlying conditions meeting a resource boundary. Chen argues that the distinction collapses here—he says the system's algorithmic selection is causally equivalent to targeted withdrawal, and describing the stoppage as "not delivering" obscures the fact that the computation actively reroutes resources away from specific people.

Have them work through real back-and-forth. Vasquez should respond to Chen's causal-equivalence claim by distinguishing between interrupting an existing causal pathway (which she concedes is doing harm) and failing to extend an artificial life-support pathway that depends on continuous external input (which she takes as allowing). Chen should then press her on whether that distinction survives normalization: if patients have been on these filters for months, isn't the system's decision to stop replenishing them structurally identical to turning off a ventilator? Vasquez might reply that the moral category depends on what obligations the institution has normatively assumed, not merely on causal structure. Chen should reject that as circular—he thinks obligations are precisely what's at stake, not a prior constraint. The dialogue should conclude without either being convinced, but with both positions sharpened and the conceptual stakes—action versus omission, causal pathway versus institutional baseline—made explicit.
