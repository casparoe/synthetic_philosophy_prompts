Write a short story set in a near-future metropolis where a tightly regulated market allows citizens to purchase "oracle-certificates"—legally binding guarantees that a specific proposition is true, delivered without explanation, evidence, or conceptual framework. The protagonist is a senior advocate who specializes in defending clients who possess these bare true beliefs but find themselves unable to navigate legal, social, or practical challenges that require articulating reasons, integrating information, or withstanding adversarial scrutiny. Focus the narrative on a particular case: her client holds an oracle-certified true belief that she did not commit a serious crime, yet in court the client cannot explain her own whereabouts, cannot reconcile her belief with physical evidence, and cannot defend the belief when the prosecution constructs a coherent, justified narrative that accounts for all observed facts. As the trial unfolds, the protagonist should come to see that the client's belief, though genuinely true and sincerely held, lacks the structural properties—stability under counter-argument, compatibility with background knowledge, and action-guiding justification—that make knowledge functionally superior to mere true belief. You may invent institutional details, such as the rules governing whether oracle-certificates count as admissible testimony or the social category of "unjustified knowers," but keep the philosophical center on the Meno problem: why knowledge is better than true belief not merely as an epistemic trophy, but as a practical resource for reasoning and survival in a world that demands explanation.
