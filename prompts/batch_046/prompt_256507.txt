I'm working through a seminar on the politics of memory and have become convinced by what I will call the structural silence thesis: that in archives produced under steep power asymmetries—colonial administrative records, prison intake ledgers, psychiatric case files—the omissions are not accidental lacunae but are positively shaped by the same classificatory logic that generates the entries. On this view, a missing name is not a neutral gap but a negative inscription; the archive's silence about a particular group is produced by the very categories that render others legible. I have begun to apply this to a specific conceptual distinction I am experimenting with: between indexical silence, where we can point to a specific missing document whose absence is recoverable in principle, and constitutive silence, where the conditions of recordability themselves preclude certain kinds of subject from ever appearing as recordable subjects. The thesis I am defending is that in severely asymmetrical archives, constitutive silence dominates, and attempts to "fill the gaps" through counter-archival recovery risk replicating the original violence by forcing the unrecorded into the categories that originally excluded them.

But I want to stress-test this properly. What would have to be true for the structural silence thesis, especially in its strong form with the indexical/constitutive distinction, to be wrong? I am not asking for a literature review—rather, an honest map of its exposure. Which empirical findings about specific archives would undermine it: for instance, evidence that omissions are genuinely random with respect to power, or that counter-archival recovery can operate outside the original classificatory scheme? What conceptual arguments might show that the distinction between indexical and constitutive silence collapses, or that treating absence as inscription commits a category mistake? I also want to know whether defenders of this view have already qualified it into unfalsifiability—if every recovered document is read as proof that the silence was never truly constitutive, then is the thesis insulated in a way that should worry us? Finally, are there rival accounts—say, that archives are primarily technologies of preservation rather than exclusion, or that power operates through over-recording rather than omission—that explain the same phenomena without committing to negative inscription? Please distinguish what would be decisive defeat from what would merely be awkward, and say whether the position risks anything meaningful or has been rendered safe by its defenders' qualifications.
