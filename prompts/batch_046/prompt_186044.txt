Having spent my professional life in experimental physics and only recently begun to read moral philosophy with any rigour, I keep returning to Bernard Williams's integrity objection—particularly the idea that certain moral demands ask an agent to entertain one thought too many when weighing personal commitments against impartial calculation. In the literature the exchange seems to have reached an impasse: defenders of utilitarian and consequentialist frameworks tend to treat the objection as either a rational failure or a contingent psychological limitation, whilst Williams's sympathisers maintain that it reveals a normative boundary, namely that some commitments must be held non-instrumentally if they are to constitute a coherent practical identity. What strikes me, perhaps because of my scientific training, is that neither side appears to have moved, despite the availability of empirical work on moral motivation and decision-making under cognitive load. I therefore wish to ask whether this stalemate is genuinely substantive—about whether moral reasons can be agent-relative—or whether it is partly verbal, with the term integrity functioning for two distinct phenomena: diachronic coherence of character over time, and synchronic resistance to instrumental calculation in a particular choice. Might the disagreement also conceal a methodological divergence, with one camp treating empirical psychology as normatively constraining and the other treating it as merely descriptive? Given this diagnosis, what kind of development—whether a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding about representational limits in practical reasoning, or a reframing of the question—would actually count as progress, and ought we to expect any?
