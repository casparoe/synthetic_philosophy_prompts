Here is an argument my study partner offered for why Van Fraassen's Reflection principle eliminates first-person reasoning altogether. (1) Reflection requires that, for any proposition P, my present rational credence in P equals my expectation of my future rational credence in P. (2) Tomorrow I will examine a statistical study I regard as well-designed but have not yet read; I anticipate that, if I evaluate it rationally, I will assign credence 0.8 to P. (3) Therefore my present expectation of my future rational credence in P is 0.8, and Reflection demands that my present credence in P be 0.8. (4) Yet I now possess direct philosophical arguments that support not-P and that I do not expect the study to address, giving me present credence 0.2. (5) So Reflection commands me to override my present reasoning with an expectation of future testimony. (6) Generalized, this means whenever I anticipate authoritative future input, my current judgments are rationally required to disappear; first-person reasoning becomes impossible. (7) Since that result is absurd, Reflection must be rejected. Identify the weakest step. Is the error in (1)'s unrestricted scope, in (3)'s identification of anticipated study-output with my own future rational credence, or in (5)'s assumption that Reflection conflicts with present reasoning rather than integrating it? And what does your diagnosis cost—does Reflection survive only as a trivial consistency constraint, or can it be restricted in a principled way that still does philosophical work?
