I'm developing a conceptual research program that takes Grice's account of implicature and speaker meaning seriously but applies it to contexts of strategic ambiguity — situations where the Cooperative Principle is not violated outright but is selectively suspended, allowing a speaker to generate implicatures precisely by signaling that certain maxims are being flouted for effect. Within this framing, several unresolved issues stand out: whether speaker meaning is fixed prior to listener uptake or is partially constituted by successful recovery; whether particularized implicatures remain stable across audiences with divergent background beliefs; how to distinguish implicatures produced by maxim-flouting from those produced by maxim-optimization in adversarial settings; and whether the saying/implicating distinction collapses when a speaker's primary intention is to induce recognition of an intention to deceive. If you were directing about three years of conceptual work — not empirical linguistics, and without access to new archival sources — which one or two of these should be settled first? Defend the ordering: identify which questions are upstream of the others, which are tractable with purely conceptual tools, which carry the most significant implications for understanding speaker meaning outside cooperative contexts, and which should be deprioritized because resolving them would change little downstream. In particular, address whether the uptake-dependency question is a genuine crux for this program or a red herring, and explain what evidence or argument over the next few years would be most likely to revise your ranking.
