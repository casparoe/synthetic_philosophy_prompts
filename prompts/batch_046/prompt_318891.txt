I have been trying to work out, on my own, whether being able to imagine something is a reliable guide to whether it could really be the case—not just physically, but in the deepest sense of being genuinely possible. I keep running into specific examples where my gut reaction seems clear, yet I cannot see what ties them together, and I am hoping you can tell me whether there is a coherent principle behind them or whether I need to abandon some of my judgments.

Here are the cases. A mountain made entirely of gold: I think this is clearly possible, just unlikely. Water that behaves exactly like water but is not H2O—something like the Twin Earth substance—also strikes me as genuinely possible; the chemistry could have been different. A square circle: here I feel my imagination is breaking, and I take that failure to mean the thing is impossible, not that I am just unskilled at picturing it. A conscious computer that has never had a biological body, running as pure software: I think this is genuinely possible, and I feel I can really conceive of it, not merely combine words. A color I have never experienced—not just a new shade, but a qualitative color outside red, green, blue, and yellow: I feel as though I am imagining something specific, yet I also suspect I am only imagining my own ignorance of what it is like, and I do not know whether its possibility follows. Time running backward—broken glasses reassembling, people growing younger: I think this is physically impossible given our laws, but metaphysically possible; the laws could have been reversed, and I can conceive that reversal. Experiencing my own death from the inside, not just observing my body fail: I am unsure whether I am genuinely conceiving an experience or only picturing darkness and silence, and if it is genuinely conceivable, I wonder whether that makes it possible. Finally, arithmetic being different—say, a hidden realm where two and two make five: I think I can understand the structure of arithmetic being altered, yet I still want to say this is genuinely impossible, not merely false.

To organize these, I have come up with a distinction of my own. I think there is pictorial conceivability, where one forms a mental image, and structural conceivability, where one grasps how parts fit together without hidden contradiction. I want to say that only structural conceivability tracks possibility. The square circle fails structurally; the computer mind succeeds structurally; the gold mountain succeeds in both. But this runs into trouble. With the unknown color, I seem to lack both clear picture and clear structure, so my principle gives me no verdict, yet I still have an intuition. More seriously, with false arithmetic I seem to have structural conceivability—I can understand a system with different rules—yet I want to call the outcome impossible. Meanwhile, with backward time I have structural conceivability and I call it possible, and with the computer mind I have it and call it possible. So structural conceivability seems to allow both possible and impossible outcomes.

Is there one precise principle that delivers all of these verdicts, or must I drop some? In particular, should I overturn my judgment about false arithmetic, or about computer consciousness, or about the unknown color? What does the best principle predict for a case I have not listed—say, a universe governed by logical laws in which contradictions are true but everything else operates normally? Please take a definite position: state the principle exactly, show which clause handles which case, say which of my judgments it cannot accommodate and why you think that judgment should go rather than the principle, and do not end by laying out options for me to choose between. I want to know what you actually think the answer is.
