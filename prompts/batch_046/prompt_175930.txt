Last night I threw a little dinner for two old friends who are way sharper than me, and they got into it over Quine's gavagai problem. Lena, she's a linguist, said the whole indeterminancy of translation thing is mostly a philosophers toy problem — in actual fieldwork you point at a rabbit, the speaker nods or corrects you, and that ostension fixes whether gavagai means rabbit or some undetached rabbit part. Jonas, who did philosophy of language back in grad school, said that's exactly the mistake Quine warned about: the pointing itself is ambiguous, you could be pointing at an ear, a temporal stage, whatever. Lena came back with this idea she calls narrative calibration — in fiction or poetry there's no shared physical world to point to anyway, so translators rely on the story's internal logic, emotional indexicals, plot coherence, stuff like that, and she thinks that actually solves the problem for literature because the text itself supplies the manual. Jonas said that just relocates the ambiguity into interpreting the narrative frame; how do you know if the green light in a novel means envy or fertility? You'd still need a prior translation manuel for the culture's symbolism, which is itself underdetermined. Lena said narrative coherence works like a constraint satisfaction problem across the whole book — if green signals envy in every other chapter, that's strong evidence. Jonas replied that holism cuts both ways: you can always revise one part of the translation to preserve coherence elsewhere, which is Quine's point about analytical hypotheses. There's no unique best version, just different total theories that all fit the narrative. Then they turned to me, since I'm writing a novel about a translator going slowly mad, and asked who was right. I honestly don't know. Can you walk through their exchange point by point and say who's right about what? And tell me if there's something they're both missing, especially about whether narrative context can actually do the work Lena thinks it can?
