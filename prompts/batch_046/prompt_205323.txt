Design a 90-minute classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy seminar of about twelve students that makes the tension in Hume’s standard of taste visceral rather than textual, with one injected twist: the adjudication must occur across a sensory divide. One subgroup experiences artworks as visual-spatial objects; the other experiences structurally equivalent representations translated into auditory or tactile analogues. Their collective task is to decide—under time pressure and with small stakes (allocation of a shared “exhibition fund” of points)—whether a single ranking of beauty can be enforced, or whether each modality generates its own legitimate standard. Specify concrete roles: arbiters who must vote without experiencing both modalities, artists presenting in one modality only, and “translators” attempting cross-modal mapping but forbidden to invoke an “ideal critic” as a shortcut. Provide a timeline: 15 minutes for role assignment and private sensory experience of the works; 30 minutes for structured deliberation with rules that penalize appeals to “what everyone really means”; 20 minutes for a forced vote in which hidden preference cards are revealed; and 25 minutes for debrief. In the debrief, map moments from the deliberation onto Hume’s criteria—delicacy of perception, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice—and press the group on whether they invented an implicit “translation protocol” between modalities, asking whether that protocol belongs to the objective standard or is a concession to relativism. Be frank about which feature of Hume’s view—the eventual convergence of refined sentiments—this simulation can only assert rather than demonstrate, and warn what will predictably go wrong on the first run (translators dominating, arbiters retreating to modality-specific voting blocs). End with a single sentence summarizing the core philosophical tension the activity is designed to surface.
