Please compare the cognitive-byproduct account of religion—according to which religious representations persist because they violate intuitive ontological expectations in minimal ways that render them especially memorable and culturally transmissible—with the costly-signaling account—according to which religious practices persist primarily because they solve collective-action problems by enabling individuals to make credible, hard-to-fake commitments to a cooperative group. Rather than treating these as straightforward rivals over a single explanatory target, examine whether they are actually addressing different phenomena—namely, the persistence of specific representational content versus the stability of institutional practices—and whether this division of labor undermines the standard framing that one must be the correct naturalistic explanation of religion. Specifically, consider whether the byproduct framework can accommodate the existence of demanding ritual behaviors without treating them as merely epiphenomenal, and whether the signaling framework can account for the distinctive cognitive profile of religious agents—such as minimally counterintuitive spirits or ancestors—without reducing those representations to instrumental devices for broadcasting loyalty. Then evaluate whether recognizing this potential compatibility resolves the theoretical tension or merely displaces it onto a further question: if beliefs arise as cognitive byproducts and practices emerge as strategic signals, why do they co-occur with such regularity across cultures, and does explaining that coupling require an additional mechanism—perhaps cultural coevolution, or a feedback loop between representational salience and social enforcement—that neither framework supplies on its own?
