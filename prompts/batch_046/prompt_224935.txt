I'm serving on the advisory board of a small cooperative housing association, and we've been asked to evaluate whether a longtime resident and close friend, Elena, should retain her seat after a serious administrative failure. Last quarter, Elena was responsible for distributing emergency repair funds, but she misallocated a substantial portion to a contractor who was also her cousin—someone she had previously insisted was the only available option during a crisis. The error was not technically fraud; she genuinely believed she was acting in the community's interest, and she had been working without compensation while caring for her mother, who has advanced dementia. Several residents suffered concrete financial setbacks. When I discuss the matter with her, I find myself experiencing an almost involuntary convergence of feeling: I can vividly imagine what it is like to be overwhelmed by filial duty and to make a desperate judgment call, and this shared emotional perspective makes her actions seem understandable in a way that softens my disapproval.

Yet when I try to advise the board impartially, I feel compelled to adopt a different standpoint—one that considers not whether I can share her feelings, but whether a well-informed, disinterested observer, knowing all the relevant circumstances but lacking any personal connection to Elena, would regard her decision as appropriate and her retention as consistent with the norms we want our association to embody. Some members argue that because our emotional identification reveals her good intentions, the failure should be treated as excusable; others insist that fairness demands we suppress such identification entirely and judge only the objective harm caused. I am struggling to see whether these two responses—feeling with her and judging her—are genuinely in conflict, or whether they can be integrated into a single coherent assessment. How should I navigate the tension between understanding her situation from within and evaluating it from a position of detached impartiality? Is there a principled way to let both perspectives inform my recommendation without allowing either to dominate?
