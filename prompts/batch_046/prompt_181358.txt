I am serving as an exam grader for an advanced course on historical moral judgment. The essay prompt asked students to evaluate whether an institutional-epistemic-closure framing undermines the traditional "man of his time" defence, comparing Thomas Jefferson's slaveholding with Winston Churchill's conduct during the 1943 Bengal famine. One philosophical proposal suggests that the relevant difference lies not in widely shared social norms but in whether an agent's institutional position systematically filtered out countervailing moral evidence.

Below is one anonymous student answer. Please assign a specific letter grade and provide point-by-point comments that justify it, treating this as an external grading task without signaling your own view of the position taken.

Student answer: The traditional "man of his time" defence excuses agents for failing to hold moral beliefs that were not dominant in their era, provided they genuinely lacked access to countervailing arguments. Bernard Williams's account of moral luck concerns only outcomes that lie beyond an agent's control, not institutional roles, so it is irrelevant to this comparison. Jefferson lived near Quaker communities that actively condemned slavery, which means abolitionist arguments were openly available to him; he therefore faced no epistemic closure and is rightly blamed for willful ignorance. Churchill, however, was embedded in the British Raj's administrative hierarchy, which actively suppressed reports about the severity of the Bengal famine; this institutional epistemic closure made the relevant moral facts genuinely inaccessible to him. Because Jefferson had open access while Churchill faced structural blindness, our asymmetrical judgments are justified. Thus the institutional-epistemic framing does not undermine the defence but refines it, showing that the defence applies exactly when such closure is present.
