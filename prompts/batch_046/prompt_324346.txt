I'm trying to fix my own view on whether "Western values" is a coherent normative category and want a consistency check before I present it. Here is my current package: (1) The phrase bundles together historically antagonistic commitments—Enlightenment universalism, Romantic particularism, liberal individualism, and democratic majoritarianism—so that no single philosophical framework can unify them; its coherence is purely retrospective and rhetorical. (2) Despite this, I invoke the phrase in argument as naming a legitimate family of institutional practices—constitutional government, rule of law, secular public reason—and I think this practical usage is defensible without essentialist definition. (3) I hold that if a normative concept is genuinely incoherent at the theoretical level, then appealing to it in moral argument is masked arbitrariness or question-begging. (4) At the same time, I accept that in pluralist societies, normative authority can rest on overlapping practical agreement about institutions rather than on shared philosophical foundations, so theoretical contradiction need not invalidate practical legitimacy. (5) I also think that pluralism and fallibilism—often cited as distinctive "Western" values—are self-subverting when applied to the category itself: if no single framework should dominate, then fixing the content of "Western values" is itself illegitimate.

Do these hang together? In particular, does (3) defeat (2) and (4), or can (4) absorb the incoherence noted in (1)? Is (5) a fatal reflexive problem or just an instance of the tension in (1)-(3)? Rank the tensions by severity, say which commitment is least costly to revise, and specify what each revision would cost—especially whether I must abandon the incoherence claim, the practical-legitimacy claim, or whether a middle position is available.
