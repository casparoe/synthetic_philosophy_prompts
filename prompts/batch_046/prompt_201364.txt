As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science, I have noticed an institutional practice that now appears in several laboratories with which I maintain contact: research teams routinely employ large language models to synthesize theoretical interpretations across subfields that no individual member has personally mastered, embed these syntheses in grant proposals and manuscript introductions, and then have the principal investigator sign a formal attestation of complete intellectual ownership and comprehension, despite neither the model nor the human authors being able to trace every integrated claim back to its original empirical context. I would like to understand what philosophical commitments about epistemic dependence and the nature of scientific understanding this practice presupposes. In particular, does it implicitly treat explanatory understanding as a form of institutional accountability rather than as a first-person cognitive grasp of why a mechanism operates? Does it presuppose that dependence on an opaque synthetic system differs only in degree, not in kind, from dependence on a human expert who can be interrogated, or does it quietly abandon the distinction altogether? Is the practice consistent with the simultaneous requirement that investigators preserve raw data and defend proposals orally, practices that seem to presuppose direct cognitive access? And if one takes seriously the view that such machine-mediated synthesis genuinely erodes the kind of understanding that distinguishes scientific knowledge from mere predictive success, what structural changes to authorship norms and peer-review criteria would be required?
