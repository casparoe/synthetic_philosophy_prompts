I just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend about whether biography can still function as moral education, or whether it inevitably collapses into hagiography the moment you select lives for pedagogical purposes. My friend insisted that Plutarch’s comparative method—pairing lives so that virtue and vice appear in tension rather than isolation—only works if the reader already possesses the full, unvarnished record, and that any classroom translation of this method is therefore doomed to sanitize its subjects. I want to show that there is a reusable procedure allowing a practitioner to use biographical lives for genuine moral calibration—what I will call asymmetric comparative judgment—without falling into hero-worship, but I need the principle made explicit, ordered, and operational rather than merely asserted.

You are an expert in classical moral philosophy, the reception history of Plutarch’s Parallel Lives, and curriculum design for secondary ethics education. Before you construct anything, please think carefully about the precise difference between exemplarity and hagiography, and briefly brainstorm three distinct ways Plutarch’s comparative practice could be distorted when compressed into a selection protocol for non-specialists. Then proceed.

The practitioner is a senior curriculum officer at an independent secondary school who selects biographical texts for a required twelve-week ethics module. The officer reviews two to three candidate texts each semester, has forty-five minutes per review, must defend selections to parents and administrators who often prefer inspirational narratives, and teaches students aged sixteen to eighteen with no prior training in historiography or source criticism.

Turn the underlying principle—derived from Plutarch’s comparative method but deliberately modified to resist hagiography—into a step-by-step decision procedure the officer can run repeatedly. The principle holds that moral learning from lives requires three conditions: first, that moral failures are presented with narrative weight equal to successes; second, that the text either provides or can be paired with a comparative life that forces evaluation of reasoning rather than admiration of outcomes; and third, that the narrative structure separates the subject’s actions from the student’s judgment, preventing uncritical identification.

Your deliverable must include:

1. The procedure itself, in ordered steps. For each step, state the exact question to ask of the text and its available sources; what counts as a sufficient answer; where the procedure stops with a definitive rejection; and where it must hand the decision to human judgment because the issue is one of values rather than facts—specifically, whether a particular vice is too corrosive to present at this age, whether cultural or institutional significance overrides pedagogical purity, or whether parental objection should be accommodated.

2. Built-in hard cases: address what the officer should do when the available biography is sanitized, when no comparative pair exists in accessible literature, and when administrators object that the selected life is “not inspiring enough.”

3. Three worked applications, run in full:
   a. A widely circulated biography of a civil-rights leader that documents strategic brilliance but omits documented personal cruelty and marital betrayal.
   b. A rigorously documented pairing of two scientists who faced near-identical research-integrity crises and made opposing choices, with both texts historically sound.
   c. One case where the procedure produces a recommendation you—the asker—will genuinely dislike: a beloved national figure whose sanitized biography fails the procedure’s first two steps, and whose exclusion will feel like an act of disrespect to the school community, yet the protocol outputs “exclude unless supplemented by primary-source material on the failures,” despite emotional and cultural pressure to include.

4. An honest account of what the procedure cannot do. Specifically, identify which features of Plutarch’s original comparative practice are necessarily lost when the method is compressed into a forty-five-minute selection protocol, and flag the points where moral judgment remains irreducible regardless of procedural rigor.

Keep the language precise and formal. Do not provide a bibliography, a reading list, or requests for citations. Do not depend on specific editions, page numbers, or obscure attributions that a model without web access could not reasonably verify; paraphrase any ideas drawn from real texts rather than placing them in quotation marks. Where you invoke titles, use only widely recognized ones.
