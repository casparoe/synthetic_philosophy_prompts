As a novelist constructing long-form narratives in which characters undergo moral transformation across non-linear timelines, I approach the metaphysics of time not as an abstract puzzle but as a structural problem for fiction. The presentism versus eternalism debate is familiar, yet I am less interested in rehearsing standard positions than in knowing which of its genuinely open lines of work carry the greatest weight for someone who must decide whether a character's past is ontologically fixed, merely remembered, or still in some sense present—because that decision shapes how guilt, forgiveness, and anticipation can be dramatized without collapsing into mere psychology.

Specifically, I seek a defended prioritization of the following unresolved questions and their downstream implications, ordered by how much resolving each one would alter the conceptual architecture available to narrative art and to ethical reflection on lives lived in time.

First, the truthmaker problem for past-tensed statements under presentism. If only present objects exist, what makes it true that a protagonist betrayed a friend ten years ago? Is this tractable without invoking presently existing traces, and does its resolution determine whether narrative memory can be more than a present mental state?

Second, the compatibility of presentism with relativistic physics, particularly whether a privileged present can be defined across reference frames. If this proves insoluble, does presentism collapse into a view too local to support any coherent narrative spanning multiple perspectives?

Third, the phenomenological argument for temporal flow—whether the subjective experience of a moving present is an illusion that ontology should ignore or a datum that must be accommodated. If the flow is real, does that privilege presentist narratives of becoming over eternalist narratives of static being?

Fourth, the implications for personal identity and moral responsibility. If eternalism holds and all temporal stages of a person exist equally, in what sense can a later stage be held responsible for an earlier stage's actions? Conversely, under presentism, does the past self's non-existence undermine the continuity required for blame?

But I am not asking for a survey. I want these questions ranked—along with any others you judge more upstream—according to three criteria: how many other questions in the area depend on this one being settled; whether it is tractable with current conceptual tools rather than requiring empirical breakthroughs; and whether its resolution would genuinely change how we think about narrative time, moral persistence, or the ontology of fiction itself.

Here I want to inject a specific conceptual tension: what I call the problem of the unwritten future. In a novel, events that have not yet been narrated exist in the author's design but not in the story's present moment. Does this literary fact mirror eternalism's claim that future events are already real, or does it reveal a category mistake between authorial knowledge and ontological existence? Which of the open questions above must be answered first before this problem can even be framed clearly?

Deliver a defended agenda: which two or three lines of work deserve the most philosophical effort now, which should be deprioritized because they are either intractable or downstream of other results, and what specific conceptual outcome over the next few years—rather than an empirical finding—would cause you to revise the ranking.
