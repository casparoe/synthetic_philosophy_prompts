Please argue that the conventional demarcation between legitimate historiography and pseudohistory—whether applied to Fomenko's New Chronology, narratives of Atlantis, or ancient-astronaut theories—fails because its standard criteria (falsifiability, peer consensus, methodological naturalism) function not as neutral epistemic filters but as institutional mechanisms enforcing what I will call chronological closure. Specifically, maintain that both mainstream ancient history and these pseudohistorical alternatives operate within an identical hermeneutic structure: each imposes narrative coherence upon discontinuous material traces, and each relies on unexamined conventions regarding temporal continuity and the authority of sources. Further, argue that the ancient-aliens hypothesis should be understood not as an empirical mistake but as a structural inversion of this closure mechanism—substituting an external causal agent for human institutional authority—while remaining fully within the same narrative logic. Conclude that demarcation is therefore a sociological boundary that marks who possesses the power to close a historical narrative, rather than an epistemological distinction between sound and unsound inference. Construct this argument through conceptual analysis alone, without bibliographic references, named scholarly citations, or appeals to specific archival findings.
