You are a philosophy educator with expertise in social choice theory and pedagogical design. Before proposing anything, briefly brainstorm: (1) what tangible mechanism—cards, tokens, or a physical board—can make a “liberal right” feel concrete rather than abstract, and (2) which formal feature of Sen’s liberal paradox is hardest to reproduce in a room of twelve people without letting the facilitator simply declare the contradiction.

Design a single 75-minute session for a graduate seminar of 12 students. The goal is for participants to feel, through structured play, the tension in Sen’s liberal paradox—specifically that minimal liberalism (each person has decisive authority over at least one pairwise comparison) conflicts with the Pareto principle and unrestricted domain—before the facilitator names the result.

Use a nested-jurisdiction variant rather than the classic two-reader book example. Create a three-option, three-dimension social-choice space (e.g., norms governing privacy, resource allocation, and expressive conduct). Assign each student one “jurisdiction token” that grants them binding authority over exactly one pairwise comparison within that space. The group must collectively produce a social ranking that respects every token-holder’s choice in at least one comparison, while also obeying an explicit “no one is made worse off without unanimous consent” Pareto rule. Provide concrete rules, role assignments, a non-monetary payoff scheme (e.g., small course-credit points tied to whether one’s jurisdiction survives intact), and a clear timeline: setup (10 min), first deliberation (15 min), crisis insertion (10 min), resolution attempt (20 min), and debrief (20 min).

In the debrief, map precise moments of the activity onto the formal pieces: the minimal liberal condition (L*), the Pareto principle (P), the unrestricted-domain assumption (U), and the cyclical preference profile that forces the contradiction. Predict exactly what will go wrong the first time you run this—likely that students will conflate strategic bargaining with a rights-violation, or try to “trade” jurisdiction tokens in a way that obscures the logical impossibility—and say how to correct it in the moment without giving away the paradox. Be explicit about which feature the activity cannot genuinely reproduce (the full unrestricted-domain condition across all logically possible preference orderings) and state precisely how you, as facilitator, must supply that gap—whether through a hidden preference card revealed only at the crisis point or through a direct verbal framing.

Keep the design self-contained: do not ask for a bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not depend on obscure technical literature beyond the standard formulation of the paradox. Write formally, with clear headings for Rules, Materials, Timeline, Debrief, Predictable Failures, and Unreproducible Feature.
