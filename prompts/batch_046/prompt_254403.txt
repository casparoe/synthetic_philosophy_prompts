I'm preparing a single fifty-minute seminar for first-year philosophy undergraduates who have never taken a law course and who tend to arrive assuming that "if a rule is deeply unjust, it isn't really law." I want to teach the disagreement between H.L.A. Hart and Lon Fuller not as a generic "positivism versus natural law" slugfest, but specifically around whether Fuller's eight procedural principles constitute an "inner morality of law" that is part of law's identity conditions, or whether they are merely efficacy conditions that leave the separation thesis untouched. To keep the discussion from collapsing into Austin's command theory or into the familiar Nazi grudge-informer example, I plan to build the session around a carrying case that is not in the standard textbooks: a hypothetical jurisdiction governed entirely by an opaque algorithm that issues rules that are general, consistent, non-retroactive, and applied with perfect equality, but that are never published—so citizens discover the rules only after being penalized for violating them. I need a teaching plan rather than the lecture itself. What idea has to be firmly in place before Fuller's eight principles are even introduced—should the separation thesis come first, or should I begin with an intuition that some procedural failures destroy legal status? Which single feature of the algorithmic example should carry the distinction between "this is law but morally monstrous" and "this fails to be law at all," and which features should I deliberately suppress so they don't distract? Map the misconceptions in the order the students will form them: likely that Fuller's principles are substantive moral commands like "be just," that Hart concedes law and morality are always intertwined, that retroactivity is the only relevant failure, and that legal positivism means "whatever the sovereign commands is law." For each, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off. Then propose one check-for-understanding question at the end that would reveal whether they have grasped the procedural-versus-moral distinction rather than just memorizing "Fuller says it isn't law, Hart says it's bad law." Finally, specify which parts of the debate—perhaps Hart's rule of recognition, Fuller's second-edition reply, or the modern inclusive-exclusivist divide—I should explicitly say we are leaving out and why, so the session doesn't sprawl beyond its purpose.
