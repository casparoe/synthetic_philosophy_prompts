I have been studying Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility and find the subsequent literature curiously static. The argument, as I understand it, contends that true moral responsibility requires being truly responsible for the way one is, which in turn requires being responsible for prior conditions, generating either an infinite regress or an arbitrary stopping point at which one is not responsible; hence, true moral responsibility is impossible. In discussion groups, I have noticed that compatibilists typically respond with refined accounts of reasons-responsiveness, mesh between hierarchical desires, or historical conditions on identification, while Strawson-style incompatibilists maintain that none of these addresses the core demand: one must be the ultimate source of one's psychological constitution, not merely coherent with it. The debate then cycles through finer distinctions without either side conceding ground.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? I suspect it may not be a straightforward empirical dispute about the mechanisms of agency, since both camps largely accept the same psychological and neuroscientific findings. Instead, it might be a clash over the criteria for explanatory closure — specifically, whether a regress is vicious only when it undermines present coherence, or whether any dependence on factors outside one's present control undermines authorship regardless of internal transparency. But I also wonder if a hidden normative divergence is doing the work: perhaps compatibilists treat moral responsibility as a practical stance adopted within social practices, whereas incompatibilists treat it as a metaphysical property requiring a kind of self-creation that is conceptually impossible. Given your diagnosis — or an alternative you find more persuasive — is there anything that could genuinely move this debate? Might a formal distinction between 'structural' conditions (the coherence of present psychological architecture) and 'historical' conditions (the causal origin of that architecture) reveal that the two sides are answering different questions under the same term? Or should we conclude that the stall is itself evidence that the Basic Argument succeeds only relative to a particular, and perhaps indefensible, conception of what it means to be the source of one's behaviour?
