I just finished a long, frustrating argument with a friend about whether individual choices—skipping meat, reducing flights—matter morally when collective harm is already massively overdetermined. I ended up defending a view I want stress-tested: the standard inefficacy objection misses the point not because marginal emissions are literally zero, but because it presupposes an aggregative, linear model of harm. On my framing, what makes participation wrong is being part of the sustaining structure of the damaging system itself, a constitutive rather than causal relation, regardless of whether one’s withdrawal would change the aggregate outcome. So what would genuinely count against this structural-complicity view? I want defeaters that go beyond the usual causal rebuttals—specifically, which conceptual distinctions in collective-action theory (say, between overdetermination and collective intentionality), which empirical patterns in large-scale cooperation, and which normative frameworks about individual versus structural responsibility would actually threaten it. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward. And be straight about whether defenders can absorb every counterexample by qualification without rendering the position unfalsifiable, and whether that kind of insulation is a virtue or a vice for a moral claim of this sort.
