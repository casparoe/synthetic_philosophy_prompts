i run a philosophy society at a uni, about 12 people, 75 minutes. we're looking at the idea of the self as a centre of narrative gravity — not a hidden core but a focal point that holds a life-story together. i want an activty that makes people feel the pull of that idea, but with a twist: i want to inject the notion that this gravity can be distributed or shared between people, like when a biography is co-authored and the centre shifts between narrators. this isn't the standard version, so they can't just recite what they've read.

design a structured game. split the group into two sets of six: 'inner' fragments (feelings, intentions, memories) and 'outer' fragments (actions, public records, other people's descriptions). give each person one card. there are 12 cards total, but two cards — one in each set — actually belong to a different fictional person inserted secretly. the group must build one coherent biography and place a 'gravity marker' (a card or small object) on the table showing where they think the self is centred. they can move the marker as they talk.

round one: 25 mins, build the story and fix the marker. round two: 15 mins, you secretly remove the fragment that the group decided was the 'centre', and they must continue the biography without it — they can relocate the marker or split it. round three: 10 mins, reveal there were two foreign cards; they must decide whether those cards belong inside the gravity well or outside it, and whether the self can absorb them.

materials: cards, a token for the marker, a timer. debrief: map round one onto the idea of a narrative centre, round two onto the idea that the centre is functional not substantial, round three onto distributed/shared gravity. be honest about what the exercise can't reproduce — probably the long temporal stability of a real life, and the fact that in reality we don't have an external facilitator removing pieces. also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (people will try to find a 'real' hidden person behind the cards, or argue about factual truth rather than narrative coherence) and how to handle it.

please write in british english, and don't worry if there's a typo or casual phrasing — that's fine. also, don't ask for citations or reading lists, since i want this to be runnable without anyone searching.
