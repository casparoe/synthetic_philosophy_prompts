I'm drafting a short essay for a philosophy-of-science seminar on whether a long-run predictive theory can be "right but early" without being rescued in an ad hoc manner. My central claim is that modern agricultural productivity and fertility decline function less like a falsification of Malthusian dynamics and more like a pressure-relief valve in a sealed vessel: they change boundary conditions without demonstrating that geometric population pressure cannot eventually outstrip arithmetic resource growth if the suppressor is removed or overwhelmed. To sharpen this, I draw an analogy to engineered redundancy systems, in which a backup circuit suppresses a primary failure mode; the failure mode is not falsified, it is simply not triggered under present operating conditions. I am concerned, however, that this framing may illegitimately separate "internal" population dynamics from "exogenous" technological or social change—after all, innovation is part of human history, not an external meteor strike. I also worry that the argument could collapse into an unfalsifiable immunization strategy if every postponement of crisis is interpreted as further evidence of suppression rather than as genuine disconfirmation.
