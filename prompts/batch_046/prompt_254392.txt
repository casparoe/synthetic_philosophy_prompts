Dr. Lena Vasquez has published a sequence of rulings on whether an entity warrants direct moral consideration on grounds of sentience. Her judgments are as follows: an adult human, yes; an adult cow, yes; an adult octopus, yes; an individual lobster, yes; an individual honeybee, no; a functional beehive of roughly forty thousand bees operating as an integrated problem-solving unit, yes, but only as a single distributed entity rather than as forty thousand separately considerable beings; a Portuguese man o’ war, despite being a colony of hundreds of specialized zooids, no, on the grounds that the zooids are functionally analogous to organs and the colony lacks emergent integrative cognition; a synthetic in-vitro network of ten thousand engineered neuronal clusters modeled on bee mushroom bodies and linked via artificial synapses, which collectively perform navigation tasks, no; a large-scale artificial language model with seventy billion parameters displaying coherent reasoning, no; and a single earthworm, no.

A new proposal seeks her ethical assessment. It involves an industrial farm maintaining three million crickets, each possessing a decentralized nervous system of roughly one million neurons, exhibiting individual nociceptive reflexes and simple avoidance learning, but lacking any centralized brain. Theoretical estimates assign each cricket a two percent probability of phenomenal consciousness, yielding an aggregate expected value of sixty thousand sentient individuals. The proposal requests clearance for harvest without individual stunning, arguing that either no crickets are sentient or, if they are, the scale renders individual-level intervention impracticable.

Predict Vasquez’s verdict—approval, rejection, or conditional approval—and defend it as the judgment most consistent with her prior track record. Then identify the implicit rule or framework she appears to be running, distinguishing between unit-level neural integration, emergent collective cognition, evolutionary substrate continuity, and scale-weighted expectation. Next, articulate at least one alternative rule that accommodates all nine prior verdicts equally well, and state precisely what that alternative predicts for the cricket farm. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case—specifying the entities and conditions involved—that would best discriminate between your primary interpretation and the alternative, explaining which verdict on that case each rule requires.
