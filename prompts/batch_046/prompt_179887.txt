The exam question read as follows: “Consider the classic Frankfurt-style case: an agent, Jones, decides on his own to vote for Candidate A, but an unseen neuroscientist, Black, monitors Jones’s neural activity and stands ready to intervene causally to ensure Jones votes for A should Jones show any sign of choosing B. In fact, Jones never wavers, so Black does not act. Is Jones morally responsible for his vote? Explain whether this scenario successfully refutes the Principle of Alternative Possibilities (PAP), and discuss whether the presence of an unexercised but causally sufficient backup mechanism eliminates the kind of alternative possibility that moral responsibility requires. My classmate answered: “Frankfurt’s case demonstrates that Jones is fully morally responsible for voting for A, even though he could not have done otherwise, because Black was prepared to force the same outcome. This directly refutes PAP, which claims that moral responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise. To clarify why, we should distinguish causal openness—the physical possibility that a different outcome could have occurred had the external mechanism activated—from deliberative openness, which is the agent’s actual access to alternative considerations within his own reasoning. Jones clearly lacks causal openness, since Black’s presence makes voting for B physically impossible, but he retains deliberative openness because he genuinely considered the options and arrived at A through his own reasoning. Frankfurt’s argument proves that only deliberative openness matters for responsibility, not causal openness. Consequently, determinism is compatible with moral responsibility: a deterministic universe can still preserve deliberative openness as long as the agent’s internal reasoning proceeds without disruption. Indeed, any hidden but unactivated influence—whether a neural monitor or an environmental constraint—is compatible with full moral responsibility provided the agent does not phenomenologically register its presence.”
