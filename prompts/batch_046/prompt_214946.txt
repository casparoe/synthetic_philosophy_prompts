I'm delivering the opening remarks at a neighborhood workshop on AI assistants, held at the public library. The audience is split: about half are engineers who work on language models, and the other half are neighbors who use chatbots daily but have no technical training. I'm a retired high school teacher with no formal background in philosophy or computer science, but I've been working through an idea on my own. I think attributing beliefs to AI is neither a sloppy metaphor nor proof of a hidden mind. Instead, I've come to see it as a practical posture—let's call it a predictive stance—where we treat a system's responses as if they come from something with opinions, not because we've solved the consciousness question, but because that's the only reliable way to navigate interaction with something that adapts to us in real time. At the same time, this stance is fragile: we can switch the system off, yet we also reshape our own behavior around what it "says." I want a ten-minute speech that makes this framework do real structural work. It should make it impossible for me to comfortably claim that AI literally knows nothing, and equally impossible to claim that it literally knows something, without breaking the posture I've described. The speech needs to speak to both halves of the room: warn the engineers that "it's just math" dismisses the genuine practical commitments users make when they adopt this stance, and warn the everyday users not to mistake predictive posture for understanding or moral presence. Don't decorate the idea; let it determine exactly what I can and can't say. Keep the language accessible but precise, and avoid references that only specialists would catch.
