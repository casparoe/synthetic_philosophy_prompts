At a workshop on historical methodology, two researchers disagreed about a municipal archive from a 1920s industrial district. The archive holds thousands of ledgers meticulously documenting wage disputes and strikes channeled through formal unions, but contains no records of the informal childcare cooperatives that local oral histories consistently describe. (1) One researcher argued that this absence is best explained by suppression: the cooperatives were led primarily by women, operated outside recognized labor structures, and were either deliberately ignored or actively excluded by an archival system controlled by industrial and state interests. (2) The other replied that this misdiagnoses the problem: the archive was never designed to be a comprehensive social record, but a contractual ledger tracking formal employment relations. The cooperatives were not "silenced" but categorically outside the system's ontology, like asking a meteorological station to record color. (3) The first countered that this lets the archive off the hook—if the taxonomy itself was built by the same interests that devalued women's labor, then "outside the ontology" is just structural erasure by another name, and the silence remains politically produced. (4) The second responded that conflating these two failures—intentional suppression versus ontological mismatch—destroys our ability to repair the record correctly: suppression requires excavation and recovery within the system, while ontological mismatch demands entirely different methods, such as oral history and ethnography, rather than deeper digging in the same ledgers. (5) The first said this distinction is academic hair-splitting, since in either case we must build counter-archives to represent what the official record omitted. (6) The second insisted it is not hair-splitting: if we treat every absence as suppression, we misdirect resources and miss that some phenomena never generated documents in the first place, which is a different kind of epistemic loss with different remedies.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Say which researcher is right about the specific distinction they draw, what, if anything, both are missing—particularly regarding whether the two types of absence can be cleanly separated in practice, and whether building counter-archives genuinely addresses both cases equally. Do not simply declare an overall winner; explain who is right about what, and identify any point on which both parties share an unstated assumption that deserves scrutiny.
