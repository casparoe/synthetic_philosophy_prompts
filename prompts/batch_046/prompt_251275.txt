The case: a third-year student failed their core analysis exam because they answered the convergence proof using Bishop-style constructive analysis — they avoided LEM and double-negation elimination entirely, and explicitly refused to invoke Markov's principle for the existence claim the question seemed to ask for. The marker (a classical analyst) gave them near-zero, saying the proof was incomplete because it never established the limit existed classically, only constructively. The student appealed, arguing the syllabus lists 'constructive and non-constructive methods' as equal strands but never says proofs must be classical, and that marking down for avoiding LEM is like marking a physics student for using Lagrangian mechanics instead of Newtonian — which would definately be wrong.

We now have to decide both the individual appeal and whether our rubrics need an explicit logic policy. Some board members want to desk-reject any intuitionistic proof in a standard exam; others think we're being dogmatic about classical logic when the philosophy side of the programme explicitly teaches intuitionistic semantics and sheaf models, and they say the student should recieve at least a partial re-mark.

Please draft the memo I'd circulate. It should have a clear bottom line up front: recommend either upholding the fail with conditions, allowing a re-mark with an intuitionist examiner, or something in between, plus a concrete policy change (or refusal to change) with a deadline for implementation. Don't just restate legal definitions — engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side, especially the worry that allowing this opens marking chaos and disadvantages students in classical grad-school exams. Keep it in the weeds a bit — I'm happy with references to Bishop's continuity principle, the difference between Russian constructivism and Bishop's school, or why intuitionistic continuity is actually stronger in some directions — but don't ask me for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and don't invent verbatim quotes.

Structure it exactly like this:

- Bottom line (around 100 words): recommendation and immediate action with a date.
- The case for the student (around 200 words): syllabus ambiguity, philosophical consistency, and why Bishop-style constructivism isn't just lazy classical.
- The case against (around 200 words): marking chaos, hidden rubric assumptions, grad-school disadvantage, and the risk of students gaming the system by rejecting LEM to avoid hard existence proofs.
- Policy recommendation (around 150 words): exactly what the rubric should say about logical frameworks, with a sample clause for exam papers.
- Honest weak spot (around 100 words): the weakest part of the recommendation, where judgement can't be eliminated.

Write casually — I'm a longtime rationalist-forum reader — in British English, and you can drop a typo or two if it sounds natural (e.g. 'recieve', 'definately').
