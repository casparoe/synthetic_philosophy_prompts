I run a 90-minute graduate seminar in political philosophy and institutional design with about 14 students. Design a structured simulation—using only paper tokens, hidden-role cards, and a facilitator sheet—that makes participants feel the structural difficulty of identifying a "safe Pareto improvement": a proposed change that improves at least one person's position without leaving anyone worse off, not merely in the goods they publicly declare but in the full set of dimensions they privately value, including those they have strategic reason to conceal.

The scenario: each student receives two visible token-types (autonomy credits and stability credits) plus a hidden weighting card that assigns private values to a third, unannounced dimension (recognition, future-option value, or procedural voice). A proposal is put forward—perhaps a restructuring of seminar governance—that visibly increases one player's autonomy credits. By the visible rules, no one's token counts fall, so unanimous consent should be immediate. However, include a "shadow audit" mechanism: after initial approval, concealed weightings are revealed, and any participant can invoke a retroactive veto if the change reduced their private net value, even though their visible tokens never dropped.

Specify the rules of proposal, the unanimous-consent procedure, the conditions for triggering the audit, the veto power, and a small-stakes payoff scheme (for example, the group earns collective bonus points toward a shared grade adjustment only if they pass a proposal that survives audit; if vetoed, they must either stay with the status quo or shift to an explicit compensation scheme that reveals hidden dimensions). The participants should run into the veto themselves—discovering that what seemed like a free improvement was contested—before the facilitator names the concept.

Then provide the debrief: map the proposal phase to the standard Pareto criterion, the unanimous-consent moment to ex-ante agreement, the audit veto to the epistemic and strategic barriers that make "safety" expensive, and the fallback to compensation to the slide toward Kaldor-Hicks reasoning. Be explicit about what will predictably fail the first time—likely, students will treat hidden weights as a trick and try to game the concealment, or they will conflate the veto with ordinary disagreement—and tell me exactly how to frame the hidden cards so the concealment is taken as a structural feature rather than a deception. Finally, state honestly which feature of safe Pareto improvement the simulation can reproduce (the dependence on unknown preference structures) and which it cannot reproduce and must be supplied by you in the closing minutes (the normative claim that hidden dimensions ought to count for justice).

No lecture before the simulation; they should not hear the term "safe Pareto improvement" until the debrief. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Keep all mechanics precise enough to run with paper tokens and cards alone.
