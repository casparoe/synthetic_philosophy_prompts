I just finished a long, somewhat frustrating argument with a friend who teaches philosophy of religion, and I am hoping you can help me settle the conceptual tension for my own seminar. We were debating whether Buddhist ethics—particularly the Theravada Abhidharma treatment of cetana (volitional intention) and the non-transferable ripening of karma (vipaka)—can be coherently interpreted as a form of consequentialism. My friend argued that because the ultimate soteriological aim is the cessation of suffering, moral evaluation must track an act's contribution to that aggregate outcome, making the framework functionally consequentialist over long time horizons. I insisted that this misses the point: in this framework, the moral quality of an action is fixed by the mental state of the agent at the moment of volition, and the karmic consequences accrue to that specific consciousness-stream in ways that cannot be pooled, traded, or canceled out by benefits that flow to other beings. We went in circles, and I realized that neither of us had a concrete pedagogical tool that could make students feel why this distinction actually blocks aggregation, rather than just reading about it.

I am leading an upper-level philosophy seminar—fourteen students, seventy-five minutes—most of whom know Western normative ethics well but have only passing familiarity with Buddhist concepts. I need a single, runnable in-class simulation (not a lecture or open debate) that makes them experience the conflict between consequentialist aggregation and the non-transferability of karmic weight before anyone names it. The activity should use roles, rules, or forced choices—ideally involving a staged scarcity dilemma, such as the distribution of a limited medicine among caregivers with different mental dispositions—so that students discover for themselves that an action producing the greatest collective welfare can still generate an irreducible ethical cost tied to a specific agent's intention. Design the rules, any small-stakes payoff scheme, and a timeline that fits seventy-five minutes. Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto cetana, vipaka, and the aggregation problem, and be frank about which feature of the Buddhist view—especially the temporal delay of karmic maturation across lives—the simulation necessarily flattens and that you, as facilitator, must supply verbally. Do not offer a menu of options or ask me to choose between designs; take a definite position on the structure and defend why it captures the tension more sharply than a simpler role-play or a purely verbal thought experiment. I also do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to specific sutras; I need something I can run tomorrow without requiring students to consult untranslated primary texts. Give me a committed answer: commit to one architecture for the game and one interpretation of what it proves about the relationship between Buddhist ethics and consequentialism, and argue for both.
