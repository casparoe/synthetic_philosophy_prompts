I'm writing a long-form feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on whether the "genetic code" metaphor has survived the molecular revolution, and I've realized that before I can evaluate any particular theory in interviews, I need a rigorous specification of what a theory of genetic information is actually obligated to deliver. Would you write out the desiderata — the requirements that any adequate account must satisfy, with the specific conceptual tension or biological case motivating each one — and flag which demands are genuinely non-negotiable and which belong to particular philosophical camps? I want the list to cover the standard puzzles: how the triplet-to-amino-acid mapping functions without being a purely arbitrary cipher; whether the ribosome, tRNA pool, and cellular context should count as part of the informational system or as external interpreters; and whether phenomena like horizontal gene transfer and viral insertion represent genuine information-bearing events or mere noise.

But I also want the criteria to address a less commonly discussed tension: whether DNA can carry literal semantic content — information that is about the phenotype it helps produce — without requiring a mind or an evolutionary history specifically selected for that representational role, or whether such "aboutness" is always just a metaphor projected by biologists. In other words, must an adequate theory distinguish between Shannon information (pure statistical reduction of uncertainty in a sequence) and semantic information (content directed at a specific functional outcome), and if so, does that distinction collapse in genetics because every functional mutation is automatically "about" survival? Please also consider whether the theory must treat DNA as a self-contained program running on generic cellular hardware, or whether it must incorporate the idea that DNA functions more like a musical score that only realizes its informational content through the specific "performance" of modified tRNAs, ribosome stalling dynamics, and epigenetic marks — meaning the information is distributed across the sequence and its reading environment rather than localized in the nucleotides alone.

Finally, mark any pair of desiderata that seem jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, if insisting on a strict, literal codebook conflicts with accommodating context-dependent translation, or if requiring evolutionary-selection conditions for genuine information excludes viral DNA that clearly carries functional instructions despite lacking a host-tied evolutionary history. Keep the weight on articulating these criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring existing theories, though you may briefly indicate how a syntactic-causal view, an evolutionary-selection view, and a "distributed performance" view fare against the list. The purpose is to give me, as a journalist without laboratory access, a clear standard against which to judge the claims I encounter.
