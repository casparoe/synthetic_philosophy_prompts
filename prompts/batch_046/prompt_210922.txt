Consider the following reductio against treating generational cohorts—Millennials, Gen Z, and similar groupings—as real kinds rather than pragmatic labels.

(1) If generations are real kinds, then membership must depend on a shared, causally unified package of formative conditions that genuinely distinguishes members from non-members.
(2) Formative conditions—technological access, economic shocks, political socialization—vary continuously over time, with no sharp discontinuities at calendar-year boundaries.
(3) So if generations are real kinds, there must be a non-arbitrary principle that selects specific years as boundaries despite the continuous gradient.
(4) No such principle exists: the differences between someone born in December 1996 and someone born in January 1997 are negligible compared to the differences within either proposed group.
(5) Therefore, either generational membership is arbitrarily assigned, or every narrow sub-cohort—down to single-month birth groups—counts as its own real kind with its own unified package.
(6) But treating every monthly cohort as a real kind makes generational explanation vacuous, since we lose any predictive or explanatory generality.
(7) Hence generations are not real kinds; they are pragmatic slices with no ontological weight.

This seems to prove too much, since an analogous argument would eliminate species, diseases, or climate zones. Identify the weakest step or hidden slide—perhaps an equivocation between metaphysical and pragmatic kinds, a quantifier shift, or an unstated assumption that continuous variation precludes kindhood altogether. Explain why that step looked plausible and what rejecting it costs. If you can, preserve (1) and (6), but say what that commitment forces elsewhere—about other continuously distributed social categories, or about the standards a useful pragmatic kind must meet.
