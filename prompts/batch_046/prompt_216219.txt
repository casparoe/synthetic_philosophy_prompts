I'm working through a comparative-historical seminar on societal transformation, and we've been examining the familiar triad—the Late Bronze Age systems collapse, the Classic Maya abandonment, and Easter Island's ecological and demographic contraction—mainly to ask whether these represent genuine societal failure or merely a change in organizational scale. To move past the usual framing, the instructor proposed a conceptual distinction I want to pressure-test: call it institutional decoherence versus infrastructural contraction. By institutional decoherence I mean the dissolution of the mechanisms that coordinate action across space and social scale—standardized writing, centralized taxation, long-distance exchange protocols—even when local population and cultural practice continue. By infrastructural contraction I mean the opposite pattern: the physical and demographic substrate shrinks sharply, yet the coordinating institutions persist, however attenuated. The claim is that the Bronze Age and the Maya cases are closer to decoherence, Easter Island closer to contraction (or a hybrid), and that the Western Roman Empire is usually misread as decoherence when it might in fact illustrate contraction, or something that breaks the dichotomy altogether.

Apply this framework to the end of the Western Roman Empire in the West, taking as your focal period roughly 410 to 476 CE and the immediate post-imperial settlement patterns under the Merovingians and Ostrogoths. Which specific institutional capacities—imperial taxation, the annona supply system, the command structure of the field armies, Roman legal administration—actually dissolved, and which survived in ecclesiastical, senatorial, or barbarian-kingdom form? Where does the demographic and economic record suggest contraction versus continuity? More importantly, I want the analysis to run both ways: not only what the framework reveals about Rome, but what Rome reveals about the framework. Does the persistence of Roman law and the Church count as institutional survival, undermining a collapse narrative? Or does the loss of imperial coordination represent decoherence regardless of local survival? And if both patterns are present simultaneously, does the collapse-versus-change distinction itself fail, or does it need to be replaced by something more granular?
