Executive education programs and self-improvement curricula built around founder biographies, military memoirs, or singular scientific lives treat long-form narrative biography as a primary technology for moral and strategic development: the student is expected to extract norms, decision heuristics, and character traits by studying the arc of one exceptional figure. This practice is institutionally widespread—required reading lists, seminar discussions centered on a single life—yet it rarely defends its pedagogical theory explicitly.

What philosophical commitments about moral learning, the transparency of character, and the epistemic status of narrative does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it assume a Plutarchan model in which virtue and vice are revealed through small, concrete actions and therefore learnable through close study of paired or singular lives? Or does it presuppose something closer to hagiography—that the life is exemplary precisely because it is exceptional rather than representative, and that admiration itself is the mechanism of improvement?

Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well. For instance, does assigning biography necessarily commit one to the view that moral education is imitative rather than discursive, or could a Kantian or rule-based pedagogy accommodate narrative study as mere illustration? Is the practice consistent with modern situationist psychology, which treats individual character as weakly predictive relative to institutional context, or does it quietly presuppose a great-man ontology of causation that the same curricula often deny in explicit historical analysis?

Consider also the lost comparative frame. Plutarch’s Parallel Lives used paired Greek and Roman figures to generate structural distance, allowing the reader to see virtue in similarity rather than in singular heroism. Modern curricula typically drop this pairing and study one life in isolation. Does that shift turn the practice from moral comparison into unreflective hero worship, and if so, what does that reveal about its implicit commitment to narrative transparency versus selection bias?

Finally, identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined—whether several rival theories of moral learning, such as Aristotelian habituation through exemplars, Stoic self-examination via comparison, or even consequentialist case-study reasoning, could license the same curriculum, or whether the absence of explicit comparison rules out some of these. Say what would have to change in the institution if the presupposed view were replaced: for example, if moral learning were understood as the acquisition of abstract decision procedures rather than the internalization of narrated character, or if biography were recognized as epistemically unreliable due to narrative fallacy and survivor bias. Is the practice then salvageable with adjustments, or does it collapse into something else entirely?
