In a clinical-ethics discussion about terminal prognosis, a counselor dismissed a patient’s dread of future non-existence by saying “death is nothing to us,” treating the anxiety as a category mistake rather than a rational harm. Lay out the precise distinct theses this slogan could be asserting—from the uncontroversial claim that corpses feel nothing to the stronger Epicurean argument that deprivation requires a subject who can suffer—indicate for each whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and say which reading, if any, justifies dismissing grief over the irreversible cancellation of one’s life-narrative. Do this with particular attention to the difference between lacking phenomenal experience after death and lacking any future state in which one’s projects could continue. Respond with exactly five numbered items, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introductory or concluding paragraph.
