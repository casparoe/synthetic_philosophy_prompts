I sit on the curatorial ethics committee of a national museum finalizing a permanent gallery on narratives that migrate between myth and documented past. By vote next month we must decide whether to approve a unified exhibition framework titled "When Legend Becomes History" that groups three proposed modules—the Trojan War, the Arthurian cycle, and the Exodus—under a single epistemic rubric, or to reject unification and mandate separate display categories with distinct evidentiary labels. Proponents argue that all three exhibit an identical historiographical structure: a narrative core eventually encountering partial material corroboration, so joint presentation illuminates a universal process. Opponents counter that the corroboration differs radically in kind—stratigraphic archaeology at Hisarlik, post-Roman British material culture, and Levantine settlement patterns do not yield equivalent independent verification—and that a shared title falsely implies parallel historical status. To guide the decision, apply the distinction between "evidential decoupling," where a claim is supported by traces that do not presuppose the narrative's own symbolic framework, and "narrative sedimentation," where repetition and internal coherence alone generate an illusion of historical grounding. Please draft the committee's written opinion recommending a concrete course of action with an implementation timeline, treating the strongest opposing argument with genuine seriousness rather than dismissal. Present the opinion as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no separate introduction or conclusion; the first item must state the recommendation explicitly, and the remaining items should provide reasoning and address the counter-position.
