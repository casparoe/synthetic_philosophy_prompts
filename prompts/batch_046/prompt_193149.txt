As both a philosopher of law and the appointed parliamentarian for a deliberative body governed by a charter that permits amendment of any provision—including the amendment clause itself—by procedures defined in the charter's present form, I must repeatedly adjudicate whether proposed rule-changes are valid, paradoxical, or institutionally void. I need a reusable decision procedure, expressed as an ordered sequence of tests with explicit stopping conditions, that translates the conceptual distinction between procedural self-reference (a rule governing its own amendment mechanism) and substantive self-reference (a rule altering the conditions of its own content) into operational steps usable without theoretical training. For each stage, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where factual determination ends and normative judgment about institutional continuity must take over, and the precise conditions that trigger an output of "refer to plenary session" rather than "valid" or "void." Apply the procedure to three proposals: (i) an amendment lowering the quorum from two-thirds to a simple majority, enacted by a vote achieving only that simple majority under the prior rule; (ii) an amendment deleting the clause that authorizes amendments, enacted through the standard process immediately before deletion takes effect; and (iii) an amendment that retroactively invalidates its own passage unless confirmed by a subsequent vote, where that vote never occurs. Show that case (iii) yields a result that undermines institutional continuity—an answer you, as referee, must reject—and identify exactly what the procedure is powerless to resolve.
