You are an expert in political philosophy and the philosophy of labor. Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm the conceptual landscape around work, leisure, and moral desert, then proceed step by step.

I keep noticing a recurring configuration in debates about working time that I do not have a name for. Take three familiar reference points: the Protestant work ethic’s treatment of labor as a moral calling; the institutionalization of the 35-hour week as a formal entitlement to rest; and karoshi, where extreme overwork kills, yet is routinely framed as an individual health failure rather than a structural outcome. Here is the pattern. Defenders of strict hour limits and defenders of long or flexible hours often share an unstated premise: leisure is not a basic right but a kind of earned discharge from prior productive obligation. The advocate for the 35-hour week argues that workers have “paid in” enough labor to deserve their weekend; the advocate for extreme work argues that rest must be proportionate to contribution, so reducing hours violates a merit-based order. Meanwhile, the Protestant ethic is mobilized bilaterally: its valorization of toil justifies long hours, but its emphasis on vocation and moderation is used to condemn karoshi as a failure of personal discipline rather than the logical endpoint of the same system. So one ethical tradition and one hidden meritocratic premise serve both sides, with the extreme case treated as an outlier that proves the rule.

Is there an established concept for this exact configuration—where leisure is treated as a reward for accumulated labor-desert, where opposing labor policies both depend on that premise, and where a tradition like the Protestant work ethic is used to both demand and limit work? How does it differ from standard critiques of productivism, from Marxist alienation, or from rights-based arguments that leisure is intrinsic? If there is no precise term, define the phenomenon with clear boundary conditions, distinguishing it from mere hypocrisy, from the economic work-leisure trade-off, and from the idea that labor builds character. I am not looking for a bare label; I want to know where the edges are and whether this is a recognized failure mode or something that needs a new name.
