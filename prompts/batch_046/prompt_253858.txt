I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy working on the structure of intergenerational value, and I am trying to stabilize my own view about why human extinction is bad in ways that do not reduce to the deaths involved—specifically, in relation to what is often called the afterlife conjecture, the thought that a significant portion of present meaning derives from the expectation that future people will exist to continue our unfinished projects. Rather than asking for a literature survey, I want to test whether my concrete judgments about variant scenarios cohere around a single principle, or whether consistency forces me to abandon one or more of them.

Here are my verdicts. In Case 1, humanity dies out suddenly with long-term scientific and moral projects left incomplete; I judge that present lives are genuinely impoverished by this prospect, not merely saddened by it. In Case 2, we go extinct, but durable autonomous systems we designed continue those same projects to completion over millennia; I think the badness is partly, though not fully, mitigated—the continuity of the work matters, yet something about the absence of living agents still leaves a loss. In Case 3, we die out, and five centuries later an entirely unrelated evolutionary line produces intelligent beings who independently solve the very problems we cared about; I feel this offers almost no consolation—the gap in causal and intentional continuity seems decisive. In Case 4, a tiny human colony survives with intact biological descent, but suffers total cultural amnesia and eventually rebuilds knowledge from scratch; despite the genetic continuity, this strikes me as closer to Case 3 than to Case 2, which suggests that biological survival alone is not doing the work. In Case 5, we learn that extinction is unavoidable in exactly one thousand years, but every project we currently value will be completed well before then; here I do not think present meaning is undermined at all—there is no unfinished business that requires future hands. In Case 6, we learn extinction is unavoidable in ten years, and our most valued work—say, a multi-generational effort to eliminate a particular form of suffering—will therefore never be finished; this feels like a direct assault on the point of engaging in that work now.

What principle, if any, captures all six verdicts? Please state it with enough precision that each clause can be matched to the case that motivates it, and say what it predicts for a scenario I have not listed: one in which humanity is replaced, through deliberate collective choice, by a causally continuous but value-divergent post-human form that abandons all our specific projects yet achieves equally complex ones of its own. If no single principle accommodates every verdict without ad hoc qualification, present the two strongest candidates, indicate exactly which of my judgments each requires me to overturn, and say which revision you think is least damaging to the underlying intuition about intergenerational meaning.
