I have been working through belief-attribution problems in artificial systems with a colleague in my philosophy of mind seminar, and she has maintained a remarkably consistent pattern of verdicts across our discussions. She holds that typical adult humans genuinely possess beliefs; that domestic dogs possess beliefs about hidden objects and the emotional states of their owners; that a 1990s symbolic expert system for medical diagnosis possesses no beliefs, only explicit inference rules; that contemporary large language models, regardless of scale or coherence, possess no beliefs; that a hypothetical embodied agent equipped with persistent long-term goals, direct sensory-motor coupling, and error-correction mechanisms but lacking phenomenal consciousness does possess beliefs; and that a simple thermostat possesses none. She consistently rejects arguments that treat behavioral mimicry alone, or internal computational complexity alone, as sufficient for belief attribution, yet she also denies that biological substrate or phenomenal consciousness is strictly necessary.

The case I now want to test against her framework is an adaptive interaction system—call it a counterfactual belief tracker—that is being developed in human-computer interaction research. It is a non-embodied neural network that maintains a continuously updated high-dimensional state-space representation of a specific human user's likely mental states, derived entirely from interaction logs rather than direct sensory observation. It selects interventions—preemptively altering interface layouts, adjusting environmental settings—to minimize divergence between predicted and observed behavior. It possesses no explicit symbolic representations, generates no linguistic output, holds no long-term goals beyond predictive accuracy for this single user, has no sensory apparatus of its own, and exhibits no capacity for self-reference or second-order monitoring.

Given this track record, please predict her verdict on whether this counterfactual belief tracker itself genuinely possesses beliefs—such as beliefs about the user's preferences or about its own predictive successes. Identify the implicit rule, or combination of rules, that best explains her earlier judgments, whether that be a Dennett-style intentional-stance criterion restricted to open-ended rational agency, an embodied representational-functionalism requiring integrated sensory-motor loops, a social-recursion condition requiring reciprocal interaction, or another coherent principle. Then specify at least one alternative rule that accommodates all six of her earlier verdicts equally well but yields a different result for the tracker. Finally, propose a further boundary case—perhaps a distributed agricultural swarm with no central controller, or a language model augmented with a persistent multi-month user-specific memory and planning module—that would best distinguish the competing rules from one another.
