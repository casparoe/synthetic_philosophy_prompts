I am preparing to lead a ninety-minute discussion for a philosophy-of-religion reading group composed mostly of non-specialist graduate students, on Kurt Gödel's rationalist argument for personal survival after death — a topic that has become personally urgent since my father's death and my own dissatisfaction with purely theological answers. The group is likely to know Gödel only through his incompleteness theorems and, at best, through popular summaries of his modal-ontological proof for God's existence; they will not know that he advanced a separate, far less formalized argument that consciousness continues beyond bodily decay, one that rests not on modal logic but on a principle of value-completeness: that a world lacking the full realization of possible values is necessarily incomplete, and that an imperfect physical cosmos therefore cannot be the whole of reality. I want to teach the internal structure of this specific argument, not the ontological proof, and I want the session to work for people who have no training in formal logic or metaphysics.

Plan the explanatory arc for me: what dependency must be secured before the claim can even be heard as philosophy rather than grief-stricken mysticism; which single carrying example or analogy — perhaps the gap between a mathematically perfect crystal and its physically flawed instantiation — should bear the weight, and exactly what features it needs to possess and to lack so it does not collapse into a generic "there must be more" intuition; the misconceptions that will arise in predictable order, including the belief that Gödel derived his afterlife view directly from his proof of God, the confusion of rationalist optimism with religious faith, the reduction of his view to private bereavement, and the assumption that he meant literal bodily resurrection; the precise point in the sequence at which each misconception should be intercepted and defused; one question I can pose at the end that reveals whether a participant has grasped the argument's dependency structure rather than merely sympathized with its emotional origin; and what I should openly decline to cover at this level — for instance, the full modal formalization, the neurobiology of consciousness, or the history of rationalist immortality doctrines — with an explicit statement of that boundary.

In constructing this plan, separate what is textually settled about Gödel's notebooks and correspondence from what remains genuinely contested among scholars who have worked directly with those materials, and do not present any contested interpretive thesis — for example, about whether his argument is genuinely independent of his theistic proof — as though it were the consensus.
