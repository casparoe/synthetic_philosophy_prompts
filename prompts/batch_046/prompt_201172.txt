Please help me understand a passage from John Stuart Mill's Chapters on Socialism (1879), in which he examines a cooperative manufacturing association where workers elect their managers and divide the surplus among themselves. Mill grants that this arrangement removes the capitalist's claim on profit derived from ownership, but he then introduces a further difficulty: without the external discipline of market competition and the risk of insolvency, the cooperative may still generate an internal hierarchy in which elected managers govern production schedules, surplus allocation, and the pace of labor—not through capital ownership, but through an administrative monopoly over coordination and information. He asks whether this merely relocates the problem of mastery from the proprietor to the functionary, leaving the worker subject to a domination that can be voted away in principle yet persists in practice. I am trying to grasp whether Mill regards democratic election within the firm as genuinely sufficient to neutralize this danger, or whether he tacitly relies on some residual competitive mechanism—perhaps rivalry with other cooperatives or with capitalist firms—to keep managerial power in check. More specifically, I want to know whether his argument here depends on an unstated psychological theory of economic motivation, namely that self-interest will always tend to centralize control unless externally disciplined, or whether he thinks the cooperative form itself transforms the incentives of the manager in a way that renders such external pressure unnecessary. If my framing imports a distinction—say, between bureaucratic extraction and capital extraction—that Mill would not have recognized, or if I am treating a tentative exploratory remark as a settled doctrine, please say so plainly rather than accommodating the misreading. I am not asking for citations, bibliographies, or further reading; I want an interpretation that respects the conceptual architecture Mill actually presents.
