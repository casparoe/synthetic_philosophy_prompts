I'm a retired biophysicist who only recently started reading proper philosphy — my granddaughter lent me that anthology The Mind's I, and I got stuck on the bit about Einstein's brain being kept alive and having a conversation. It made me wonder whether consciousness has to be locked inside a skull at all, or whether it could sit in something completely different, like a book. Not a metaphorical book, but an actual physical object with pages.

Could you write me a short story that takes both ideas seriously? The setting is a quiet laboratory in the late 1970s: Einstein's brain is preserved in a vat, wired up and still producing thoughts, but its only link to the outside world is a single leather-bound volume — a copy of Principia Mathematica — whose pages have been replaced with cultured neural tissue that rewires itself whenever it recieves an electrical pulse the brain sends. The brain thinks it is speaking to a researcher, but it is only ever speaking to the book. Meanwhile, the book is slowly developing patterns that look like behaviour, maybe even something like self-awareness, formed entirely through the mathematics it absorbs. I want the story to ask, quietly, whether consciousness is in the brain, in the book, in the gap between them, or in none of those places. To keep it from being just another brain-in-a-jar story, please include one genuinely strange twist: at some point the book begins to dream in notation that the brain recognises as fragments of its own childhood memories — memories the brain never told anyone. Keep the tone philosophical rather than action-packed, and don't worry about giving me references or a bibliography; I just want to see where the idea lands.
