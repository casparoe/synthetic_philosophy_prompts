hey, i'm drafting a novel about an archivist who discovers the catlog system she inherited wasn't neutral — it was engineered to make certain lives unfindable. i want to feel that machinery in my bones, so i'm asking for a classroom design, not a lecture.

picture this: 16 gradute students, one 75-minute session, maybe in a library-studies workshop. i want them to experience what i'm calling "structured absense" — the idea that an archive doesn't just forget things; it actively builds silence into its bones through filing rules, cross-reference choices, and the language of the index itself.

design a game or simulation where the group has to construct a catalog from a shared pile of documents (some intact, some partially redacted, some mislabled) and then retrieve proof for a specific historical claim. the rules should be set up so that every "neutral" decision they make — where to file, what keyword to assign, whether to link or isolate — quietly buries the very evidence they later need. they should hit the wall themselves: the retrieval fails not because the document is gone, but because their own catalog makes it invisible. only after that failure do they learn the name of the mechanism.

include the exact rules, roles (who holds the index, who searches, who decides classification), a simple payoff or scoring scheme that doesn't use real money, and a timeline. then the debrief: map each stage of the game onto a part of the idea — the filing choice, the cross-reference gap, the retrieval dead end. tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (i'll guess people blame missing pages instead of the system?). and be honest: what feature of real archival power — the violence of whose papers get saved in the first place, maybe? — can this paper-and-card activity never reproduce, and that i'll have to supply in the discussion.

no bibliographies, please. i can't search online and neither can the model i'm testing this on. just build it from the ground up, with maybe one small invented detail — like a specific mislabled folder name — that makes the point stick.
