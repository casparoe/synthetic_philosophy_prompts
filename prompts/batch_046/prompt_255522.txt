I was arguing with my roommate about causation by omission — like, does not watering your plant actually cause it to die, or is that just sloppy talk? We tried to list the main positions people defend but I'm not sure I got them right. Here are the five I scribbled down: (1) omissions cause stuff only when there's a background norm or expectation that gets violated — no expectation, no causation. (2) omissions are causes in exactly the same metaphysical sense as actions, they just lack physical motion, so not flipping the switch is as causal as flipping it. (3) omissions are never real causes; at best they figure in explanations because we care about duty, but metaphyscially it's just other events doing the work. (4) an omission causes an outcome only if the agent had a specific plan or intention that included not acting, so pure neglect isn't causal but strategic inaction is. (5) omissions cause outcomes counterfactualy — if they had acted, the outcome wouldn't have happened — and that's enough, no extra metaphysics needed.

Could you rank these five from the most defensible to the least defensible, giving each a score from 1 to 10? I especially want to know where (1) and (5) land relative to eachother, since they both lean on counterfactuals but in different ways.
