I just spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether linguistic relativity—the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis—retains any defensible form that is not either trivially true or empirically dead, and I now need to teach the concept properly rather than defend it rhetorically. I am running a single ninety-minute session for a reading group of philosophy-of-science graduates who arrive convinced they have already dismissed Whorfianism; they are not novices, but they treat the thesis as a settled failure. I do not want an explanation for my own benefit. I want a teaching plan for one precise, non-obvious version of the idea: that grammatical aspect and lexical aspect jointly constrain how continuous experience is segmented into bounded events, and that this segmentation feeds back into causal attribution without ever amounting to a deterministic prison of thought. Plan the dependency sequence—what must be in place first (probably the distinction between linguistic coding and conceptual availability, and then the separation of lexical gap from structural framing)—and justify why that order is necessary rather than pedagogically convenient. Identify the single carrying example or comparative scenario that should anchor the entire session: I am considering a contrast involving agentive marking in Turkish versus English for accidental actions, but only if it possesses the specific properties required—namely, that it resists the "this is just a translation difference" objection and that it visibly operates at the event-structural level rather than the vocabulary level; if it fails on either count, specify what properties the substitute example must have and propose a different structural contrast. Map the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring and form—particularly "this is merely about vocabulary differences," "correlation in these experiments does not establish causation," and "if the effect were real it would appear in physics reasoning"—and state exactly where in the sequence each should be intercepted, with the reasoning for that placement. Provide one diagnostic question for the end that distinguishes whether they have grasped the structural-coupling claim from whether they have merely accepted that language "influences" cognition in some vague way. Finally, specify what should be explicitly left out at this level—the neuroimaging literature and the well-known debate about immediate-experience counting in an Amazonian language—and state how to frame that omission so it does not sound like an evasion.
