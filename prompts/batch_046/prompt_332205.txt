In my philosophy-of-social-science reading group, the methodological individualism versus holism debate has reached a peculiar standstill: everyone accepts that you can describe a bank run, a strike wave, or the diffusion of a linguistic norm either as an aggregation of individual beliefs and actions or as the operation of an institutional structure, and neither side denies the other’s empirical narratives. Yet the individualists still insist that macro-level explanations are merely “heuristic abbreviations” that are causally inert unless decomposed, while the holists maintain that structures possess “explanatory autonomy” even when every micro-mechanism is mapped. Decades of exchange—going back to the classical formulations in the 1950s and the later rational-choice responses—have not moved either camp, despite near-total agreement on the cases. I am beginning to suspect the disagreement is not genuinely ontological but concerns what I will call explanatory licensing: the unstated disciplinary rules that determine when it is legitimate to invoke a macro-regularity without reducing it, with each side treating its own licensing conditions as if they were discoveries about reality rather than norms of adequate proof. Is this diagnosis correct—does the stalemate hide a substantive dispute about downward causation or emergent properties, or is it a methodological quarrel about standards of evidence and compositional stability masquerading as metaphysics? And if the licensing hypothesis holds, is the debate ill-posed in a way that should be dissolved by separating descriptive sociology from normative methodology, or is there a precise conceptual distinction—perhaps about causal granularity or the conditions under which macro-variables remain invariant across micro-reconfigurations—that could genuinely break the deadlock? Give me a diagnosis of the disagreement’s actual structure, supported by what each side is really doing when they invoke examples like institutional racism or market bubbles, and tell me honestly what would change your mind: which kind of argument, formal result, or concrete empirical case would convince you that the impasse is ontological rather than methodological, and where in your own reasoning is the risk of collapse greatest?
