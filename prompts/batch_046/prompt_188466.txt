hey, I'm a retired cortical neuroscientist — spent thirty years mapping sensory pathways — and since I retired I've been getting into philosophy at the local library. I run a small workshop there, about ten people mostly in thier sixties and seventies, two hours every other thursday. We've been talking about hedonism, specifically this heterogeneity problem: if pleasure is just one thing, why do eating dark chocolate, hearing a dissonance finally resolve, and finishing a long run all feel so radically different? I keep thinking about my old work on interoceptive prediction errors — the idea that a "good" feeling might just be a mismatch being corrected, but corrected in totally different brain regions — and I want the group to feel the tension between that unifying story and the obvious diversity of experiance. Could you design a runnable game or structured exercise for us? The rules should make them try to rank, trade, or price these different pleasures using one shared "pleasure currency" that they build themselves, so they only notice near the end that the currency forces them to erase what makes each pleasure distinct. Then I need a debrief that maps those breakdown moments onto the heterogeneity problem and onto where the interoceptive-prediction view helps or collapses, plus an honest warning about what will predictably go wrong with this competitive group and what part of the philosophical argument the activity simply can't reproduce — I'll have to supply that myself in discussion. No lecture up front, don't name the problem until afterwards, and please don't ask anyone to memorize philosopher names or cite texts; they should come away seeing that unifying pleasure might cost us too much of its texture.
