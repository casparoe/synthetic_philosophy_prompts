Please argue that the standard historiographic framing of "who started the Cold War" is conceptually confused rather than merely empirically unresolved. Specifically, defend the view that the question presupposes a model of collective agency—treating states or alliances as unitary intentional actors capable of bearing blame in roughly the interpersonal sense—when the actual causal structure of 1945-1949 is better described as institutional path dependence interacting with strategic misperception, making outcome-based blame assignment inappropriate. In developing this, use the philosophical distinction between control-based and outcome-based responsibility, and explain why applying it to macrohistorical processes either collapses into triviality or requires a theory of collective intentionality that orthodox, revisionist, and post-revisionist accounts alike fail to provide. Acknowledge that it is settled that these three narratives differ chiefly in how they distribute causal weight between American economic hegemony, Soviet security imperatives, and structural bipolarity; but treat as genuinely contested whether any of them can support a non-metaphorical blame judgment, and whether counterfactual reasoning about what Truman or Stalin could have done differently is coherent at the scale of alliance systems. Separate clearly what follows conceptually from your premises—for instance, that if collective blame requires collective intentionality then the "who started it" question dissolves—from what depends on contested empirical premises about decision-making in 1945-1947. Write in a formal, precise register appropriate to a long-form rationalist discussion, do not present any single historiographic school as representing scholarly consensus, and do not ask for citations, author lists, or bibliographies.
