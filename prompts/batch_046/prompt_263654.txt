I am preparing to defend my seminar paper in our animal ethics course, tentatively titled Beyond Marginal Cases: Relational Obligations Without Capacity Thresholds. My core claim is that the canonical marginal-cases objection to speciesism—most familiar from Peter Singer’s equal-consideration framework—unwittingly preserves the very structure it attacks by treating moral status as a binary property triggered by a capacity threshold such as sentience or rationality. I propose replacing this with a graduated relational model: moral obligations to non-human animals arise not from shared universal capacities but from the specific modes of ecological and intersubjective entanglement available with particular beings, so that obligations vary in kind rather than merely in degree. The audience will be my professor, who works on moral status and is broadly Singerian but genuinely open to revision; one graduate student who is a committed Tom Regan-style rights theorist; two environmental philosophers who worry that extending individual moral status to animals distracts from systemic ecological ethics; and several undergraduates who have read only Singer and Regan. I need a prep sheet ordered by how damaging each objection is: for each, phrase it in the vocabulary that particular interlocutor would use, note what I should concede, what I must hold, the weakest point in my reply that I should defend rather than hope goes unnoticed, and a brief reply suitable for a seminar defense. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody raises—specifically, the charge that my relational model collapses into a form of contractarianism that excludes wild animals with whom humans share no direct social relationship—and flag where my position is genuinely soft. Do not provide bibliographies or reading lists; I need conceptual ammunition, not citations.
