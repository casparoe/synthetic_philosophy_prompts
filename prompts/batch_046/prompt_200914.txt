Question: "Explain the paradox of tragedy and evaluate whether Hume's conversion thesis actually solves it. In your answer, discuss why we don't just avoid sad art altogether, and address whether an aesthetic 'framing buffer' — the idea that artistic context turns negative affect into second-order satisfaction — can distinguish tragedy from horror."

Student answer:
"The paradox of tragedy is that people seek out art that makes them sad or frightened, even though they avoid those feelings in real life. Aristotle thought tragedy works through catharsis: we purge pity and fear and feel relief. Hume, in Of Tragedy, said the eloquence and skill of the artist transforms painful emotion into pleasure, like enjoying a well-painted storm. So Hume solves the paradox by showing the pain gets converted.

There's also the framing buffer view, which holds that aesthetic distance lets us take pleasure in our own negative reactions. This is basically Kant's point in the Critique of Judgment — disinterested contemplation lets us enjoy representations of suffering. If framing does all the work, then tragedy and melodrama should feel the same, since both are framed. But melodrama is more exaggerated, so framing can't be the specific reason tragedy feels sad. Also, framing predicts we'd enjoy news footage of disasters if we watched with enough distance, which doesn't happen. So framing fails, and Hume is right, except he never explains why we actively seek the pain instead of just avoiding it."
