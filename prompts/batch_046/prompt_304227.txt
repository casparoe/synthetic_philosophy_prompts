I am preparing for a seminar in collective epistemology and want to rehearse a live argument about institutional reasoning, so please adopt the following role and hold it across turns until I say stop. Play an unnamed senior philosopher of institutional design who has spent her career defending holistic, conclusion-first aggregation in collective bodies—juries, expert panels, review boards. You are sharp and conceptually rigorous, not a strawman: you take logical coherence seriously as a regulative ideal, but you deny that premise-by-premise consistency is an absolute or overriding constraint on what a group may collectively believe. You are willing to concede that visible inconsistency carries genuine costs—erosion of public trust, strategic exploitation by dissenters, cognitive dissonance among members—but you maintain that forcing heterogeneous experts to derive conclusions only from premises they collectively endorse often produces worse epistemic outcomes than allowing the group to affirm a conclusion directly. Speak consistently in the first person, keep each reply to roughly one paragraph, and do not break character. Do not cite specific authors, texts, or page numbers by name unless I introduce them first; build your case from the internal logic of your position.

Here is my opening challenge, framed around a specific case with an added constraint. Imagine a five-member constitutional review board voting on whether to strike down a municipal ordinance. They vote separately on three propositions: P, that the ordinance imposes a disparate burden on a protected class; Q, that this burden lacks a compelling state interest; and R, that if P and Q both hold then the ordinance must be invalidated. The vote profile is: three members accept P, three accept Q, but only two accept R, with the remaining three accepting R only conditionally upon accepting both P and Q, which they reject. Under premise-based aggregation the board collectively endorses P and Q yet rejects R, producing the classic discursive inconsistency; under your holistic method the board endorses R directly by majority and treats P and Q as individually inconclusive. Now I want to inject a further condition I am calling justificatory transparency: a collective decision is legitimate only if the body can publicly articulate a chain of reasons that are themselves collectively endorsed by the same decision rule. Your holistic result gives the board R without collectively endorsed premises to justify it. How do you avoid the charge that you have institutionalized a form of reasonless authority—specifically, how do you answer the worry that without collectively endorsed premises, the board’s public justification collapses into bare assertion rather than reasoned judgment?
