I have been refining an argument about emergency ethics that now strikes me as overreaching. (1) If an emergency act is permissible only when it minimizes expected harm among available options, then torture in a verified ticking-bomb scenario is permissible exactly when no less harmful extraction method exists. (2) The same calculus applies to institutional rules: if a narrowly designed exception—judicial authorization, medical supervision, automatic sunset—yields lower expected harm than an absolute prohibition, then maintaining that exception is not merely permitted but required. (3) Because real bombs are rare and verification is imperfect, the expected harm of a standing framework depends heavily on error rates; but if the framework is restricted to genuinely verified cases, its institutional risk can still fall below that of an unconditional ban. (4) Therefore society must maintain an active, staffed regulatory apparatus for exceptional torture, and refusal by qualified professionals to participate in it is morally blameworthy. (5) By parity of reasoning, anyone with relevant skills who could be conscripted into that apparatus acquires a positive duty to cooperate, which transforms a limited exception into a generalized obligation of compelled service. The conclusion—that we are required to maintain an ongoing torture infrastructure with broad conscription—seems absurd, yet each premise looks defensible. Identify the weakest step, explain why it initially seemed plausible, and state what giving it up costs the broader emergency-ethics position. Give your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
