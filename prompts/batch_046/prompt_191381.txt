After a three-hour argument with a friend about whether Grice's cooperative principle is merely descriptive armchair psychology or can be turned into an actual enforcement mechanism, I need your help operationalizing it. I serve on a disciplinary panel for a professional scientific association that adjudicates complaints about whether members made misleading public statements—press releases, consent-form language, or testimony—by exploiting the gap between what was said and what was implicated. The friend insists that speaker meaning is too context-sensitive to be proceduralized; I think the machinery Grice actually offers—calculability, cancellation, non-detachability, and the distinction between particularized and generalized implicature—can be turned into a reusable checklist for non-philosopher panelists, provided we are honest about where it breaks.

Please write a decision procedure my panel can run in under thirty minutes per case, with members who have no philosophy background. The procedure should be ordered as a sequence of tests: first, isolate the semantic content of the utterance (what is said) from any implicated content; second, determine whether the cooperative principle applies in this institutional context or is suspended (e.g., adversarial cross-examination, marketing, or regulatory compliance); third, apply the calculability test using the maxims of Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner to see whether a reasonable audience could recover the implicature; fourth, check for explicit or implicit cancellation and for non-detachability; fifth, classify the implicature as generalized or particularized; sixth, determine whether the speaker's intended meaning diverges from the recoverable audience meaning in a way that violates our association's norm of good-faith communication. At each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure yields a definite finding of no violation or of violation, and where it must hand the decision to a human judgment about values rather than facts.

Include a small set of concrete test sentences the panel can apply—covering understatement, euphemism, and strategic ambiguity—and flag the cases the procedure cannot settle, especially where institutional norms conflict with conversational norms.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases: (1) a researcher who states in a press release that "the study found no significant effect of the compound on memory," knowing the study was underpowered and that lay readers will take this as evidence of absence rather than absence of evidence; (2) a tenure-letter writer who calls a rival's work "interesting" in an explicitly adversarial evaluation context where the cooperative principle is arguably suspended; and (3) a clinical-trial consent form that describes a painful side effect as "mild discomfort," relying on what may be a conventional medical euphemism. The second case should produce an answer—likely that no conversational violation occurred—that I will not like, and you should explain what the procedure can and cannot do about that discomfort. Conclude by stating the limits of the procedure: what kinds of disputes it will misfire on, which distinctions must survive the simplification and which can be dropped without betrayal, and what remains genuinely a matter of institutional values rather than Gricean mechanics. The deliverable must be reusable, not tailored to a single dispute, and should specify who signs off when the procedure reaches its judgment boundary.
