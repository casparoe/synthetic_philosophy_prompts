A recent philosophy podcast summarized Hume's account of taste as follows. Hume holds that beauty is a property that inheres in objects, much like shape or color, but our access to it is mediated by sentiment rather than by direct sensory detection. Disagreement arises not because beauty is subjective, but because most observers suffer from defective faculties—poor education, prejudice, or lack of comparative exposure. The remedy is the ideal critic, whose refined sentiments function like a calibration procedure in psychophysics: by aggregating the responses of trained observers under standardized conditions, we fix an inter-subjective norm that tracks the objective aesthetic property. Consequently, Hume is best understood as a response-dependent realist: the property is real and mind-independent in its existence, yet necessarily accessed through a particular kind of mental state. The podcast concluded that this makes Hume's view unstable, because if beauty is genuinely in the object, it should not require a calibrated observer to be present, and if it requires the observer, then calling it mind-independent is contradictory.
