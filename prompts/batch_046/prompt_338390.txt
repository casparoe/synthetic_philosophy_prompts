I am grading the final exam for our graduate seminar on moral agency and the structure of ethical theory, and I need a careful, itemized assessment of one student’s response—no general impression, please. The exam asked the following:

Bernard Williams, in Problems of the Self, advances two well-known criticisms of utilitarianism: the integrity objection (illustrated by Jim and the Indians) and the “one thought too many” objection (illustrated by the husband who saves his wife from drowning). First, explain the distinction between these two objections, specifying the case that illustrates each and the structural feature of utilitarian reasoning that each targets—particularly the role of “ground projects” in the integrity objection and the nature of the reason that is “one too many” in the second case. Second, evaluate the claim—advanced in some recent commentary—that both objections are ultimately expressions of a single concern about the “transparency” of moral justification, i.e., that the agent must be able to treat her own attachments as direct, non-derivative sources of reasons without filtering them through an impersonal calculus. Is this unification defensible? In answering, distinguish carefully between a problem in the content of the moral theory and a problem in the psychology of the agent who adopts it.

The student wrote:

“The integrity objection is shown in Jim and the Indians: Jim can kill one Indian and save nineteen, but if he does so because it maximizes welfare, he violates his integrity by making his own life a vehicle for utility. His ground projects—such as not being a murderer—are sacrificed. The “one thought too many” objection is shown when a husband saves his wife from drowning but thinks “it is permissible for me to save her because she is my wife.” Here, too, a ground project—his attachment to her—is overridden by an impersonal justification, so the structure is the same: both cases show that utilitarianism forces the agent to subordinate personal attachments to an external calculus.

Some commentators try to unify the two by saying both are about transparency: the agent must be able to see her reasons without moral mediation. I think this is correct, but it misses that Williams is really making Stocker’s point about moral schizophrenia—the agent is split between her motives and her moral reasons. In the husband case, the problem is not that the attachment is sacrificed, since he does save her, but that he needs a moral license to act on it. This is indeed a transparency failure. However, Williams’s deeper concern is psychological: the agent who always filters her attachments through utility loses the ability to identify with her actions. Therefore, both objections are best understood as variants of the schizophrenia critique, showing that utilitarianism alienates the agent from her ground projects by requiring normative transparency at the expense of first-personal motivation.”
