I hold that collective moral life carries an active duty to remember past wrongs that exceeds mere archival preservation. Remembrance must take the form of living transmission—continuously performed through present social practices that structurally preserve victims' perspectives—rather than static commemoration such as monuments, museums, or one-time apologies. On this view, institutional practices of deliberate forgetting or mnemonic closure, which treat historical trauma as settled and available for political reconciliation, are not mere omissions but active moral harms: they sever the epistemic and affective links that sustain present accountability. I also take arguments for collective forgetting—appeals to psychological healing, social cohesion, or the political utility of moving on—to confuse individual relief with collective moral structure, and therefore to miss the point of the duty.
