Write a proposal for a one-semester independent study (deliverable: approximately 5,000 words) on the conceptual structure of Johannes Kepler’s astrological reasoning. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation apparatus; refer to texts and positions only by general description, and assume execution from general familiarity with Kepler’s astronomical and astrological writings rather than from specialized archival access.

Structure the response in exactly these sections, in order, with the approximate lengths indicated:

1. The precise question (c. 200 words): Formulate a determinately answerable question about whether Kepler’s astrological judgments relied on a mechanistic-causal model continuous with his celestial physics—implying physical transmission or influence—or on a distinct semiotic or correlational logic that required no such physical mechanism. State the two competing interpretations clearly, and specify what conceptual distinction or pattern of reasoning would decide between them.

2. Why standard accounts leave it open (c. 250 words): Explain how the familiar framing—Kepler as an astronomer who practiced astrology only for income, or as a mystical thinker whose astrology was a pre-scientific residue—fails to engage the internal form of his arguments. Identify the specific ambiguity in his reasoning that allows both the mechanistic and the semiotic readings to seem textually supported.

3. Approach and method (c. 300 words): Describe the analytical steps you would take: comparing the structure of explanatory claims in his astrological practice with those in his physical astronomy, isolating whether predictions depend on causal narratives or on symbolic correspondence, and determining whether the two domains share a unified logic of justification. Justify why a conceptual-analysis approach makes progress where biographical or reception-history approaches stall. State explicitly what material you will treat as primary and what you will bracket to keep the project tractable.

4. Possible conclusions and failure conditions (c. 200 words): Say what it would mean if the reasoning patterns turned out to be inseparable, or if one clearly dominated across the texts you examine. Define failure precisely—for instance, if the distinction collapses into a mere difference in vocabulary with no methodological consequence, or if the texts provide no consistent pattern at all.

5. Scope and risk assessment (c. 150 words): Give an honest appraisal of what could make the project too large for one semester, and specify the boundary you will enforce (for example, limiting analysis to a defined class of predictions, to specific genres of writing, or to a single comparative dimension). Explain why that boundary preserves the core question rather than evading it.
