I have recently become convinced that a serious error in my own professional judgment—specifically, the sustained misinterpretation of ambiguous data in a long-running project—was not adequately explained by ordinary confirmation bias, but rather by a structured form of self-deception in which one allocates attentional weights strategically without positing a literal second agent inside the mind. Next month I will lead a single ninety-minute session for advanced undergraduates in philosophy and cognitive science, none of whom have read beyond a popular essay on cognitive dissonance. I do not want an explanation of self-deception delivered to me; I want a teaching plan for how to make the question “is self-deception even possible?” genuinely puzzling to them.

Plan the explanatory arc: first, the conceptual dependency that must be in place before the paradox can even appear—distinguishing intentional interpersonal deception from mere motivated reasoning, and clarifying what it means for a single mind to bear contradictory attitudes without literal partitioning; second, the one concrete scenario that should carry the entire discussion, specifying exactly what it must include (a subject who acts on evidence she has not fully acknowledged, in a domain where the cost of acknowledgment is specific and personal) and what it must exclude (do not use the overused smoker-denying-cancer-risk case, and do not use split-brain or purely fictional homuncular examples); third, the misconceptions these students will form in predictable sequence—I expect they will start with “self-deception is just lying to yourself,” then slide into “it requires two agents inside one head,” and finally settle on “it is impossible because one cannot simultaneously believe p and not-p”—and the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted and redirected; fourth, one diagnostic question I can ask at the end that reveals whether they have understood the possibility conditions rather than merely memorized a taxonomy of named positions; and finally, what should be explicitly refused at this level—perhaps formal belief-revision models, clinical taxonomies of denial, or specific philosophical attributions—and how I should state that refusal openly so the boundaries of the session are clear.

The session should treat self-deception as an open conceptual problem rather than a settled psychological category, building toward the idea that its possibility hinges on resolving the tension between doxastic transparency and strategic ignorance, and it should proceed without requiring the students to recognize any particular philosopher’s name.
