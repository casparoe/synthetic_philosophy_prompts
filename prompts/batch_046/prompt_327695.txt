I'm putting together a unit for my grad seminar on antinatalism and Benatar's asymmetry, and I was chatting with a colleague over coffee about this institutional double-standard that keeps bugging me. Adoption agencies run exhaustive screening—psych evals, home inspections, financial audits—to make sure a child who doesn't yet exist won't be harmed. But the exact same couple can conceive naturally with zero oversight, even if their risk profile is identicle. I want my students to think about what moral ontology this practice actually presupposes.

Does it quietly assume Benatar's asymmetry—that preventing suffering in a potential life is obligatory, but failing to create a happy life is not a harm—so we only police the 'entry' into existence when there's a gatekeeper? Or does it presuppose that biological reproduction is a private right while adoption is a state-licensed privilege, and if so, is that distinction doing any real philosophical work? Reconstruct the implicit theory of harm, non-existence, and moral status. Say whether the practice is actually consistent with antinatalist committments—if existence is always a harm, shouldn't we screen biological parents even more aggressively?—and tell me what adoption law and reproductive policy would have to change if we replaced the implicit view with a straightforward asymmetry or with a symmetric view where both benefits and harms to potential people count.
