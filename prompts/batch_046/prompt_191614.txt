As a novelist who constructs predictive models of character behavior to anticipate how readers will respond, I have come to hold what I call the insulation thesis: a strong reading of Friedman’s F-twist according to which the descriptive falsity of a model’s core premises is not a tolerable cost but an active design feature, because maximally unrealistic assumptions—premises not merely simplified but actively false about human psychology—insulate the predictive mechanism from descriptive noise and ad hoc adjustment. I apply this beyond economics to any domain where we model intentional agents. What would genuinely count against this view? I am not asking for the familiar objection that false premises preclude genuine explanation; I want the specific defeaters—whether formal results about robustness, comparative cases in which descriptively richer models systematically outperformed sparser false ones, or conceptual arguments showing that insulation eventually collapses the model into unfalsifiability. For each, say whether a committed insulation theorist would concede it as damaging or dismiss it as missing the instrumental point, and rank them from fatal to merely awkward. In particular, has the thesis been qualified so heavily that nothing could in principle dislodge it, and if so, is that insulation a virtue or a vice for a view of this kind? Keep your answer in a single paragraph of no more than three hundred words.
