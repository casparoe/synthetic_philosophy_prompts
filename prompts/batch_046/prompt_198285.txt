As a novelist constructing the central philosophical episode of a literary novel, I require a short narrative—approximately one thousand words—that stages the tension between disjunctivism and the argument from illusion without naming either doctrine. The protagonist should be a conservator of historical glass who, while examining a medieval mirror in a dim archive, perceives what appears to be a direct visual acquaintance with a figure behind her. When she shifts her angle, the figure vanishes, yet the phenomenal quality of the initial experience—its luminosity, spatial presence, and emotional weight—remains indistinguishable from a genuine perception. The narrative must move through three phases: first, the unreflective perceptual judgment that she is seeing another person; second, the discovery that the mirror's curvature produces an identical phenomenal state in the absence of any corresponding object; third, her internal deliberation about whether consciousness ever puts her in direct contact with reality, or whether it always presents a mere appearance that could be duplicated without the object being present. Do not resolve the ambiguity; end with her decision to record the experience in her journal, leaving the ontological status of her perception suspended. Use precise, restrained prose; avoid both technical philosophical vocabulary and conventional supernatural tropes. The story should make the reader feel the exact force of the argument from illusion while simultaneously leaving open the disjunctivist claim that veridical and illusory states differ in kind despite their phenomenal similarity.
