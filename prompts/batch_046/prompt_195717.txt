Write a dialogue between Dr. Elena Voss, a philosopher of social ontology who defends generational realism, and Dr. Marcus Chen, a critical demographer who denies that labels such as Boomers, Millennials, and Gen Z denote anything beyond statistical artifacts. The setting is a post-colloquium seminar on the ontology of social groups.

Both interlocutors must be equally competent; neither may be a strawman. The exchange should feature genuine back-and-forth, with each speaker responding directly to the specific claim just advanced rather than advancing a pre-scripted monologue.

The dialogue must address the following points in turn:

1. The distinction between a statistical cohort (a birth-interval aggregate) and a Mannheim-style generational unit—defined not by shared birth years alone but by shared formative historical experiences that produce durable cognitive-affective dispositions.

2. The structural-replacement argument—sometimes referred to as demographic metabolism—according to which the rate at which cohorts move through institutional positions (labor markets, legislatures, cultural gatekeeping roles) can generate causal effects that are not reducible to age or period effects, even if no essential shared trait unites the cohort members.

3. A hypothetical institutional-design thought experiment: a state randomly assigns decennial generational names to birth brackets and enforces them through standardized curricula and segmented labor-market entry tracks. Voss should argue that, under sustained institutional embedding, these initially arbitrary categories would eventually acquire predictive and causal properties analogous to those claimed for "natural" generations; Chen should resist this by showing that any observed outcomes remain reducible to age, period, and path-dependent institutional design, with no residual ontological weight for the generational label itself.

4. The interpretation of residual cohort variance in statistical models: whether a cohort coefficient that persists after controlling for age and period is best read as evidence of a real historical kind, or as an artifact of model misspecification, unobserved heterogeneity, and the non-linear interaction of life-course position with macro-historical change.

The dialogue should conclude without a declared victor, but with the central ontological disagreement—whether generational categories name real causal structures or are retrofitted fictions—sharply clarified. Avoid bibliographic references, reading lists, or citations to specific empirical studies; the argument should proceed by conceptual analysis and by appeal to the hypothetical scenario.
