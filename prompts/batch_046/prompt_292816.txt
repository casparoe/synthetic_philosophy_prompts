Here are notes for a short essay arguing that a substantial class of normative and explanatory reasons can operate without being anyone’s reasons—what I call “free-floating rationales.” The core claim is that when an institution, algorithm, or social system produces an outcome that is systematically responsive to certain considerations (efficiency, risk, distributive balance), those considerations can function as reasons for the outcome even when no particular agent has entertained them, endorsed them, or is even capable of doing so. I distinguish three varieties: (1) distributed rationales, spread across many agents none of whom grasps the whole pattern; (2) embedded rationales, encoded in rules or code that respond to considerations the original designers did not explicitly represent; and (3) emergent rationales, arising from system dynamics that track normative or explanatory considerations—such as market prices tracking relative scarcity—without anyone representing them as reasons. I then ask whether this collapses into a functionalist theory of reasons, on which a reason is just whatever plays the reason-role in a system, or whether it requires a genuinely non-mental ontology. I also worry that the argument conflates causal explanation with normative justification: the fact that a system reliably tracks efficiency may be an explanation of its behavior, not a reason that justifies it to anyone.

Please comment on these notes. Specifically: (a) Is the tripartite distinction coherent, or do the categories overlap in ways that undermine it? (b) Does the move from algorithmic or institutional responsiveness to “free-floating rationales” commit a category error—treating functional tracking as reason-giving? (c) Could a committed mentalist about reasons accommodate all three varieties by appealing to implicit beliefs, collective intentionality, or design intentions, and if so, what stronger argument is needed to show those accommodations fail? (d) Is there a meaningful difference between saying reasons are “free-floating” and saying they are “dispositional properties of systems,” and does the essay obscure that distinction? Suggest revisions that sharpen the central claim without requiring empirical case studies or appeals to specific technical literature.
