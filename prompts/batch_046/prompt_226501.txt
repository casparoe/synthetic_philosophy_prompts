After a protracted argument with a friend over whether moral laws could have been different had God created another world, I tried to articulate Leibniz's view that ethical truths are eternal and rational rather than products of divine command. My friend maintained that Leibniz's doctrine of the best possible world collapses into Cartesian voluntarism: since God freely selects which world to actualize, and since nothing outside God constrains that selection, the criteria of perfection and harmony that distinguish the best world must themselves be contingent on God's will. I responded that Leibniz explicitly rejects the claim that eternal truths depend on divine decree, insisting instead that God recognizes necessary relations among essences and chooses the world that maximizes them; yet my friend argued that this recognition does not eliminate the dependency, because the essences themselves exist only as objects of divine understanding. I am now caught between thinking my friend has smuggled in a false presupposition about the direction of metaphysical dependence in Leibniz's system, and worrying that I have underestimated the voluntarist elements in his theology. Could you help me identify exactly where the confusion lies—specifically, whether Leibniz's commitment to the principle of sufficient reason and to the objectivity of perfection allows him to maintain that moral necessity is grounded in the nature of things rather than in arbitrary will, and whether there is a coherent reading on which God's freedom to choose the best world is compatible with the absolute necessity of the moral laws that govern it?
