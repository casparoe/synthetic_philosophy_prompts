Consider the following conceptually precise but hypothetical scenario. A synthetic microbiome is engineered to degrade an industrial polymer. Within it, a gene module was inserted horizontally from a distantly related taxon and has never undergone natural selection in this host lineage; nevertheless, it stably produces a depolymerase enzyme that catalyzes breakdown of the polymer. Separately, an artificial feedback circuit—designed by bioengineers rather than evolved—monitors and corrects cytosolic pH to protect the host cell from metabolic stress. No evolutionary selection history in this organism explains either feature.

Your task is to rate, on a scale of 1 (very poor fit) to 10 (excellent fit), how well each of the following three accounts applies to attributing a proper biological function to (i) the horizontally transferred depolymerase module and (ii) the engineered pH-feedback circuit.

Account A—Etiological: a trait has function F if and only if F is the effect for which traits of that kind were positively selected in the evolutionary history of the relevant lineage.

Account B—Causal-role: a trait has function F if and only if F is the contribution the trait makes to a specific capacity of the containing system, where that capacity is identified within an explanatory framework that treats it as the system's goal or target state.

Account C—Hybrid design-plus-role: a trait has function F if and only if (1) F is a stable causal contribution to a system-level capacity, and (2) the trait's presence is explained either by natural selection for producing F or by intentional design specifically aimed at producing F.

Please present your ratings in a clear table or list, then provide a concise adjudication: which account scores highest overall across both cases, where each account fails or succeeds, and whether honoring past selection or design origin should be treated as necessary, sufficient, or merely supportive for functional ascription in synthetic contexts.
