I am preparing a contribution for a symposium on philosophy of language, and I would appreciate your critical assessment of the argument sketched below. The essay extends Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation—famously introduced through the gavagai scenario—to the interpretation of outputs produced by large language models. My claim is that when a user assigns semantic content to a generated token sequence, the available behavioral and distributional evidence underdetermines whether the model's internal representations correspond to rabbit, rabbit-stage, or undetached rabbit part, not merely epistemically but ontologically, in precisely Quine's holistic sense. I distinguish this position from the familiar stochastic-parrot criticism, arguing that the latter illicitly assumes a determinacy of reference that Quine's argument undermines. I further propose that the attention patterns in transformer architectures function analogously to Quine's analytical hypotheses: they constrain interpretation without uniquely fixing reference, though I worry this comparison may conflate architectural mechanics with epistemological methodology. Could you comment specifically on three points? First, is the extension of radical translation to artificial token sequences a legitimate philosophical maneuver, or does it depend on attributing a kind of linguistic competence to models that Quine's argument requires? Second, have I adequately separated ontological indeterminacy from the predictive uncertainty inherent in probabilistic generation? Third, does the analogy between attention mechanisms and analytical hypotheses illuminate the issue, or does it risk reducing a semantic thesis to an engineering observation? The intended audience is philosophers of language with limited technical background in machine learning, so precision in the conceptual framing matters more than technical depth, though I want to avoid misrepresenting either Quine's view or the relevant architecture.
