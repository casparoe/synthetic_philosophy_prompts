I'm designing the final examination for my upper-level seminar on Conservative Political Thought and Institutional Epistemology, and I need one essay prompt that students cannot answer by reciting standard summaries of Edmund Burke's critique of rationalist constitutional design and G.K. Chesterton's fence parable. To force engagement with the actual conceptual machinery, the question should be anchored in a precisely drawn hypothetical scenario: a national legislature proposes the Rational Allocation and Sunset Reform, which would dissolve customary arbitration boards that have managed inter-village water-rights disputes since the late nineteenth century through uncodified precedent, replacing them with a centralized algorithmic allocation system that distributes water according to explicit, transparent utility-maximizing criteria. The boards' internal reasoning is only partially documented, reform advocates argue that tradition obscures trade-offs and embeds arbitrary bias, and defenders claim that the opacity encodes generations of distributed local ecological knowledge that no formal dataset fully captures.

The exam question should ask students to evaluate this proposal through both Burkean and Chestertonian lenses, but with a twist that pushes past memorized labels: they must determine whether Chesterton's fence operates primarily as an individual epistemic heuristic against hasty removal or as a claim about collective, distributed institutional knowledge, and they must assess whether Burke's skepticism toward abstract constitutional redesign extends with equal force to algorithmic governance systems that present themselves as fully transparent and complete. Crucially, top answers must identify where the two frameworks might come apart—specifically, whether the arbitration boards' opacity is an epistemic feature to be preserved because its function exceeds explicit documentation (Burke) or a failure to understand the fence's purpose that should be corrected (Chesterton)—and must evaluate whether the algorithm's transparency constitutes a genuine epistemic improvement or a dangerous illusion of completeness that severs prescription from tacit practice.

Please provide: (1) the exam question itself, suitable for a 90-minute essay of approximately 800 to 1000 words; (2) a model answer of roughly 600 to 750 words; and (3) a marking scheme out of 25 points that distinguishes an A-range response (22–25) from a B-range response (16–19) by listing the specific conceptual moves each tier must contain. The scheme should explicitly reward students who interpret the fence as a warning about unknown functional dependencies rather than as blanket traditionalism, who locate Burke's argument about prescription and tacit institutional knowledge rather than mere risk aversion, who apply both frameworks to the algorithmic case without collapsing them into a generic anti-change stance, and who identify at least one genuine tension between the frameworks rather than harmonizing them too easily. Include brief notes on common errors—such as treating algorithmic transparency as automatically epistemically superior, or conflating Burke's institutional skepticism with Chesterton's individual-level heuristic.
