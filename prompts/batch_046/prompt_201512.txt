I am preparing the closing material for an advanced philosophy seminar on normativity and ontology, and I wish to read aloud a short poem that takes seriously the notion of free-floating rationales — normative considerations that obtain even in the absence of any reasoner to grasp, entertain, or be moved by them. The piece must be conceptually precise rather than merely atmospheric: a specialist in metaethics or the philosophy of reasons should recognise the exact tension between anchored and unanchored normativity being staged, while a listener without that background should still find the verse coherent and compelling.

Please compose a poem of exactly twenty lines, arranged in four quintains, in unrhymed iambic pentameter. The formal constraints must perform philosophical work. Every line ought to state a normative claim — what counts in favour of something, what ought to obtain, or what justifies — yet the poem must contain no first-person pronoun, no second-person address, and no named grammatical subject who reasons or deliberates. In this way the syntax itself enacts the absence of the reasoner. Place the volta at the close of the second quintain: the first half should present reasons that appear tied to conscious agency (promissory obligations, logical entailments, deliberate commitments), rendered in measured, end-stopped lines; the second half should shift to reasons that seem to persist without anyone to hold them (evolutionary equilibria, structural incentives, the normative pull of possibilities never instantiated), using enjambment to carry claims across line breaks so that the rationales appear grammatically unmoored. The absence of rhyme should heighten the sense that nothing anchors these considerations except their own internal structure.

Do not resolve the tension. The final quintain should leave ambiguous whether these free-floating rationales are genuinely normative or merely explanatory patterns misdescribed as reasons. Avoid all bibliographic references, citations, named philosophers, and verbatim quotations from existing texts; the language should feel like an original contribution to the discussion rather than a summary of familiar literature. Use British spelling throughout, and ensure the diction is polished, formal, and suitable for an academic audience.
