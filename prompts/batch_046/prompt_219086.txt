I'm drafting the take-home final for my upper-level seminar on intergenerational ethics, and I need one essay question on the nonidentity problem that students can't answer by summarizing the classic 14-year-old case. The prompt should present a collective policy scenario: a present government chooses to lock in carbon-intensive infrastructure, knowing that over the next century climate disruption will alter migration patterns and economic conditions so severely that an entirely different set of people will be conceived and born in 2100, and knowing further that these future people will have lives that are barely worth living rather than never existing at all.

Please write the exam question—answerable in about 60 minutes, roughly 800 words—that asks students to do three things: (a) reconstruct exactly why this creates a nonidentity problem for any claim that the policy wrongs particular future individuals; (b) evaluate whether an impersonal harm principle, according to which we should minimize total suffering regardless of whose suffering it is, genuinely escapes the problem or merely relocates the moral tension; and (c) assess a proposed "relational corruption" objection, which holds that the wrong lies not in harming identifiable people but in degrading the moral relation between present choosers and the future by treating future lives as interchangeable inputs to an aggregate calculation.

Along with the question, provide a model answer of about 800 words and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points totaling 20 marks, each with a one-line note on how candidates typically miss it. The difficulty should turn on seeing precisely where the impersonal principle shifts the burden rather than dissolving it, and on recognizing that the relational objection requires its own defense against the charge that it substitutes sentiment for moral argument. Do not ask for bibliographies or specific citations.
