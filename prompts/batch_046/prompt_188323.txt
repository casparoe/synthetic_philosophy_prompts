Popular neuroscience and philosophy of mind often treat the split-brain patient—someone whose corpus callosum has been severed—as being, in effect, two persons housed in a single body: the verbal left hemisphere and the mute but perceptive right hemisphere, unable to share information and sometimes issuing conflicting commands. This analogy is not merely descriptive; it is used to license the stronger claim that even in intact brains, consciousness is not a genuinely unified field but rather a coalition of semi-independent modules that only appear integrated because the callosal bridge suppresses disagreement. Before accepting that inference, audit the analogy itself. Lay out the explicit mapping: what corresponds to a person, to a shared body, to communication or its absence, to conflict resolution, and to the boundary conditions that would distinguish one mind from two? Then identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended philosophical conclusion: for example, whether split-brain behavior involves anything like interpersonal negotiation, moral accountability, or autobiographical memory that is genuinely split rather than merely inaccessible to verbal report; whether cross-cueing and subcortical integration undermine the claim of complete separation; and whether describing hemispheres as agents imports intentional language that obscures mechanistic specialization. Assess whether the conclusion—that ordinary unity is an illusion maintained only by neural plumbing—survives once these breaks in the mapping are made explicit, and suggest whether a different analogy (perhaps a federal state, or a bilingual translator working with incomplete dictionaries) does less misleading work.
