Two friends of mine, Maya and Riley, got into it over beers about illusionism about phenomenal conciousness and I need an outside take because I'm stuck.

Maya opened by saying illusionism is self-refuting. If phenomenal consciousness is just an illusion, she argued, then there has to be something it's like to be under that illusion -- otherwise there's no illusion of consciousness happening at all, just nothing. So illusionism presupposes exactly what it tries to deny.

Riley said thats smuggling in a hidden Cartesian owner. The illusion isn't happening to some separate experiencer; it's just a representational error in the brain's self-model. The brain misrepresents its own processing as having intrinsic qualia, but there's no audience required beyond the system itself. What it's like is just the self-model running, not a separate phenomenal fact.

Maya then brought up the thermostat problem. A thermostat can misrepresent temperature but it doesn't feel like anything, so saying the brain misrepresents doesn't explain why this particular error comes with a specific seeming. If illusionism is right, why does this misrepresentation feel like something?

Riley replied that brains aren't thermostats -- they have transparent self-modeling loops. She mentioned phantom limb pain: there's no limb, but the representation of pain is real as a representational state with causal power. Normal consciousness is similar, except the brain adds a this-is-happening-now-to-me flag. The phenomenal character is just that flag being activated, not an extra property hanging out there.

Maya pushed back with a comparison: in phantom limb cases patients report feeling pain, and if illusionism holds then in normal cases we're also just reporting representations. But then illusionism can't distinguish real pain from phantom pain at the phenomenal level -- they should feel identical. And we do think they're different in a way that isn't just about causation or peripheral input.

Riley said that's precisely illusionism's claim -- there's no phenomenal difference, only representational and causal ones. The brain tags phantom pain differently because it lacks sensory input, but both involve the same kind of representational tagging. Even the feeling of this-is-real is just another layer of representation.

Maya then asked about transparency. If everything is representation, why do our experiences seem to present themselves directly instead of as representations? It doesn't feel like holding beliefs about experience; it feels like direct contact. Riley answered that presentational quality is just the representational format being transparent -- like seeing through glass. The brain's self-model is transparent, so it seems like direct access but is actually mediated representation.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? Tell me who's right on the self-refutation point, on the thermostat analogy, on the phantom limb comparison, and on the transparency reply. Also, what are they both missing -- maybe about whether illusionism actually needs to preserve a difference between real and phantom experience, or whether Maya's argument really proves there's an experiencer? Don't just split the difference; say who wins where and where the whole debate actually breaks down.
