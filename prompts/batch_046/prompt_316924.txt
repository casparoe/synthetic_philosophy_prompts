I have been thinking, without any formal training, about whether an ant colony can actually be a mind, especially the thought experiment involving a colony named Aunt Hillary whose signals form something like a fugue—interweaving and feeding back on themselves. Rather than asking students to retell that story, I want a rigorous exam question that forces them to wrestle with the underlying problem. Please draft a 40-minute essay question for an upper-level philosophy-of-mind seminar, together with a model answer and a marking scheme totaling 20 marks. The exam prompt should present a specific claim I have invented: that if every ant behaves solely according to local pheromone gradients that carry no representational content, then even highly complex collective tracking of the environment cannot amount to intentionality, because aboutness cannot be genuinely distributed across nodes that individually possess none. Require the student to evaluate this claim, specifically asking them to distinguish collective information-processing from collective mentality, to examine whether emergent aboutness is coherent, and to reach a defended verdict. In the model answer, identify five essential moves—clarifying the gradient-tracking premise, separating derived from original intentionality, assessing emergent aboutness, addressing analogies with simple brains, and delivering a reasoned conclusion—assign marks to each, and for each point include a one-line note on the typical mistake that should cost marks.
