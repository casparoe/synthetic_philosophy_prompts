I am a doctoral student in epistemology and philosophy of language, and I have been following the debate over norms of assertion since the knowledge-norm proposal gained prominence. For over two decades, defenders of a knowledge norm, defenders of a truth norm, and defenders of a belief norm have traded the same family of counterexamples—the lottery case, the false-but-justified-belief case, the expert-with-luck case—without apparent movement. Each side absorbs the pressure by reclassifying the utterance: knowledge-norm defenders treat problematic cases as hedged, non-assertoric, or conversationally defective; belief-norm and truth-norm defenders treat accommodating cases as bare assertions that lack the mental state or accuracy the knowledge norm requires. The cycle feels stalled: no new empirical study has settled which reclassification is correct, and no formal result in pragmatics or epistemic logic has been accepted by all camps.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not a single substantive disagreement but at least three layered ones: (1) a verbal dispute about whether “assertion” denotes a speech-act type with uniform conditions or a family of utterances whose normative profile shifts with institutional context; (2) a hidden substantive divergence about whether the norm regulates the speaker’s internal mental state, the conversational effect on the audience, or the distribution of epistemic risk in a group; and (3) a methodological disagreement about whether the correct theory must preserve every intuitive judgment of assertibility or only those made in “pure” contexts stripped of social and professional risk. To make this concrete, consider a research group in which a principal investigator asserts a finding based on a postdoc’s computation she has not personally verified. She might say, “The data suggest X,” or “We have shown X.” Does the knowledge norm require her to withhold the bare form, and does inserting “suggest” change the speech act or merely decorate it? Defenders and critics seem to answer differently not because they disagree about her mental state, but because they are asking different questions—one about authorization, one about conversational entitlement, one about institutional liability—under the single heading “norm of assertion.”

Please diagnose this impasse. Is the disagreement primarily verbal, substantive, or methodological? Do the sides share the same target—regulating one speech act—or are they answering different questions (semantic, ethical, sociolinguistic) under one name?
