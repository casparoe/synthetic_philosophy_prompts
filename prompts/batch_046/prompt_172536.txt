I'm putting together a proposal for a one-semester philosophy essay—probably around 5,000 words—on a tight corner of anthropic reasoning that I think gets glossed over. Everyone knows the self-sampling versus self-indication split for Sleeping Beauty-type cases, and everyone knows the presumptuous philosopher worry: that picking a reference class of billions of observers feels epistemically arrogant, like assuming our cosmological theory is special just because we happen to be in it. But I haven’t seen anyone ask whether that arrogance charge lands with the same force on both assumptions, or whether it only sticks to one of them once you think about how the observer’s own reasoning gets counted inside the sample space.

My specific angle is a bit niche: instead of defining the reference class by biological species or by 'all conscious minds', I want to try defining it by 'all token instances of this excat reasoning procedure being applied to the same cosmological data'. Under self-sampling, that seems to push us into treating our own cognitive act as one random draw among countless identical draws, which looks like the presumptuous philosopher problem in spades. Under self-indication, the weighting by actual existence might either dissolve the arrogance or amplify it, depending on whether you think existence-weighting is a legitimate methodological move or just a cheat that smuggles in the conclusion. I want to trace the asymmetry precisely, say what it takes for either assumption to escape the charge, and ask whether escaping it actually gives us any real epistemic gain or just shifts the problem elsewhere, noting carefully where the asymmetry occured.
