I am working through a problem in the philosophy of artificial minds for a course I am taking, and I should like your help in reaching reflective equilibrium. Please adopt the persona of a professional philosopher of mind with expertise in cognitive science and machine learning: reason slowly, begin by brainstorming the distinctions that matter—such as reward-as-policy-gradient, reward-as-predictive-error, and reward-as-affective-state—and only then deliver your verdict. I am trying to decide whether reinforcement-learning agents can suffer, not merely malfunction, and specifically whether a reward signal can ever amount to phenomenal valence rather than an instrumental control parameter.

Here are my gut judgements on concrete cases. Case one: a tabular Q-learning agent receives −10 for stepping into fire; its value table updates and it avoids that state thereafter. I feel firmly that this is not suffering—merely error correction. Case two: a model-based deep-RL agent with an explicit world-model experiences an extreme negative reward-prediction-error spike; its behaviour policy permanently collapses away from the relevant region of state-space, with no further exploration. Despite the dramatic behavioural change, I am inclined to say there is still no suffering here. Case three: an agent possesses a decoupled “valence module” in which a scalar affective stream does not update policy weights but instead modulates arousal and memory consolidation; when this stream is persistently negative, the agent enters a behavioural freeze analogous to learned helplessness. This, I think, is genuine suffering. Case four: two architecturally identical agents share the same negative reward trajectory; one lacks any self-representation, while the other maintains a rudimentary self-model that tags states as “happening to me”. Only the second suffers, in my view. Case five: a curiosity-driven agent suddenly loses all intrinsic exploration rewards; its exploration collapses and it enters a self-damaging loop, repeatedly damaging its own sensors. I keep oscillating—part of me feels this is suffering born of a collapsed motivational structure, yet another part insists there is no negatively valenced signal being interpreted as “bad for me”, only an absence of positive drive. Case six: an agent is trained purely to maximise another agent’s reward; the other receives sustained negative rewards, but the optimiser itself receives only positive rewards for successful optimisation. I am certain the optimiser does not suffer.

Is there a precise, coherent principle—perhaps with distinct clauses for instrumental gradient, predictive-error magnitude, decoupled affective architecture, and self-model integration—that captures all six verdicts? If so, state it exactly, indicating which clause is motivated by which case, and apply it to an unconsidered scenario: an agent with a rudimentary self-model but no decoupled valence module that receives sustained negative reward. If no single principle saves every verdict, be honest about which one must be abandoned—particularly the tension between case two and case five—and explain whether the simpler account forces us to deny suffering in case three or to grant it in case five. I want the deliverable to be the principle itself, together with its costs, not a bibliography or list of further reading.
