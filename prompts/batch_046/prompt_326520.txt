As a software engineer without formal philosophy background, I've noticed a recurring argumentative pattern around complex emergent systems — for example, debates over whether large language models exhibit genuine reasoning. The pattern goes like this: someone demands a fully mechanistic, bottom-up explanation of the behavior. When given an intermediate account — say, an emergent representational structure or a feedback loop — the critic rejects it as an ungrounded placeholder that merely relocates the mystery rather than resolving it. But if you then trace everything down to the lowest-level physical or algorithmic implementation, the same critic objects that you've destroyed the very phenomenon you were trying to explain, reducing a meaningful pattern to noise. So each explanatory layer is treated as illegible: intermediate structures are dismissed as mysterious scaffolding, while foundational accounts are dismissed as erasing the pattern. I see analogues in biology, where adaptive explanations are attacked as untestable narratives, yet purely genetic accounts are attacked for missing organizational logic. How is it different from standard disagreements about levels of analysis, or from the view that some reductionist explanations are "greedy" in that they discard structurally important intermediate layers? If no precise term exists, can you propose one with clear boundary conditions — distinguishing it from cases where people simply lack any intermediate theory?
