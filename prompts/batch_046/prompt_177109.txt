I came across a summary of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument in a philosophy forum thread, and I'm trying to audit it before I cite it anywhere. The summary runs as follows. Strawson argues that true moral responsibility requires being causa sui—being the ultimate source of one's own mental nature. Since every aspect of one's character derives from prior causes (genetics, environment, prior choices), attempting to claim responsibility for one's own nature generates an infinite regress of prior conditions. The summary concludes that determinism is therefore true, that alternative possibilities are impossible, and that no one can ever deserve blame or praise in the basic sense. The poster further claims that Strawson is a hard incompatibilist, that the infinite regress is mainly an intuitive illustration rather than the argument's logical core, and that the real thrust is a modal point about self-authorship: in no possible world could an agent originate its own axiological framework ex nihilo.

This strikes me as mostly right in outline but possibly wrong in several details—especially the inference to determinism, and the characterization of the argument as modal rather than causal. Is this a fair summary? Please distinguish: (1) what accurately reflects Strawson's actual position, including his claim that the argument works whether determinism is true or false; (2) which parts are common simplifications or conflations (for instance, collapsing ultimate responsibility into moral responsibility simpliciter, or treating the regress as decorative); and (3) what Strawson explicitly avoids or denies—particularly any reliance on determinism, on the principle of alternative possibilities, or on modal metaphysics about possible worlds. Finally, does the poster's framing of the argument as fundamentally about modal self-authorship survive these corrections, or is it a misdirection?
