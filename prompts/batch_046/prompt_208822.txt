I serve as the ethics compliance officer for a national professional association of approximately four thousand members, and we are currently revising our procurement, travel, and governance policies to address a recurring problem: individual members or committees make choices—approving vendor contracts, booking conference travel, casting board votes, or selecting catering options—that are causally negligible in isolation, yet aggregate into substantial collective harms or democratic failures. I need a reusable decision procedure that my team can apply to any proposal within ten minutes, without requiring philosophical training, that translates the debate over the inefficacy objection and individual contribution to collective harm into an ordered checklist. The procedure should distinguish between linear-aggregation harms and threshold-governed harms as its first branching question; then ask whether the action crosses a context-specific efficacy boundary, defined in terms of whether the collective outcome is already overdetermined or whether the contribution is genuinely substitutable; then ask whether a coordination mechanism exists that the individual could join instead; and finally ask whether the action carries expressive or complicity value regardless of causal impact. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates with a recommendation to reject the proposal or abstain, and where it must escalate to a values-based judgment that requires human sign-off rather than procedural resolution. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls disguised as algorithmic checks.

Apply the procedure to three concrete proposals: first, renewing our cloud-hosting contract with a provider whose data centers are predominantly fossil-fuel-powered, where our association's usage constitutes roughly 0.003 percent of the provider's total energy load; second, instituting mandatory voting in board elections where historical turnout is twelve percent and the quorum is fifteen percent, meaning a single vote can technically break quorum but is highly unlikely to alter the election result; and third, adopting a conference catering policy that permits meat, given that our annual orders represent approximately eight hundred meals among millions served nationally by the same vendors. Include at least one case where the procedure produces an answer the association is likely to reject—perhaps because it permits participation in a harm that feels morally salient despite passing all procedural steps—and identify precisely which feature of the case drives that counterintuitive result. Additionally, flag how the procedure misfires or requires adaptation when applied to actions with no direct physical footprint but indirect computational demand, such as members routinely using large-scale AI inference services for research drafting, since the causal chain runs through energy markets rather than direct emission.

The deliverable should be presented as a flowchart in words—questions in fixed order, decision nodes, termination conditions, and escalation points—followed by the three worked applications. I am preparing this for colleagues divided between those who believe the inefficacy objection dissolves all individual obligations and those who believe it dissolves none; the procedure should be honest about both risks without collapsing into either position, and it should clearly mark where it stops being a procedure and becomes a policy choice that the board, not the compliance office, must own.
