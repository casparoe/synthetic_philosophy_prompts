I have been thinking about philosophical zombies—beings physically identical to conscious people but lacking any inner experience—and I have come to accept a restricted version of the conceivability argument. Rather than imagining a complete zombie, I focus on what I would call a "modal isolate": a being that matches me in every physical and functional respect except that one specific phenomenal quality, such as the experience of a particular shade of crimson, is replaced by an unconscious functional analog, while all my other conscious states remain intact. I can apparently conceive of such an isolate without contradiction, and from this I infer that that specific phenomenal quality is not metaphysically entailed by the underlying physical facts. Tell me what would count against this restricted claim—not only the familiar physicalist objections about the gap between conceivability and genuine possibility, but also whether there are results from the study of imagination, or from work on phenomenal concepts and their reference, that undermine the inference from "I can imagine the absence of crimson-experience" to "it is possible for that absence to obtain given these exact physical conditions." Which defeaters would a committed defender of my restricted argument concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they dismiss as missing the point? Please rank them from fatal to merely awkward. I am also unsure whether narrowing the argument to a single isolated phenomenal quality strengthens it or weakens it relative to the full-zombie version; explain how that choice affects its vulnerability to the defeaters you describe.
