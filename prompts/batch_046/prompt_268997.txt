I've been arguing with my roommate about animal ethics — he's in a neuroscience course and keeps insisting that brain size is totally irrelevant to morality, yet he also thinks we should ban octopus farming because they have way too many neurons packed into a small body. That contradiction got me thinking about how actual research ethics boards handle this in practice. Most IACUC protocols use tiered review levels where the required justification for invasive procedures scales with something like encephalization quotient, or in some newer frameworks even raw cortical neuron count. So macaques get heavy scrutiny, rats get moderate, and frogs are basically administrative rubber-stamp territory. But here's what confuses me: those same boards never apply any biological brain metric to human subjects. If someone has severe microcephaly or advanced dementia, you don't downgrade thier protection based on brain volume or neuron count; instead the framework switches completely to consent, autonomy, and best-interest standards. So what philosophical view of moral status does that institutional split actually presuppose? Does the animal tiering commit the board to a capacity-based account where neuron density tracks the ability to suffer or have self-awareness? Or could a rights-based view accommodate the same tiering by treating species membership as just a practical proxy? And is the human/animal divide consistent — does it presuppose that moral status is binary for humans but scalar for non-humans — or is the practice philosophically underdetermined, fitting several incompatible theories depending on which committee room you're sitting in? I'm also wondering whether there's a hidden commitment about potential versus actual capacities, since human infants have smaller absolute brains than adult pigs but recieve full protection regardless.
