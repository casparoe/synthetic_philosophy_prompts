Hey, I'm a software engineer with basically zero formal phil training, but I got interested in this sphexishness concept—like how that wasp keeps dragging prey back to the burrow even if you move it, just running the same loop over and over. I'm trying to map it onto free will for a side project on agent architectures, and I built this argument that seems to prove way too much. Here it is:

1. Sphexishness is behavioral rigidity: repeating a subrutine regardless of enviroment change.
2. Free will requires counterflexive capacity—the ability to interrupt your own loops and respond to reasons rather than just cues.
3. But any loop-breaker is itself a subrutine with rigid trigger conditions (if X then break Y).
4. So counterflexivity is just higher-order sphexishness.
5. Therefore either free will requires sphexishness, which collapses the distinction between reflex and reason, or free will requires escaping all loops entirely, which is impossible for any implemented agent.
6. So we have to pick: either humans have free will and sphexishness doesn't actually threaten it, or we don't have free will and moral responsibility is out the window.

This feels suspicious because it wipes out the difference between a reflexive withdrawal and a considered change of mind. Where does it go wrong? If I want to keep (2)—that free will needs loop-breaking—what am I paying to reject one of the other steps? Is there an equivocation hiding between running a loop and being unable to modify your own architecture?
