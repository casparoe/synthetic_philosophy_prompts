I was arguing with my roommate last week about whether comparing the fall of the Roman Republic to modern democratic backsliding is actually useful, or if it's just a fancy way of saying things feel unstable right now. We both know the basic chronology—Gracchus getting killed over land reform, Marius breaking consular term norms, Sulla marching on Rome, then the First Triumvirate, Catiline's conspiracy, Caesar crossing the Rubicon, and eventually the Senate just voting itself out of power. And we both agree contemporary democracies face populist pressure, legislative gridlock, and norms being eroded from inside. But the argument stalls everytime we reach the same spot: I say the analogy works because the Republic collapsed when informal norms failed faster than formal institutions could adapt, and he says that's wrong because Rome never had democratic norms to begin with—it was always an oligarchy with a populist facade, so comparing it to modern liberal democracy is comparing apples to a completely different kind of fruit.

My working theory is that we're not actually disagreeing about Roman history or about modern politics, but about something I'm tentatively calling instutional plasticity—whether a political system's unwritten rules have enough elasticity to absorb stress without snapping, and whether that elasticity is even measurable across two milennia. He thinks I'm smuggling in a modern liberal assumption that Rome had something like normative bandwith that could be depleted, when in reality the Republic was always a low-plasticity system where violence was just formal politics by other means. So is this stall a hidden factual disagreement about what kind of regime Rome actually was, or is it a conceptual disagreement about which features count as the system versus the background conditions? Or are we answering totally different questions—me asking what warns us about institutional fatigue, him asking whether the analogy is historiographically sound—while pretending we're debating the same thing?

If it's the first, what kind of evidence or distinction could actually move it? If it's the second, is there any point in continuing the comparison, or should we just admit the analogy is dead and look for a different framework altogether? I want an honest diagnosis, not a history lecture, and I'm especially curious whether there's a conceptual move—maybe separating norm erosion from procedural overload—that could unblock us, or whether we're just stuck with bedrock intuitions about whether history repeats at all.
