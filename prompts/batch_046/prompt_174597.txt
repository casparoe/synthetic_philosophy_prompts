I have been convening a small reading group on rescue obligations and moral aggregation, and over several months we have reached a peculiar impasse that I cannot quite diagnose. The focal case is a standard life-raft scenario: one stranger is stranded to the east and five to the west, and you can reach only one group. Those in our group who reject aggregation—roughly in a Taurekian spirit—maintain that once there is a sufficient reason to save any particular individual, the presence of four further individuals does not generate a numerically stronger reason to save the larger group; at most it provides a tie-breaking consideration, but there is no moral fact that makes saving the five obligatory in a way that saving the one is not. Their opponents reply that this view collapses a distributive judgment into a non-comparative one: if each life carries equal value, then saving five realises that value five times over, and any framework that treats the choice as morally symmetric must be implicitly denying that value can be summed across disjoint persons. What strikes me is that both factions accept the same baseline premises—equal moral status, the badness of avoidable death, the permissibility of aiding strangers—yet neither has conceded an inch.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not ultimately about first-order moral intuitions, nor about hidden empirical facts, but about whether moral reasons are the kind of entities that can be aggregated across individuals, a question that structurally mirrors the old debate in welfare economics over whether interpersonal utility comparisons require cardinal aggregation or merely ordinal ranking. Is this the right diagnosis? Or are the two sides actually answering different questions—one about the phenomenology of a virtuous agent's deliberation, the other about the formal properties of a decision procedure—while speaking the same language of 'reasons'? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, not a verdict on who is correct: is this a verbal dispute about the meaning of 'counting', a substantive disagreement about the ontology of reasons, a clash of bedrock intuitions about equality that neither side can argue for, or a sign that the framing conflates distributive justice with rescue obligations and is therefore ill-posed? And given that diagnosis, what would count as progress—perhaps a formal result showing that non-aggregation violates transitivity or completeness, a conceptual distinction separating distributive from aggregative reasons, an empirical study of reasoning under scarcity, or an argument that no resolution should be expected and why?
