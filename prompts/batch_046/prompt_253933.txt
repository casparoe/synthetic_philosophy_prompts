I have observed a particular distortion in the application of the outside view to long-lived institutions, and I am unsure whether it corresponds to an established failure mode or requires a new classification. Consider an analyst forecasting the stability of a large federation. When arguing that collapse is probable, she selects a reference class of disintegrated empires that share superficial narrative features—say, pronounced internal factionalism and external military pressure—while setting aside structurally similar federations that survived because their constitutional mechanisms differed. When the same analyst later argues for resilience, she reverses the criteria: the reference class is now restricted to surviving federations with analogous constitutional designs, and the previously decisive narrative features are dismissed as historically contingent rather than predictive. In both cases the forecast is presented as an objective base-rate inference, yet the boundary of the class is reconstructed to preserve the directional conclusion. Is there an established concept that captures this specific manoeuvre—distinct from ordinary reference-class ambiguity, from the general problem of selecting the wrong comparison set, and from straightforward motivated reasoning? If no precise term exists, I would like a rigorous delineation of the phenomenon: specifically, whether it requires conscious awareness of the shift on the forecaster's part, whether it applies exclusively to collapse predictions or extends to any base-rate comparison where narrative salience conflicts with structural similarity, and what distinguishes it from legitimate updates to one's model of which features are causally relevant.
