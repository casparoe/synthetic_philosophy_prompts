You are an expert in the epistemology of ancient historiography. Think carefully, step by step, and adjudicate the disagreement below point by point, answering in American English and without providing bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.

In my study group, two people argued about whether we can claim to know anything about events centuries before living memory, using Herodotus and Thucydides as contrasting case studies.

Alex said Herodotus is actually more epistemically responsible for distant-past claims than Thucydides, because Herodotus preserves multiple competing oral traditions about Egypt and Persia and explicitly flags their contradictions, letting readers weigh them against one another. By contrast, Thucydides excludes mythic material entirely and imposes a single causal chain in his Archaeology, which suppresses alternative traditions without direct argument—essentially substituting one narrative authority for plural testimony.

Jordan replied that this inverts the real problem. Herodotus’s multiple accounts are mostly layered hearsay separated by generations; preserving them does not improve reliability, it just multiplies noise. Thucydides at least applies proximity criteria—preferring direct witnesses and rejecting the fabulous—which is exactly the kind of source criticism needed for distant events, even if his exclusions sometimes rely on negative judgments about absent evidence.

Alex then introduced what she called a transmission asymmetry: negative claims about the distant past—asserting that something did not happen—require universal absence of testimony across all lost archives, which is impossible to establish. So Thucydides’s silence-based exclusions risk an argument from ignorance, whereas Herodotus’s positive reports of traditions, however unreliable, provide actual data points that can be cross-checked. The burden should fall on exclusion, not on preservation.

Jordan countered that this asymmetry collapses once you recognize that Herodotus never stratifies his sources by chronological distance; he treats a priest’s story about ancient Egypt as equivalent to a traveler’s recent observation. Without temporal stratification, those positive reports are not checkable data points—they are undifferentiated noise. The core issue is not preservation versus exclusion, but whether a historian applies proximity filters, which Thucydides attempts and Herodotus largely ignores.

Neither would concede. Who is right about which specific point—plural testimony versus proximity filtering, the transmission asymmetry, the need for source stratification, and whether either historian actually achieves meaningful epistemic access to the distant past? What, if anything, are both parties missing? Give your verdict point by point.
