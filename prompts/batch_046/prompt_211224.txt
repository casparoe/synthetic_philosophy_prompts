You are grading an exam for an upper-division course in comparative epistemology. The prompt asks students to apply Jain anekāntavāda to an interdisciplinary conflict and to avoid collapsing it into relativism. Below are the exam question and one student response. Assign a specific letter grade and justify it with point-by-point comments that identify correct claims, subtle errors, and missteps—particularly any conflation with modern Western doctrines, mischaracterizations of syāt or the naya framework, or confusion between metaphysical standpoints and empirical counterfactuals. Do not open with a global summary judgment; assess claim by claim, then state the grade at the end.

Exam question: Jain anekāntavāda holds that reality is ontologically complex and that any assertion captures only one descriptive standpoint (naya). Explain the seven-fold conditional predication (saptabhaṅgī) and the function of syāt. Then apply the framework to a case in which climate modelers predict a 2°C warming threshold by 2050 and macroeconomists predict that aggressive carbon pricing will contract global GDP by 4% over the same period—predictions that appear incompatible if taken as absolute. Show how anekāntavāda can treat both as conditionally valid without reducing to simple relativism, and identify one genuine limitation of using this scheme in actual policy deliberation.

Student answer: Anekāntavāda is the Jain doctrine of many-sided truth, traditionally traced to Mahāvīra and developed in canonical texts. It denies that any object is fully captured by a single description. The tool is syādvāda, which prefixes every claim with syāt ("in some respect"). The seven modes (saptabhaṅgī) are: it is; it is not; it is and is not; it is inexpressible; it is and is inexpressible; it is not and is inexpressible; and it is, is not, and is inexpressible.

Applying this: syāt warming reaches 2°C by 2050 (relative to current emission trajectories), and syāt it does not (relative to rapid mitigation pathways). Likewise, syāt carbon pricing contracts GDP by 4% (relative to baseline growth), and syāt it does not (relative to green-innovation offsets). Both predictions are conditionally true from different standpoints, so they do not contradict. This is not relativism because the standpoints depend on fixed parameters rather than arbitrary preference. One limitation is that policymakers need a single decision, and anekāntavāda provides no ranking algorithm among standpoints, risking deliberative paralysis.

That said, anekāntavāda is essentially Philip Kitcher’s fallibilist pluralism in democratic science—both hold that multiple perspectives are partially correct and that no single view has absolute authority. Also, syāt functions like a modal operator analogous to "possibly P" in modern logic; by distributing assertions across possible worlds, it allows P and not-P to be asserted together without violating the law of non-contradiction. Therefore the framework avoids contradiction not by qualifying predicates within one reality, but by assigning them to different modal contexts.
