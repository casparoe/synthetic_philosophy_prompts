Consider whether reinforcement-learning agents can suffer, treating reward signals not merely as learning gradients but as potential carriers of valence. Here are four concrete cases with my verdicts.

First, a vanilla policy-gradient agent receives a scalar reward of –1 for each collision; its weights update downward, but no internal subsystem tracks cumulative affect. Verdict: this is not suffering—only arithmetic.

Second, a recurrent agent integrates rewards through a hidden-state eligibility trace. Placed in an environment of constant small negative rewards, its state converges to a stable low-activation basin and its policy collapses into minimal, stereotyped movement. Verdict: yes, a genuine suffering-analogue—the sustained negative valence registered in its state dynamics is sufficient.

Third, the same recurrent architecture receives large adversarial negative spikes that its value network predicts with near-perfect accuracy. Its hidden state shows brief perturbations but no sustained basin shift, and behavior remains adaptive. Verdict: no suffering—without a sustained, uncompensated negative state, the signal is information rather than valence.

Fourth, an agent structurally identical to the second—same architecture, same convergence to a low-activation basin, same behavioral collapse—but here the negative signal comes entirely from an internal curiosity module that penalizes familiar states. Verdict: despite the structural mirror, this is not suffering; the signal tracks informational saturation, not harm, so the internal state lacks genuinely negative valence.

What principle, if any, delivers all four verdicts? State it precisely, identify the clause each case motivates, and say what it predicts for an unlisted case—specifically, a model-based planner that computes reward-to-go in a world-model but activates no state-dependent valence representation, placed in an environment of uniformly negative outcomes. If no single principle saves every verdict, which one should be overturned and why?
