I serve as the philosophical advisor to our university's intergenerational capital-planning board, which reviews infrastructure proposals—geological repositories, climate-adaptation pathways, long-horizon endowments—whose welfare effects extend centuries forward. Analysts routinely apply a pure time preference rate to future benefits, but the board lacks a reusable procedure for distinguishing conceptually justified discounting (uncertainty, opportunity cost, causal attenuation) from pure temporal partiality. I want an operational decision procedure—ordered questions, stopping rules, and explicit flags marking irreducible judgment calls—that a non-philosopher analyst can run in about thirty minutes per proposal. The procedure must incorporate the counterfactual dependency problem: when a proposal determines which specific future people exist, the spatial-distance analogy for temporal discounting collapses, yet analysts still need structured guidance rather than an automatic veto. Specify: (1) a preliminary classification of the proposal's population structure (path-dependent versus fixed); (2) a three-filter test for whether applying a pure rate is conceptually permissible; (3) the precise hand-off point where the decision becomes one of values rather than procedural fact. Apply the procedure to three cases: (A) a nuclear-waste repository affecting populations three centuries hence whose existence is largely independent of present energy policy; (B) a carbon-mitigation pathway that shapes which future individuals come to exist; and (C) a case the board will resist—where the procedure permits a positive pure discount rate despite strong ethical objections, with an explicit statement of exactly where the board must overrule on non-procedural grounds. Conclude by stating two questions the procedure cannot settle and one systematic misfire it is prone to produce.
