I am currently taking a course on philosophy of mind and metaphysics, and my study group recently became stuck in a rather technical disagreement about Daniel Dennett’s notion of real patterns across the physical, design, and intentional stances. I am writing to ask for an adjudication, as neither side will concede any ground.

The disagreement began when one student argued that real patterns are essentially predictive compressions of physical data. On her view, the centre of gravity of an object is a real pattern because it allows us to predict behaviour without tracking every particle, but it is ontologically derivative. She then claimed that intentional-stance patterns — attributing beliefs and desires to an agent — are real patterns in exactly this same weak, derivative sense: they are useful heuristics for predicting behaviour, but they do not correspond to anything over and above the physical or design-stance regularities that ground them. She insisted that calling them ‘merely heuristic’ does not diminish their reality, because all real patterns are ultimately heuristic relative to the physical stance.

The other student objected that this collapses an important distinction. He granted that design-stance patterns — such as saying that a heart pumps blood to maintain circulation — are genuinely real, because they capture objective functional organisations that have been selected for and that possess counterfactual stability independent of our interpretive practices. But he argued that intentional-stance patterns are different in kind, not merely in degree. Unlike functional design patterns, intentional attributions depend on what he called ‘rational reconstruction’: we attribute beliefs and desires only when we can make the agent’s behaviour broadly rational from our interpretive perspective. This means that intentional patterns lack the independent projectibility that design patterns enjoy; if our interpretive framework shifts, the pattern dissolves, whereas a heart’s function remains stable regardless of our interpretive stance.

The first student replied that this proves too much. If counterfactual stability and independence from interpretation are the criteria for being a real pattern, then many design-stance patterns also fail — for instance, attributing a ‘function’ to a randomly formed rock that happens to filter water is clearly an interpretive projection, yet we do not say the pattern is unreal, only that it is misapplied. She then suggested that the intentional stance is simply the design stance applied to systems with sufficient behavioural complexity and internal feedback loops, and that the apparent dependence on interpretation is just a reflection of our epistemic limitations, not an ontological deficiency in the pattern itself.

The second student countered that there is still a categorical difference: design-stance patterns are anchored in causal-historical selection or in reliable causal roles, whereas intentional-stance patterns require normative assessment of rational coherence. He concluded that intentional patterns are therefore ‘real’ only in a pragmatic, instrumental sense, while design patterns are real in a stronger, metaphysically robust sense — and that this undermines the first student’s claim that all three stances yield patterns of uniform ontological status.

Neither would accept the other’s framing of the criteria for pattern reality, and the discussion has stalled. Could you adjudicate this disagreement point by point? Specifically: is the first student correct that all real patterns are ultimately heuristic relative to physics? Is the second student right that design-stance patterns have a stronger ontological status because of selection or causal role? And does the intentional stance genuinely depend on interpretation in a way that makes its patterns categorically weaker, or is that dependence merely epistemic? I would appreciate a verdict that addresses the arguments exactly as they have been made, noting where either party is missing something important.
