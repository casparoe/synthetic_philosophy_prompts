I'm writing from a graduate seminar on the ethics of collective memory and political forgetting. We have been working through the tension between what is usually associated with Margalit's claim that communities owe remembrance to victims of grave injustice, and the counter-argument—often developed in a Rieffian register—that modern societies can be crushed by an unprocessed past and sometimes need structured release from it. The assigned literature is rich on case studies—monuments, archives, reparations—but thin on the prior question: what must an adequate normative theory of collective remembering and forgetting actually deliver before we can adjudicate any case?

Please provide the full desiderata. That is, specify the phenomena a theory must accommodate, the distinctions it must preserve, the consistency conditions it must meet, and which of these are genuinely non-negotiable versus preferences of particular camps—always with the motivating case or argument behind each.

Specifically:

First, the boundary cases and phenomena. The theory must handle voluntary versus coerced forgetting; individual trauma recovery versus collective narrative revision; commemorative practices that honor victims versus those that instrumentalize suffering for present politics. Include, as a non-obvious test case, a society that technically preserves every document in intact digital archives but systematically degrades the navigational and interpretive pathways by which citizens access them—so that forgetting occurs without erasure. Explain why the theory needs a clear verdict here and what that verdict reveals about its underlying mechanism.

Second, the distinctions that must not be collapsed: between forgetting as moral forgiveness and forgetting as epistemic denial; between a duty to remember specific historical events and a duty to preserve awareness of structural patterns that persist across generations; between the moral failure of willful amnesia and the practical failure of being psychologically unable to integrate a past. Give the reason each distinction must survive theoretical reduction.

Third, the consistency conditions. The theory should not contradict well-established results in the psychology of trauma and narrative identity without explicit argument. It must avoid licensing contradictory obligations—say, requiring both the preservation and the suppression of the same historical record for the same collective agent without contextual differentiation. And it must be compatible with democratic legitimacy, since state-mediated memory is coercive in ways that private remembrance is not.

Fourth, identify the single desideratum that does the most exclusionary work—likely the requirement that the theory explain why some pasts generate standing obligations of remembrance while others can be legitimately released, without reducing this difference to a simple aggregate-cost calculus.

Fifth, flag any pair of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable given these constraints, and explain the tension precisely.

Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on a scorecard of named theories, but do briefly indicate how the Margalit-style ethics-of-memory position and the Rieff-inspired therapeutic-release view fare against this list—without treating either as a finalized doctrine and without providing a bibliography or citation list. I am using this to structure a course paper, so rigor and conceptual clarity matter more than historical coverage.
