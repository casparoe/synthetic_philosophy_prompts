I've been working through debates on upzoning, exclusionary zoning, and what is sometimes called the right to the city, and I keep running into an argumentative move I don't have a precise word for. A homeowner opposes a proposed four-story building near a rail station on the grounds that they bought into a low-rise neighborhood and that the change violates an implicit contract about local character. But the same homeowner typically supports, or at least does not resist, a historic-preservation ordinance that severely restricts alterations to existing structures, or a design-review board with sweeping aesthetic authority — both of which also impose collective preferences on individual property decisions. And if an identical building is proposed in a neighboring district, they often stay silent, which suggests the objection is not grounded in a universal principle about urban scale or architectural harmony, but in a geographically bounded veto. The pattern looks like this: zoning is treated not as a public regulatory framework but as a kind of distributed property right held collectively by current residents over the demographic and visual future of a specific, parcel-adjacent territory — a right invoked selectively against market-rate density but not against other collective controls. Is there an established concept for this selective, territorially bounded extension of property-rights rhetoric into an aesthetic and density veto? How is it different from standard NIMBYism, from simple status-quo bias, and from a coherent theory of neighborhood self-determination? If no standard term captures it, what features — the selectivity, the territorial boundary, the framing as rights rather than preferences — should define its edges?
