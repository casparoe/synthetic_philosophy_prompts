I am preparing a discussion for an undergraduate jurisprudence seminar that has just worked through the exchange between H.L.A. Hart and Lon Fuller on whether moral defectiveness can ever undermine legal validity, and a colleague’s offhand remark—that Creon’s edict in Sophocles’ Antigone might be not merely unjust but a failure of lawmaking altogether—has made me want to test the play against the debate in detail rather than treat it as a familiar morality-tale illustration. Read Antigone through the specific terms of the Hart-Fuller dispute: treat Creon’s prohibition on burying Polynices as a test case for Fuller’s eight principles of the internal morality of law, especially generality, promulgation, and the congruence between an enacted rule and official enforcement, while also holding in view Hart’s insistence that even a gravely immoral command can satisfy every formal criterion of validity and remain law, however repugnant. Does Antigone herself draw a distinction between a command that is not law at all and one that is law but wicked, or does she treat Creon’s word as a genuine legal obligation that she overrides for higher reasons? How do Ismene’s refusal to join her and the Chorus’s gradual shift in judgment complicate any simple mapping of Antigone onto natural-law resistance and Creon onto legal-positivist authority? I am particularly interested in whether the play’s appeal to unwritten, divine ordinances supports Fuller’s claim that extreme procedural immorality dissolves legal obligation, or whether it instead vindicates Hart’s separation thesis by portraying Antigone as obeying a coexisting normative system—religious or familial—that conflicts with, but does not invalidate, the city’s positive rules. Where does the drama resist being reduced to either philosopher’s framework, and what does Sophocles’s portrayal of political collapse add to the jurisprudential argument that a purely conceptual analysis of “valid law” tends to miss?
