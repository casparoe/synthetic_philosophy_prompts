As a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, I have been reading histories of pandemics and climate crises as an outside interest, largely because I am fascinated by how complex systems fail and recover; yet I keep encountering a rhetorical pattern I cannot quite place. In popular and academic accounts alike, the Black Death is said not merely to have killed labourers but to have “dismantled” feudal contracts, “forced” landowners into conceding wages, or “cleared space” for peasant revolts. Similarly, a volcanic winter or a prolonged drought is described as having “pressured” a dynasty into reform, “driven” populations across borders, or “reshaped” agricultural economies. In each case, a germ, a climate shift, or a volcano is granted verbs—exploiting, destabilising, opening pathways—that ordinarily belong to strategic agents, while the human communities involved are portrayed as largely reactive, almost like modules processing an external input.

What makes this puzzling is that it does not appear to be straightforward determinism, since the same authors typically insist that people retained meaningful choice; yet it is clearly not harmless metaphor, because the explanatory architecture collapses if you remove the pathogen or the frost. The causal weight genuinely seems to rest with the non-human factor, even though no explicit claim is made that human action was impossible. I have tentatively labelled this “determinism with plausible deniability”, but I suspect there is a more precise concept—perhaps within philosophy of history or in analyses of causal explanation—that captures exactly this transfer of explanatory efficacy through agent-like language, together with the simultaneous denial of determinism. Does this configuration have an established name?

If there is a recognised term, I would like to know how it is distinguished from ordinary figurative speech on one side and from explicit environmental determinism on the other; if there is not, could you delineate its boundaries precisely? In particular, I am keen to understand at what point describing a volcano or a microbe as “destabilising” an empire crosses from convenient shorthand into an illegitimate ascription of agency, and whether there is a principled way to separate the explanatory work done by the non-human force from the explanatory work that properly belongs to human decision-making under pressure. I am not after a bare label, but rather a careful carving of the phenomenon: what it is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
