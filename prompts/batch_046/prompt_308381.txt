A colleague who has read some introductory texts on Buddhist ethics summarized the tradition for me in these terms: it is fundamentally consequentialist, because every volitional act (cetana) is evaluated by its karmic result (vipaka)—specifically whether it produces dukkha or advances the agent toward nibbana—so that the Noble Eightfold Path functions as an instrumental optimization framework for reducing aggregate suffering across rebirths. On her reading, the well-known emphasis on right intention (samma sankappa) is best understood not as an independent moral criterion but as a motivational heuristic, comparable to the personal rules a rule-utilitarian adopts to stabilize long-term utility. The only real departure from Western consequentialism, she said, is that Buddhists include the agent’s own mental purity in the outcome set, which makes the view an agent-inclusive, long-horizon form of consequentialism rather than a simple maximization of external welfare. I think this captures the outcome-sensitive language in the suttas, but I suspect it flattens something important about how karma and intention actually interact. Which parts of her summary would a careful defender of the tradition accept, where does it conflate karmic evaluation with outcome-based assessment, and if those errors are corrected, does Buddhist ethics end up closer to virtue ethics, closer to deontology, or structured in a way that does not map cleanly onto any of those three categories?
