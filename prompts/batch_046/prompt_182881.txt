There's this debate in my city about renaming a street named after a local factory owner who built the public library but also used prison labor in the 1920s. The keep-the-name crowd keeps saying a street sign is basicly a footnote in the town's shared history book — pull it down and you're not changing the past, you're just tearing out the page so nobody can learn from it. They say thats why we should leave it up, maybe with a plaque. Meanwhile people arguing for renaming say the footnote analogy is garbage because street names arent passive records, they're more like a daily roll call that forces everyone living there to rehearse the honor every time they write their adress. Can you audit the analogy? Give me the correspondence table — sign, page, reader, tearing, plaque — then where it breaks, especially around the idea that keeping the name requires active mnemonic labor from residents rather than just sitting there like paper. Does the "dont erase history" conclusion survive once you factor in that the sign is doing commemorative work, not archival work? And is there a better analogy for what renaming actually does — maybe updating an index or changing a bookmark?
