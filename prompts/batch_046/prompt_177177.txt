It now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion seems to undermine the very practice I'm studying, yet each premise looks defensible in isolation.

(1) Mechanistic anomaly detection requires comparing the internal computations of a target system against a reference mechanism in order to flag meaningful deviations.

(2) For that comparison to be non-arbitrary, we need trans-contextual identity conditions for mechanisms—conditions that determine whether two systems, architectures, or training runs instantiate the same mechanism.

(3) If mechanisms are individuated functionally—by causal role or input-output profile within the network—then any two architectures with isomorphic causal graphs share identical mechanisms. But then a structural deviation that preserves functional role is invisible as an anomaly, and genuinely different implementations are treated as the same mechanism, rendering detection either impossible or trivial.

(4) If mechanisms are individuated structurally—by exact weights, activation patterns, or circuit topology—then no two trained instances of the "same" architecture share any mechanism. Every comparison becomes self-comparison, so anomaly detection collapses into universal divergence.

(5) One might reply that mechanisms should be individuated at an intermediate level of abstraction—algorithmic patterns invariant across some but not all implementation details.

(6) But selecting that intermediate level requires a non-arbitrary principle of abstraction. Without one, any observed deviation can be redescribed either as an anomalous token of the reference mechanism or as an entirely different mechanism, depending solely on where we draw the boundary.

(7) Therefore mechanistic anomaly detection is either impossible (under functional or structural individuation) or arbitrarily conventional (under intermediate abstraction), despite being a productive and apparently non-arbitrary research practice.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I am inclined to defend (5), but I worry that defending it requires smuggling in normative assumptions about which abstractions are "natural" or "explanatorily relevant."
