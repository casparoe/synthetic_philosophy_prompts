Consider the claim that in any administratively feasible welfare system, conditionality tied to active work-search behavior cannot track moral desert, because compliance is overwhelmingly predicted by prior cognitive bandwidth, health stability, and social capital—factors themselves distributed by structural luck—so that the category of the "undeserving poor" collapses into a misattribution of distributive disadvantage to individual choice. What is your credence that this claim is correct, or at minimum that it renders the deserving/undeserving distinction normatively inert for policy design?

Do not provide only a bare number. Decompose your reasoning by assigning rough probabilities to four sub-questions: (i) whether willingness-to-work and capacity-to-comply are empirically separable in real-world workfare and conditional-benefit regimes; (ii) whether moral desert requires that agents possess the relevant capacities and opportunities independently of prior distributive luck; (iii) whether the purpose of welfare provision is properly understood as tracking desert rather than securing welfare, social cooperation, or economic stability; and (iv) whether the concept of the "undeserving poor" retains any coherent normative function once (i)–(iii) are granted. Show how these components combine into your overall credence, identify which sub-question does the most work in determining your final number, and specify what kind of empirical finding or conceptual argument would most substantially move you up or down.

Please separate what is settled—such as the documented correlation between program compliance and prior socioeconomic resources, or the philosophical distinction between choice and circumstance—from what remains genuinely contested among scholars who understand both the policy literature and normative theory. Do not present any contested interpretation, for instance that welfare is fundamentally about desert-tracking, or that conditionality is purely symbolic rather than behaviorally selective, as though it were the consensus view.
