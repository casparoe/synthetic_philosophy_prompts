Exam prompt: Compare the German folk-moral tradition, which emphasizes rule-following, social order (Ordnung), and collective duty, with the American folk-moral tradition, which emphasizes individual conscience, personal autonomy, and the legitimacy of dissent from collective norms. Assess which framework offers a more defensible account of moral responsibility, and identify at least one significant weakness in each approach. Avoid treating either culture as a monolith; focus on the underlying moral logic rather than on empirical stereotypes.

Student response:

The German tradition treats moral life chiefly as the preservation of order through strict adherence to rules. This outlook is rooted in Kant’s categorical imperative, which demands that agents obey universal moral laws regardless of personal inclination or consequence. Within this framework, responsibility means fulfilling one’s designated role without appealing to private judgment, which explains the historical efficiency of bureaucratic administration in German-speaking societies. Its central weakness is that it permits evil to be carried out with perfect regularity whenever rules are themselves corrupt or inhumane.

American folk morality, by contrast, locates authority in the individual conscience. Shaped by Protestant notions of direct moral access and reinforced by traditions of civil disobedience, it holds that no external rule can bind a person who sincerely judges it wrong. Responsibility therefore consists in authentic self-scrutiny rather than in social compliance. This safeguards against authoritarian abuse because no official can hide behind procedure.

Yet this strength also generates a structural flaw. Because private conviction becomes the ultimate criterion, the framework offers no principled barrier against refusing any collective obligation—taxation, public-health mandates, or legal duties—whenever an individual claims conscientious objection. American society therefore exhibits systematically lower deference to institutional rules than societies organized around collective duty.

On balance, the American framework is superior because moral personhood requires the capacity for critical reflection that rule-following suppresses. The German model, by making social order the highest good, risks reducing moral agents to instruments of administration.
