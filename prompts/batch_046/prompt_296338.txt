I have adopted a rigorous version of Maimonides' accommodationist thesis regarding the sacrificial commandments: that they were instituted not because blood-offering is intrinsically valuable, but solely as a pedagogical diversion from the idolatrous practices to which the Israelites were habituated, and that once that psychological dependency is permanently dissolved, the rationale for the concession expires entirely. From this I infer that any modern revival of animal sacrifice—whether in eschatological Temple reconstruction or in contemporary religious experimentation—would be theologically illegitimate, not merely impractical, because it reinstates a practice whose only justification was a now-obsolete therapeutic function. I want an honest map of what would count against this position. Specifically: what internal tensions in Maimonides' broader philosophy of law, particularly between his claim that the Law is eternally perfect and his historicist explanation of particular commandments, threaten to collapse the accommodationist reading? What conceptual distinction—say, between a temporary pedagogical measure and a permanent statutory form—would need to fail for my inference to hold? Are there rival interpretations of sacrifice within Maimonides' rationalist framework, perhaps ones that treat the ritual as encoding a metaphysical relation between material substance and divine presence rather than mere habit-management, that are not only incompatible with my view but actually more faithful to the text? Rank the defeaters from fatal to awkward: which ones must a committed accommodationist concede damage the view, and which can be absorbed by refining the thesis? Finally, does the conclusion that sacrifice is permanently illegitimate risk undermining Maimonides' own insistence on the unchangeable perfection of the entire legal code?
