I'm writing a paper for my philosophy of history seminar on the institutional practice of analogical reasoning in security-policy debates. The practice is familiar: advisers invoke Munich to warn against appeasement, Vietnam to warn against quagmire, and the so-called Thucydides Trap to frame rising-power conflict. These analogies function not merely as illustrations but as normative templates—if structural conditions match, the moral and strategic verdict is supposed to carry over. Yet the same institutions treat historical knowledge as irreducibly contingent and reject deterministic social science. What philosophical commitments about the ontology of historical structures, cross-case moral responsibility, and analogical justification does this practice presuppose? Must it assume a covert form of structural determinism, or can it be reconciled with a radically agent-centered, contingency-rich view? And if we replaced its implicit determinism with the latter view, what exactly would have to change in how these analogies are deployed?
