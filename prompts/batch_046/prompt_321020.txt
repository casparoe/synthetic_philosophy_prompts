I have been revisiting Benjamin Franklin's scheme of moral perfection as outlined in his autobiography, particularly the thirteen virtues and the method of tracking them in a little book. Most commentators treat his admission that he never achieved perfection in any of them—least of all humility—as either a charming piece of self-deprecating realism or evidence that he was essentially an Aristotelian habituator who recognized that virtue lies in continuous practice rather than in terminal achievement. But I want to defend a more structurally ambitious reading. Please argue that Franklin's insistence on the unattainability of moral perfection is not an empirical concession about human weakness but a necessary feature of his ethical architecture: specifically, that perfect virtue in any one category would undermine the conditions for moral agency itself, because Franklin's virtues are interdependent in a way that requires ongoing failure and self-correction to maintain their functional coherence.

In particular, make the case that the ordering of the virtues—temperance first, humility last—is not merely pedagogical but metaphysically load-bearing: humility, for Franklin, is intelligible only as a response to recognized failures in prior virtues, which means that a state of perfect humility would presuppose a history of imperfection that perfect virtue would erase. From this, argue that Franklin's system implicitly treats moral perfection as a regulative ideal in something very close to the Kantian sense—an infinite progress rather than a reachable state—despite Franklin's reputation as a pragmatic empiricist and despite the absence of any explicit transcendental framing. Develop this argument in American English, with attention to how the interdependence of the virtues prevents any single virtue from being maximized without destabilizing the others, and with a clear explanation of why a defender of this view should reject the standard interpretation that Franklin was simply being modest about human limits.
