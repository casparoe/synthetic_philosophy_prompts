At a philosophy colloquium on moral psychology, two scholars—Dr. Elena Vasquez and Dr. Marcus Chen—debated whether the situationist critique genuinely undermines virtue ethics. Vasquez opened by arguing that virtue ethics depends on global character traits that cause behavior across diverse contexts, yet decades of social-psychological research show that situational variables—authority cues, time pressure, framing—routinely overwhelm individual differences, with cross-situational consistency coefficients often near or below 0.30. She concluded that virtues are not real psychological dispositions, and that virtue ethics must either abandon its descriptive core or retreat into unfalsifiable normativity.

Chen replied that this misinterprets the Aristotelian tradition. Virtues, he said, are not global behavioral constants but ecologically embedded practical skills—like linguistic fluency—that manifest differently across contexts without being illusory. The situationist data, he argued, refine virtue ethics by revealing the supporting conditions virtues require, rather than refuting them.

Vasquez countered that even local embedding fails because of trait suppression across domains: a person's compassion in one setting is easily overridden by an authority cue in another, with minimal individual variation in resistance. She proposed a specific standard—"predictive calibration"—according to which a disposition counts as psychologically real only if adding it to a predictive model improves accuracy beyond situational variables alone. By this metric, she claimed, virtues consistently fail.

Chen answered that predictive calibration applies to statistical predictors, not to normative dispositions. He introduced a distinction between "behavioral manifestation ranges" and "counterfactual stability structures." A virtue, he argued, is defined by its counterfactual profile within a "normative resilience range"—the class of normal perturbations appropriate to that virtue—not by behavior in extreme or deceptive conditions. The fact that compassion collapses under severe, artificial coercion no more disproves compassion than a bridge collapsing in an earthquake disproves its structural integrity; situationist experiments often deploy pathological conditions that fall outside the ecological range where virtue is meant to operate.

Vasquez responded that Chen's "normative resilience range" is specified post-hoc to exclude counterexamples. Without an independent criterion for membership in that range, she said, the defense is an immunizing stratagem. Meanwhile, situationist psychology offers a positive program: predict behavior from role and framing rather than from character.

Chen retorted that the range is not post-hoc but derived from the virtue's normative purpose. Courage is not tested by demanding someone face a firing squad for no reason; it is tested where risk is proportionate to value. Because classic situationist setups involve deceptive, high-stakes artificial environments, they test failure under pathological conditions, not absence of disposition.

Please go through their exchange step by step and say who is right about each specific point: whether predictive calibration is the correct standard for assessing virtues; whether local ecological embedding saves virtue ethics from global consistency objections; whether Chen's normative resilience range is a legitimate conceptual move or an ad hoc immunization; and whether Vasquez is correct that situationist experiments fall outside the relevant ecological range. Then state who is more right overall, and identify anything important that both are missing—for example, whether a thinner, modular account of virtue might accommodate situationist variability without collapsing into full-blown trait skepticism.
