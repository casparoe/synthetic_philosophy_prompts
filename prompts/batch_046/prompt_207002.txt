In my philosophy seminar this term, we have spent several weeks examining Hannah Arendt's report on the trial of Adolf Eichmann and her claim that extreme moral wrongs can be committed not by demons driven by grand ideological hatred, but by ordinary bureaucrats who lack the capacity to think from another person's perspective and who experience their actions as routine professional duties. Our classroom debate, however, has stalled in a way that feels less like a historical argument and more like a conceptual knot. On one side, students who have consulted recent biographical scholarship argue that Arendt was empirically wrong about Eichmann's psychology: private letters and post-capture interviews reveal an active, committed antisemitism, so to describe him as an unthinking clerk is not only inaccurate but risks excusing the perpetrator by stripping him of malicious intent. On the other side, defenders reply that Arendt was never writing psychological biography; she was describing a structural feature of modern administrative violence, in which moral responsibility is fragmented across bureaucratic roles and monstrous outcomes become the byproduct of careerist conformity rather than personal hatred. A third position holds that whether Eichmann hated Jews in his heart is morally irrelevant, since the scale of the crime demands condemnation regardless of inner motive.

My working theory is that the disagreement is not really about the historical Eichmann, but about three separate questions that have become entangled under Arendt's concept of the banality of evil. First, is malicious intentionality a necessary condition for grave evil, or can thoughtlessness and role-compliance generate the same moral magnitude? Second, should explanations of genocide prioritize individual psychology or the organizational structures that make individual psychology irrelevant? Third, is the idea of banal evil itself a descriptive claim about how perpetrators experience their actions, or is it a normative claim about what kind of explanation victims and societies are owed? I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is—whether verbal, about the meanings of evil and responsibility; substantive, about hidden empirical facts regarding Eichmann's motives; or methodological, about whether psychology or institutional analysis should take precedence. Given that diagnosis, I also want to know what could actually resolve it: for instance, would a sharper distinction between phenomenological description and moral evaluation break the impasse, or is the conflict inevitable because the same evidence serves two incompatible frameworks answering different questions? Please do not provide a survey of interpretations; instead, analyze the structure of the stall using what each side actually claims, and identify what, if anything, would count as genuine progress.
