I am designing a ninety-minute seminar for an advanced undergraduate philosophy-of-science reading group. The participants have encountered Gödel’s incompleteness theorem in passing but have never studied strange loops, and they tend to treat visual paradoxes as mere illusion. I want them to grasp precisely why Escher’s Drawing Hands illustrates a tangled hierarchy rather than simple recursion, and specifically what it means for an inviolate level—the plane of representation, or a governing meta-language—to be collapsed back into the system it is supposed to ground.

Plan the explanatory arc, not the explanation itself. First, lay out the dependency order: which idea must be secured before the hierarchy can be recognized as tangled rather than merely circular, and why the concept of an inviolate level must be established before the image is shown, so that the violation registers as structural rather than aesthetic. Second, specify the carrying example: either Drawing Hands itself or a constructed parallel—such as a full-scale architectural blueprint printed onto the floor of the building it depicts—detailing exactly what properties the example must possess (a clear separation between depicting system and depicted system that is then undermined) and what it must lack (ambiguous perspective tricks that allow the audience to dismiss the loop as an optical error). Third, map the predictable misconceptions in the sequence they will arise—likely beginning with “it is only a perspective illusion,” proceeding to “it is self-reference like the liar sentence,” and then to “there is an implicit artist outside the frame who breaks the loop”—and indicate the precise point in the sequence where each should be defused. Fourth, propose a single check at the end that distinguishes whether a student has understood the collapse of the inviolate level from whether they have merely found the image clever or paradoxical. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out and acknowledged as beyond the session’s scope—perhaps the full syntactic/semantic distinction or the formal logic of self-reference—and say so openly.
