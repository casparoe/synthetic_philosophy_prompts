As a novelist who has spent the last several years writing historical fiction in which characters must revise convictions upon discovering hidden motives behind their own reasoning, I have become preoccupied with a specific pattern I keep encountering in philosophical discussions and in my own attempts to construct believable intellectual arcs. I want to know whether philosophy has already given it a precise name.

Here is the pattern, observed in concrete instances rather than in the abstract. First, a person holds a belief—say, that a particular historical crisis made a certain policy inevitable—supported by reasoning that seems rigorous. Then she acquires evidence not about the policy itself, but about the conditions under which she formed the belief: she realizes it was constructed during exhaustion, or because it serves a narrative pattern she finds aesthetically compelling, or because she possesses a documented tendency to overestimate inevitability in moments of drama. Ordinarily this higher-order evidence should reduce her confidence. But the pattern I keep seeing involves a further turn. She notices that her very recognition of this bias is occurring under conditions that might themselves be biased—perhaps her suspicion of her own reasoning is itself a dramatic reflex, or a cultivated false modesty, or an overcorrection she has learned to perform. So she attempts to calibrate the calibration downward. Yet that second-order adjustment can be suspected on the same grounds: if her reasoning is unreliable, then her reasoning about its unreliability is unreliable too. The result is not the familiar state of believing against one’s better judgment, which I take to be epistemic akrasia. Nor is it a simple refusal to update on higher-order evidence. Instead, it is a recursive stall: every layer of self-assessment introduces a symmetrical reason to distrust that layer, leaving the belief structurally intact but held in a state of perpetual, self-cancelling scrutiny. I have observed analogues in climate modelers who learn about cognitive bias research, in jurors who are instructed about heuristic failure, and in my own attempts to decide whether my characters’ convictions should be portrayed as rational or as self-deceived.

Is there an established concept for this exact recursive structure—this particular form of higher-order paralysis in which the mechanism intended to revise confidence is itself perpetually undermined by the same kind of evidence that triggered the revision? I am not satisfied with a bare label. I want to know precisely what the phenomenon is, what it is not (how it differs from ordinary epistemic akrasia, from self-defeating beliefs, and from the familiar infinite regress of justification), and where its boundaries lie—especially the distinction between this loop and the simpler case in which an agent merely ignores higher-order evidence, and the distinction from cases in which an agent successfully calibrates downward without entering the cycle. If there is no established term, I would welcome a coined name together with a precise definition and boundary cases that capture the symmetry of distrust at each successive layer.
