A colleague passed me a summary of Ian Hacking's looping-effect argument in psychiatric classification, and I want to check whether I'm being misled. The summary says Hacking divides kinds into "interactive" and "indifferent" ones: mental disorders fall into the first group because assigning a diagnostic label changes how patients understand and behave themselves, which in turn alters the clinical presentation and forces revisions to the criteria—explaining, for instance, why successive editions of the DSM have broadened conditions like autism and ADHD. From this, the summary concludes that Hacking regards psychiatric taxonomy as permanently unstable and therefore incapable of being genuinely scientific in the manner of chemistry or botany; it is, on this reading, a discipline that constructs the very phenomena it claims merely to discover, and its pretensions to objectivity are undermined by an unavoidable circularity. The summary also claims that Hacking extends this critique to all human-science classification—including economics and sociology—arguing that every such field is doomed to the same self-undermining loop, with medicine being distinctive only in refusing to acknowledge it. There is also a passing remark that Hacking's "dynamic nominalism" implies that once a psychiatric category exists, it inevitably generates the behaviors it describes ex nihilo, as if the label alone creates the disorder without any prior biological or psychological substrate. I suspect this overstates Hacking's skepticism and may conflate looping with a stronger form of social constructionism, but I am not certain where the line falls. Could you audit the summary? Please distinguish what accurately reflects Hacking's position, where it subtly misrepresents his view—particularly regarding whether he denies scientific legitimacy to psychiatry or merely describes a different kind of science, and whether looping operates without any underlying substrate—from what is a plausible but inaccurate extension.
