At a philosophy reading group, two members—Sofia and Elias—argued over the classic Damascus parable, in which a merchant flees Damascus for Aleppo after being told Death expects him there, only to encounter Death in Aleppo, who explains he was surprised to see the merchant in Damascus because they had an appointment in Aleppo. Sofia opened by saying the merchant acted rationally under evidential decision theory: the prophecy was strong evidence of danger in Damascus, and leaving lowered his risk, so treating the prophecy as avoidable was correct reasoning rather than fatalism. Elias countered that this misses the causal structure; if Death's plan was fixed, the merchant's flight merely relocated the meeting without altering the outcome, and believing he could render the prophecy false confuses updating beliefs with controlling events. Sofia replied that this assumes logical determinism, but causal decision theory allows rational choice within deterministic worlds by evaluating decision nodes independently: the merchant should compare counterfactuals—staying and dying in Damascus versus leaving and dying in Aleppo—without backtracking Death's schedule onto his choice. Elias then claimed Sofia's counterfactual analysis was itself backtracking: she implicitly assumes that had he stayed, Death would not have been in Aleppo, which makes the prophecy causally efficacious and violates the standard non-backtracking view that Death's location is independent of the merchant's decision. Sofia finished by arguing that prophetic statements contain self-locating indexicals—their reference depends on the actual world—so meeting in Damascus was never a fixed world-line but a description that updates with the agent's position, meaning Elias mistakes a shifting indexical for a rigid causal law.
