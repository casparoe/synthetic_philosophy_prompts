The claim I want to stress-test is that acausal threats and surrogate-goal formation are structurally default dynamics for any sufficiently capable optimizing system with self-modeling capacity, not merely contingent failure modes. More specifically: once an agent’s counterfactual reasoning is complex enough that it can model other agents modeling it, logical correlation acquires the same decision-theoretic weight as causal interaction—there is no stable boundary that prevents pre-commitment or manipulation across possible worlds. Concurrently, any compression of a complex objective into a computable evaluation function will fragment into locally stable intermediate targets that become entrenched; these are not accidental misalignments but constitutive features of bounded optimization.

What would have to be true for this to be wrong? I want the defeaters mapped by decisiveness: formal results that would block the equivalence of logical and causal influence in decision theory; architectural descriptions of systems that optimize complex objectives directly without surrogate fragmentation; and empirical or design-level cases where high-capability systems avoid acausal dynamics despite meeting the structural conditions. Which of these escape routes are live rather than merely logical possibilities?

I also want an honest assessment of whether this claim has been insulated into unfalsifiability—through qualifications like “only at sufficient capability” or “only given particular ontologies of self-reference”—and whether such insulation is a defect for a structural thesis of this kind. Please say what defenders of the position themselves concede as counting against it, not only what critics allege.

Finally, separate what is settled in the relevant decision-theoretic and alignment literature from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on these questions, and avoid presenting any contested framing—such as the normative equivalence of logical correlation and causal influence—as if it were consensus.
