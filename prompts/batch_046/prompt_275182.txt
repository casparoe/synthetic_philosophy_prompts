I'm moderating a colloquium Q&A with a historian who has just published a monograph defending what she calls "terminal structuralism": a neo-Braudelian thesis that long-duration geographic and climatic structures do not merely constrain but effectively determine the terminal state of social formations, allowing individual agents and discrete events only to alter velocity or intermediate path, never the endpoint. She accepts that without Hitler the specific institutional form of the German state in 1945 might have differed, but insists that the geopolitical restructuring of Central Europe and the demographic and economic shifts driven by long-term climatic and agrarian patterns would have produced functionally equivalent outcomes by the mid-twentieth century. Write me a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that avoid generic biographical openings and instead press on the mechanics of her view. I want at least one question on how she distinguishes empirically between a "structural channel" and an apparently open historical path; one on whether her velocity/path distinction collapses once we admit that different intermediate paths can feed back to alter the structural conditions themselves; one on what her framework implies for the moral evaluation of individuals who operate inside tightly constrained channels—specifically whether attributing blame requires the possibility of having accessed a genuinely different structural endpoint; and for each, include a sharp follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give (for instance, that feedback loops are already priced into the structural model, or that moral evaluation attaches to path-choice rather than endpoint-selection). End with a question that asks what kind of evidence—say, a counterfactual case where climate and geography remained constant but leadership produced a radically divergent long-term outcome—would count as falsifying her view rather than being absorbed as a mere variation in velocity.
