I am preparing a module for my graduate seminar on the philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence, and I find myself repeatedly noticing an argumentative pattern in the recent interpretability literature that I should like to classify with some precision. When researchers intervene upon a particular feature map and observe a pronounced degradation in the network's performance on a target concept, they frequently present this as direct evidence for a localised representational vehicle. Yet when an equivalent intervention produces no discernible effect, the same authors tend to invoke distributed or redundant encoding, treating the absence of a localised effect not as a failure of the representational hypothesis but as evidence that the representation is merely spread across multiple units. In a further variant, a feature activates for stimuli that bear no obvious semantic relation to the target concept — for instance, responding both to images of a specific breed of dog and to particular background textures — and the interpretation offered is that the feature is polysemantic or context-dependent, thereby preserving its representational status within the appropriate contextual boundary. In each of these three configurations, the underlying commitment to representational realism remains intact, but the evidential standard shifts to accommodate the structural finding rather than being constrained by it in advance.

What I am seeking is not a general complaint about unfalsifiability, but the specific name — if one exists — for this kind of theoretical elasticity, in which contradictory empirical structures are systematically re-described as complementary manifestations of a single underlying representational reality. How precisely does it differ from a conventional auxiliary hypothesis, from protective-belt adjustments within a research programme, or from straightforward ad hoc immunisation? If the literature offers no established term for exactly this rhythm, I should welcome a carefully defined coinage, together with its boundary conditions: under what circumstances does adaptive reinterpretation cross the line into this particular failure mode, and what pattern of empirical results would genuinely disrupt the framework rather than being absorbed into it? I should be grateful if you would present your analysis in British English, and please avoid bibliographic lists or citation requests, as I am interested in the conceptual carving rather than in a survey of sources.
