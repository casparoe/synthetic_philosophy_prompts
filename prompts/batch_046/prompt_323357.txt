You are advising a consortium of regional museums and digital archives that is piloting an "interpretive escrow" protocol for artifacts with contested provenance or use. Under this protocol, when primary documentation supports multiple incompatible historical narratives, the public record must preserve full documentary fidelity—presenting sources with minimal framing—while also explicitly displaying the competing interpretive frameworks that different historians might apply. The designers intend this to honor both Leopold von Ranke's source-critical commitment to presenting the past as it actually occurred and the sceptical challenges raised by E.H. Carr and Hayden White regarding the inevitability of narrative selection, emplotment, and the historian's constructive role.

Please describe the most important implications of this tension for the practical design and ethical obligations of the escrow system. Your analysis should address: (1) what documentary fidelity can mean when the choice of which sources to include, in what order, and with what contextual annotations is itself an act of narrative construction; (2) whether the escrow model resolves the objectivity problem or merely relocates it into the procedural rules governing admission and exclusion of documents; (3) the consequences for public epistemic trust when museum visitors are presented with unresolved, competing accounts rather than a single authoritative narrative; and (4) whether a defensible middle position exists that allows the archive to be transparent about its constructive choices without collapsing into a relativism that undermines its institutional authority. Ground your answer in the actual positions associated with Ranke, Carr, and White, but apply them specifically to the mechanics of this escrow protocol rather than to historiography in general.
