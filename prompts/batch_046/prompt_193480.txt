I'm in AP bio and we just finished the unit on evolution, and my teacher mentioned how biologists fight about whether natural selection works on genes, on individuals, or on whole groups like populations. Ever since then I've been noticing this weird move in explanations I see online and in my textbook's sidebars, but I don't know if it has a name. For example, when people explain why a worker bee dies after stinging, they say it's because her genes are 'selfish' and she's just a vehicle for them—so the gene is the real unit being selected. But then when they talk about deer in some region that evolved smaller antlers so they don't overgraze and wipe out the herd, suddenly they switch and say the group is the unit and group selection explains it. The thing is, nobody lays out rules in advance for which level applies. It feels like the level gets picked after you already know the outcome: if the behavior looks self-sacrificing, zoom in to genes; if it looks cooperative, zoom out to groups. That makes evolutionary theory feel unfalsifiable, like there's always a level you can point to that saves the explanation.

I know multilevel selection theory is legit and people argue about it for real, and I know there's that gene-centered view from back in the 70s, but this feels different from just disagreeing about levels. It's more like using 'unit of selection' as a free dial you can turn to protect the theory from being wrong. Is there an established concept for that exact methodological pattern—this after-the-fact sliding between gene-level and group-level framing to avoid falsification? If there isn't a name, can you coin one with a clear definition, and explain how it differs from ordinary multilevel selection and from plain ad hoc reasoning? Also please seperate what's settled in evolutionary biology from what's genuinely contested among people who actually study this, and don't present one view as the consensus if experts are still divided.
