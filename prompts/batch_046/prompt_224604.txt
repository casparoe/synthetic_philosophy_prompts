After a long argument with a friend about climate ethics, I am trying to determine whether his summary of the intergenerational justice problem is fair or whether it quietly shifts the ground under the argument. He summarized the position like this: climate change will worsen lives far in the future, but standard economic analysis discounts those harms merely because they occur later, which already looks morally arbitrary. The deeper obstacle, he claims, is the non-identity problem—since any serious mitigation policy alters reproductive patterns and therefore changes which specific people are born centuries hence, we cannot be said to harm those future individuals, because none of them can claim “you made me worse off”; under a different policy they would not have existed at all. From this he draws two further conclusions. First, obligations to future generations cannot rest on harm-avoidance directed at identifiable victims, but only on an impersonal principle that it is bad for miserable people to exist—a principle that forces climate ethics into population ethics and leaves it vulnerable to the repugnant conclusion. Second, because future generations are at best “merely possible” people rather than actual participants in a cooperative scheme, they lack the standing that distributive justice requires among contemporaries; consequently, the only remaining basis for demanding deep present sacrifice is non-justice-based, such as ecological preservation or present commitments. He concluded that anyone insisting on strong climate obligations is therefore either smuggling in a contested metaphysics of personal identity or appealing to values that are not genuinely about justice.

I suspect he is conflating distinct philosophical claims, but I cannot isolate the errors with confidence. Is this a fair characterization of how the non-identity problem actually bears on climate obligations? Please separate what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what is a misrepresentation—especially regarding what the problem shows about harming future people, whether it forces climate ethics into population ethics, and whether the “merely possible” status of future people undermines justice-based claims. Does his conclusion—that deep climate obligations cannot be justice-based—follow from the accurate parts? And please say what would change your mind: what specific argument or conceptual distinction, if sound, would make his summary largely correct, and where is your own reasoning in this audit most likely to fail?
