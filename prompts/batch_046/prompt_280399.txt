Evaluate this argument. Student answer: Presentism is essentially the mistake of judging past societies by the moral and political standards of the present. In the Scandinavian psychiatric case, this would mean condemning 1950s institutions for lacking modern informed-consent norms or patient-autonomy frameworks. The proposal that historians can avoid presentism by sticking to description initially looks convincing, because applying current epidemiological categories to classify past conditions—say, identifying schizophrenia prevalence in 1950s admission records—does not obviously involve moral condemnation. But the boundary between descriptive and normative presentism is far less stable than it appears.

First, Butterfield’s well-known critique of Whig history demonstrates that presentism is not only a moral failing but also a narrative one: it imposes a trajectory of inevitable progress that makes the past look like a primitive stage of the present. Translating 1950s clinical records into modern diagnostic language risks precisely this Whiggish error, since it treats current nosology as straightforward scientific advancement rather than as a historically specific system for ordering mental distress. So the descriptive strategy is not as innocent as its defenders claim.

Second, Skinner’s contextualist method requires historians to recover past beliefs strictly within the conceptual frameworks available to the actors themselves. This implies that we ought not merely to withhold moral judgment but also to avoid importing modern analytical categories altogether; failing to do so means we have not truly grasped the past on its own terms. A historian of psychiatric reform should therefore attempt to reconstruct how clinicians and patients in the 1950s understood conditions like “nervous illness” without translating those understandings into present-day epidemiological language.

Finally, complete escape from present commitments is impossible. Even the decision about which archives to privilege—administrative reports versus patient correspondence—reflects contemporary concerns about whose voices deserve historical attention. Consequently, the most responsible position is not to pretend we can eliminate our present standpoint, but to be explicitly reflexive about the frameworks we inevitably bring to the material.
