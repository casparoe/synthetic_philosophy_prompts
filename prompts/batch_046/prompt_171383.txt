A colleague recently summarized the contrast between counterfactual and interventionist theories of causation for me as follows: "Lewis's counterfactual analysis defines causation in terms of world-comparison—X causes Y if, holding the actual laws fixed, there are nearby worlds in which X is absent and Y is absent as well. Woodward's interventionist account rejects this modal machinery entirely: rather than asking what would happen in non-actual worlds, it asks whether an intervention on X—defined as an exogenous change that severs all incoming causal arrows to X—would change Y in accordance with an invariant structural equation. Because Woodward never appeals to counterfactual worlds, his theory avoids all the well-known puzzles that plague Lewis, such as late preemption and symmetric overdetermination. But this victory comes at a specific cost: since an intervention must be a physically realizable perturbation to a variable, the account is implicitly restricted to domains where such perturbations are technically feasible, which is why critics say interventionism is not a general metaphysics of causation but merely a theory of experimental control."
