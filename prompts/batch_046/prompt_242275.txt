I've been thinking about longtermism ever since a close colleague began working on existential-risk mitigation, and I've come to hold a somewhat contrarian view about the simulation argument. Rather than amplifying our obligations to the far future, I think it dampens them: if the standard ancestor-simulation reasoning is sound, then the future we are being urged to shape is likely nested inside a truncated, observationally bounded run whose terminal conditions and evaluative criteria are set by simulators with unknown—probably non-cosmic—interests. Our present actions would then influence at best a simulated trajectory, not an open-ended base-reality future, and the moral weight we should assign to that influence is constrained by our ignorance of what the simulators value. This introduces a kind of causal-evaluative insulation that undermines the urgency of far-future optimization. What would have to be true for this to be wrong? I want the defeaters ranked by severity: which premises about simulation prevalence, nested escape, or the moral relevance of simulated outcomes must fail? Which of these would defenders of the view concede as damaging, and which could they absorb? And is there a danger that the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if every longtermist reply can be recast as just another layer of nesting—and does that insulation count as a philosophical virtue or a vice?
