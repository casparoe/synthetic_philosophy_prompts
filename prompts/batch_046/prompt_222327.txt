Having spent thirty years in experimental physics studying emergent behavior in complex systems, I have recently turned to philosophy of mind with a working scientist’s suspicion of analogies that borrow mathematical authority without paying the full conceptual debt. The family of theories I keep encountering—those that treat Gödel’s first incompleteness result, specifically the construction of a self-referential arithmetical sentence that asserts its own unprovability, not as a formal curiosity but as a structural template for human self-consciousness—strikes me as precisely such a case. Whether cast in strange-loop terms, in arguments that human mathematicians can recognize truths that escape any fixed formal system, or in newer attempts to map formal self-reference onto distributed predictive self-modeling in neuroscience, these accounts share a core move: they take syntactic self-reference within a formal system and claim it explains, or at least illuminates, first-person awareness.

Before I evaluate any particular version, I want the full desiderata—the complete specification of what a Gödelian theory of mind must actually deliver to count as adequate rather than merely evocative. Give me the requirements list, with the motivating case or conceptual distinction behind each. Specifically: (1) how the theory must bridge formal, symbol-level self-reference (a formula encoding its own Gödel number) and phenomenological self-awareness, without collapsing the distinction between syntactic manipulation and subjective presence or transparency; (2) whether it must preserve Gödel’s consistency constraint—that a sufficiently powerful formal system cannot prove its own consistency—and if so, whether applying this to biological cognition requires treating the brain as a fixed-axiom system, given neural plasticity, learning, and evolutionary development; (3) the scope of self-awareness it must cover—explicit logical self-knowledge, embodied agency, emotional self-monitoring, implicit predictive self-modeling—and whether failure on any subset is fatal; (4) its required stance on mechanism: must it demonstrate that self-awareness is non-computational, or can it remain neutral between mechanist and anti-mechanist interpretations; (5) consistency with empirical neuroscience and psychology, particularly findings that self-representation is distributed, context-dependent, and lacking any single centralized module; and (6) whether any pair of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable—for example, strict fidelity to Gödel’s formal conditions combined with evolutionary biological plausibility, or the demand for phenomenological immediacy combined with the opacity inherent to formal undecidability. For each desideratum, indicate whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp, identify which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates, and flag any subset you believe cannot be jointly satisfied given what we know about formal systems and cognitive architecture. Keep the weight on the criteria and their internal tensions rather than on scoring theorists, though brief remarks on how Hofstadter’s strange-loop proposal, Penrose’s anti-computationalist argument, and Lucas’s original formulation fare against your list are welcome.

Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am working without access to external sources and need the conceptual architecture alone. If you invoke specific results—such as the conditions under which Gödel’s theorem applies, or analogies to Turing’s halting problem—state them in your own terms rather than quoting sources. I am particularly interested in whether the analogy survives once we insist that biological minds are not closed formal systems with fixed axiomatizations, and whether that insistence is itself a non-negotiable desideratum or an unnecessary prejudice imported from natural science.
