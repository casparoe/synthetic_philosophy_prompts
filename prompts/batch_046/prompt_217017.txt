Consider an intergenerational justice panel that applies Karl Jaspers's fourfold distinction—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt—to collective cases. Its rulings so far: citizens of a regime bear political guilt for state actions without personal criminal guilt; descendants do not inherit metaphysical guilt, since solidarity with human failure is not genealogical; a nation may hold collective political responsibility requiring institutional repair; moral guilt for collaboration is not transferable to those born after the events; and cultural guilt, understood as lasting damage to a shared ethical and linguistic environment, can persist across generations without personal culpability. Now examine present-day citizens of a democracy whose grandparents implemented a forced assimilation policy against an indigenous minority that ended fifty years ago, after which the state formally apologized, the minority's language transmission was permanently disrupted, and current citizens continue to benefit from the land and resource allocations that resulted. Predict whether the panel will find any Jaspers-style guilt here, articulate the two competing interpretive rules that could account for all five prior rulings, indicate which rule you take to be implicit and why, describe the single further case that would adjudicate between those rules, and state the principle the panel would be adopting with each possible verdict. Present exactly five numbered items, each limited to one or two sentences, with no introductory or concluding text, using American English.
