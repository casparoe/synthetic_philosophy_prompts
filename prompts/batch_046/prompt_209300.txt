So far this sounds correct to me. The essay then argues that because this centre lacks any discrete neural substrate—there is no self-module that could be removed or damaged in isolation—the self is therefore nothing more than a pure fiction, comparable to Sherlock Holmes or a character in a novel, possessing no causal powers whatsoever. The author adds that Dennett's view therefore amounts to a form of eliminativism about personal identity, despite Dennett's own insistence that he is not denying the reality of the self. Furthermore, the essay connects this narrative abstraction to recent work in predictive processing, proposing that the brain constructs what the author calls a narrative Markov blanket in order to minimise free energy, so that Dennett's abstract centre is simultaneously a statistical regularity instantiated in hierarchical Bayesian inference. From this the author concludes that the self is both fictional and physically real, but only as a probabilistic boundary, and that this resolves the tension in Dennett's position.

Something about this strikes me as confused, though I cannot pinpoint exactly where. I have heard that Dennett intends the centre-of-gravity analogy to show that the self is real but not a thing in the ordinary sense—rather like a centre of mass, which is perfectly real and has causal effects even though you cannot point to it as a separate object. Yet the essay's comparison to Sherlock Holmes makes it sound as though Dennett regards the self as causally inert, and I am unsure whether the invocation of Markov blankets and free energy is a legitimate extension of his view or an anachronistic distortion. I am also uncertain whether Dennett really is an eliminativist in disguise. Could you please assess whether this summary is fair?
