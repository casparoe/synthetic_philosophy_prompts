Question 3: In Nyāya philosophy, śabda (testimony) counts as an independent pramāṇa, not just a subtype of anumāna. Explain the argument for this independence, especially addressing the objection that understanding a speaker requires first inferring their mental state or intention. How do the commentators resolve this without collapsing testimony back into inference?

Student answer: Nyāya philosophers like Gautama in the Nyāya-sūtra and later Udayana in the Nyāyavārttika accept four pramāṇas: pratyakṣa, anumāna, upamāna, and śabda. The independence of śabda comes from the fact that language operates through conventional association (saṅketa) rather than logical entailment. For example, if someone says there is fire on the hill, I don't need to observe smoke and then infer fire; the sentence itself conveys the knowledge directly once I understand the words.

The objection about mental states gets things backwards. Vātsyāyana in his commentary points out that checking whether the speaker is reliable or what they intend is only necessary when there's already doubt — it's an exceptional repair mechanism, not part of normal comprehension. So the inference about the speaker's mind happens after testimony has done its work, not before. That preserves śabda as its own source.

There's also a practical point from Udayana: if testimony were really inference, then every unfamiliar word or sentence would require constructing a full syllogism, which clearly doesn't match how we actually learn language. Plus, comparison (upamāna) only works for things we've seen analogies for, whereas testimony lets us know about distant planets or past events we've never observed. So reducing śabda to anumāna fails both conceptually and in practice.
