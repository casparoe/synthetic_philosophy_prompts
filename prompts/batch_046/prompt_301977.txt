A friend and I were arguing about Davidson's radical interpertation for a seminar paper I'm sketching, and I think I built an argument that makes his view collapse into something way stronger than he wants — like, it doesn't just block radical conceptual schemes but wipes out ontological disagreement altogether. But I can't spot the leak. Here's the chain:

(1) In radical interpretation we start with no shared language and only behavorial evidence — assent and dissent in observable conditions. (2) Davidson says we solve for beleif and meaning simultaneously, since there's no independent access to mental content outside interpretation. (3) The principle of charity forces us to maximize true belief and rational coherence in the speaker. (4) Now imagine an interpreter who is a nominalist about properties, facing a speaker who consistently assents to sentences in the presence of what the interpreter takes to be merely similar particular objects. (5) To preserve charity, the interpreter can't just say the speaker is systematically wrong about properties; instead she revises her own ontology and interprets the speaker as tracking real universals, since that makes the speaker's beliefs mostly true. (6) But this reasoning applies across the board — whenever there's ontological disagreement, charity pressures the interpreter to adopt the speaker's ontology rather than attributing widespread error. (7) So radical interpretation doesn't just determine word-meaning; it forces the interpreter's own world-view to converge on whatever makes the speaker maximally rational. (8) If interpretation is always possible in principle, then there can be only one ontology compatible with rationality — the one shared by all interpreters. (9) That means genuine ontological disagreement (say about whether moral properties exist, or whether sets are real) isn't just hard to understand but literally uninterpretible, since attributing false beliefs about ontology violates charity. (10) But Davidson clearly wants to allow disagreement; he just denies radically alien conceptual schemes. So where's the slip? Is it in (5) that charity doesn't require ontological revision? Or in (7) that interpretation doesn't reshape the interpreter's world? Or is the problem in (3) — that charity is only a default, not an iron law? I'd really like to keep (2) and (6), since the interdependence of beleif and meaning and the pressure to converge seem central to Davidson, but I'm open to paying elsewhere if that's where the cost is lowest. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost the view?
