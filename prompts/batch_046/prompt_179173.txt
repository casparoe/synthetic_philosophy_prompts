I am attempting to articulate the principles that govern warranted inferences from material remains to past beliefs and institutions, and I should like to test my intuitions against a set of concrete archaeological cases.

My verdicts are as follows. First, when dozens of ceramic vessels in a single pit exhibit identical patterns of clean, rim-focused breakage with no scatter of wear, I take this as strongly supporting ritual destruction rather than accidental discard. Second, when a large administrative structure contains writing tablets but no implements for inscription, I do not take the absence of styluses as evidence that literacy was restricted elsewhere; absence here seems uninformative. Third, a solitary foreign coin within an otherwise homogeneous local hoard strikes me as insufficient to infer economic syncretism or institutional integration. Fourth, when three successive rebuildings of a sanctuary maintain post alignments corresponding to the same solar event, I think we are warranted in inferring an enduring institutional commitment to calendrical practice, even in the absence of textual corroboration. Fifth, the discovery of metrically standardised weights in domestic contexts across a settlement supports, in my view, an inference to formal institutional regulation of exchange rather than merely customary reciprocity.

Yet I am troubled by an apparent asymmetry. In one cemetery, grave goods are remarkably uniform across all burial strata, and I am inclined to treat this as only weak evidence for egalitarian social institutions, preferring to explain it through shared mortuary custom. In another region, pottery decoration is remarkably uniform across distant sites, and I am similarly inclined to resist inferring centralised political control, citing instead the diffusion of craft tradition. By contrast, I allow uniformity of weights and of architectural alignment to carry institutional weight. Is there a principled distinction—perhaps concerning the independence of the pattern from individual preference, or the differential cost of false positives—that reconciles these verdicts? Or must I abandon one?

Please set out the principle, or competing candidates, with precision; indicate which of my verdicts each fails to accommodate; and apply it to a case I have not listed—say, the inference from a sudden, region-wide shift in burial orientation to a change in cosmological belief versus a change in practical land-use. I should be grateful for the answer in British English, and I do not require bibliographical references.
