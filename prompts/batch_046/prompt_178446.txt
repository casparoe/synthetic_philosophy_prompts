I've recently worked through Yuval Noah Harari's Sapiens and then David Graeber and David Wengrow's The Dawn of Everything, and I'm stuck trying to reconcile two very different accounts of how human societies grew large and stayed coherent. Harari's story centers on what he describes as intersubjective realities — shared fictions like money, nations, or religious narratives that allow strangers to cooperate without personal trust. Graeber and Wengrow, by contrast, seem to emphasize that ancient communities often maintained large-scale settlements not by locking everyone into a single belief system, but by deliberately oscillating between hierarchical and egalitarian social forms, so that power could never fully crystallize. I find both pictures compelling, but they point in opposite directions about what actually binds a big society together.

To think about this more precisely, I've been using a frame I invented for myself: the binding problem of scale. Once a group is too large for kinship and daily face-to-face negotiation to hold it together, something else has to do the work of coordination. Harari's answer is that an abstract shared fiction — something that exists only in collective imagination — becomes the glue. The alternative I draw from Graeber and Wengrow is what I might call institutional schismogenesis: a social architecture in which authority and equality alternate seasonally or ritually, preventing any permanent hierarchy from forming and thereby removing the need for a permanent legitimating myth. In this view, the binding agent is not a shared story about reality, but a shared commitment to keeping every story about reality temporary.

Can you compare these two frameworks — Harari's intersubjective fiction versus this idea of institutional schismogenesis — as rival solutions to the binding problem of scale? In particular, which one offers a more convincing explanation for large, dense settlements such as Çatalhöyük, where the archaeological record suggests extensive collective ritual and shared storage yet very little evidence of centralized rulers, palaces, or fixed social stratification? And if the two explanations are not simply incompatible, is there a coherent way to combine them that does not collapse into the vague claim that both matter? I want to understand whether shared fiction is genuinely doing the coordinating work, or whether the real work is being done by a flexible institutional design that stops fiction from ever hardening into domination.
