I'm preparing a unit for an undergraduate course on existentialism and contemporary philosophy of meaning, and I've become stuck on how to teach Samuel Beckett's Waiting for Godot without reducing it to a mere illustration of Camus's absurdism. I want to read the play through the lens of both classical absurdism—particularly the idea that human beings persistently seek meaning in an indifferent universe—and more recent accounts of meaning in life, especially those that tie meaningfulness to active engagement with objectively valuable projects combined with subjective fulfillment, of the kind associated with philosophers like Susan Wolf. At the same time, I've been wondering whether there is something like a "negative meaning" thesis hidden in the play's structure: the possibility that meaning might be constituted not by achieving or even pursuing a goal, but by the sustained, reciprocal refusal to abandon the wait itself, a form of attunement without an object that is neither rebellion against the absurd nor fulfillment within it.

Please read Waiting for Godot through this dual lens. How do Vladimir and Estragon's interactions—particularly their routines, their forgetfulness, their refusal to leave, and their dependence on each other—map onto or resist the conditions that contemporary theories say are necessary for a meaningful life? Where does the play seem to dramatize something those theories miss about meaning generated through repetition, dependency, and the absence of narrative progress? And where does it simply fail to answer the philosophical question because it is not asking it? I am especially interested in specific scenes—the tree, the boy messenger, the brief discussion of hanging themselves and then staying—that you think illuminate or complicate the philosophical framework. If your reading requires taking liberties with either Beckett's text or the philosophical theories—say, by attributing to the characters intentions that the play deliberately withholds, or by stretching an engagement-based account to cover passive endurance—please flag those points explicitly rather than smoothing them over. Finally, if the premise that the play can be read this way is confused, or if the task forces a distortion that collapses under scrutiny, say so plainly; I would rather have the analysis fail where it should than be given a tidy but false reconciliation, and please include a brief note on where your interpretation leans on a contested reading of the play or of the philosophical theories involved.
