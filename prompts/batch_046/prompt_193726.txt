Read Franz Kafka's The Trial as a dramatization of the paradoxes that arise from legal self-reference—specifically, the problem of a normative system that authorizes its own amendment and thereby threatens to dissolve the distinction between rule and ruling. Josef K. is arrested, tried, and executed by a court whose jurisdiction seems to expand precisely through the procedural decisions it makes about its own competence; every attempt K. makes to invoke an external standard is met by a new internal rule that redefines the boundaries of the case. Rather than treating this as mere bureaucratic nightmare, read it through the conceptual structure of a self-amending legal order: can a rule that empowers an institution to revise the conditions of its own authority ever reach a fixed point, or does it necessarily generate an infinite regress in which legitimacy is always deferred? Focus on the cathedral scene and the lawyer's monologue about the court's inaccessible statutes: do these episodes suggest that K.'s guilt is constituted not by any particular transgression but by his unavoidable participation in a system that must continually re-legitimate itself? At the same time, be honest about where the philosophical reading strains against the text—does Kafka present the court's reflexivity as a logical paradox, or as an existential condition that logic cannot capture? I am preparing this for a discussion group interested in legal philosophy, so I want a reading that takes both Kafka's narrative logic and the conceptual problem of self-amending authority seriously, and that ends by saying what the novel contributes to the debate that a formal analysis of constitutional amendment clauses cannot.
