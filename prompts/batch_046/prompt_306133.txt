I'm a journalist writing a feature on pedagogical experiments in moral philosophy, and I'm preparing a piece on classroom activities that make the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism felt rather than merely stated. I run an advanced undergraduate ethics seminar — about sixteen students, one ninety-minute session — and I want a structured, participatory exercise that makes them encounter the conceptual gap between intra-personal trade-offs and inter-personal aggregation. The core idea should emerge from the rules and stakes, not from a lecture up front: participants should physically enact the choice between preserving subject-boundaries and optimizing a collective welfare sum, and only afterwards should the facilitator name the objection, map the experience onto the standard critique of utilitarian aggregation, and show where each step could be resisted.

Please design a fully runnable activity: specify roles or rules, materials, a timeline, and a symbolic payoff or feedback scheme that requires no real money. To keep the design out of well-trodden pre-training territory, inject a non-standard framing — for instance, a boundary protocol in which every welfare token carries an invisible phenomenal ownership tag that is erased the moment aggregation is activated — so that the group must decide whether to preserve these tags or dissolve them for a larger pooled total. The mechanics should ensure that participants run into the problem themselves before it is named.

Then provide a debrief that connects specific moments in the game to parts of the separateness objection (the non-transferability of experience, the questionable analogy with distribution over time within one life, the loss of distinct moral standing), predicts what will go wrong the first time the activity is run and how to handle it, and is frank about which feature of the philosophical view the exercise cannot reproduce and must be supplied by discussion.

Finally, please say what argument or consideration would change your mind about whether this design genuinely captures the force of the objection, and point to the exact place in your reasoning about the activity where it is most vulnerable to collapse.
