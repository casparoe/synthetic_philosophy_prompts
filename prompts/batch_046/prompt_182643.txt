Thomson's violinist case shows that having a right to life does not entail a right to use another person's body without consent. If I am hooked up to a violinist against my will, I may unplug even if he dies. So in the remote-connection case, Alex never agreed to the metabolic bridge, which means the burden is imposed without agreement. Therefore Alex can flip the switch.

Marquis argues that killing is wrong because it deprives a victim of a future like ours. The foetus in the synthetic womb has a future like ours — it could develop into a person with projects and experiences. So disconnecting kills it and takes away that future, which makes the act wrong.

However, because the link is wireless and not physically inside Alex's body, Thomson's bodily-autonomy argument does not apply here. The invasion is not bodily, so there is no right to disconnect on autonomy grounds.

Also, the foetus has not yet developed consciousness or personal identity, so it does not actually possess a future like ours in the relevant sense. Marquis requires an actual future of lived experiences, not mere potential. Therefore his argument also fails in this scenario.

Overall, I think Alex is not required to stay connected, because synthetic-womb technology removes the bodily burden, but they are still wrong to disconnect since it kills a being with potential. Actually, if both arguments fail then there is no clear duty either way, though the lack of consent probably supports disconnection.
