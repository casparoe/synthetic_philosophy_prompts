Having retired from experimental physics after thirty years and only recently begun serious study of metaphysics, I find myself struck by a disciplinary asymmetry: in physics we tolerate claims about unobservable entities only if they yield testable predictions, whereas in philosophy one may posit infinitely many concrete universes without specifying the instrumentation needed to distinguish them from noise. I therefore request a satirical piece—approximately 600 to 700 words, formatted as a deadpan grant proposal submitted by an invented retired physicist-turned-philosopher, Dr. Elias Vance, to a fictional university committee called the Interdisciplinary Board for Ontological Instrumentation. The proposal should seek funding to construct a “Modal Isolation Chamber” and apply a fictional experimental protocol designated MIP-7 (Modal Isolation Protocol 7) intended to detect “counterpart resonance” across David Lewis’s concrete possible worlds. The satire should target both the bureaucratic genre of scientific funding applications—complete with numbered sections: Abstract, Theoretical Background, Proposed Apparatus, Expected Outcomes, and Budget Justification—and the literalist interpretation of Lewisian modal realism that treats spatiotemporally isolated worlds as empirically accessible entities. The humor must come from exaggerating a genuine tension rather than inventing a strawman: modal realism’s insistence on the concreteness of worlds invites an empirical reading, yet the causal and spatiotemporal isolation of those worlds renders verification impossible, leaving the applicant to propose ever more expensive detectors for signals that are definitionally undetectable. The voice should remain deadpan, formal, and slightly bewildered, as though the author genuinely believes metaphysical hypotheses ought to be subjected to laboratory protocols. To keep the piece legible without specialist knowledge, explain counterpart relations and the indexicality of “actual” in passing, using plain language; to keep it fair to specialists, do not misrepresent Lewis’s view—rather, push its literal implications to an absurd but logically consistent endpoint. Do not include bibliographies, citations, references to living scholars, or requests for reading lists. You may invent institutional names, budget figures, and technical specifications freely, since these are part of the parody’s machinery and should not depend on any obscure real-world knowledge. The result should be readable to someone unfamiliar with modal realism and recognizably fair to someone who knows the literature well.
