In my philosophy of language seminar last week we turned to Austin’s distinction between constative and performative utterances, and the discussion quickly drifted into digital contexts: whether typing “I bet fifty dollars on this” in a group chat, clicking “I agree” on a terms-of-service banner, or posting “I hereby resign” in a work Slack channel counts as genuinely performing the named act. One student kept invoking the slogan “saying is doing” to shut down every qualification—if the words are uttered in what looks like the right context, the deed is done, so asking about speaker intent, institutional framing, or whether a chat interface supplies the requisite authority is just a category mistake. I am sure the move is too quick, but I cannot pinpoint exactly where the slide occurs between a harmless reminder that language is action and the claim that all ambiguity about commitment is dissolved.

Can you lay out, as a menu rather than a single reading, the distinct precise theses that “saying is doing” could be expressing? Start with the near-trivial claim that vocalizing or typing is itself a physical event; then move through the specifically Austinian thesis about explicit performative verbs operating within felicity conditions; then a stronger conventionalist thesis that institutional rules alone generate obligations regardless of speaker psychology; then something in the neighborhood of Searle’s claim that collective acceptance makes an utterance count as a promise, contract, or resignation within an institutional reality; and finally any radical or metaphysical readings in which language literally brings states of affairs into being without mediating conditions. For each, indicate whether it is a platitude, defensible only with heavy qualification, or straightforwardly false. Then tell me which of these readings actually supports my classmate’s use of the slogan—to dismiss questions about intent, platform architecture, or the gap between a casual message and a notarized contract in the digital examples above—and which readings, if taken consistently, would actually undermine his dismissal rather than reinforce it.
