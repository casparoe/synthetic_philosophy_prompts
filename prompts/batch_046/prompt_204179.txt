Following a rather protracted argument with a friend over dinner last evening, I found myself defending an extension of Daniel Dennett’s notion of the self as a centre of narrative gravity that I now suspect may be either genuinely illuminating or simply a category error dressed in philosophical language. The dispute concerned whether the concept, originally framed to dissolve the illusion of a Cartesian ego by substituting a theoretical point around which autobiographical stories orbit, can be coherently applied to distributed, non-biological entities—what one might call institutional or collective selves. By this I mean long-standing bodies such as constitutional courts, enduring scientific collaborations, or even certain algorithmic systems, where memory, decision-procedure, and self-description are spread across changing members or nodes, yet a recognisable narrative thread persists across time. My friend maintained that without a single locus of conscious experience to serve as the gravitational mass, the metaphor collapses into mere legal fiction; I countered that if the centre is fundamentally an explanatory posit for predicting behaviour and attributing responsibility, then a well-documented, self-revising institutional history might function analogously, with far-reaching consequences for how we understand diachronic identity and moral agency.

Please describe the most important implications of treating such distributed entities as genuine centres of narrative gravity. I should like you to address, in particular, whether their conditions of diachronic identity reduce to bare legal or functional continuity, or whether they retain a distinct metaphysical status that parallels individual personal identity; what becomes of moral responsibility when the purported centre lacks any unified phenomenal experience through which to integrate its actions; whether this extension obliges us to jettison the phenomenological dimension of Dennett’s original account or merely relegates it to one species within a broader taxonomy; and, finally, whether accepting institutional narrative selves commits us to granting analogous status to artificial agents that maintain coherent, self-revising autobiographical logs in the absence of biological embodiment. Please write your answer in British English, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, as I am interested solely in the conceptual architecture of the position itself.
