I am a doctoral researcher in archaeological theory and comparative historical sociology, and I have been asked to help draft the final examination for our MPhil seminar on societal transformation. In recent supervision meetings, my colleagues and I noticed that students can competently summarise the canonical literature—the environmental-collapse thesis associated with Jared Diamond, the diminishing-returns framework associated with Joseph Tainter, and the resilience-theory counter-narrative—without ever confronting what I have come to call the continuity paradox: the fact that archaeological discontinuity (abandoned urban centres, broken ceramic sequences, settlement desertion) is routinely interpreted as evidence that a society has objectively failed, when the very same material signatures might equally indicate successful adaptive dispersal, institutional devolution, or the migration of social knowledge into non-durable media that the archaeological record cannot capture. I want an essay question that makes this conceptual tension unavoidable. It should ask students to evaluate whether 'collapse' denotes an ontological state—a polity's inability to reproduce its core institutions—or whether it is primarily an epistemic framing effect generated by our dependence on durable material proxies. The question must require students to treat at least two of the three cases—the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya lowlands, and Rapa Nui—not as empirical illustrations to be described, but as conceptual test-beds through which to examine that distinction.

Please write a single essay question suitable for a 90-minute examination, together with a model answer of approximately 800 words and a marking scheme. The model answer should be written in British English. The marking scheme must identify five specific analytical moves that separate an excellent answer from a merely competent one, assign relative weight to each, and note the characteristic errors that should cost marks—particularly the error of treating the three cases as straightforward empirical confirmations of collapse rather than as problems for the concept itself. The question should specify an approximate word count (around 1,200 words) and should not ask students to produce bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations; the model answer should avoid page references and should be answerable by a well-prepared student who understands the conceptual structure of the debate rather than one who has memorised monographic detail.
