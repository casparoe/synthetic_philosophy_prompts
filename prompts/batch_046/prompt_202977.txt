Hey, I've been going to local planning meetings for like a year now and there's this one rhetorical move people make that I can't quite name. A homeowner gets up and says the proposed apartment building "isn't what we bought into," talking like there's an unwritten contract that their block stays single-family forever. They frame the zoning map as a promise the city made to them personally, even though zoning can change by vote and they never signed anything. What's funny is that the same people are totally fine with an ADU or a lot split if it makes them money — so it's not just "I hate change." It's more like they treat the current rules as a vested right running with their property, but only when it keeps other people out.

Does this pattern have a specifc name in planning theory or law? I know about NIMBYism, status-quo bias, and regulatory takings, but none of those really grab the pseudo-contractual vibe or the one-way-street aspect. Maybe it's close to the endowment effect applied to zoning, but that feels too general. If there's no exact term, could you coin one that captures the asymetrical nature — where people invoke an implicit deal only defensively — and map out exactly what's in, what's out, and were it blurs into plain selfishness? I care more about the carving than the label.

Please also say how confident you are in your answer and point out the part you're least sure about — I think the weakest link might be whether this is actually distinct from ordinary status-quo bias, so flag that if that's where your confidence drops.
