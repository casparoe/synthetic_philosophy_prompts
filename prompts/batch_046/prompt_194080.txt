Design a decision-tree taxonomy for the absent-minded driver problem, intended as a workshop handout that sorts participants by their substantive commitments rather than by the labels they prefer. The tree should begin with one root question—whether the rationality of the driver's plan is fixed at the outset or revisable at each indistinguishable node—and then branch through three further conceptual distinctions: (i) whether self-locating uncertainty at the exit nodes is treated as ordinary evidential uncertainty or as a structurally different kind; (ii) whether dynamic consistency is required across nodes the agent cannot tell apart; and (iii) whether the payoff of taking the correct exit is assessed from an ex-ante perspective or indexed to the agent's temporal standpoint at each node. Each leaf should carry a named position where one exists—such as a plan-based view, a causal-decision-theoretic resolution, an evidential resolution, or a no-plan dynamic view—together with its most awkward commitment, specifically what it implies about the driver's reasoning at the first exit when he remembers his plan but not his location. Note any pairs of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and diagnose any unoccupied combinations (for instance, a view that accepts evidential reasoning while insisting on strict dynamic consistency). To keep the instrument from being a mere reproduction of standard textbook classifications, build one branch around a distinction rarely emphasized in introductory treatments: whether the driver's absent-mindedness is best modeled as a contingent failure of memory or as an unavoidable structural feature of self-locating belief. Limit the tree to six or eight leaves. Conclude with a brief self-critical assessment: state what specific argument or observation about self-locating belief would change your verdict about which branches are coherent, and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
