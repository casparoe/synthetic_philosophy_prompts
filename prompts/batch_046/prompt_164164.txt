I am acting as an external examiner for a postgraduate module on comparative metaphysics and African philosophy, and I should be grateful for a rigorous assessment of the following examination response. The essay question asked candidates to distinguish Ubuntu’s relational conception of personhood from liberal-atomistic accounts of the self, specifically requiring them to clarify whether Ubuntu’s relationality should be read as an ontological thesis about the constitution of the self or as an ethical thesis about the conditions for human flourishing. Candidates were further asked to ground their analysis in one concrete social practice—such as communal deliberation, restorative justice, or the conferral of elder status—and to explain whether that practice supports an ontological or ethical interpretation.

The student begins by observing, correctly, that in the formulations associated with Menkiti and Gyekye personhood is treated as an acquired status rather than a biological given, which clearly distinguishes the Ubuntu tradition from the Lockean or Kantian presumption of an underlying atomic individual possessed of intrinsic dignity. They then examine the lekgotla—noting that it operates not merely as a procedural mechanism for reaching consensus but as a performative space in which recognition is reciprocally enacted, and they argue persuasively that this supports an ontological reading in which the self is literally constituted through communal interaction. At this point, however, they introduce a synthetic interpretation: they claim that Ubuntu’s relational self is best modelled through Whitehead’s process ontology, describing the individual as a societal actual occasion composed of prehensions of other community members. This is not a reading found in the classical Ubuntu literature and seems to conflate an indigenous metaphysical commitment with an imported metaphysical framework. They also misrepresent Kwasi Wiredu’s position, asserting that Wiredu explicitly rejects any ethical dimension of personhood in favour of a purely ontological account, when in fact Wiredu allows both dimensions to operate in tandem. Most subtly, they conclude that if personhood is genuinely relational in this strong sense, then individual moral responsibility for isolated actions is conceptually impossible—an inference that does not follow, since relational accounts typically distribute agency across networks rather than dissolving it altogether.

I should like you to identify which errors are merely awkward and which are genuinely damaging to the argument, and to distinguish between misreadings that a charitable examiner might excuse and those that reveal a fundamental misunderstanding. In particular, please indicate where your assessment is most vulnerable to error—what interpretation of the student’s Whitehead analogy or of Wiredu’s dual account, if adopted more charitably, would most likely lead you to revise your grade, and which part of your reasoning you regard as least secure.
