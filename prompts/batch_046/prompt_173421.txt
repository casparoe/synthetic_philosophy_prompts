I am currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on collective rationality and democratic theory (fourteen students, mostly MA and PhD, meeting for eighty minutes). I would like you to design a classroom simulation that lets us experience—rather than merely learn about—the discursive dilemma in judgment aggregation. To keep the exercise from feeling like a standard logic puzzle, I want you to embed a two-level structure that I have sketched out: first, the group votes separately on three concrete, logically interdependent propositions concerning a proposed university AI-use policy; second, once the inconsistent majority profile is revealed, the group must vote on which aggregation rule (premise-based versus conclusion-based) should govern the resolution. The rules should be arranged so that this meta-vote is itself vulnerable to the same structural paradox—majorities form on whether logical coherence should trump democratic equality and vice versa, with those priorities linked in a way that reproduces the inconsistency at the second order. The contradiction should emerge organically from the voting procedures before anyone names it; please avoid introducing terms such as “discursive dilemma” or “doctrinal paradox” until the debrief.

Specifically, please provide:
1. The concrete scenario with the three propositions, clearly stating the logical dependency among them.
2. The procedural rules, roles, or incentives that force the group into the contradiction at both levels.
3. A debrief plan that maps each procedural moment onto the formal structure of judgment aggregation, identifies what will predictably go wrong (e.g., attempts to dissolve logical links by reinterpretation, social pressure on minority voters), and states frankly which normative feature—most likely the claim that a collective is a unitary agent capable of holding beliefs—this simulation cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator.
4. A clear statement of what we should come away seeing: that maintaining democratic equality across all judgments conflicts with collective logical consistency, and that selecting an aggregation rule is itself a political decision that cannot be settled by further aggregation.

If this framing is confused—if a two-level classroom simulation cannot genuinely reproduce the normative tension, or if the task asks you to construct something incoherent—please say so plainly rather than accommodating the request politely.
