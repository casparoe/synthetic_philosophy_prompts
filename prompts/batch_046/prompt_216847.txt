I'm writing a paper on the architecture of practical reasoning and keep returning to a refined version of the absent-minded driver problem that I think standard treatments systematically misdiagnose. The case is familiar enough: a driver forms a clear intention to stop at a particular store on his usual route, recognizes the landmark as he approaches, yet drives straight past without braking; afterward he insists the intention was present—right there—but never became active in the decision-relevant moment. He is not distracted, not fatigued, and does not report any conflict between desire and action. The conventional explanations divide between those that posit a missing premise in the practical syllogism—he failed to form the occurrent belief that this is the store—and those that diagnose weakness of will, treating the lapse as an executive failure despite intact representational content.

I want you to argue for a structural, external-coupling explanation instead. Specifically, defend the view that the failure arises not inside the agent's reasoning chain but from the way his navigational plan was coupled to an environmental scaffold—his habitual route, the car's momentum, the absence of an explicit reminder—such that the standing intention was never retrieved into the control context, not because it was absent or suppressed, but because the routine that should have mediated its activation was configured to bypass that very retrieval point. To keep the argument from collapsing back into the standard internalist accounts, inject a somewhat niche framing: treat the car and the habitual route as a Gregorian artifact—an external structure that solves navigational problems by generating internal representations—whose presence actually suppresses the representational retrieval the driver needs. Your argument should precisely distinguish this account from both the missing-premise and the akrasia explanations; it should show why a genuinely standing intention can systematically fail to guide action without this being weakness of will by another name; and it should address the objection that if an intention is decoupled from its triggering environment in this way, the agent's practical reasoning is defective in exactly the manner the syllogistic account claims.
