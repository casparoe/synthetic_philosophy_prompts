I'm currently drafting a term paper for a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and I've become stuck on what seems like an unexamined tension in the Sleeping Beauty problem. In the canonical formulation, Beauty is sedated on Sunday; a fair coin is then flipped, and she is awakened once on Monday if the result is heads, or on both Monday and Tuesday—separated by amnesia—if the result is tails. Upon each awakening, she is asked for her credence that the coin landed heads. The prevailing "thirder" view maintains that her rational credence should be one-third, apparently on the grounds that there are three symmetric "centered worlds" she might currently inhabit: Heads-Monday, Tails-Monday, and Tails-Tuesday.

However, this reasoning seems to rest on a subtle confusion. Beauty does not learn that she has been randomly selected from a pool of awakening-instances; she merely learns the indexical fact that she is awake now, which is guaranteed under both outcomes—once under heads and twice under tails. If self-locating belief is genuinely about one's position within a centered world, then shouldn't the evidential weight attach only to the objective chance of the coin, leaving her prior credence unchanged at one-half? It strikes me that the one-third answer illegitimately treats temporal location as an empirically discovered random variable, and perhaps even smuggles in a metaphysics of multiplied subjects—Monday-Beauty and Tuesday-Beauty as distinct epistemic agents whose beliefs must be aggregated—rather than recognizing a single rational agent whose indexical framework conditions do not constitute new evidence. Could you clarify whether there is a principled way to defend the one-third result without committing to such ontological multiplication? Or does the problem genuinely demonstrate that standard Bayesian conditionalization must be abandoned for indexical propositions in favor of some separate calculus of observer-moment weighting?
