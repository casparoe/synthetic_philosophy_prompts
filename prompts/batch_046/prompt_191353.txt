The examination question was this: In his essay What Is Art?, Tolstoy argues that genuine art is defined by the transmission of feeling from artist to audience, and he distinguishes it from counterfeit art that relies on technical skill or addresses only an elite. Explain how Tolstoy’s criterion of sincerity relates to his broader moral purpose for art, and identify one feature that distinguishes his view from the expressive theory later developed by R. G. Collingwood.

The student wrote:

Tolstoy maintains that art is essentially a mechanism for emotional transmission: the artist experiences a particular feeling, encodes it in a work, and the audience becomes infected with the same feeling. Genuine art requires three conditions—sincerity on the part of the artist, clarity of expression, and universal accessibility—while counterfeit art fails on at least one, usually by substituting technical virtuosity for sincere feeling or by addressing only a cultivated minority. Tolstoy’s moral aim is to foster universal brotherhood, so he privileges art that unites people across class and education. His account is essentially the same as Collingwood’s later expressive theory, except that Collingwood removes the moral and accessibility constraints, treating any successfully clarified and expressed emotion as artistic. Consequently, the only real divergence between them concerns whether art must serve a social-moral function.
