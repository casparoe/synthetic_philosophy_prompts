I have been working with a strict framework for when a legend becomes history—call it the triad of historiographical independence. It holds that an event crosses from legend into history only when all three conditions are satisfied together: (i) documentary attestation from sources composed within living memory of the event by agents lacking institutional or narrative investment in the event’s framing; (ii) material correlation that is chronologically and geographically specific, rather than merely typologically compatible with the narrative; and (iii) absence of structural motifs that recur in demonstrably non-historical tales across unrelated traditions. Applying this consistently yields an uncomfortable pattern.

(1) The Exodus, the Arthurian court, and the Trojan War each fail at least two conditions—there are no Egyptian court records within a generation of any departure, no specific fifth- or sixth-century site for Camelot, and Homer’s account comes centuries after any plausible event, with archaeological correlation at Hisarlik that is geographically plausible but not event-specific.

(2) So by the triad, all three remain legend rather than history.

(3) Now apply the same triad to the assassination of Julius Caesar on the Ides of March, 44 BCE, an event standardly treated as firmly historical.

(4) Our principal narrative sources—Suetonius, Plutarch, Appian—were composed roughly a century or more later, outside living memory, and each carries a literary or political investment in the tyrannicide framing.

(5) The identification of the Curia of Pompey is architecturally generic; no artifact specifically marks the stabbing event itself.

(6) The motif of a ruler murdered by conspirators on a predetermined date appears in other traditions that are clearly legendary.

(7) Therefore, by the triad, Caesar’s assassination should be demoted to legend.

(8) That result is hard to accept: if the framework strips away not only Exodus, Arthur, and Troy but also a core political event like Caesar’s death, it has overreached.

(9) So the triad must be flawed—some condition must be abandoned or qualified.

But I cannot locate the damage. Is condition (i) unrealistically strict, since we routinely accept later but convergent testimony? Is condition (ii) impossible to meet for singular political acts, since material traces are almost always generic? Or does step (6) commit an equivocation between a recurring motif and actual mythic contamination? There may also be a scope shift: perhaps the triad applies differently to collective origin narratives than to discrete occurrences witnessed by a small group. Which premise or condition should I reject, and what does each rejection cost? If I relax (i), do I open the gate to pious fabrication? If I relax (ii), do I lose the ability to distinguish generic settlement layers from specific battlefields? And if the flaw is not in the conditions but in the inference from (7) to (8), what hidden assumption about uniform application is being smuggled in? I want an honest pricing of the alternatives, including whether the triad can be saved by being restricted to one kind of event rather than applied across the board.
