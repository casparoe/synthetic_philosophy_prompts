You co-founded a neighborhood mutual-aid network that allows members to borrow specialized equipment—industrial sewing machines, soil-testing kits, construction scaffolding—without cash changing hands. To cover wear and storage, borrowers pay a "time-credit" fee calculated as a small fraction of the item's replacement cost for each day of use; these credits flow into a communal maintenance pool. Recently, the cooperative adopted an algorithm that adjusts fee rates in real time according to seasonal demand, so lending a rarely-used item during a high-demand window yields disproportionately more credits than lending it in a lull. A small group of members has begun specializing in lending high-value, low-use equipment precisely during these peaks, accumulating substantial credit balances, which they then redeem for labor-intensive services—catering, elder care, tutoring—from other members. They defend this practice by noting that no currency is involved, the accounting is fully transparent and democratic, and every credit is eventually redeemed for tangible upkeep. Another member argues that the arrangement has quietly become an engine that rewards ownership over labor, where the algorithmically-scaled "maintenance" charge behaves like a self-multiplying claim that breeds further claims regardless of actual depreciation. She asks you whether the cooperative has crossed a moral line. In your answer, distinguish exchange oriented toward the genuine use and flourishing of participants from exchange oriented toward the accumulation of further claims as an end in itself, and assess whether the absence of coinage, the democratic governance, or the algorithmic neutrality of the fee structure actually blocks the concern.
