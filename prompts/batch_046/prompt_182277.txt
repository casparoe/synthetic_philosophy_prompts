I serve on an institutional review board that oversees multi-phase research protocols, and over the past year I have encountered a recurring pattern in consent processes for which I lack precise vocabulary. I want to know whether philosophy or bioethics has already carved out this specific shape, or whether it remains unnamed.

The pattern arises in protocols divided into discrete modules—say, an initial screening biopsy, a secondary genetic analysis of the tissue, a long-term linkage to clinical records, and a future re-contact clause. For each module, the disclosure form is accurate and complete in isolation: the risks, benefits, and alternatives specific to that module are plainly stated. The prospective participant is clearly competent, faces no overt coercion, and is not actively deceived; every explicit claim in each form is true. The participant gives valid consent to Module A, then separately to Module B, then to Module C. Yet the modules are structurally interdependent: the risk profile of Module B shifts substantially depending on findings in Module A, and the rational evaluation of Module C depends on having already accepted the combined risk profile of A and B together. Because the consent architecture presents each module as an independent decision point rather than as nodes in a unified risk graph, the participant never assesses the protocol as an integrated whole. They consent to each branch, but they do not—and perhaps cannot within this framing—consent to the tree.

This is not the familiar problem of cumulative risk disclosure, where people underestimate the sum of separate dangers. Here, the participant may correctly understand each individual risk; what is missing is the relational structure that binds the risks together. It is also not deception in the ordinary sense, since nothing false is said; not coercion, since refusal of any module is genuinely permitted; and not incompetence, since the participant is fully capable of understanding each module on its own terms. The failure, if it is one, seems to lie in the design of the consent architecture itself.

My question is whether there is an established concept—for instance in bioethics, philosophy of action, or decision theory—for this specific failure mode: consent that is locally valid at every node but globally misaligned because the disclosure framework fragments an interdependent decision space into apparently independent choice points. How does it differ from an ordinary auxiliary failure of comprehension, and from related notions such as therapeutic misconception? If the literature does not name it precisely, I want a careful delineation: what exactly belongs in the phenomenon, what clearly falls outside it (for example, the simple omission of a single risk factor), and what boundary conditions distinguish it from other failures of informed consent. I am not asking for references or a bibliography; I want the conceptual carving—what the thing is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
