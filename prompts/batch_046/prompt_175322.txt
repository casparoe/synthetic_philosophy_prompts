Consider the well-documented pattern that occupational segregation by sex frequently intensifies in societies with stronger legal and economic gender equality—a phenomenon sometimes discussed as the gender-equality paradox. I want you to compare two interpretive frameworks that attempt to make philosophical sense of this pattern, not merely to explain it empirically.

The first framework, call it structural preference revelation, maintains that dismantling coercive barriers allows underlying preference distributions—potentially shaped by evolutionary or deep psychological factors—to express themselves more freely in career selection. The second framework, call it normative displacement, maintains that egalitarian institutions do not erase gendered norms but relocate them into ostensibly voluntary domains—such as the design of school curricula, parental-leave structures, and cultural signaling—where they function as invisible defaults rather than explicit rules.

Compare these frameworks with respect to their implicit commitments on three philosophical issues. First, autonomy: does structural preference revelation presuppose a conception of authentic preference that can be cleanly separated from social formation? Second, the nature-culture boundary: does normative displacement implicitly treat culture as infinitely malleable, or does it acknowledge hard constraints? Third, justice: if both frameworks happen to license identical policy interventions—say, targeted mentoring programs in STEM—does that convergence show that the paradox poses no genuine normative dilemma for egalitarianism, or does it conceal a deeper disagreement about what equality requires?

Your answer should distinguish what each framework strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, and should indicate where the practice of gender-equal policy design is philosophically underdetermined.
