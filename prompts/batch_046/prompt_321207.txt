question: explain the difference between indicative and counterfactual conditionals. lay out Stalnaker's selection-function account and Adams's thesis about indicative conditionals. then say whether Adams's thesis can be extended to counterfactuals and why or why not.

student answer:
Indicative conditionals are about what we think is going on in the actual world, so "if it rains tomorrow, the picnic is cancelled" just means i think rain and cancellation go together given my beliefs. Counterfactuals are about other worlds, like "if it had rained yesterday, the picnic would have been cancelled" — that looks at a world where it rained but everything else is as close as possible to ours.

Stalnaker says for counterfactuals you pick the closest possible world where the antecedent is true and check the consequent there. He uses a selection function that ranks worlds by similarity. For indicative conditionals he does basically the same thing but the worlds are ranked by how likely they are given my evidence, so its still a selection function just with an epistemic ordering.

Adams's thesis is that the probability of an indicative conditional equals the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent. So if P(C|A) is high, i should accept "if A then C". This works well for indicatives because they depend on my beliefs.

But i think Adams's thesis can also work for counterfactuals if we just treat P(C|A) as measuring how close the worlds are. Even if A is false in the actual world, conditioning on it gives us a kind of similarity metric, so the thesis extends naturally. For example, "if this match had been struck, it would have lit" — the conditional probability that it lights given its being struck is high, so by Adams's thesis the counterfactual is probable too.
