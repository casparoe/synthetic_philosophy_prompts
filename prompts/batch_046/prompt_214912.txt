Having retired from a career in experimental molecular biology, I have lately turned to philosophical questions about how societies manage traumatic pasts. In a reading group that meets fortnightly, we have been debating the disagreement between those who follow Avishai Margalit's ethics of collective remembrance — holding that societies bear a duty to preserve the memory of grave injustices — and those who follow David Rieff's argument for strategic forgetting, which maintains that obsessive commemoration can perpetuate harm and that forgiveness sometimes requires allowing the past to recede. Rather than continuing our circular exchanges, I should like to draft, for our group, a formal document of adversarial collaboration between a committed Margalit-style memorialist and a committed Rieff-style advocate of forgetting, written so that both could sign it without abandoning their core commitments.

The document should open with a numbered section of agreed claims — for instance, that collective memory is not a single mental state but an institutional and cultural practice; that both enforced silence and compulsive repetition cause identifiable harms; that preserving testimony differs in kind from preserving grievance; and that scientific communities face an analogous choice between archiving failed experiments for methodological transparency and isolating hazardous data to prevent present contamination, an analogy I have introduced from my own laboratory experience. It should then present a disagreements table in which each position is formulated in language its own adherent accepts and its opponent recognises as accurate. I take the central cruxes to be these: first, whether the primary moral addressee of remembrance is the victim (as a matter of dignity) or the future citizen (as a matter of prevention); second, whether there exists a morally significant distinction between remembering an atrocity and continuously reliving it; and third, whether societies should treat traumatic archives like laboratory notebooks, preserving negative results, or like hazardous waste, sequestering them to protect present conditions.

For each crux, please identify a concrete case or institutional arrangement — perhaps the transformation of a former camp site into a museum versus a memorial park, or the decision to seal versus publish survivor testimonies after a fixed interval — that both sides agree bears on the question. Then record, in advance, what each side predicts ought to be done or will result in that case, so that the disagreement is rendered tractable. Finally, include a brief section diagnosing whether any residual disagreement is merely verbal, particularly around the term 'closure', which one side may treat as a legitimate political goal and the other as a dangerous illusion, and if so specify the translation manual that dissolves it. Write in polished British English, avoid any request for bibliographies or reading lists, do not quote any author verbatim without verified sourcing, and keep the theoretical apparatus accessible to a philosopher who is not a specialist in either memory studies or decision theory.
