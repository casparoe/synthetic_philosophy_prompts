Consider an institutional practice maintained by a national poetry archive: the archive admits translations of foreign verse into its canonical collection only if the English version reproduces the source poem’s syllable count per line, its rhyme scheme, and its stanzaic architecture with exact fidelity, yet it imposes no comparable constraint on lexical accuracy, propositional content, or even thematic coherence. Translators working within this framework regularly produce texts in which the English lines assert the precise opposite of the original’s claims, provided the meter and rhyme remain intact. The archive’s curators defend the policy by maintaining that the identity of a poem across languages is carried by its musical-formal structure rather than by what it says, and that a translation which sacrifices rhyme or syllabic architecture to preserve meaning has therefore failed to translate the poem itself. What philosophical commitments about linguistic meaning, about the identity conditions of a literary work, and about the relation between form and content does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, assess whether rival accounts—such as a purely aesthetic-institutional conventionalism or a content-essentialist view—could license the same admission rules, and specify what features of the archive’s policy would have to be abandoned or revised if the underlying form-first view of work-identity were replaced by a view that treated propositional content as the essential bearer of cross-linguistic identity.
