Student answer:

"Self-deception seems impossible because deception requires that the deceiver knows the truth and intends to hide it from the deceived party. If I deceive myself, I must both know p and intend to make myself believe not-p, which seems incoherent. This is the classic paradox.

One response is the divided-mind account, developed by Donald Davidson. Davidson proposes that the mind is split into rational subsystems, analogous to Freud’s id, ego, and superego, with one subsystem holding p and another holding not-p, separated by doxastic insulation. Because the beliefs are compartmentalized, there is no direct contradiction within a single rational agent. The deceptive subsystem actively suppresses evidence, which requires it to be aware of p.

The deflationary account denies that there are contradictory beliefs at all. Instead, self-deception is just motivated reasoning: we selectively gather and weigh evidence in favor of not-p without ever genuinely believing p. This avoids the paradox entirely because there is only one belief state, not-p, formed through biased but otherwise normal cognitive processes. However, this account struggles to explain cases where we seem genuinely surprised by the revealed truth, since if we never believed p, there should be nothing to suppress.

Motivated reasoning actually supports the divided-mind view better than the deflationary one. If reasoning is genuinely motivated, there must be a motivational source that knows p and drives the bias toward not-p. Therefore, epistemic akrasia—simultaneously accepting and rejecting the same evidence—is necessary for self-deception, and only Davidson’s partition model can accommodate it. The deflationary account, by eliminating the contradictory belief, also eliminates the very phenomenon it seeks to explain."
