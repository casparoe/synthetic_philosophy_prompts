I've come round to a hybrid view on grounding: there's hard grounding—the necessary, irreflexive, transative stuff—for physical-to-physical relations, but for normative or mental facts there's only what I'm calling 'soft grounding', which is defeasible, context-sensitive, and doesn't chain. Specifically, I think physical facts are normatively inert—no atom-configuration ever hard-grounds a moral obligation unless there's already a social practise assigning deontic weight. So fundementality is domain-relative; physics is fundamental in physics but not in ethics.

What would actually count against this? Not the generic 'ethics is spooky' line. I want the real defeaters: which intuitions about specific cases—say a moral fact tracking physical change perfectly, or mental causation working without correllates—stand or fall with it? Which results in other areas does the split depend on? Does 'soft grounding' collapse into non-cognitivism or pluralism about fundamentality? Rank them fatal to awkward, say which ones defenders should concede versus dismiss, and take a committed stance: is the view genuinely exposed, or has it been qualified into unfalsifiability? If the latter, is that a problem for a view of this kind? Give me definite choices with reasons, not a menu of options.
