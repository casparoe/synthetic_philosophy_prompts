Design a classroom activity for about 12-14 grad or advanced undergrad students in a philosophy of history or enviro humanities seminar, 90 minutes, no tech needed. I want them to physically feel how easy it is to slide from "this climate event reshaped conditions" to "this climate event explains the political outcome"—specifically with non-human actors like volcanic winters or plague strains—without just being told that's determinism.

Inject this framing: a "double-entry causation" game where every human decision in a historical episode must be paired with a non-human condition on a shared ledger, but the pairing rules include a hidden veto. Each pair gets 20 "causal weight" tokens and bids in secret to assign veto power to environmental conditions above a magnitude they negotiate. After allocation they apply the vetoes to their own event (e.g., Tambora in 1815 and teh 1816 grain crisis, or plague labor shifts). They then have 2 minutes to defend a narrative using only the un-vetoed human moves, and the group votes on which ones feel forced or thin.

Structure: 10 min silent mapping of one event into human/non-human "moves"; 30 min hidden token auction and veto application; 30 min paired defense and group vote; 20 min debrief. The debrief should map exactly which moment in the game corresponded to determinism creeping in, flag what the token game can't reproduce (probably long-term cultural adaptation or multi-causal overlap), say what will predictably go wrong the first run (students hoard tokens or reject veto rules as artificial—have a backup rule where the facilitator breaks ties), and be honest about limits.

Also: separate in teh debrief what's settled (Tambora changed crop yields, plague affected labor supply) from what's genuinely contested among specialists—like whether non-human forces count as "actors" in any metaphysically interesting sense, or whether determinism is even coherent for complex social systems. Don't present one side as the consensus. No bibliographies, no "see X," no citation requests—just flag where people who know the literature actually disagree. Keep it runnable with just cards, tokens, and a board.
