I recently helped navigate end-of-life care decisions for a family member, and I was struck by how philosophical treatments of deontological ethics almost always assume a single, temporally discrete decision-maker weighing agent-centered constraints against outcomes. That experience motivates a four-month conceptual study, delivering one 8,000-word paper, on whether the classic paradox of deontology—in which an agent-centered prohibition on actively causing harm can seem to mandate allowing a greater harm—depends on precisely this unitary model of agency. The precise question: if moral agency is distributed across a family deliberation or a clinical ethics committee rather than located in one person, does the paradox replicate intact, fragment into distinct sub-problems, or dissolve altogether? Existing debates ask whether the paradox undermines deontology itself, but they treat the agent as an isolated individual and leave entirely open whether the contradiction is partly an artifact of an outdated metaphysics of moral agency. My approach will be conceptual comparison rather than empirical study: I will construct three parallel case sets—an individual decider, a family consensus process, and an institutional committee—each facing the same constraint (a prohibition on actively hastening death versus a duty to prevent suffering) and analyze whether the logical structure of the paradox applies symmetrically. I choose this comparative structure over a historical or formal-logic approach because the live issue is whether agent-centered restrictions have a clear subject when deliberation is collective and extended in time. A successful result would either show that distributed agency eliminates the paradox—implying that deontology needs a pluralist account of moral agency—or demonstrate that the paradox persists regardless, strengthening the critique of the constraint itself. The project fails if the distributed and unitary cases collapse into moral equivalence, leaving no distinctive conceptual work to do. To keep the scope realistic, I will not attempt a full theory of collective agency; I will restrict analysis to end-of-life decision structures and will supply references myself. What I need is a precise framing of the question, an honest assessment of whether the distributed-agency angle is genuinely distinctive or merely decorative, and a month-by-month plan for constructing and testing the three case sets.
