Benatar's asymmetry argument — that the absence of pleasure in a potential person is not bad, whereas the presence of pain in an actual person is bad — has generated sustained disagreement for decades without clear resolution. Critics frequently object that the asymmetry presupposes an illicit comparison between existence and non-existence; defenders respond that harm requires an actual subject, and those who never exist are not subjects of harm. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not fundamentally about the metaphysics of harm, but rather about whether normative evaluation requires an actual bearer for every valence, coupled with concealed disagreements about whether the relation 'better for X' can be satisfied over an empty domain. Is that diagnosis correct? I want an analysis of the disagreement's structure rather than a verdict on antinatalism: do the sides operate with the same concept of harm but apply different conditions of instantiation; is a hidden empirical claim about the psychology of regret or procreative motivation doing the silent work; are they in fact answering distinct questions — one concerning the ethics of bringing people into existence, the other concerning the logic of comparative evaluation across possible worlds? Specifically, assess whether introducing the distinction between person-affecting and impersonal reasons resolves the tension or merely relocates it, and whether formalising the argument in a deontic or modal framework exposes a suppressed premise. Conclude with an honest assessment of what would count as progress — a conceptual distinction, a formal result, or a reframing of the question — or with a reasoned argument that the dispute is structurally irresolvable and why.
