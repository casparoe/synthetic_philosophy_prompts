At a reading group on moral responsibility, two members—Maya and Leo—disagreed about the philosophical payoff of Strawson's distinction between reactive attitudes and the objective attitude. I was present but could not decide who had the stronger case, and I would like a point-by-point adjudication.

Maya opened by arguing that Strawson's framework is essentially a sociological description dressed in philosophical language. She said reactive attitudes such as resentment and gratitude are evolved emotional responses that societies reinforce; calling them normative adds nothing beyond observing that people expect certain behaviors. Therefore, Strawson has not shown that moral responsibility is genuinely grounded—only that we have a widespread habit of blaming.

Leo replied that Maya was treating reactive attitudes as mere causal mechanisms, which is precisely the error Strawson warns against. The reactive attitudes are not just feelings but involve seeing others as members of the moral community who are answerable to demands for goodwill. The objective attitude, by contrast, treats someone as an object of management or treatment. The distinction is not between sociology and philosophy, but between two genuinely different ways of relating to persons.

Maya came back: if determinism is true, then every human action is equally the product of causes outside the agent's control. So the only reason we switch to the objective attitude for children or people with severe mental illness must be that their behavior is caused in an abnormal or especially deterministic way. That means Strawson has not escaped metaphysics—he has merely smuggled it into psychology by labeling some causal patterns normal and others abnormal.

Leo answered that this mislocates the criterion entirely. We do not take the objective attitude because causation is stronger in abnormal cases, but because the capacities for reciprocal moral recognition and address are absent or severely diminished. A young child or someone in acute psychosis is not excluded from the moral community because they are more determined, but because they cannot participate in the exchange of reasons and demands that makes reactive attitudes fitting.

Maya then pressed: but capacities come in degrees. People with addiction, severe depression, or brain injuries often have diminished but not absent capacities for moral address. If Strawson offers no principled boundary—only a vague appeal to normalcy—then whether we blame or excuse becomes a matter of social convenience rather than philosophical consistency. Does that not show the framework is underdetermined?

Leo responded that this is a practical difficulty in applying the distinction, not a failure of the distinction itself. Strawson never promised an algorithm for every borderline case; he argued that our practices of holding responsible and excusing are internal to our form of life, learned through moral education rather than derived from a metaphysical manual. The absence of a sharp line does not make the line unreal.
