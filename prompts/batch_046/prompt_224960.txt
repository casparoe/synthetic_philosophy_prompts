I've been arguing with a friend about whether history is just big personalities making snap choices, and I brought up Braudel's idea of the long duree—those slow geographic and climate structures that outlast any king or battle—but he said thats just academic handwaving. So I want an activity for my grad reading group, maybe 11 people mixing history and philosophy students, 90 minutes, that makes them feel in their gut how those slow structures swallow individual events whole. Not a lecture, not a slide deck. I want a simulation/game where everyone starts out thinking they're playing as rulers, inventors, or generals making strategic moves (wars, treaties, tech breakthroughs), but there's a hidden mechanic—I'm calling it "tectonic accounting," I made that term up—where climate, soil, and geographic cards accumulate invisibly each round and eventually crush whatever "brilliant" decision they just made. By round 4 or 5 they should hit a wall and realize their agency was always smaller than they thought, and only then do we name what's been happening. Design the roles—maybe some players are "event-agents" and others are "structure-feeders" but keep the split hidden for a while—the rules for the deck, a round-by-round timeline, payoff or stakes that don't need real money, and a debrief that maps the shock moment onto Braudel's three durations. Be honest about what a 90-minute game can't reproduce—like actual centuries of erosion or glacier movement—and say how you'd supply that missing piece as facilitator. And please don't ask them for a bibliography or citation list at the end; the point is they leave with the intuition in their bones, not a reading assignment.
