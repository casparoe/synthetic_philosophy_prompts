The issue is pain asymbolia and what it reveals about why pain is bad. The standard reading treats these cases — patients who report intact nociception but show no negative affect, urgency, or withdrawal motivation — as evidence for a two-component model: a sensory-discriminative layer that can survive intact while an affective-motivational layer is stripped away. From there, philosophers usually conclude that the normative weight of pain lives in the affective part.

I want you to push past that familiar framing and defend a more specific, less common view. Treat asymbolia not as "pain minus unpleasantness," but as a selective decoupling of nociception from what we can call stake-binding: a second-order integrative mechanism that couples a first-order sensory representation to an egocentric priority structure registering bodily damage as personally consequential. On this account, the asymbolic patient doesn't merely lack an emotional overlay; she retains a world-directed informational signal about tissue damage but fails to bind that signal to her self-preservational model. The sensory map is intact, but the indexical link — the "this threatens my continued functioning" registration — is disrupted.

Either (a) defend the claim that this decoupling proves the badness of pain is relational and constituted entirely by the stake-binding mechanism, so that without successful binding nociception is normatively inert; or (b) defend the opposing claim that asymbolia actually conceals a hidden intrinsic badness, and that the apparent absence of affect is an artifact of damaging the very reporting and motivational machinery needed to manifest it.
