I serve as the student representative on my high school’s Humanities Curriculum Review Panel, which must vote next month on whether to retain the eleventh-grade unit “Lives and Character.” The unit pairs selections from Plutarch’s Parallel Lives with modern biographical profiles of activists and leaders; a faculty coalition contends that both genres lapse into hagiography, erasing moral failure to present virtue as innate perfection and thereby undermining students’ capacity for critical moral reasoning. They propose replacing the unit with a case-study module on moral psychology, to be implemented by fall semester. The opposing faction argues that Plutarch’s comparative practice—synkrisis—was never hagiographic in the modern sense, since it deliberately juxtaposes paired subjects to highlight shared virtues and distinctive flaws, and that narrative biography remains essential for exemplarist moral learning, in which students identify with lived struggles rather than with abstract rules. I need a brief written opinion to circulate before Thursday’s meeting: recommend a concrete course of action with a clear implementation timeline, treat the faculty coalition’s pedagogical concern with genuine intellectual seriousness rather than dismissal, and address whether restoring Plutarch’s unvarnished comparative framing actually solves the problem or whether biography itself is structurally unsuited to moral education. Please respond with exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
