I am a high school student working on an independent philosophy project about artificial intelligence, and last week I listened to a sustained debate between two members of my school's philosophy club about Alan Turing's treatment of extrasensory perception in his 1950 essay Computing Machinery and Intelligence, which I have been trying to understand on my own. The first student argued that Turing introduces telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition, and psychokinesis as a serious metaphysical objection to machine cognition: if human beings genuinely possess an extrasensory capacity that digital computers cannot replicate, then behavioral tests like the imitation game can never establish true cognitive equivalence, because the interrogator might distinguish human from machine through means that have nothing to do with reasoning or language. The second student objected that this reads Turing exactly backwards; she maintained that he lists ESP not to prove human uniqueness but to identify a practical confounding factor for the test protocol, noting that if telepathy is statistically well-supported then the players in the game should be isolated from one another, but this adjustment protects the test's validity rather than undermining the possibility of machine thought.

The first student then replied that isolating the players only solves the procedural problem while ignoring the deeper issue: if intelligence is defined purely through behavioral success, yet we know humans possess at least one demonstrable cognitive capacity—telepathy—that operates outside observable behavior, then an operationalist account is either incomplete or arbitrarily exclusionary. He pressed further that automation aimed at replicating human cognition therefore misses something essential, not merely because machines lack ESP, but because the criterion for success was designed to ignore capacities that resist third-person verification. The second student countered by distinguishing functional equivalence from total replication: Turing asks whether machines can think, not whether they can duplicate every human faculty, and if ESP is real but methodologically invisible under standard conditions, then excluding it from the operational criterion is a feature of rigorous testing, not an oversight. At this point they reached an impasse, and I could not tell whether the first student had correctly identified a hidden metaphysical commitment in Turing's argument or whether the second student had properly confined the issue to methodology.

Could you adjudicate this disagreement move by move? Please rate each of the four moves I have outlined—from the initial metaphysical interpretation, to the counter-reading about procedural confounding, to the claim that operationalism becomes arbitrarily exclusionary, to the distinction between functional equivalence and total replication—indicating which are clearly mistaken, which are clearly right, and which are partially correct but strained. In particular, I would like to know whether both students are neglecting some further implication about how an operationalist definition should treat capacities that are, by their nature, resistant to third-person verification, or whether one of them has already captured that point without realizing it. I am not seeking citations or a reading list; I simply want a careful point-by-point analysis that clarifies what Turing's ESP argument actually establishes about machine intelligence and about the limits of behavioral testing.
