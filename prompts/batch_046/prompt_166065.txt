I've more or less bought into this reconstructed Gödel-style line: if the universe actually rotates in the right way, you get closed timelike curves, which means there's no globally consistent time ordering. From there it seems like the 'flow' of time can't be an objective feature of the world, just a local quirk of our patch. I want to know what would genuinely count against this specific chain of reasoning — not generic anti-eternalist stuff, but things that hit the Gödel-to-unreality inference directly. Like, if cosmological data rules out large-scale rotation, does that kill the argument or just make it moot? What if global hyperbolicity is imposed as a boundry condition — is that a physical constraint or an ad hoc save? And on the philosophy side, could you accept the rotating metric but deny that local temporal ordering needs a global backbone, and if so does that save objective passage or just change the subject? I'm also curious whether defenders of this view concede that closed timelike curves being physically impossible (say, via some cosmic censorship) would be fatal, or whether they'd retreat to saying the argument was only ever modal. Rank the defeaters: which ones are live, which are merely logical possibilities, and which ones the view's advocates would actually take seriously. Basically I want an honest map of how exposed this position is.
