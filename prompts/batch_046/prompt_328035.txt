Consider the following passage from Newton's Principia, in the Scholium to the Definitions: a bucket of water hangs from a twisted cord; when released, the bucket spins but the water at first remains flat, and only later, once it spins with the bucket, does its surface become concave and climb the sides. Newton argues that because the water and bucket are then at rest relative to each other, yet the concavity persists, true circular motion cannot consist in mere relational change but must be referred to absolute space.

I am trying to understand whether a modern relationist can answer this without simply invoking Mach's principle in the standard form. One reply says the concavity reflects not motion relative to empty space, but global dynamical coupling to the distant distribution of matter—so rotation is globally relational, not locally pairwise. Critics counter that this introduces a privileged cosmic frame defined by the total mass distribution, which functions just like absolute space, merely substituting material relations for spatial ones.

Can you help me assess whether this Machian strategy genuinely saves relationism, or whether it concedes Newton's core point—that there is an objective fact about which bodies are genuinely rotating that cannot be captured by local relations alone? In particular, does the concavity show that rotation is absolute, or only that it is non-local, leaving open whether the 'absolute' is empty space or the total material universe?
