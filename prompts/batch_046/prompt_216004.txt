I'm drafting notes for an essay that applies Zahavi's handicap principle to philosophical methodology and asks whether the field is now facing an epistemic version of signal inflation. The core analogy is straightforward: in evolutionary biology, a costly trait—such as an elaborate tail—honestly signals underlying fitness because low-quality individuals cannot bear the cost of faking it. I want to argue that certain traditional markers of philosophical rigor—deep mastery of dead languages, sustained engagement with obscure primary texts, and the willingness to defend highly counterintuitive theses in public—function as analogous epistemic handicaps. They consume enormous time, demand rare cognitive resources, and expose the thinker to high social and professional risk; precisely because they are so expensive, they can serve as honest signals of intellectual capacity in a domain where the quality of output is notoriously hard to verify ex ante.

The twist I want to develop is a problem of collapsing cost differentials, or what I will call philosophical signal inflation. In biological contexts, handicaps become unreliable when predators vanish or resources become unlimited, allowing weak individuals to sustain costly displays. In philosophy, the rapid diffusion of open-access archives, automated translation, and large language models is sharply lowering the cost of producing prose that mimics traditional rigor: dense historical allusions, syntactically complex objections, and formal apparatus can now be generated or approximated with minimal investment. If the handicap can be faked cheaply, it ceases to distinguish the genuinely capable from the merely well-equipped. The essay would then ask whether philosophy must cultivate new, harder-to-fake handicaps—perhaps demonstrated long-term predictive success in conceptual analysis, or verified formal competence that resists automation—or whether it should abandon costly signaling altogether and move toward fully transparent, replicable evaluation mechanisms.

I also want to address an objection that might undermine the whole framework: philosophical disagreement may be fundamentally cooperative rather than competitive, oriented toward shared truth-seeking rather than status display. If so, importing evolutionary logic about competitive signaling might mischaracterize the social function of philosophical difficulty, reducing an epistemic practice to a status game.

Could you comment on these notes? Specifically, I am interested in whether the analogy between biological handicaps and philosophical rigor holds without conflating social prestige with epistemic reliability; whether the claim about technological signal collapse is plausible or overstates how easily philosophical competence can be automated; whether the cooperative-versus-competitive distinction successfully saves the framework from reducing philosophy to status competition; and which part of the outline you think is strongest and which is most likely to collapse under closer scrutiny.
