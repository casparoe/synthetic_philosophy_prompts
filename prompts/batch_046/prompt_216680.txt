I'm trying to map my own intuitions about moral luck — specifically, when an outcome or circumstance outside an agent's control should affect our moral assessment of them — and see whether my judgments form a coherent pattern or whether I have to abandon some.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

Case 1: Two drivers are equally reckless — both drive at 100 mph through a school zone at dismissal time while texting. By pure chance, Driver A strikes and kills a child; Driver B encounters an empty road. Verdict: they are equally culpable. The death is outcome luck, but it doesn't deepen Driver A's blame.

Case 2: Two structural engineers approve an identical bridge design with a hidden flaw carrying a 1% failure risk. One bridge collapses in an unforeseeable storm, killing twenty; the other stands for thirty years. Verdict: equal professional negligence; the collapse doesn't make the second engineer more blameworthy.

Case 3: Two assassins with identical skill, resolve, and opportunity fire at the same target. One bullet strikes; the other is deflected by a bird that happens to fly into its path. Verdict: equal moral guilt. Success or failure is outside their control.

Case 4: Two children grow up in radically different societies. Child A is raised in a regime where cruelty is systematically rewarded and kindness punished; Child B is raised in a liberal democracy but freely chooses cruelty. Both, as adults, commit the same cruel act. Verdict: Child A is somewhat less blameworthy. The circumstances that formed their will matter.

Case 5: A philosopher writes a dense, abstract metaphysical treatise with no practical intent and no foreseeable misuse. Two centuries later, a political movement misreads it and uses it to justify mass violence. Verdict: the philosopher bears no moral stain. Blame doesn't travel through long, unpredictable chains of misinterpretation.

Case 6 — the one I keep revisiting: Activist A organizes a peaceful, legal highway roadblock. By bad luck, an ambulance is unexpectedly rerouted onto that highway and a patient dies. Activist B organizes an identical protest on the same highway on a different day; no emergency vehicles appear. Verdict: I'm genuinely torn. Activist A's action seems worse because a death resulted, yet the risk was unforeseeable and identical to B's. Is this outcome luck like Case 1, or is there hidden negligence?

I'm drawn to the idea that assessment should track only an agent's "moral exposure" — the set of risks they knowingly and controllably impose, weighted by their epistemic probabilities at the time of action — while ignoring outcomes outside that exposure, but that it should also include circumstantial factors that shaped the agent's capacity for choice. But I don't know if that captures everything.

Is there a precise principle, or a small family of candidates, that delivers Cases 1–5 and resolves Case 6? For each candidate, please state the principle, identify which case motivates each clause, predict its verdict on an unlisted case (a surgeon performs a standard risky procedure with full informed consent; in World 1 the patient dies of a rare, unforeseeable reaction, in World 2 they survive), and be explicit about which of my verdicts it must overturn if it is to remain simple.

Please present your answer as a JSON object with exactly these fields:

"principle_candidates": an array of objects, each with "name" (string), "statement" (string), "motivating_cases" (array of integers), "prediction_on_surgeon_case" (string), and "overturned_verdicts" (array of integers, possibly empty).

"tension_analysis": a string analyzing Case 6 and stating which principle, if any, resolves the tension without overturning another verdict.

"unlisted_case_prediction": an object with "case_description" (string) and "predicted_verdict" (string) for the surgeon scenario, drawn from the best-supported principle.

"final_recommendation": a string stating which principle is best supported and which of my original verdicts, if any, should be revised.
