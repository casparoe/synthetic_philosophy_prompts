I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind with a special interest in comparative philosophy, and I am trying to evaluate a reconstructed argument that draws on Schrödinger's Vedantic reflections. A colleague has presented me with the following chain of reasoning, which appears to derive an implausible collapse of personal identity and moral distinction from Schrödinger's claim that consciousness is never experienced in the plural. I would like to locate the error without abandoning the core Vedantic insight, but I am unsure whether the flaw is logical or metaphysical.

The argument, reconstructed from discussions of Schrödinger's writings on consciousness and Vedanta — particularly his claim that consciousness is experienced only ever in the singular, and his analogy of a single awareness manifesting through different bodily forms — runs as follows:

(1) In every actual experience, there is exactly one consciousness that serves as the subject of that experience; plural consciousness is never phenomenologically given.

(2) Therefore, across all experiences — whether apparently mine, yours, or belonging to some other embodied being — there is exactly one consciousness, numerically identical in each case.

(3) If consciousness is numerically identical across bodies, then the distinction between self and other is not metaphysically fundamental but merely an artefact of bodily differentiation, comparable to the distinction between waves produced by the same ocean.

(4) This applies diachronically as well as synchronically: the consciousness in my present experience is identical to that in my past experience and to that in any stranger's present experience.

(5) Consequently, prudential concern for my future self and moral concern for others are not responses to genuinely distinct subjects, but rather different affective orientations towards states of the very same subject.

(6) But then there is no rational basis for privileging my own future suffering over an equally intense suffering occurring in another body, since both are states of me — the single consciousness.

(7) Therefore, Schrödinger's view entails the collapse of both personal identity over time and the self-other distinction, leaving no ground for the ordinary practices of moral responsibility and prudential planning.

My colleague insists that this is not a misreading but the inevitable consequence of taking the Vedantic identity of Atman and Brahman seriously in a philosophical rather than merely mystical register. I find this hard to accept, but I cannot name the precise step at which the reasoning slips. Is there a hidden quantifier shift or equivocation between steps (1) and (2)? Or is the argument valid, in which case which premise should be rejected — and what would rejecting it cost for someone who wishes to maintain that consciousness is fundamentally unitary without dissolving ethics?

Please answer in British English. I do not require bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I am asking for conceptual analysis only. I am particularly interested in whether a defender of Schrödinger's position could block the argument by distinguishing the phenomenological singularity of consciousness from a metaphysical identity claim, or whether that distinction undermines the Vedantic realism that Schrödinger seems to endorse.
