I am defending a strong reconstruction of Aristotle's position in Politics I.9–10: chrematistike is not merely ethically suspect but metaphysically misaligned because nomisma is barren—money, unlike seed or livestock, cannot reproduce itself—so that oikonomia has a natural telos that fixes the proper limit of acquisition. The claim is not just historical but normative: the sterility of coin reveals a conceptual boundary between use-oriented exchange and unlimited accumulation that should constrain any account of economic rationality.

What would actually count against this? I want more than the familiar point that Greek banking already practiced interest-bearing lending, and I am not asking for a bibliography. Instead, sort the defeaters by severity: conceptual arguments that the barrenness analogy fails within Aristotle's own hylomorphism (e.g., if form can be imposed on matter to generate value artificially); institutional or conceptual countermodels—perhaps relational or credit-based accounts of money that treat value as socially constituted rather than substance-bound—that show the sterility claim depends on an obsolete commodity ontology; and cross-domain results (say, about functional kinds or artificial reproduction) that dissolve the natural/unnatural boundary on which the distinction rests. For each, indicate whether a committed Aristotelian should concede it as damaging, dismiss it as question-begging, or absorb it by qualification. Finally, assess whether the view risks being insulated into unfalsifiability—e.g., by redefining 'natural' functionally—and if so, whether that insulation is a defect for a normative economic theory or a legitimate feature of it.
