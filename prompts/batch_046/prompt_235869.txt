There is an institutional norm in the search for extraterrestrial intelligence: observatories listen passively, and proposals to actively broadcast messages—so-called METI—are treated with deep suspicion, often requiring broad ethical review and international agreement. Proponents of caution sometimes invoke the dark forest hypothesis: the idea that the universe is a hostile environment in which revealing one’s location invites annihilation by competing civilisations. What philosophical commitments about risk, knowledge, and moral obligation to unknown others does this institutional reluctance presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory—does it assume a form of cosmic consequentialism in which the potential harm of disclosure outweighs any epistemic or communicative good, or does it rest on an epistemic humility about our ability to predict alien intentions? Is the practice consistent with the same scientific community’s commitment to open data and transparency in terrestrial research, or does it quietly presuppose that inter-civilisational ethics are categorically different from intra-human ones? And if we replaced the underlying view with a straightforwardly cooperative or contractarian framework—one that treats disclosure as a default moral good unless specific harm is demonstrated—what would have to change in how SETI institutions govern transmission proposals?
