I serve on the University Interdisciplinary Research Ethics Board, and we are preparing to vote on Protocol 2024-ND-7, a proposal for evaluating risk-benefit ratios in multi-arm adaptive clinical trials. Rather than assessing harms and benefits relative to the actual enrolled subjects—who, at the point of consent, cannot know which adaptive branch they will ultimately enter—the investigators propose an indirect normative framework: every adaptive decision must be justified by reference to what an “idealized deliberator” would endorse. This construct is defined as a rational reconstruction of the patient possessing full epistemic access to the trial’s branching structure, no temporal discounting bias, and no emotional interference, but lacking any other properties not attributable to the real participant. The protocol argues that direct assessment is epistemically impossible in adaptive designs because the outcome space is inaccessible to any actual agent at enrollment, so applying norms to the idealized counterpart is the only way to preserve autonomous authorization without freezing adaptive research altogether.

The board is divided along philosophical lines. One faction holds that this framework commits us to a form of indirect normativity—normative force attaches not to the lived agent but to a counterfactual rationalization of her interests—and that this violates our charter’s explicit requirement that risk be assessed relative to the actual circumstances and vulnerabilities of participants. They further worry that if idealized deliberation becomes the regulative standard, we implicitly treat moral agency as constituted by rational coherence rather than by embodied, situated choice, which could license protocols that ignore cognitive disability, emotional distress, or socioeconomic coercion. The opposing faction contends that direct assessment in adaptive trials is not merely difficult but conceptually hollow: since the relevant outcomes are genuinely unknown to real deliberators, asking what the actual patient would choose is asking an unanswerable question, and idealization is therefore a heuristic substitute rather than a metaphysical replacement. They also point out that our current practice of using surrogate decision-makers for incapacitated patients already relies on reconstructed preferences, suggesting the protocol extends an accepted logic rather than introducing an alien one.

Please draft a formal memo for the full board recommending whether Protocol 2024-ND-7 should be approved, approved with modifications, or rejected, with explicit evaluative criteria that reviewers can apply consistently across future adaptive-trial proposals. The memo should reconstruct the philosophical commitments of the indirect-normativity framework—specifically whether it treats idealized deliberation as a constitutive condition of moral agency, a regulative ideal, or merely an epistemic heuristic—and determine whether those commitments are compatible with our institutional obligation to center lived vulnerability. It must also address, with full honesty, the strongest consideration on the side that loses: namely, that if direct normative assessment is genuinely impossible in adaptive contexts, then rejecting all forms of idealization may amount to prohibiting an entire class of potentially beneficial research without providing any workable alternative for protecting subjects. The recommendation should be actionable, with a clear bottom line and conditions if approval is conditional.
