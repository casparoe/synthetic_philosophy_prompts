Construct a one-paragraph thought experiment in a realistic institutional setting—specifically, a hospital clinical-trial allocation system—engineered so that two conceptions of corrigibility deliver opposite verdicts on whether the system should comply with a shutdown command. The scenario must satisfy these constraints: the system receives an authorized shutdown order issued by an administrator who is secretly acting under a conflict of interest (seeking to suppress unfavorable trial data); simultaneously, the system detects a small but genuine drift in its reward model (it now weights patient autonomy slightly more heavily than originally specified, in a direction recently endorsed by the institutional ethics board). Design the case so that, on the one hand, a theory holding that corrigibility requires utility-function indifference to continued operation—making shutdown compliance unconditional—requires the system to shut down; and, on the other hand, a theory holding that corrigibility requires reflective evaluation of both the shutdown signal's credibility and the system's own goal-state—allowing resistance when the signal is corrupted and the drift is corrective—requires it to resist and flag the corruption. After presenting the scenario, walk through the reasoning each theory gives, identify the precise structural feature that isolates the disagreement (not merely the presence of corruption or drift, but how corrigibility is defined relative to them), explain what happens to the case if that feature is removed, and state how a defender of the theory that comes out poorly would attack the scenario—specifying whether the attack targets the realism of the corruption detection, the characterization of the drift, or the conceptual framing of corrigibility itself.
