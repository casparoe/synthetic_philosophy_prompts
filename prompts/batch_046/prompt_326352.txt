Consider four ways of analysing socially constructed kinds—specifically race, gender, and money—when the aim is to explain why some of these kinds prove remarkably resistant to collective revision despite lacking a biological or physical substrate. The four are: (a) Haslanger’s ameliorative projectivism, which treats race and gender as real in virtue of being systematically causally efficacious within unjust social structures; (b) Searle’s institutional-fact framework, which explains money and legal status through collective intentionality and the assignment of status functions; (c) Butler’s performative-iteration model, where gender is constituted through the repeated stylisation of bodily acts rather than through static institutional rules; and (d) what I shall call a diachronic-anchoring view, according to which a social kind is maintained not merely by present practices or intentions but by the accumulated institutional recognitions of past generations, which constrain present revision in much the way that sedimented grammar constrains a speaker.

Please rank these four accounts into tiers—say, first tier, second tier, and third tier—according to how well they jointly explain (i) the rapid malleability of monetary denominations after a currency reform, and (ii) the comparative inertia of gendered categories across societies that have formally abolished legal sex distinctions. In a sentence for each tier, explain what makes the accounts in that tier succeed or fail at explaining both phenomena together. Then state, as a fraction between 0 and 1, how confident you are that your top tier genuinely outperforms the bottom tier on both criteria simultaneously.

Finally, please say explicitly what consideration or argument would lead you to reorder these tiers: which of the four accounts, if shown to handle a specific counter-example—such as the near-instantaneous social recognition of cryptocurrency as a store of value despite lacking any historical institutional sediment—would cause you to revise your ranking, and where in your reasoning is the comparison between monetary and gendered kinds most likely to go wrong?
