I recently acquired citizenship in a country where elections are formally open but deeply contested, and with a major election approaching in which both leading candidates have records I find seriously objectionable, I have been trying to systematize my philosophy of voting rather than relying on ad hoc intuitions. Below is the package of commitments I have reached; I would like an audit of whether they hang together.

(1) I accept a conditional civic-duty view: citizens have a pro tanto obligation to participate in democratic elections that substantially affect others, but only if they have made a good-faith effort to understand the stakes; the duty is defeasible and not absolute.

(2) I regard voting as fundamentally expressive: the ballot registers one's judgment about justice and the common good, functioning more like public testimony or bearing witness than like contracting for a representative's services.

(3) In high-stakes contests where one candidate poses substantially greater risk to vulnerable populations, I ought to vote for the less harmful option, even when that requires suppressing an authentic preference for protest or a third-party candidate.

(4) Such strategic voting does not constitute moral complicity in the wrongdoings of the selected candidate, since complicity requires shared intent or endorsement, neither of which is present when one votes with an explicit defensive intention.

(5) Democratic legitimacy also rests partly on an epistemic argument—elections pool dispersed judgments—so systematically suppressing true preferences to block worse outcomes introduces noise that weakens the very justification for democratic decision-making.

(6) I hold that abstention is not morally neutral but amounts to tacit acquiescence in the result, which makes abstention rarely permissible when stakes are high.

(7) At the same time, I think that even fully justified strategic voting leaves a moral remainder: one should not treat the defensive choice as costless, but should acknowledge that being compelled to suppress one's authentic judgment for harm prevention involves a kind of tragic loss.

My concern is that (2) and (3) may conflict more deeply than they appear—if the primary point of voting is to express my true judgment, then strategic suppression seems to undermine the activity's core value—while (3) and (5) may pull in opposite directions, since (3) requires the very preference suppression that (5) identifies as damaging. I also wonder whether (4) genuinely resolves the complicity worry or merely redefines it away, and whether (1) and (6) together collapse into an unconditional duty, rendering my conditional framing empty. Meanwhile, (4) and (7) might seem incompatible—if there is no complicity, why should there be a moral remainder?—though I suspect they address different moral dimensions.

Please rank these tensions by severity, specify for each pair what must be revised and at what cost, identify the single change that would resolve the most conflicts, and indicate which commitments are only apparently in tension. Throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic—particularly regarding expressive versus instrumental theories of voting, the conditions for moral complicity, and the epistemic defense of democracy—and do not present any contested position as though it were the consensus view.
