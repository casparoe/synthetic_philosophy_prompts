I am a high school student writing as a neutral observer. Two members of my school’s philosophy of science club—let me call them Marcus and Elena—recently debated whether Wolpert’s no-free-lunch theorems for supervised learning resolve, deepen, or leave untouched Hume’s classical problem of induction. They specifically brought in Solomonoff’s universal prior and the question of reference-machine dependence. I take no side; I am reporting their exchange exactly as I heard it. Please adjudicate the argument move by move: for each of the four contributions below, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and briefly justify the rating. Then state which party is right about which specific point, identify what both are missing—especially regarding the relationship between inductive bias, evolutionary justification, and the formal properties of universal prediction schemes—and note whether their disagreement actually touches the core of Hume’s challenge or talks past it.

(1) Marcus opened by stating that the no-free-lunch theorems prove that, averaged uniformly over all possible target functions, every learning algorithm achieves exactly the same expected error. He concluded that Hume’s demand for a logical justification of induction is therefore impossible to satisfy: there is no universally superior inductive method. The best we can do is adopt a bias—such as preferring simpler hypotheses—that happens to align with our environment, and accept that its justification is purely pragmatic and empirical rather than rational.

(2) Elena replied that this misreads both Hume and the theorems. Hume’s problem, she said, is not a demand for universal superiority across all possible worlds, but a question about why we are entitled to project patterns observed in our actual world into the future. The no-free-lunch result actually intensifies the difficulty: it demonstrates that any algorithm’s success is entirely contingent on the alignment between its built-in bias and the true structure of the environment. If that alignment is explained only by evolutionary history, then justifying induction requires assuming that evolutionary processes themselves track truth—an assumption that is itself inductive and therefore circular.

(3) Marcus countered that Elena was ignoring a formal escape route. He pointed to Solomonoff’s universal prior, which assigns weights to all computable hypotheses according to algorithmic complexity. While he acknowledged that this prior depends technically on the choice of a reference universal Turing machine, he appealed to the asymptotic invariance result—that predictions converge up to a constant factor regardless of the machine—to argue that algorithmic probability supplies a near-neutral, principled default bias. If such a bias exists, then Hume’s problem is partly solved: we possess a rational prior whose structure is not tied to particular empirical histories, and the no-free-lunch theorems merely show that no algorithm dominates every possible world, which does not undermine this special status.

(4) Elena responded that Marcus was overstating the practical significance of the invariance theorem. She noted that for any finite dataset—the only kind we ever actually possess—the choice of reference machine can dominate predictions, so in practice Solomonoff induction is not bias-free but merely relocates the bias into the formalism. She added that applying no-free-lunch reasoning to prediction rather than optimization reveals that even a universal predictor can be systematically misled by environments constructed to exploit its specific weighting scheme. Consequently, appealing to algorithmic probability does not break the circularity of inductive justification; it only conceals it behind mathematical machinery. The evolutionary account and the formal account, she concluded, remain equally in need of the very inductive license they claim to provide.
