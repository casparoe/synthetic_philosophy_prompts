I am preparing an essay for my moral philosophy module comparing particularism with generalism, and I have become stuck on what my tutor described as the holism of reasons. Jonathan Dancy maintains that the moral force of a consideration can shift with context — for instance, that the cruelty of an act might weigh against it in one situation yet be neutralised, or even become irrelevant, in another. I take this to show that moral judgement is responsive to particular configurations rather than governed by universal principles. Yet I cannot see how this avoids collapsing into a form of subjectivism: if the reason that an act is cruel carries no consistent normative weight across cases, then it seems to function merely as a label for my present emotional response rather than as a genuinely binding consideration. Furthermore, if reasons are particular in this strong sense, I do not understand how moral learning is possible — we could never apply an insight gained in one case to another, since the shape of every reason would be unique. Is my mistake in assuming that particularism denies all cross-case consistency, or is the deeper confusion my equation of context-sensitivity with arbitrariness? I should be grateful for a clarification of exactly where the inference from holism to subjectivism fails, and whether particularists can account for the accumulation of moral knowledge without tacitly appealing to general principles.
