As a novelist whose conceptual toolkit turns on narrative determinism, the implied author, free indirect discourse, focalization, dramatic irony, and the narrative contract that sustains a reader's belief in character autonomy, I want you to restate Sartre's doctrine of radical freedom and the structure of bad faith—specifically, the claim that consciousness is condemned to be free and that bad faith is a flight from that freedom through self-deceptive identification with one's facticity—using only the vocabulary and conceptual machinery of fiction-writing. Translate Sartre's ontological distinction between being-in-itself and being-for-itself into the difference between a fully authored fictional entity and a focalizing narrative consciousness; render his illustrative figures—the café waiter who performs his role and the woman who refuses to acknowledge the hand being held—into specific narrative devices such as plot necessity, character typing, dramatic irony, and authorial foreknowledge that produce the illusion of a fixed essence. Then provide the residue report: which parts of Sartre's argument survive this translation intact, which must be distorted to fit a framework in which every action is technically inscribed by an author, and which assertions about unscripted self-making and consciousness have no counterpart in narratology whatsoever. Finally, tell me whether that absence reveals a genuine blind spot in how novels conceive of agency, or whether Sartre's ontology depends on a pre-narrative notion of being that fiction necessarily excludes.
