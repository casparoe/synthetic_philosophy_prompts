I recently heard a summary of teleosemantics — the attempt to ground mental representation in biological proper function — presented as follows. According to the summary, a mental state represents what it was naturally selected to cause or indicate, so its content is fixed entirely by its evolutionary etiology; consciousness, language, and normative evaluation are therefore unnecessary for intentionality, and the view provides a fully reductive naturalization of mind. The speaker added two further claims: first, that artificial neural networks cannot possess genuine mental content because they lack an evolutionary history of selection, and second, that cases of misrepresentation — such as a frog's prey-detection mechanism firing at a non-prey dark dot — are not genuinely false representations but rather content-less misfirings, since the state fails to perform its selected function. I want to know whether this characterization is accurate or whether it distorts the view. Please assess the summary: does teleosemantics really reduce content to evolutionary selection history, or does it allow for derived, learned, or non-selected proper functions? Is the exclusion of artificial systems entailed by the theory or an independent assumption? And does the treatment of misrepresentation as absence of content rather than false content match the standard account, or does it conflate malfunction with lack of intentionality? Identify which parts of the summary are fair, which are subtle misrepresentations or overstatements, and whether the speaker's conclusions survive correction. Write your answer in American English, and do not provide a bibliography or reading list.
