I'm presenting a paper defending what I call stabilized epistemic akrasia to an interdisciplinary workshop on belief, bias, and self-knowledge. The thesis is this: when an agent acquires robust higher-order evidence—specifically, calibrated debiasing feedback from a statistical literacy or forecasting-training protocol—that her first-order reasoning in a well-defined domain is systematically unreliable, she should downgrade her meta-cognitive confidence in the beliefs produced by that process without revising the first-order beliefs themselves, provided those beliefs remain anchored by independent external checks (e.g., peer replication, instrument readings, or formal derivation). The result is a sustained, rational state in which the agent believes p and simultaneously believes that she lacks adequate first-person reason to believe p. I argue that this state avoids conciliationist collapse into wholesale belief revision, escapes closure-based skepticism of the kind associated with Nozick-style tracking conditions, and in fact describes the cognitive posture of expert forecasters who maintain high-accuracy predictions after being shown their own bias profiles.

The audience is precisely composed: four epistemologists, two committed conciliationists about peer disagreement who treat higher-order evidence as automatically defeating; one philosopher of science who defends strong epistemic closure and will press knowledge-attribution problems; one cognitive psychologist who runs calibration-training studies with expert forecasters and cares intensely about psychological realism and whether anyone can actually maintain this posture without cognitive dissonance or performance collapse; and three graduate students in virtue epistemology who will ask whether sustained akrasia is a rational vice rather than an epistemic virtue. They know the standard literature on higher-order defeat, they are skeptical of "having it both ways," and they will test whether my view collapses into mere wishful thinking or hidden steadfastness.
