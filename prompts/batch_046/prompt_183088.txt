I'm a retired physicist — did condensed matter for thirty years — and since I stopped working I've gotten weirdly into philosophy of science and epistemology. Right now I'm stuck on Nozick's tracking theory and that closure problem he raises. I sit on a small advisory board for our state science agency, and we have to decide every month whether some research claim is "known" well enough to be cited in a regulatory brief. The problem is always closure: the original study tracks okay, but then people derive all sorts of policy conclusions and we have no procedure to check if those derived claims still track the truth.

So I want you to build me an actual decision procedure — like a flowchart in words — that my board can run on a proposal. It should use Nozick's four conditions (truth, belief, sensitivity, adherence) but translate them into practical questions we ask in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and clear stop points where the answer is "no, not knowledge." It also needs to handle closure explicitely: if we already accepted P and we accept that P entails Q, the procedure should show us exactly where tracking might break for Q, especially when the entailment is statistical or causal rather than logical. Mark every place where the call is a value judgement, not a fact, so we know to hand it to the chair.

Please include the hard cases: what if the counterfactual world is far away? What if belief is distributed across a team? And be honest about where the procedure will misfire or just be useless.

Then run it on three cases:
1. A toxicology study finds compound Y disrupts cell division in rats; the staff wants to close this to "Y causes cancer in humans at environmental doses." The study tracks well, but the closure feels shaky.
2. A climate model tracks global temperature in nearby modal worlds, but the board wants to derive "we must ban fossil fuels immediately" — I actually want this policy, but I want the procedure to reject the closure step anyway, and I need you to say so clearly.
3. A well-known physics result is replicated in 11 out of 12 labs; one failure is in a lab with a known calibration error. Does tracking survive?

Make it usable for board members who've never heard of Nozick. And don't just list steps — show me how the judgement calls actually work.
