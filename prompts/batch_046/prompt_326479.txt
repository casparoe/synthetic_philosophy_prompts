I'm trying to determine whether philosophy of mind recognizes a distinct concept—separate from Mary's room itself—for a variant of the knowledge argument that targets cognitive restructuring rather than raw sensory qualia. The case runs as follows. Imagine a researcher who possesses exhaustive physical, neurological, and functional information about the mechanisms underlying human recognition of irony: she knows every activation pattern, every inferential pathway, and every representational condition that distinguishes grasping an ironic remark from failing to grasp it. She can predict with perfect accuracy which utterances any subject will find ironic, yet she herself has never experienced the internal shift in interpretive framework that accompanies actually "getting" the irony; her comprehension remains entirely predictive and third-personal. Then, through some intervention, she acquires the capacity firsthand and suddenly understands an ironic statement not merely by correct classification but by experiencing the restructuring of context that makes the irony salient. The classic Mary's-room scenario involves seeing color for the first time and learning what it is like; this case seems closer to acquiring a particular mode of understanding or practical grasp, though it carries its own distinctive phenomenology. Does this configuration have an established name in the literature—perhaps within discussions of explanatory gaps applied to cognition, or within distinctions between propositional knowledge and ability? If it is treated only as Mary's room applied to a different domain, I want to understand precisely where the analogy holds and where it fractures: is the missing element genuinely phenomenal, genuinely practical, or a hybrid? And if there is no separate term, how should one delimit the boundaries of this case so that it is neither collapsed into standard knowing-how arguments nor absorbed back into the original sensory-knowledge argument? I am not looking for a reading list or citations; I want to know whether the carving is recognized, and if it is not, what its precise edges are.
