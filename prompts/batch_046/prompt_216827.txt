In reporting on the growing resistance to algorithmic checklists in expert surgical training, I have repeatedly heard mentors invoke the maxim "knowing how is not knowing that" to argue that operative mastery cannot be translated into explicit protocols. The phrase is clearly doing argumentative work, but its precise philosophical content remains obscure. Can you lay out the slogan as a menu of distinct theses—ranging from the modest claim that some skills involve non-propositional capacities, to the stronger view that explicit rules are causally inert during expert performance, to the radical thesis that propositional knowledge actively degrades mastery? For each reading, say whether it actually supports resisting the codification of surgical skill, flag which versions collapse into trivialities, and note which depend on an implausible dualism between mind and body. I am drafting a column for an educated general audience, so keep the distinctions sharp but frame them in terms of practical stakes: how should we design apprenticeship if one of these readings is true?
