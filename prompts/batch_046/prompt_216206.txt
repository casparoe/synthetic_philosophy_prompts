At a workshop on metaphysics, two participants argued over whether an omission can cause an outcome when that outcome was already overdetermined by other failures. The case: a water-treatment plant has two independent chlorination units, either of which suffices to purify the supply. Unit A is not activated by the technician on duty (an omission); Unit B is inoperable owing to a manufacturing defect. The supply is contaminated. (1) The first argued that the technician's omission is a genuine cause: had she activated Unit A, contamination would have been prevented, and the omission belongs to the actual causal history. (2) The second replied that counterfactual dependence is broken — because Unit B was already inoperable, activating Unit A would have made no difference — and without difference-making there is only a missed opportunity, not causation. (3) The first countered that redundant positive causes are still causes (if two arsonists each light sufficient fires), so redundancy should not disqualify omissions; the omission belongs to a minimal sufficient set. (4) The second said this ignores a structural asymmetry: positive causes produce change, whereas omissions are absences selected against a normative or functional baseline, so treating them like redundant fires illegitimately reifies absence. (5) The first then claimed that on an interventionist account the baseline is built into the causal model, so the omission is represented as a variable taking the value 'not activated', and its causal relevance is therefore objective within that model. (6) The second concluded that this renders omission-causation model-relative in a way that undermines its claim to be metaphysically on a par with production-causation.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of (1) to (6), say whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing — in particular, whether there is a principled way to allow redundant omissions to count as causes without collapsing every absence into causation.
