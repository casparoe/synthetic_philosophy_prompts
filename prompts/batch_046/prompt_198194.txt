Consider the following examination question and one student's answer, both concerning the philosophy of dreaming and the debate between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett.

Question: Malcolm maintains that dreams are not experiences because there is no criterion of wrongness for dream reports. Dennett, in his response, introduces what is commonly called the cassette theory and raises a temporal-location problem for the received view. Answer all three parts precisely. (a) Reconstruct Malcolm's argument from the absence of an independent criterion of error, clarifying what kind of mental event he denies is occurring. (b) Explain Dennett's cassette theory as a model of narrative composition rather than as a literal claim about neurological storage; then identify the specific problem he raises about attributing structured experiential content to REM sleep itself, as opposed to attributing it to the hypnopompic transition. (c) Evaluate whether Dennett's position commits him to denying all conscious states during sleep, or only to denying that narrative experience is temporally located in REM. In particular, discuss whether a non-narrative hypnopompic auditory sensation would count as a genuine sleep-related experience on his account.

Student answer: Malcolm argues that dream reports fail to describe experiences because there is no criterion by which a dreamer could be mistaken about the content of her report. Unlike perceptual judgments, which are answerable to an independent world, the statement "I dreamed of a red sphere" has no falsifying conditions accessible to anyone, including the dreamer upon waking. Dennett's cassette theory addresses this by proposing that the brain stores raw experiential data during REM in a format analogous to magnetic tape, and that upon waking the mind plays back and narrativizes this stored data into the report we call a dream. The temporal problem Dennett raises is that if the received view is correct—if there is a continuous experiential stream during REM—then there ought to be reportable content available immediately upon arousal without any consolidation delay, yet subjects consistently require a brief interval to articulate the narrative. From this, Dennett concludes that the structured experience is not located in REM but is constructed entirely during the hypnopompic transition. Consequently, Dennett is committed to the view that REM sleep contains no conscious experience of any kind—not even non-narrative sensations—because the cassette mechanism is the sole source of all mental content associated with the sleep period. A hypnopompic auditory hallucination, on this account, would not be a sleep-experience but a purely waking-state artifact triggered by the playback process.
