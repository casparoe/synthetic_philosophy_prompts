I was arguing with a close friend late into the evening after a seminar on environmental ethics, and we found ourselves going in circles about whether large-scale habitat destruction actually reduces wild animal suffering. My friend maintains that high-biomass ecosystems—especially those with dense populations of r-selected species—function as net-negative welfare systems because chronic predation, parasitism, starvation, and exposure produce enormous cumulative suffering; on this view, clearing a forest or draining a wetland is, in the long run, analogous to preventing births, since lower productivity means fewer sentient beings experiencing harm. I objected that this treats suffering as a simple additive quantity across individuals and across time, ignoring that habitat destruction inflicts acute, intense suffering—displacement trauma, starvation, social dissolution—on existing beings, and that evaluating an "ecosystem's net welfare" presupposes a coherent object of evaluation that may not exist, since welfare is indexed to individuals rather than to landscapes. But we quickly realized we do not actually disagree about the underlying biology: we both accept that wild animals suffer extensively, that destruction kills and displaces, and that reduced productivity lowers future population numbers. Despite this agreement, neither of us has moved an inch, which makes me think we are not having an empirical dispute at all. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is: is it a hidden formal problem—perhaps that without a metric for comparing qualitatively different welfare-state types (chronic vigilance stress versus acute starvation versus social collapse) the "net welfare" calculation is empty, something I have started calling welfare incommensurability across trophic restructuring—or is it a verbal disagreement about whether "flourishing" can be predicated of an ecosystem? Could it be that we are answering different questions, one about what actions suffering-reduction obligations permit and another about whether ecological complexity carries non-instrumental value? Or is the stall a sign that our bedrock intuitions about the moral standing of natural processes are simply irreconcilable? Given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress: an empirical study comparing suffering intensity across trophic levels, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the problem, a formal result showing the calculation is ill-posed, or an honest argument that no resolution should be expected and why.
