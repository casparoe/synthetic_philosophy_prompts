You're delivering the opening remarks at a one-day workshop titled "Actors Without Intentions," convened by a university history department but attended by epidemiologists, climate scientists, philosophers of science, and historians of medicine and environment. The audience is genuinely divided: roughly half are historians who worry that attributing any kind of "agency" to germs, climate shifts, or volcanic winters is either anthropomorphic confusion or an environmental determinism that erases human choice, structural violence, and political responsibility; the other half are interdisciplinary scholars who believe conventional human-centered narratives systematically understate the causal weight of non-human forces by treating them as passive background scenery rather than as forces that restructure the field of human possibility. You have been asked to frame the day's discussions, and you need to do so in about six minutes, in American English, without offering a bibliography, reading list, or citations.

Write a speech whose structure is governed by a three-part conceptual distinction: causal protagonism (the capacity of a non-human entity to restructure events without intending them), strategic intentionality (the kind of goal-directed agency that belongs to persons and perhaps some institutions), and moral responsibility (the kind of accountability that requires the first of these but not the second). The philosophical idea should do real work in shaping the address—determining what you can legitimately praise about a pathogen's epidemiological path, what you must withhold (intentions, blame, praise), and what you can ask the room to hold in tension—rather than serving as decorative framing. Ground the abstraction with at least one concrete but not overused reference, such as the cascading effects of the 1815 Tambora eruption on grain markets and public-health policy, or the way fourteenth-century plague routes reshaped labor law, but do not turn the speech into a lecture. The tone should be formal but conversational: it must leave the skeptical historians convinced that human agency and moral accountability have not been erased, and leave the scientists convinced that non-human causation has not been reduced to inert backdrop. Close with an invitation that both halves of the room can genuinely accept, one that asks them to treat the day's papers not as a debate over whether nature "matters," but over how to describe its mattering without either mystifying it or dismissing it.
