hey so i'm working on a novel where the narrator is this old lighthouse keeper who might be hallucinating the voices in the fog, and my workshop group got into it about whether his "inner life" is actually accesible to me as the writer. there's this unwritten rule in fiction workshops — let's call it the Mirror Test, though i just made that up — where if you're writing close third you're not claiming to know the character's private feelings, you're just borrowing the grammar of intimacy. but then the same people will say a grief scene is "unconvincing" because the character didn't react like a real person would, which seems to assume we do have access to some shared template of consciousness. and here's the weird part: if i write an animal narrator with human-like regret, nobody blinks, but if i write an AI with ambiguous feelings they call it anthropomorphism and reject it. so what philosophy of other minds is hiding in our craft norms? do we think human interiority is transparent enough to check for accuracy, but non-human minds are either blank or purely projected? and could you actually hold both views without contradiction, or is the workshop just running on two inconsistant theories? what would have to change in how we critique fiction if we replaced the hidden view with something more uniform?
