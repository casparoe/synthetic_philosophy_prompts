I've been thinking about epiphenomenalism lately, specifically the version where phenomenal properties are causally inert but we still form reliable judgments about them via some kind of structural correlation with brain states. The paradox of phenomenal judgment usually gets framed as: if my belief that I'm in pain isn't caused by the pain itself, why should I trust it? But I'm more interested in a slightly different position: call it the 'shadow-correlation view' — the idea that phenomenal judgments are actually about the physical corellates all along, and we only think they're about qualia because those corellates are systematically misidentifed in introspection. So the position I'm holding is that there's no real paradox, just a widespread category error, and that this saves epiphenomenalism from self-defeat. What would actually count against this? I want the arguments and observations that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be.

Specifically: what kind of empirical findings on introspective error would undermine the misidentifcation story, and which ones would defenders of the view concede as genuinely damaging? Would a demonstrated case of accurate phenomenal judgment in the absence of the usual neural corellate be fatal, or just awkward? And has this view been qualified so much — say, allowing that qualia do have some minimal causal role in judgment-formation — that it's no longer really epiphenomenalism but just a watered-down physicalism? I want an honest map of where it's exposed, including whether the position risks being insulated into unfalsifiability, and if so, whether that's actually a problem for a metaphysical view of this kind.
