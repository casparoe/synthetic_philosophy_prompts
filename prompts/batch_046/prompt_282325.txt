Design a diagnostic decision tree for a graduate seminar in comparative political ethics, intended to classify respondents' substantive commitments—not their preferred theoretical labels—on whether clientelism and multigenerational gift-giving constitute corruption and whether Western legal standards apply universally.

Begin with this root case: a municipal official belongs to a community network that has provided her family with sustained, multigenerational support, including crisis mediation, kinship recognition, and ceremonial exchange, with no discrete quid-pro-quo agreement; she subsequently grants members of that network preferential access to public goods and regulatory forbearance.

Construct four successive binary branch nodes that present concrete normative claims or scenario judgments, phrased so that a respondent can answer each without knowing which theoretical position it supports. The nodes should address:
(i) whether corruption conceptually requires a discrete, intentional exchange, or whether sustained reciprocal obligation without explicit transaction can suffice;
(ii) whether the official's formal bureaucratic role can be coherently separated from her socially constituted reciprocal duties, or whether the two are inextricable in this context;
(iii) whether the primary wrong, if present, lies in the impairment of bureaucratic rationality and state capacity, or in the violation of an abstract universal norm independent of institutional function;
(iv) whether external evaluators using Western legal categories are justified in labeling the practice corrupt when local participants do not recognize those categories as binding on their obligations.

This structure yields sixteen leaf combinations. Map onto these leaves named positions that genuinely occupy distinct nodes—such as universal legalism, thick cultural relativism, institutional functionalism, and relational contractarianism—together with any additional positions supported by the logical space. For every unoccupied combination, briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, normatively unattractive, or merely unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate, explaining why the relevant distinction collapses at the level of practical judgment.

Present the tree in formal outline form, followed by a scoring key that translates each occupied leaf's pattern of answers into a concise paragraph stating the core commitment it expresses. Fix the audience as advanced students in political philosophy, restrict the instrument to exactly these four nodes, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or references to specific texts.
