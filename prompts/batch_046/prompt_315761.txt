Question: Some philosophers of physics argue that universality classes in critical phenomena—where totally different microphysical systems share identical critical exponents—challenge constructive reductionism. Explain how that challenge works, and then evaluate whether it actually establishes strong emergence (with downward causation and ontological autonomy) or only weak emergence (supervenience without reducibility). Be careful about the logical relation between universality and downward causation.

Student answer:
Universality classes show that near a critical point, macroscopic properties like the critical exponents depend only on dimensionality and symmetry, not on the specific microscopic Hamiltonian. The renormalization group explains this: as you scale up, irrelevant micro-parameters flow to fixed points and get washed out. So this is a direct counterexample to constructive reductionism, which claims you should be able to derive macro-behavior just by aggregating micro-facts.

Now, about emergence: the macro-properties still supervene on the micro-ones—no micro-change means no macro-change—so it's not ontologically independent in the strong sense Kim defends. But there is downward causation, because the critical point itself—the macro-state—determines which microscopic fluctuations are long-range and which get suppressed. So the macro-level causally constrains the micro-level, not just vice versa.

So I'd classify this as weak emergence, but with genuine downward causal influence. It's not full strong emergence since supervenience holds, but it's more than epistemic emergence because the macro-state actively selects which micro-interactions are relevant. The failure of reductionism here comes from causal autonomy at the macro-level, not just from our cognitive limits.
