After a long and circular argument with a friend about Bernard Williams's integrity objection — the case of a husband who saves his wife and, in Williams's formulation, might have one thought too many should he stop to ask whether impartial morality permits it — we remain at an impasse. I maintain that any rational agent must be able to justify the rescue, and that refusing to weigh spousal love against impartial considerations is not integrity but arbitrary favoritism; my friend holds that the very presence of such weighing in deliberation corrupts the attachment, and that integrity requires certain reasons to be structurally excluded from practical reasoning. The exchange has become repetitive. Is this stall substantive — about whether agent-relative reasons are genuinely normative — or partly verbal, turning on competing conceptions of integrity and justification? Could a hidden empirical premise be doing the work: that entertaining an impartial reason necessarily crowds out attachment, a claim about the psychology of deliberation that neither side has defended? Or are we answering different questions, with one of us asking what good character requires and the other asking what rational justification permits? Diagnose the disagreement, engaging specifically with what Williams and his critics say, and tell me what would count as progress — a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding, a formal result — or whether the dispute is ill-posed.
