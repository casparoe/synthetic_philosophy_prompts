In a lot of constitutional theory people say a constitution is basicly a Ulysses contract -- the people tie themselves to the mast so future politicians (or future majorities) can't steer onto the rocks of populism or rights violations. The analogy is usually wheeled out to justify entrenchment: if Ulysses was rational to bind his future self, then we're rational to bind ours through supermajority rules and judicial review.

But I'm not convinced the mapping holds up. Can you lay out the correspondence table in detail -- who is Ulysses, who are the Sirens, what counts as the rope, what's the ship, and who plays the crew that actually ties the knot? Then tell me where it frays: is a democratic majority really like a single person with divided temporal selves, or is it more like replacing the captain and half the crew every few years? Does the analogy quietly import a false picture of "the people" as one unified agent with stable, coherent preferences across generations?

Also, here's a twist that doesn't come up much: philosophers of psychiatry use "Ulysses contracts" for psychiatric advance directives, where a patient binds her future manic self. If we take that clinical version seriously, does it strengthen the constitutional case, or does it show that precommitment only works when there's a clear "healthy" self and a clear "pathological" self -- a distinction democracies don't have? So audit the analogy properly: what's the correspondence, where does it break, what normative baggage about agency and rationality does it smuggle in, and does the argument for constitutional entrenchment survive once the disanalogies -- especially the psychiatric one -- are on the table?
