Since I retired from the lab I've been poking around philosophy of science, and I'm stuck on this verisimilitude business -- how a false theory can still be closer to truth than another false one. I cooked up an argument that seems to blow the whole idea up, but I can't find the leak. Here it is:

(1) If theory A gets every prediction right that B gets right, plus some extra ones B misses, then A is closer to the truth than B, other things being equal.
(2) Take any false theory F. For each false predicition of F, build a "disjunctive shield" clause: either F's mechanism applies, or that result is caused by an unmodeled local exception.
(3) Call the result F-shielded. Whenever F is right, F-shielded is right. Whenever F is wrong, the exception clause saves it, so F-shielded is still right.
(4) So F-shielded has all of F's true predictions, plus true ones in every case where F failed. It never makes a false predicition.
(5) By (1), F-shielded is closer to truth than F, and closer than any rival that misses those cases.
(6) But F-shielded is just F with ad-hoc escape hatches bolted on. It's unfalsifable, explains nothing new, and clearly isn't scientific progress.
(7) So if verisimilitude is just true predictive content, every false theory can be patched into a monster that outranks honest rivals.
(8) That can't be right -- we do think Newton was closer than Ptolemy with enough epicycles, even though both are false.
(9) So either (1) is wrong, or verisimilitude has nothing to do with predictive success, which guts the concept.
(10) I'd really like to keep (1) because it captures why science feels like it's moving forward. Where does the argument slip? Is it (3), where the shield actually preserves truth? Or (5), where I apply the comparison? And if I have to drop (1), what am I actually paying -- do I lose the idea that theories can approximate reality, or just that approximation is monotonic?
