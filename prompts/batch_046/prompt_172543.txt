I chair the Professional Conduct Board of a graduate school of clinical social work, and we must issue a formal decision memo before next week’s accreditation review. A doctoral candidate in her final practicum admits she falsified attendance logs for three consecutive weeks in order to remain at the bedside of her dying father; she is an only child, and she completed all required clinical hours remotely with no harm to clients. Our accreditation standards treat documentation integrity as non-negotiable, and her advisor urges leniency on philosophical grounds: Confucian role ethics, he argues, structures obligations through differential care (chà bié ài), giving filial piety (xiào) hierarchical priority over institutional roles during an irreplaceable familial crisis. Half the board wants expulsion; half wants full exoneration on the grounds that role obligations are context-relative rather than universal. Draft the board’s decision memo recommending a concrete disciplinary outcome—expulsion, full exoneration, or approval with conditions and a clear time frame—together with reasoning the faculty and the student could actually respond to. The memo should engage seriously the strongest version of the exoneration argument (that institutional roles are temporarily suspended, not merely balanced, when a primary relational role reaches a crisis threshold) and the strongest version of the expulsion argument (that professional integrity is a universal precondition of trust, not one role among others). It should also address, without relying on citation, whether Confucian role ethics is better read as supporting “contextual harmonization” (seeking institutional accommodation rather than unilateral falsification) rather than “hierarchical suspension,” and whether the concept of zhèngmíng (rectification of names) implies that her duty as “daughter” and her duty as “student-clinician” must be integrated through transparent negotiation with the institution rather than through concealment. State the recommendation plainly, treat the losing side with intellectual honesty, and avoid asking for bibliographies or outside readings.
