I cover philosophy of science and metaphysics for a general intellectual audience, and lately I have been preparing a feature on how classic thought experiments survive modern conceptual refinements. One pairing that keeps surfacing is Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles—roughly, that no two distinct entities can share all properties—set against Max Black's counterexample of two featureless spheres suspended in an otherwise empty, symmetric universe. The standard classroom comparison treats Black's spheres as a clean refutation: if the spheres differ only in spatial location, and location is not an intrinsic property, then Leibniz's principle seems to fail.

But I want to move past that familiar script. I am particularly interested in comparing how the two frameworks treat the boundary between qualitative description and relational structure. To make the comparison less derivative, imagine a modified Black-world in which each sphere generates a continuous, locally uniform field, yet the global topology imposes an asymptotic boundary condition—so that from any local vantage point the spheres remain indistinguishable, while the universe as a whole carries a directional asymmetry anchored at infinity. Under this specific injection, does Leibniz's principle regain traction by treating the boundary condition as a genuine relational property of each sphere, or does Black's counterexample adapt by insisting that such global conditions are not properties of the spheres themselves? Please compare the identity of indiscernibles and Black's spheres precisely along this axis: determine whether the dispute collapses into an epistemic question about observational access, or whether it exposes a deeper ambiguity in the metaphysical status of boundary-dependent properties. I want the comparison to be rigorous enough for readers familiar with the literature, but without requiring any citation or bibliographic apparatus—just a direct conceptual analysis.
