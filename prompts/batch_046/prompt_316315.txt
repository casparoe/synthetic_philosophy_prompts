I have been reading Kierkegaard's essays and journal entries on my own, without formal training in philosophy or theology, and I have tried to extract a consistent pattern in his judgments about different forms of public communication. So far, the pattern looks like this. He condemns the daily newspapers of his era because they generate an abstract collective—he calls it the public—in which opinions circulate without anyone taking individual responsibility for them. He likewise condemns large crowds assembled for spectacle or political rhetoric, where individuals dissolve into an anonymous mass and avoid personal commitment. On the other hand, he approves of direct face-to-face dialogue in which specific persons address one another about ethical or religious obligations. He also appears to praise a signed, passionately argued pamphlet in which one individual takes explicit ownership of a definite position, even though such a text might reach many readers. Finally, he distinguishes a small study circle, where each participant must personally defend and live out their beliefs, from any form of mass dissemination.

Now I want to apply this pattern to a phenomenon I observe today. Imagine a communication system in which every message is permanently tied to the sender's verified real-world identity; there is no shared editorial board and no common public feed, because an automated process curates a unique stream for each user based on predictions about what will sustain that user's attention. Interaction occurs through rapid, brief responses, and while people frequently express strong opinions, those opinions rarely translate into tangible action outside the system. There is no physical gathering, and technically no anonymity, yet the scale is enormous and the mediation is constant.

If I try to be consistent with the pattern above, I see at least two competing principles that could explain his earlier verdicts. One principle concerns anonymity and abstraction: communication becomes corrupt when it allows individuals to hide in a collective that is nobody in particular, or when it severs speech from identifiable personal responsibility. Another principle concerns reflection versus commitment: communication becomes corrupt when it encourages detached opinion-forming and rapid reaction without demanding concrete, sustained personal commitment or action. A third possibility is about scale and institutional mediation: the problem is specifically the mass dissemination of messages through an organized medium, regardless of whether the speakers are named.

Given these alternatives, what should I predict Kierkegaard would say about this system? Which principle best accounts for his approval of the signed pamphlet alongside his condemnation of newspapers and crowds, and what do the other principles predict instead? Give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and then tell me what further case—perhaps involving a named individual broadcasting to millions through the same automated system, or an anonymous small group that demands concrete action from each member—would settle which principle he is actually running.
