In engineering discussions about whether to instrument every variable before a deployment decision, my colleagues often defend exhaustive telemetry with the analogy that adding sensors is like widening a telescope aperture: more photons never hurt, the image only gets sharper, and deliberately narrowing the aperture before an observation would be self-sabotage. They use this to license the policy that we should always collect every available metric before deciding on a rollback. Audit this analogy against Good's result that free information never reduces expected utility in a decision problem: provide the correspondence table—what plays the role of the decision problem, the observation, the utility payoff, the prior belief, and the condition that observation is genuinely free—then isolate the fractures. In particular, address whether telemetry is actually cost-free given compute and latency budgets; whether additional variables can introduce spurious correlations that degrade the decision rule rather than sharpening it; whether the telescope model falsely treats measurement as passive observation when in software systems the act of instrumentation can alter latency, failure modes, or user behavior; and whether there are decision contexts in which observing more can paralyze action without improving the outcome. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the rollback decision from those that do not, say whether the normative conclusion that one should always observe survives once these breaks are on the table, and if the telescope comparison collapses, suggest whether a different analogy—perhaps from signal processing or controlled debugging—better captures the conditions under which additional observation actually improves expected utility.
