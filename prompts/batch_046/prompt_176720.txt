I am part of a small worker-owned design studio that, over the past year, adopted an internal accountability framework drawn from the recent popular revival of Stoic practice in organizational culture. Rather than measuring productivity by outcomes, we try to classify every collective effort as either an "interior move"—something that depends entirely on our own deliberation and effort, such as drafting a proposal or revising a workflow—or an "exterior move," which depends on clients, markets, or luck, such as winning a contract or retaining a lease. We agreed to evaluate one another only on interior moves, treating anxiety about exterior results as a private emotional discipline rather than a collective strategic concern.

Now our landlord has signaled that she may not renew our lease next spring, and the studio is divided. One partner argues that we should immediately prepare a detailed portfolio presentation, gather letters of support from neighboring businesses, and rehearse a face-to-face negotiation; she insists these activities are clearly interior moves, since they depend only on our labor and coordination, even if the landlord's final decision remains outside our control. Another partner counters that this is precisely the kind of disguised outcome-attachment the framework was meant to eliminate: the presentations and letters are not genuinely internal goods but instrumental levers aimed at manipulating an external result, and the only consistent Stoic response is to regulate our disappointment and begin searching for new space. I find myself caught between them. The preparation does seem to lie within our power, yet its entire point is to causally influence something we have classified as exterior; meanwhile, my other partner's position seems to forbid any strategic action that aims beyond our immediate will, which feels paralyzing in practice.

Can you advise me on how to think through this disagreement? Specifically, I want to know whether my first partner is smuggling outcome-dependence back into our framework, or whether my second partner is interpreting the distinction too rigidly—perhaps by ignoring that one can pursue an external goal through genuinely internal means without violating the underlying philosophy. More broadly, does applying this ancient distinction to modern collective decision-making reveal a coherence problem in the revived practice itself, or is our conflict merely a matter of practical judgment? I am less interested in generic conflict-resolution advice than in understanding what philosophical commitments about agency, responsibility, and the boundary between action and outcome are actually at stake when we try to run an organization this way.
