Having retired from experimental physics and taken up philosophy of science, I have been working through Quine's Two Dogmas of Empiricism with a study partner who specializes in philosophy of language. Over several sessions she has given firm verdicts: she treats explicit logical tautologies and definitional abbreviations as analytic only in a trivial, non-substantive sense; she denies that mathematical truths beyond pure logic are analytic, insisting that even arithmetic belongs to the empirical web and is revisable in principle; she classifies central scientific laws, such as energy conservation, as synthetic and entrenched; she places normative ethical claims entirely outside the analytic-synthetic framework; and she argues that Kripkean necessary a posteriori truths, like the identity of water and H2O, dissolve the distinction by separating metaphysical necessity from epistemic analyticity. Now I want to test the consistency of her view against a claim from my former field: that the free-energy principle—holding that any system maintaining its own existence against entropy must minimize variational free energy—is not merely an empirical model but a formal, necessary condition on self-organizing dynamics, analogous to a conservation law but derived from information-theoretic premises. Would she classify this as analytic (a consequence of how we define self-organization), synthetic yet metaphysically necessary, or as a category error that illegitimately mixes formal mathematics with empirical science? I am less interested in the answer itself than in identifying the principle she is running—whether she tracks Quine's web-of-belief centrality, a strict logical-empirical boundary, or a separation of formal and empirical discourse. Which of these rules fit her five earlier verdicts equally well, what does each predict for the free-energy case, and which further example—say, a computer-verified mathematical proof or a constitutional principle declared structurally fundamental by a court—would best distinguish between them?
