The exam question was:

Consider a hypothetical constitutional order containing two provisions. Article 42 establishes the standard amendment procedure: a two-thirds vote in the legislature followed by popular ratification. Article 43 states that Article 42 may only be amended by a procedure that is not the one specified in Article 42—specifically, by unanimous consent of a constitutional court. Analyze whether the interaction between Article 42 and Article 43 generates a paradox of legal self-reference analogous to the classic formulation of Ross's paradox. In your analysis, discuss whether appealing to hierarchical norms (in the Kelsenian sense) or to a rule of recognition (in the Hartian sense) can dissolve the tension, and be explicit about whether legal self-reference here is structurally parallel to semantic self-reference (as in the Liar paradox).

Here is the student’s answer:

The problem sets up a direct tension between Article 42 and Article 43. Article 42 authorizes amendment through legislative supermajority and referendum, but Article 43 demands that any change to Article 42 use judicial unanimity instead. If we attempt to amend Article 42 via Article 42's own procedure, we violate Article 43; if we use the court procedure, we violate Article 42's claim to govern its own revision. This is precisely Ross's paradox applied to constitutional design: a rule that purports to regulate its own conditions of change creates a logical loop.

The structure is fundamentally the same as the Liar paradox. In the semantic case, a sentence asserts its own falsity, yielding an unstable truth-value; here, the constitutional provisions assign incompatible validity conditions to the same act of amendment. So legal self-reference produces the same kind of contradiction—an institutional analogue of logical inconsistency.

We can resolve this by appealing to Kelsen's hierarchical model. Kelsen's basic norm (Grundnorm) sits outside the constitutional text and provides the ultimate ground of validity for the entire order. By locating the power to revise Article 42 in the basic norm rather than inside Article 42 itself, we break the circularity: the basic norm authorizes whatever procedure is ultimately accepted, so Article 43 functions not as an internal contradiction but as a substantive constraint on the content of constitutional change.

Hart's rule of recognition performs a similar dissolving function. The rule of recognition is the social criterion that officials actually use to identify valid law. If officials collectively treat Article 43 as the governing standard for amendments to Article 42, then the rule of recognition validates that practice, and the system updates itself without paradox. There is therefore no genuine contradiction—only a shift in the criteria of legal recognition.

Consequently, the apparent paradox is dissolved once we distinguish the constitutional text (where the loop appears) from the deeper normative layers—the basic norm and the social practice of recognition—that actually determine validity. The contradiction was only superficial, arising from treating institutional rules as if they were self-interpreting semantic statements.
