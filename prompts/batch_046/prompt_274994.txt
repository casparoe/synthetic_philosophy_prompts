I am preparing a graduate seminar on Strawson's account of reactive attitudes and moral responsibility, and I want an exercise that moves well beyond the standard summary of the participant and objective stances. To that end, I have constructed a taxonomy of five reactive postures that might be directed at an institution—say, a carbon-credit trading body—whose collective actions cause harm even though no individual member possesses malicious intent. The five postures are: first, direct interpersonal resentment aimed at identifiable officials within the body; second, genuinely institutional resentment that treats the corporation or agency itself as the proper object of moral anger; third, what I call distributed indignation, in which affected communities experience reactive feeling without fixing on any particular agent, whether human or collective; fourth, the purely objective attitude, viewing the institution solely as a mechanism to be regulated or redesigned; and fifth, a posture I term anticipatory forgiveness, which deliberately suspends blame in the present but retains emotional engagement with the institution's future conduct in a manner that is not merely managerial.

Please rank these five postures from the one that is most fully continuous with Strawson's conception of reactive attitudes as constitutive of interpersonal moral life to the one that most radically departs from it. Alongside this ranking, assign each posture a score between one and ten for its internal coherence with Strawson's claim that these attitudes are not optional instruments but are woven into the fabric of human relationships. Present your reasoning entirely in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold type, tables, or numbered lists, as though you were writing a single sustained letter explaining why the ordering holds and precisely where each posture either preserves or fractures the moral psychology Strawson describes.
