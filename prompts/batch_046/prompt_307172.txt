Consider this argument about what fixes the identity of a letterform across styles, framed within the Letter Spirit conception of gridfonts and parametric design spaces. (1) Every token of the letter 'a' belongs to a single category not because it resembles every other token, but because all tokens share an invariant topological graph — a bowl-with-tail relational structure — that remains constant whether the letter is rendered in blackletter, geometric sans-serif, or unsteady handwriting; surface properties like stroke weight, curvature, and serif presence are merely continuous stylistic parameters that do not affect categorical identity. (2) Because these parameters vary continuously, there exists a path through the style space linking any canonical 'a' to shapes that share no visible resemblance to it, so long as the parser that tracks the graph ignores metric distance and spatial proximity. (3) If the graph is genuinely preserved along every point on such a path, then shapes consisting of disconnected fragments — an open loop in the upper left quadrant and a detached vertical stroke in the lower right — are still 'a's, since the essential relational structure is technically intact. (4) So the theory entails that the category 'a' extends infinitely into regions of the space that every human reader classifies as noise, broken glyphs, or entirely different letters. (5) Yet human recognition is clearly bounded and perceptual: readers reject shapes that preserve abstract relations but destroy visible coherence, and they learn letter categories from exposed exemplars rather than from hidden structural descriptions. (6) Therefore either letter identity is not fixed by an invariant graph, or human recognition is systematically misaligned with real category membership — both conclusions seem unacceptable.

Which step should I reject? I would prefer to keep (1), that there is a genuine style-invariant essence, and (5), that recognition is perceptually bounded, but they appear to be in direct tension. Identify the weakest premise or the hidden slide — whether an equivocation on what counts as preserving the graph, a modal mistake about continuous traversal, or a false dichotomy in (6) — explain why that step initially looked defensible, and price what accepting or rejecting it costs for a theory of letterform categories.
