Following a long and somewhat circular debate with a friend over abortion ethics, I have drafted notes for an essay that attempts to sidestep the usual stalemate between Thomson's violinist case and the future-like-ours argument. Instead of framing the conflict as bodily autonomy versus the value of a potential life, I propose that we understand pregnancy as an asymmetrical dependency that generates what I am calling a temporary moral office—a form of trusteeship that is neither fully chosen nor purely coerced. On this view, the fetus is not best analogized to an intruding stranger who happens to need your kidneys, because its existence is structurally bound to a specific host in a way that makes the "unplugging" metaphor miss the institutional and social mediation of refusal: terminating a pregnancy is rarely a purely private act, but is shaped by economic conditions, gendered labor expectations, and healthcare access that distribute the moral burden of refusal unevenly. At the same time, I want to argue that the future-like-ours reasoning relies on an implicit theory of personal identity that treats psychological continuity as sufficient for harm, without accounting for the fact that the "future" in question is not yet anchored in the social and relational networks that make death a harm to a person rather than merely the cessation of a biological process.

Would you be willing to comment on these notes? I am especially interested in three concerns. First, does my characterization of the violinist case misrepresent Thomson's point, since she intended precisely to show that even a recognized person with full rights has no claim to your body? Second, does my reliance on "social anchoring" to distinguish fetal from adult harm commit me to an implausible social constructivism about moral status? Third, is the concept of a temporary moral office coherent, or does it inevitably collapse into either a rights-based or a consequentialist framework? I am not looking for citations or further reading—just an assessment of whether the conceptual architecture holds together and whether this is a genuinely interesting reframing or merely a convoluted restatement of bodily autonomy.
