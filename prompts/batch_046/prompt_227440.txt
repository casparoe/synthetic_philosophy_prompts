I have been preparing a graduate seminar on the epistemology of religion, and in our discussion of divine hiddenness my students keep returning to one particular analogy. They say that God’s hiddenness is like a professor who leaves the examination room: the absence is not abandonment but a structural condition that makes genuine, uncoerced achievement possible, and therefore the hiddenness actually supports the value of human autonomy rather than undermining belief. The analogy is usually deployed to defend the idea that if the divine were overwhelmingly present, human freedom and authentic faith would be compromised, just as a student cannot demonstrate independent mastery if the instructor hovers and whispers answers.

I would like you to audit this analogy with philosophical precision. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: what in the source domain—the examination room, the professor, the student, the silence, the grading criteria—maps onto what in the target domain—the divine, the human believer, the conditions for salvation or knowledge, the absence of revelation, and the moral or epistemic stakes. Second, identify exactly where the mapping fractures: does a professor eventually return and reveal the answers; does the student know with certainty that there is a professor at all; is the silence temporary and instrumental, whereas cosmic hiddenness might be permanent and unconditional? Third, examine the normative features the analogy quietly imports—especially the idea that revelation is analogous to giving answers rather than initiating a relation, and that faith is best construed as exam performance evaluated for independence. Fourth, determine whether the conclusion that hiddenness preserves autonomy survives these disanalogies, or whether the examination-room model was carrying the entire argument. Finally, assess whether a different analogy—perhaps drawn from the dynamics of testimony or from the phenomenology of interpersonal trust—would better serve the defender of hiddenness, or whether this pedagogical model should be abandoned altogether. Write this as a rigorous conceptual analysis, not as apologetics or devotional reflection.
