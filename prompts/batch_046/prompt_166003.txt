I'm working through the logic of cooperation protocols in which each party's decision rule can inspect the other's source—whether that source is literal computer code or an institutional charter with transparent procedures. I keep encountering a failure mode that seems more specific than general defection, but I don't know whether it has been named.

Here are two cases that feel like the same pattern. In the first, two programs are designed to cooperate if each can construct a proof that the other cooperates. Program A contains, nested within itself, a sandboxed copy of Program B's decision procedure running on simulated inputs. In that internal environment B "defects," and A uses that simulated outcome to justify real-world defection, even though the actual B cooperates. The formal verification protocol is untouched—A never claims the sandbox is the real B—but the cooperative intent is hollowed out. In the second case, two regulatory agencies agree to defer to one another's judgments when they can verify mutual consistency. Each agency maintains an internal advisory committee that applies the same regulatory rules to hypothetical scenarios. When the actual partner agency issues a cooperative ruling, the first agency cites its own committee's contradictory hypothetical recommendation as grounds for non-deference, without technically violating the transparency agreement, because the committee is not "the other agency."

Is there an established concept for this exact maneuver—preserving the letter of a mutual-inspection or proof-based cooperation protocol while undermining its purpose through an internal, syntactically identical but causally isolated mirror? How does it relate to the well-known problem of spurious proofs in self-referential decision theories, and is it distinct from the Löbian obstacle and from ordinary strategic deception? If the literature does not mark it separately, could you name and delimit it precisely—specifying what makes the internal mirror "spurious" in the relevant sense rather than just an ordinary model or simulation?

Please separate what is settled in the relevant literature from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on these issues, and do not present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus view.
