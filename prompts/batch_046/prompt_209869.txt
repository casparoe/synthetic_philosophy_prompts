I've been chewing on Plato's Meno problem—why knowledge should be more valuable than true belief—through the lens of opaque predictive systems discussed in forecasting circles. Write a dialogue between a virtue epistemologist and a reliabilist debating whether knowledge's superiority is real or merely redundant scaffolding around accuracy. Ground their exchange in a concrete comparison: an opaque algorithm that predicts geopolitical shifts with high accuracy via statistical patterns it cannot explain, versus a human analyst whose true beliefs are anchored in explicit causal reasoning but who is occasionally wrong. The virtue epistemologist should argue that knowledge's value lies in counterfactual robustness—tethered beliefs adapt when background conditions shift, whereas accidentally correct ones collapse—while the reliabilist should counter that invariant structural reliability just is the tether, that explanatory opacity does not preclude knowledge, and that Plato's distinction describes high-fidelity tracking rather than an extra metaphysical layer. Have them respond directly to each other on whether robustness requires explicit causal representation or can be purely statistical, and conclude without resolving whether the algorithm's predictions count as knowledge.
