You are the senior clinical data steward at a regional neurorehabilitation centre. Every Tuesday and Thursday you receive first-person reports from patients emerging from prolonged dissociative anaesthesia, and you must decide within fifteen minutes whether each report describes a coherent qualitative state that should be entered into the research dataset, or whether it attempts to posit an incoherent "quale" that should be flagged and excluded. You have no formal philosophical training, but you must apply a decision procedure derived from the sceptical argument that qualia are an incoherent posit — specifically, that any claim to an intrinsic, non-relational, ineffable property collapses under translation, comparison, and functional substitution. Your centre's ethics board requires that every exclusion be defensible in writing, and that any case where judgment replaces rule be explicitly marked as such and co-signed by the attending neurologist.

Write a reusable decision procedure — a sequence of ordered tests with pass/fail criteria — that operationalises this scepticism about qualia without requiring you to understand the underlying metaphysics. The procedure must: (i) distinguish reports that describe functional or relational disruptions (which may be retained) from reports that invoke bare intrinsic feels (which must be excluded); (ii) include a "translation check" that attempts to render the patient's language in behavioural or neurophysiological terms, with clear rules for when the translation succeeds or fails; (iii) handle comparison claims (e.g., "this red is different from yesterday's red") by testing whether the comparison can be sustained without circular appeal to the very quality being compared; (iv) identify exactly where the procedure stops and requires a human judgement call, and specify who must sign off; and (v) be honest about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedural rules.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: (a) a patient who reports "a raw, unnameable colour that has no relation to anything I have seen before"; (b) a patient who reports "my left arm felt absent, and I could not initiate movement, though I could see it"; and (c) a patient who reports "the pain was not worse, but it was a different kind of wrongness — a private shade I have never shared." One of these should yield an exclusion that the patient and their family will find unwelcome, and you must state what the procedure can and cannot do in that situation — specifically, whether it can justify the exclusion to a lay audience without appealing to the metaphysical argument itself.

Keep the procedure practical for someone running it twice weekly under time pressure, with only a printed checklist and no access to philosophical literature. State the frequency and stakes of the decisions, the time and expertise constraints, and flag every point where the procedure will misfire or where an editor — in this case, the data steward — must decide rather than apply a rule.
