Please grade the following examination response, submitted for an upper-division course on comparative metaphysics and epistemology. The exam prompt was: “In Jain philosophy, anekāntavāda holds that reality is ontologically complex (anekānta) and that any single predicate captures only one aspect; syādvāda expresses this through qualified predication with the particle syāt. The doctrine of nayavāda distinguishes standpoints such as dravyāstika (substance) and paryāyāstika (mode). A philosopher argues: ‘Since every moral judgment depends on a contingent cultural framework, there is no moral fact that holds independently of such frameworks; hence, moral realism is false in every respect.’ Evaluate this argument using anekāntavāda, syādvāda, and nayavāda. Specifically: (a) identify the ekānta (one-sided) move in the argument; (b) show how syādvāda’s logical form—particularly the distinction between unqualified (niścaya) and qualified (vyavahāra) assertion—permits partial affirmation of both cultural dependency and objective moral structure; (c) explain whether adopting syādvāda here commits one to assigning equal epistemic weight to all moral frameworks, and whether this follows from the doctrine of naya.”

The student’s answer reads as follows.

“Anekāntavāda teaches that reality has many sides, so no single view is complete. Syādvāda puts this into practice by prefixing assertions with syāt, which means they are only conditionally true. The philosopher’s claim that moral values are entirely culturally constructed is clearly ekānta, because it denies any objective moral structure without qualification. From the standpoint of anekāntavāda, the correct response is to say that moral values are partly cultural and partly objective.

But syādvāda does more than hedge. Because the particle syāt cancels contradiction, anekāntavāda actually allows literally contradictory predicates to apply to the same object in the same respect and at the same time. So we can simultaneously assert both ‘moral values are objective’ and ‘moral values are not objective,’ and both are true in an unqualified sense, since syāt removes the contradiction. This shows that anekāntavāda is not merely epistemic humility but a form of logical dialetheism.

Regarding nayavāda, Kundakunda’s Samayasāra lists exactly five standpoints—dravyāstika, paryāyāstika, and three others—through which reality can be approached. Applying these to ethics, cultural dependency corresponds to the paryāyāstika standpoint and objectivity corresponds to the dravyāstika standpoint. Because syādvāda permits free movement between all standpoints, it follows that every moral framework must be assigned equal epistemic weight. Therefore we should not merely qualify the philosopher’s denial, but embrace a full meta-ethical pluralism in which all cultural moral systems are equally valid.

Finally, syādvāda’s logical form is the saptabhaṅgī, or sevenfold predication, in which every proposition is simultaneously affirmed and denied in all possible combinations. This means that in the absolute sense (niścaya-naya) moral realism is false, while in the conventional sense (vyavahāra-naya) it is true, and the two judgments are equally correct. Thus the philosopher is wrong to deny objectivity entirely, but also wrong to think objectivity could ever be more true than its denial.”
