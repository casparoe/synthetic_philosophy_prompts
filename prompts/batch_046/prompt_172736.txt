I'm serving on an institutional review board that allocates limited funding for interdisciplinary research. We've received a proposal that applies a methodological framework many expert reviewers consider conceptually unstable—though a vocal minority defends it as an essential corrective to standard practice—to address a social problem of undeniable urgency. A senior administrator on the board argues that we should approve the proposal not on the basis of any settled scientific assessment, but primarily because the lead researcher has spent years advancing the board's strategic priorities through extensive unpaid service and internal advocacy. He frames this not as corruption or favoritism, but as institutional reciprocity: the board owes a form of loyalty to those who have built its institutional capacity, and that loyalty provides a legitimate, freestanding reason to fund the work regardless of how contested its methodological merits remain.

I feel a strong and somewhat visceral resistance to this reasoning, yet I also recognize that in this particular case scientific merit is genuinely indeterminate—there is no uncontroversial standard by which the proposal can be declared clearly worthy or clearly deficient. Given that the evaluation criteria themselves are unsettled, I am unsure whether my discomfort reflects a real moral defect in allowing loyalty to function as a funding criterion, or whether it is merely an aesthetic attachment to the fiction of purely merit-based evaluation. How should I determine whether institutional reciprocity counts as an illegitimate basis for this decision when the epistemic grounds for judgment are themselves ambiguous? And does the fact that merit is genuinely contested change which kinds of non-epistemic considerations are appropriate to invoke, or does it simply make the wrong kind of reason harder to identify?
