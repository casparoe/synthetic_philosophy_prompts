I've been trying to pin down my actual concept of societal "collapse" by testing it against historical cases, and the pattern is messy. The Bronze Age Eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE feels like collapse: palace economies, writing systems, and long-distance trade dissolve together in a rapid cascade, and the interdependence makes the failure feel systemic. The Classic Maya southern lowlands in the 8th-9th centuries also feel like collapse to me: the dynastic urban order and monumental political culture cease in the region, even though populations and language persist elsewhere. By contrast, Easter Island never strikes me as collapse: despite ecological transformation and demographic contraction, Rapa Nui's institutions and cultural practices adapt rather than break, and framing it as collapse seems to confuse environmental stress with societal failure. The Western Roman Empire's end in the West during the 5th century, however, feels like transformation rather than collapse: Roman legal, linguistic, and administrative forms are absorbed and continued by successor kingdoms, with no sharp rupture experienced by inhabitants. The Indus Valley's deurbanization around 1900 BCE feels the same way—gradual dispersal, little evidence of widespread violence, and cultural continuity in rural contexts—despite the disappearance of writing and standardized urban planning. So I have collapse for Bronze Age and Maya, but transformation for Easter Island, Western Rome, and Indus, even though all involve major institutional or demographic disruption. Can you state the principle—if there is one—that distinguishes my collapses from my transformations, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it? Apply it to the Soviet dissolution in 1991 and to a hypothetical society that abandons its capital due to unavoidable climate shift but preserves its government, religion, and language elsewhere. And be explicit: which of my five verdicts does the best principle force me to overturn, and why?
