I'm taking a course on Wittgenstein's philosophy of religion, and we've spent several weeks on the idea that religious belief is not a hypothesis subject to empirical testing but rather operates within its own framework of meaning. In our seminar discussions and in the assigned readings, one analogy comes up repeatedly: religious belief functions like commitment to a scientific paradigm in the Kuhnian sense, where practitioners work within a framework, treat anomalies as puzzles rather than falsifications, and where conversion involves something closer to a gestalt shift than to rational persuasion. Some of my classmates use this analogy to defend the rationality of religious belief—arguing that just as scientists do not abandon physics over every failed prediction, believers should not be expected to abandon faith over suffering or the absence of empirical evidence—while others worry that this comparison makes religious commitment sound too much like a collective, theoretical enterprise that could, in principle, be overthrown by internal crisis.

There's also a second analogy that our instructor has pressed us to consider: religious belief is more like accepting a basic grammatical rule—say, the rule that other people have minds, or that pain is not a private object—where the "belief" is not an empirical claim at all but a condition for the possibility of certain judgments and forms of life. I'm trying to work out exactly how far each comparison can be pushed before it collapses. For the paradigm analogy, I want you to lay out the explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the normal practitioner, the anomalous result, the crisis period, the revolutionary figure, and the allegedly incommensurable framework? For the grammatical-rule analogy, what corresponds to the rule itself, the rule-follower, the conditions of application, and the specific sense in which rejecting the rule is not false but somehow nonsensical or empty? Then separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the intended conclusion—namely, that religious belief is meaningful and rational despite lacking empirical support—from the ones that are merely decorative. In particular, does the paradigm analogy wrongly import the idea that religious commitment is theoretical, collective, and vulnerable to revolutionary overthrow? Does the grammar analogy wrongly eliminate the passionate, voluntary, and existential dimension of religious life that Wittgenstein himself seems to acknowledge in his remarks on the Last Judgment? Once both analogies are audited in this way, does the conclusion that religious belief is immune to empirical refutation but still fully meaningful survive, or was one analogy carrying the entire argumentative load all along? If the latter, which one, and what would a better analogy need to preserve from each?
