Imagine Niccolò Machiavelli were alive today and asked to advise a modern republic that has begun substituting algorithmic forecasting—prediction markets for legislative outcomes, climate-risk models that constrain policy, and machine-learning systems that claim to reduce contingency—for traditional political judgment. Drawing on his distinction between fortuna, understood as the irreducible sphere of contingency and chance, and virtù, understood as the flexible, adaptive capacity to seize and shape events, how would he evaluate this substitution? Would he see these technologies as an authentic expansion of virtù, in that they extend human foresight and preparation, or as a dangerous illusion that attempts to eliminate fortuna altogether, thereby atrophying the decisive, bold capacities he insisted were necessary precisely because fortune can never be fully mastered? Address in particular whether he would distinguish between using forecasts to prepare for unexpected turns—consistent with his image of fortuna as a river that may be channeled but never fully controlled—and allowing forecasts to replace the moment of audacious decision, which he treated as the true test of political virtue. Your answer should treat Machiavelli's framework not as a static doctrine to be applied but as a living tension: does his thought contain resources for welcoming predictive technology, or does it ultimately require that some residue of unmastered contingency remain as the field upon which virtù proves itself?
