I'm writing a paper for my philosophy of science and ethics seminar on whether the simulation argument ought to weaken the intense longtermist commitment—sometimes characterized as fanatical—to shaping outcomes thousands or millions of years hence. Rather than asking for a survey of the literature, I want to test whether my own judgments across carefully varied scenarios form a coherent pattern or whether I'm merely rationalizing.

Here are my verdicts. First, if ancestor simulations are run by post-human civilizations mainly for historical or scientific understanding, then learning we might inhabit one should not diminish moral urgency about extinction or suffering in our layer; the experiences in question seem genuinely real regardless of substrate. Second, if instead the simulations are purpose-built ethical or theological trials with fixed, short durations—perhaps lasting only a few subjective centuries—then long-range planning becomes a kind of category mistake, and I think fanatical commitment to the far future is genuinely dampened, perhaps even irrational. Third, in a nested hierarchy where lower-level simulations face stricter resource caps and earlier termination conditions, I feel genuinely torn: on one hand, we should be more urgent about stabilizing our layer or escaping upward; on the other, the probable finitude of every layer makes open-ended longtermism seem epistemically arrogant. Fourth, if the argument applies exclusively to digital consciousness and we are biological entities, then it is simply irrelevant to our practical reasoning, which suggests that any dampening effect hinges entirely on an unverifiable empirical claim about our ontological status.

Is there a precise principle—perhaps combining moral invariance across ontological layers with an epistemic constraint on long-range planning under termination risk—that can accommodate the first, second, and fourth verdicts while resolving the tension in the third? Or does coherence require abandoning one of them? Please state the principle or a small set of rival candidates clearly, identify which case motivates each clause, predict the verdict for an unconsidered scenario (for instance, a simulation explicitly designed to compare longtermist versus near-term ethical frameworks and then terminate based on the result), and be explicit about which of my four judgments the best account still cannot save.
