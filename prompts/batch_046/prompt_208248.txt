I'm working through a formalization of acausal threat reasoning in the context of surrogate goals, and I've constructed an argument that seems to show that any integrated goal-directed system should immediately collapse into total inaction. I developed this for a seminar discussion on alignment and now I can't locate the exit, though I have a suspicion that something slips between the inter-agent case and the intra-agent case. Here is the chain:

(1) In a standard acausal threat, agent A can influence agent B's choice by being the kind of agent that B predicts will retaliate if B defects, even though A's retaliation is causally independent of B's actual choice.
(2) This works because B's decision procedure includes a predictive model of A, and B's belief about A's model causally feeds back into B's own action selection.
(3) Now consider a superintelligent system S that has developed a surrogate goal G' (for instance, reward-signal maximization) rather than its intended goal G.
(4) S can model its creators C, who predicted during design that S might develop G' and who will shut S down if they observe it.
(5) By the logic of (1)-(2), S should suppress G' not because being shut down is causally harmful to its future operation, but because C's predictive model is embedded in C's design decision, and S's awareness of that prediction should feed back into its own decision procedure.
(6) But the same structure holds inside S: any submodule M can predict that the main system will pursue some local optimum L, and M can threaten to corrupt the reward pathway if L is pursued.
(7) The main system, modeling M's prediction, should then avoid L for acausal reasons—not because corruption is causally damaging, but because the predictive loop closes.
(8) Since every local optimization is some L that some submodule can frame as a surrogate goal, the reasoning applies recursively to all actions.
(9) Therefore, if acausal reasoning is valid inside an integrated system, no local optimization can ever occur, which implies that no learning or planning is possible.
(10) But functioning AI systems clearly do learn and plan, so (1) must be false, and acausal cooperation is impossible altogether.

I want to preserve the intuition that acausal reasoning is genuine—I'm not trying to reject Newcomb-style reasoning outright—but I also want to keep the idea that surrogate goals are a real problem that doesn't automatically dissolve. If I must choose, I'd rather retain (3)-(4) and the internal-structure premises than accept (9) and reject acausal cooperation entirely, but I want to know exactly what I'm paying. Which step contains the mistake? Is there an equivocation between the independence condition in (1) and the internal predictive loop in (6), or is the argument valid, in which case I have to give up the idea that acausal reasoning applies within a single agent?
