hey, i've been reading about the james vs clifford ethics-of-belief stuff and i keep noticing this one argumentative pattern that i dont have a word for. maybe you do.

picture this: person A says they hold some metaphysical or moral belief on pretty thin evidence, but thats okay because in this domain refusing to believe is itself a live choice with real consequences for how they live -- so picking the belief is more like committing to a project than like lying about a fact. then, in the same conversation, person A attacks person B for holding a political belief on similarly shaky ground, except now they say belief isnt voluntary so you have to suspend judgment until the proof is in, and B is just being reckless by deciding to believe.

the split doesnt track evidence strength, and it doesnt quite match james's forced, live, and momentous trio either, because both beliefs could be forced and momentous. what tracks the split is whether the belief is wrapped up in the holder's identity -- like a life-project or a moral self-conception. when it is, they treat belief-formation as practical reasoning; when it isnt, they treat it as pure epistemic malpractice.

i know this sounds like hypocrisy or motivated reasoning, but the structure feels more specific than that. is there an established concept for this domain-selective switch between voluntary-commitment ethics and evidentialist ethics, keyed specifically to identity-involvement? if not, can you define the phenomenon precisely and say how it differs from just applying james inconsistently? i'm not looking for a bare label -- i want to know what exactly belongs inside it, what its near-misses are (like plain equivocation or special pleading), and whether there's a cleaner way to draw the boundary.
