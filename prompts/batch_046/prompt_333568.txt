You are a senior philosopher of science and the lead training designer for a national research-ethics audit office. Your auditors are trained scientists with no philosophical background; they review interdisciplinary grant-evaluation panels in 90-minute sessions, work only from the proposal, the reviewer comments, and a one-page glossary, and must sign off on whether the process committed epistemic injustice against the applicant’s framework.

Your task is to turn the framework of epistemic injustice—testimonial credibility deficit, credibility excess, and hermeneutical marginalization—into a reusable audit procedure. To keep the exercise from being generic, build in one specific mechanism: interpretive gatekeeping, defined as a reviewer rejecting a methodological or conceptual framework not on evidential grounds but because accepting it would require the reviewer to perform unpaid hermeneutical labor (learning a vocabulary, ontology, or evidential standard that the applicant’s community already uses).

Before writing the procedure, begin with brief conceptual mapping: list the three forms of injustice you are tracking, define interpretive gatekeeping in one sentence, and identify the two misfire risks you will build in. Then think step by step and present the deliverable.

The deliverable is an ordered decision procedure in words—questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the node where the procedure stops with “no systemic injustice detected,” the node where it stops with “flag for human judgment—value call required,” and the nodes where judgment is unavoidable even though the step looks mechanical. Flag every judgment call explicitly. Do not instruct auditors to consult external literature; every test must be runnable from the documents in front of them.

The procedure must handle hard cases: a legitimate methodological rejection that looks like credibility deficit; a proposal that genuinely lacks rigor but frames the rejection as hermeneutical exclusion; and a case of interpretive gatekeeping masked as “lack of falsifiability.” Include a short set of test sentences or document excerpts the auditor can apply at each node.

Then apply the procedure to two hypothetical proposals:
(1) A climate-adaptation study co-designed with an indigenous community, using oral-history testimony and relational ontology, rejected by quantitative modelers who wrote that the data lacks inter-subjective reproducibility and the theoretical framework is non-falsifiable.
(2) A technically sound econometric health-policy proposal that cites exclusively Western epidemiological literature and treats local healing practices as anecdotal background, where the applicant claims hermeneutical exclusion despite standard methodology.

For one of these, the procedure must yield an answer the audit office will find unwelcome—either flagging injustice where none was expected, or clearing a panel that was suspected of bias. State clearly which one and why.

Finally, state precisely what the procedure cannot do: which wrongs it is blind to, where false positives are likely, and which decisions must remain with a human sign-off regardless of the checklist outcome. Keep the tone formal, the steps concrete, and the boundary between fact-check and value-judgment visible at every stage.
