I hold the view that, once wild-animal suffering and crop-cultivation mortality are fully internalized, beef raised on genuinely non-arable pasture is morally preferable to conventionally raised chicken—because the usual recommendation to substitute chicken for beef commits what I call the "trophic substitution error": it treats feed-conversion ratios as morally neutral rather than as vectors of massive indirect wild-animal killing, and it compares direct suffering per kilogram while ignoring that grain production for poultry drives far more wild-animal deaths per unit of human-edible protein than grazing on marginal lands. Specifically, I claim that per calorie of net human nutrition, chicken production generates more total wild-animal mortality through plowing, planting, harvesting, and pesticide use in feed-crop cycles than beef production on pasture that could not otherwise yield crops, and that direct-suffering metrics alone therefore reverse the correct ranking. What would count against this position? I want the arguments, empirical findings, and conceptual objections that should actually move me, sorted from fatal to merely awkward—including, for example, comparative land-use data for beef feed versus direct pasture, estimates of wild-animal death per hectare for different grain and forage regimes, and normative challenges to treating indirect killing as equivalent to direct harm. I also want an honest assessment of whether defenders of this view (including me) can qualify it—say, by restricting it to particular pasture ecologies, specific beef-finishing diets, or idealized supply chains—until it becomes unfalsifiable, and if so, whether that insulation is a problem for a position of this kind. Please include what the view stands or falls with in adjacent domains (agricultural ecology, feed-economics, or normative ethics about indirect harm), distinguish what committed defenders would concede as damaging from what they would dismiss, and respond in American English.
