As a journalist covering philosophy and the science of moral psychology, I am developing an interactive module for a feature on whether justice is pursued for its own sake or only for the rewards of reputation. Design a 75-minute, in-person classroom activity for an upper-level ethics seminar of about 14 undergraduates that stages Glaucon's challenge—the ring of Gyges and the problem of invisibility—without reducing it to a familiar survey question about theft. Instead, build a two-phase simulation around a mechanism I will call reversible moral escrow. In Phase One, students work in pairs to accumulate shared points through cooperative choices; every contribution is visibly tracked and attributed, so reciprocity operates under full transparency. In Phase Two, a randomly assigned subset enters escrow: their actions still affect others' point totals, but the causal chain is masked, meaning neither praise nor blame can attach to them. These participants may exit escrow at any round by paying a disclosure cost drawn from their accumulated points. The group then votes—while still unaware of who was in escrow—on whether to adopt an institutional transparency rule for a final cooperative round. The rules, payoff structure (using only paper tokens or a visible scoreboard, no real money), and role assignments should be arranged so that participants first feel the stability of attribution-based cooperation, then experience the strategic pull of unaccountable action, and only afterward confront the normative question of whether they want transparency enforced. Provide a minute-by-minute timeline, a list of materials, explicit instructions for the facilitator, and a debrief that maps Phase One onto the construction of moral reputation, Phase Two onto Glaucon's claim that justice is merely the appearance of virtue maintained for social reward, and the vote onto the institutional choice between valuing morality and fearing exposure. Be frank about what will predictably go wrong on the first run—likely that students will treat the escrow vote as purely strategic rather than as a normative judgment about transparency—and explain how the facilitator should recover. Finally, state clearly which feature of an intrinsicist view—perhaps the first-person phenomenology of moral shame or the idea that virtue is its own reward—the simulation structurally cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied through conceptual framing rather than emerging from the game mechanics. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; construct the design entirely from the logic of the challenge and the mechanics of social attribution.
