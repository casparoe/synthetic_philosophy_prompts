I am writing a literary novel about two rival antiquarians trapped in a ruinous bidding contest over a worthless estate, and I need you to engineer a thought experiment—realistic, ordinary-life, no longer than a paragraph, with no lotteries, demons, perfect predictors, or science-fictional devices—that isolates the mechanism of escalation in a dollar-auction dynamic. The case must be constructed so that, at one specific decision node, a pure sunk-cost model predicts the bidder will continue raising, while a pure reputational model predicts she will withdraw, and both predictions must arise simultaneously from the identical situation rather than from separate framings. Once you have built the scenario, demonstrate that it actually produces this split: state the belief in play, the bidding mechanism, the relevant nearby worlds, and precisely why each model delivers its verdict. Be committed—do not merely present the two options and leave me to choose. Tell me exactly which feature of your design does the isolating work, what happens to both predictions if that feature is removed, and which model you judge actually explains real-world escalation in small, visible markets; defend that judgment with reasons rather than hedging. Finally, include an honest note on where your scenario takes liberties with Shubik’s 1971 formalization of the game or with the standard treatment of escalation as a discrete war of attrition, and flag any contested interpretation of the sunk-cost fallacy or reputational signaling that you rely upon.
