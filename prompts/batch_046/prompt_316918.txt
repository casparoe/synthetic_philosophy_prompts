Write an essay that addresses the following controversy: James permits belief in the absence of sufficient evidence only when the option is forced, living, and momentous; Clifford insists that belief without proportionate evidence is always wrong, regardless of practical stakes. Now consider a particular kind of belief that is not often discussed in this literature — what we might call narrative self-constitution beliefs. These are beliefs about one's own moral character or life-direction that are formed not through direct evidential tracking but through the active, retrospective construction of a coherent personal narrative. Withholding such belief, one might argue, does not leave one in suspended judgment but rather fractures one's practical identity.

Your essay should weigh whether this narrative-constitution case strengthens James's position, undermines Clifford's universal prohibition, or instead reveals that James's conditions are more permissive than he intended. Specifically, address whether the momentous condition can be satisfied purely by internal psychological stakes — the coherence of one's self-conception — rather than by external practical outcomes. Do not treat this as an open-ended survey; reach and defend a specific verdict, and in doing so, honestly acknowledge the strongest consideration that favors the opposing side.
