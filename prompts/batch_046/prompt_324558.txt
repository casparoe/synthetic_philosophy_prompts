At a workshop on the metaphysics of social reality, Mira and Jonas got into a sustained exchange about whether David Lewis's account of convention can accommodate gradual, non-catastrophic norm drift—say, a slow phonological shift in a speech community where no speaker ever faces a discrete coordination failure and no one explicitly notices the regularity breaking down. Mira opened by insisting that Lewis's framework is structurally tied to solving coordination problems through common knowledge of rational reasoning: agents must recognize that they share an interest in coordination and that each expects the other to reason similarly. A gradual statistical drift, she argued, involves no such moment of mutual recognition and therefore cannot be analyzed as a convention in Lewis's sense; at best it is an evolutionary stable strategy or a habit, and treating it as a Lewisian convention stretches the concept beyond recognition.

Jonas replied that this ignores the role of precedent and analogical reasoning in Lewis's account. Once a regularity R is established as a convention, agents can extend it to novel or modified cases by reasoning analogically—"this case is relevantly like those covered by R, so I should do the analog"—without needing a fresh coordination problem. A phonological shift, on this view, is just a chain of such analogical extensions: each speaker slightly modifies her pronunciation in response to perceived statistical shifts in others, maintaining the reasoning structure ("if they are shifting toward X, I should shift too to preserve coordination"). The macro-pattern looks like drift, but at the micro-level each step involves the common-knowledge reasoning Lewis requires.

Mira countered that Jonas is smuggling evolutionary dynamics into a rational-choice framework. The distinctive feature of Lewisian conventions is not merely that people adapt to others, but that they adapt through explicit reasoning about shared interests and mutual expectations. In gradual drift, speakers are not reasoning about shared interests in maintaining a convention; they are simply tracking frequency distributions. If every adaptive response to statistical change counts as analogical reasoning, then evolutionary game-theoretic accounts become indistinguishable from Lewis's, which undermines the very contrast he drew between conventions and evolutionary equilibria.

Jonas responded that the contrast is preserved because evolutionary accounts require only causal feedback—imitation, selection, reinforcement—whereas his analogical-extension model requires that agents represent the regularity as a reason for action. Even in drift, a speaker can represent "others are pronouncing it this way, and I want to coordinate with them" as her reason for shifting, not merely as a causal trigger. The fact that the shift is gradual does not eliminate the representational structure; it just distributes it across time. He added that if Mira's stricter view were correct, then Lewis could not explain how conventions survive minor perturbations or adapt to changing circumstances without dissolving and reforming, which seems descriptively inadequate.

Mira then argued that Jonas's representational requirement is too thin: any adaptive behavior can be redescribed post hoc as involving a representation of others' behavior as a reason. Without an independent criterion for when analogical reasoning is genuinely present—beyond mere behavioral adaptation—Jonas's defense collapses into a tautology. She suggested that the real issue is whether Lewis's framework needs to be supplemented by a dynamic theory of salience: if salience itself evolves endogenously through drift, then the conditions for common knowledge are never stably satisfied, and the convention either dissolves continuously or was never a convention to begin with.

Jonas conceded that salience dynamics are underdeveloped in Lewis's original account, but maintained that this is a gap to be filled rather than a fatal objection. He proposed that we treat salience as a graded, context-dependent parameter that can shift incrementally while remaining common knowledge at each stage—much like a slowly moving equilibrium in physics. Mira replied that this "moving equilibrium" metaphor abandons the discrete, binary character of common knowledge (you either know that we know, or you don't) and therefore sacrifices the logical rigor that makes Lewis's analysis attractive.

They turned to me and asked for adjudication.
