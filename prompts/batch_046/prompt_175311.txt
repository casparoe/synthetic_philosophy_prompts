As a retired physicist who has come to philosophy of science only after leaving the laboratory, I have spent the last year in a reading group that keeps returning to structural realism, and I am struck by how little movement there is. Every participant accepts that physical theories preserve mathematical form across revolutions and that quantum mechanics and general relativity appear to describe relations rather than intrinsic properties. Yet one faction insists that this shows we can know only the structure of reality, leaving its intrinsic nature hidden, while the other faction claims that relations are ontologically primary and that there are simply no relata behind them. Despite months of discussion, no one has revised a position, and I cannot locate a factual disagreement about physics or mathematics that explains the division. I wonder whether we are facing a verbal ambiguity—perhaps philosophers are using structure to cover both the approximate, scale-dependent regularities of effective field theories and the exact symmetries of putatively fundamental laws, distinctions that working scientists treat separately—or whether the split runs deeper, involving different standards for what counts as explanatory adequacy, incompatible intuitions about whether relations require relata, or the possibility that the two camps are answering different questions, one epistemic and one ontological, under a single name. Could you diagnose the nature of this impasse, grounding your account in what each side actually maintains about knowledge and ontology, and then say what would genuinely advance the discussion: a formal distinction among kinds of structural preservation, a reconceptualization of objecthood that dissolves the relata worry, an empirical result about theory change, or perhaps an argument that no progress should be expected and why?
