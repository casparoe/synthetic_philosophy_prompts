I've been facilitating a philosophy-of-technology reading group that has spent months arguing about Marx's analysis of machinery, and the participants have split into two camps that now talk past each other. I want you to draft the adversarial-collaboration statement they should have produced: a document both sides could sign that separates real disagreement from verbal confusion, using a concrete case rather than slogans. Camp One — call them the Fragment Readers — maintains that Marx's discussion of machinery in the Grundrisse (the passage often labeled the Fragment on Machines) identifies an immanent technical contradiction: as automation expands fixed capital and shrinks the direct labor time that creates value, capitalism tends to undermine its own value-form, pointing toward a post-labor transition that is technically driven regardless of political organization. Camp Two — the Capital Formalists — replies that Marx's mature analysis in Capital, Volume One treats machinery strictly as constant capital that transfers existing value but produces none; capital compensates for displaced direct labor through real subsumption, intensifying labor elsewhere, expanding the collective worker, extending the working day or lowering wages, so there is no automatic technical trajectory out of capitalism, only a social-form-specific crisis that capital can absorb and reproduce.

To keep the exercise precise and avoid generic repetition, frame every claim around a specific, somewhat niche scenario: modern algorithmic warehouse management, in which software directs human pickers, drivers, and packers via real-time metrics without physical robots replacing them. Both camps should agree on the empirical profile of such systems and on which Marxian categories apply uncontroversially — for instance, that the software functions as fixed capital, that the workers remain formally subsumed wage-laborers, and that surplus value still requires living labor. Then articulate the exact disagreement: do these systems show machinery reducing human labor to a supervisory residue that erodes the value-basis of production (the Fragment Readers), or do they show real subsumption reorganizing and intensifying the collective worker's labor so that living labor remains the sole source of surplus value, now distributed across tighter rhythms and larger bodies (the Capital Formalists)? Structure the output in four parts: (1) a numbered list of agreed claims; (2) a disagreements table with each camp's position phrased in wording its own members would accept; (3) a cruxes section identifying at least two sub-questions that would move the debate — for example, whether cognitive automation (large language models performing clerical mental labor) should be analyzed as machinery substituting for labor-power or as a new mode of real subsumption reorganizing mental labor, and whether Marx's category of the collective worker can absorb an arbitrarily large share of supervisory labor without losing its value-creating character — with, for each crux, a concrete case or argument both sides accept as relevant and each side's prediction recorded in advance; and (4) a brief assessment of whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not quote Marx verbatim; paraphrase all textual references. End with a single line beginning Residual disagreement: followed by a one-sentence summary of what remains unsettled.
