I am a high school student working through classical Chinese philosophy for an independent study, and I have been tracking an argument that has persisted for years without resolution among the readers and commentators I follow. The dispute centers on the well-known parable from the Zhuangzi in which Zhuang Zhou dreams he is a butterfly and, upon waking, is uncertain whether he is a man who dreamed he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming he is a man. One side treats this as a direct argument for global dream skepticism: because the phenomenology of vivid dreaming can match waking experience, and because any proposed criterion for being awake can apparently be duplicated within a dream, we lack a non-circular warrant for claiming we are awake now. The other side insists that this reading imports a modern epistemological framework the text does not contain; for them, the point is metaphysical and concerns the fluidity of identity—what they call transformative awareness—so that asking which one is really Zhuang Zhou presupposes a fixed self that the parable is meant to dissolve.

My working theory is that the disagreement is neither about epistemology nor about metaphysics in any straightforward sense, but about an unstated disagreement over the ontology of consciousness states. The skeptics seem to assume a binary framework in which wakefulness and dreaming are mutually exclusive, discrete conditions that require a decisive criterion to separate them. The metaphysical interpreters, by contrast, appear to operate with what I can only call a gradient ontology, in which states of awareness exist on a continuum and the demand for a binary criterion is itself a category mistake. If that is right, then the two sides are not answering the same question under one name; they are addressing different problems—one about justification under uncertainty, the other about the coherence of fixed identity across transformation.

Is this diagnosis correct, or is there a substantive empirical or formal disagreement doing hidden work? I want to know whether the sides differ about which experiential reports count as data, about the standards of evidence required to settle a claim about one's own state, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for. Could a conceptual distinction—say, between a defeasible marker of wakefulness and an indefeasible criterion—actually end the debate, or is the impasse a sign that the underlying question is ill-posed? Alternatively, is there an argument that no progress should be expected, and if so, what does that imply about how we should read the text? Please give me a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, together with an honest assessment of what would count as progress given that diagnosis.
