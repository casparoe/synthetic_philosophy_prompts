I'm writing this up for a column on forgotten self-improvement schemes, and I got into a proper row with two collegues over Franklin's moral perfection project — you know, the thirteen virtues, the little grid in his notebook, all that. (I'm a science journalist, usually cover neuroscience and odd bits of philosophy, but I got sidetracked.)

Anyway, my friend Ravi said Franklin was genuinely trying to engineer himself into a perfect man, and that the grid system — tracking his failures day by day — proves he treated virtue like a kind of Pareto optimisation across competing traits. He pointed out that Franklin added humility last after being told he was proud, and said that shows he was actually responsive to feedback, not just performing for posterity. But then my other colleague, Jules, said the whole thing was essentially a literary device. She argued that Franklin knew perfection was impossible, that the grid was designed to produce charming failure narratives for his autobiograpy, and that adding humility as virtue number thirteen was a self-deprecating punchline, not a serious amendment. Ravi came back saying if it was just a joke, why did Franklin keep the notebooks for decades and actually adjust his behavour? Jules said people maintain ironic projects for years if the persona serves them, and that the failure was the brand.

They kept going — Ravi claimed the tension between order and flexibility in Franklin's list shows he was wrestling with real moral psychology, not writing satire; Jules said that tension proves the list was incoherent and therefore not meant as a practical programme. I want you to adjudicate this move by move: who's right about the grid, about the humility addition, and about whether the internal contradictions in the virtues undermine Franklin's seriousness? Also, what are they both missing about how Franklin's idea of moral perfection differs from modern quantified-self optimisation? Don't just pick a winner — tell me where each point lands, and whether there's anything about Franklin's actual practice that neither of them has accounted for.
