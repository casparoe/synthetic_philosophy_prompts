I am delivering the opening remarks at the retirement workshop for Dr. Elena Vasquez, who spent thirty years redesigning safety protocols for chemical plants. The room is split: half are philosophers of science familiar with dispositions, finks, and masks; half are practitioners and regulators for whom those terms are unfamiliar. I want the metaphysics of masked and finked dispositions to do real structural work in the tribute—specifically, to block me from saying something comforting but false about her legacy, such as that every near-miss revealed a hidden strength in the systems she built. The concrete case is this: she inherited redundant cooling loops whose backup was silently disabled by an automated override that activated only during the exact failure mode the backup was meant to cover, so that in ordinary conditions and in crisis conditions alike the redundancy never manifested. I need the practitioners to understand why attributing to those loops a genuine disposition to fail safely was either empty or wrong, and I need the philosophers to see that I am not smuggling in a disguised account of potentiality. At the same time, the speech must honor what she actually did—recognizing these hidden interferences and rebuilding the protocols so the redundancy was real, not masked. Write a five-minute address that treats the philosophical point as a constraint on praise rather than as decoration, that includes the cooling-loop example explicitly, and that lands as a sincere tribute rather than a lecture. Do not include citations, author names, bibliographies, or references to specific texts; the argument should stand on its own. Ensure the speech offers something genuinely meaningful to both halves of the audience without losing either.
