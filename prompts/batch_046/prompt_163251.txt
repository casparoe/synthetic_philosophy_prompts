I'm a PhD student in politcal philosophy and I'm helping co-organize this small workshop next month — we're calling it "Justice Without Force?" and the crowd is weirdly mixed: some law grads, a couple activists from local housing groups, and a few philosophers who actually know Pascal. I'm giving the opening remarks, maybe six or seven minutes, and I want them to do real structural work, not just set the vibe. The idea I want to center is Pascal's fragment about justice and force — how justice is disputed because it isn't self-evident, but force is obvious and undisputed, so we dress up force as justice just to make it survive in the world. But here's the thing: I don't want to quote him and sit down. The remarks need to make it impossible for anyone in the room to either celebrate law as pure reason backed by consent, or dismiss it as pure violence with a dress code. Pascal's point, as I read it, is that justice needs force to exist, but force never needs justice — that asymmetry should hang over the whole occassion. I also want to nod to the distinction he draws between physical coercion and the "force" of custom or institutional inertia, which I think gets lost in the standard justice-vs-power reading and is actually the more interesting part for our discussion. Can you write these remarks? They should land for the philosophers without losing the activists, avoid any bibliography or "go read Pensées fragment X" homework, and not resolve the tension — just hold it open like Pascal does. Oh, and please don't say anything he would reject, like that justice is impossible or that might makes right in some simple sense — he wasn't a nihilist, just clear-eyed about the marriage.
