I'm currently in my final year of a philosophy programme, and I'm writing a position paper for our seminar on distributive justice. We've spent several weeks on prioritarianism and the leveling-down objection, but the discussion has stayed fairly close to the standard Parfit-style cases. I want to push the argument in a slightly different direction, and I'd like you to construct the defence for me.

Here is the specific view to defend: imagine a modified prioritarian framework—call it 'threshold-calibrated prioritarianism'—in which moral weights are indexed not to absolute levels of well-being but to an individual's distance from a context-sensitive baseline of basic capability. On this account, the weight assigned to a person's improvement collapses to zero once they fall beneath the baseline, and is strictly capped once they exceed it by a substantial margin. Argue that, under this framework, the leveling-down objection is not simply evaded but conceptually misdirected. In particular, show why reducing the well-being of the best-off to match that of the worst-off destroys value without generating any compensatory gain: the downward shift does not alter the (already collapsed) weight of those below the threshold, and it risks pushing the formerly best-off toward the collapse point. You should address the classic intuition pump—blinding the sighted to achieve equality—and explain precisely why this does not smuggle egalitarian commitments back in through the threshold mechanism. There is one internal tension I want you to resolve as part of the argument: if the baseline is genuinely context-sensitive, does this not introduce a problematic relativism that undermines the objectivity prioritarianism typically claims? Do not treat this as an afterthought; weave the resolution into the architecture of the view. Please avoid empirical case studies or historical examples, and do not provide a bibliography—just focus on the conceptual structure.
