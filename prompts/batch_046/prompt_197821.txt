I am preparing the final assessment for an honors-level high school seminar on philosophy and the social sciences, and I need a single essay question together with a model answer and a detailed marking scheme. The exam is closed-book, sixty minutes long, and the question should carry exactly twenty-five marks; students should write roughly eight hundred to one thousand words.

The domain is whether culture can be a cause, centered on Weber’s Protestant ethic thesis and the problem of explaining with values. I do not want a prompt that can be answered by summarizing the familiar claim that Calvinism caused capitalism. Instead, please construct the question around a specific conceptual crux that forces students to do philosophical work: a hypothetical critic charges that Weber’s idea of an “elective affinity” between Protestant doctrine and capitalist practice is only a metaphor—borrowed from chemistry—that disguises a fallacy of misplaced concreteness, treating an abstract cultural pattern as if it were a substance with causal powers. A defender replies that Weber’s distinction between “causal adequacy” and “meaningful adequacy” allows values to explain action without reducing them to material conditions. The exam prompt should ask students to adjudicate this dispute. Specifically, they must explain what meaningful adequacy contributes that causal adequacy alone cannot supply, and they must assess whether that contribution actually solves the reification problem or merely renames it by making the relationship sound like chemical attraction rather than human agency.

Please provide:
1. The exam question itself, worded clearly for a sixty-minute essay.
2. A model answer showing the conceptual moves a top response must make.
3. A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with marks assigned to each totaling exactly twenty-five, and a one-line note on the typical mistake that costs marks at each point.

The prompt should not request a bibliography, reading list, or citations. It should also avoid dependence on obscure secondary scholarship; students should be able to answer from core Weberian concepts and from the logical structure of the objection as presented in the question.
