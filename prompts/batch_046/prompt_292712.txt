I'm delivering the opening remarks for the Concordia workshop, a closed three-day session bringing together moral philosophers, constitutional lawyers, and grassroots organizers to draft preliminary guidelines for a transnational water-rights tribunal. The room is deliberately split: roughly half the participants are academics deeply versed in debates over value pluralism, incommensurability, and the limits of moral translation; the other half are practitioners—diplomats, legal drafters, and community representatives—who need to produce a workable framework by Sunday evening and have little patience for metaphysical stalemate. I want the address to do real structural work around the concept I'm calling "moral gains from trade": the claim that when value systems are genuinely incompatible, compromise is not merely a second-best surrender but can operate analogously to economic exchange, with each side conceding what it values less in order to secure what it values more, without either party being required to translate its core commitments into the other's normative currency. The speech should establish ground rules that flow directly from this framing—specifically, that participants will not demand that others justify concessions using the conceding party's own deepest moral vocabulary, and that we will treat durable disagreement about foundational reasons as a stabilizing feature of the agreement rather than as a failure to reach true consensus. At the same time, the address must avoid both the fantasy of a hidden universal metric that renders all values commensurable and the paralysis of treating conflict as irreducible. Write a polished fifteen-minute opening address that sets these boundaries clearly, respects the philosophers without losing the practitioners, and uses the trade analogy not as decorative rhetoric but as the organizing architecture of the workshop's method.
