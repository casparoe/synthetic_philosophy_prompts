I serve on my university's Committee on Professional Integrity, which advises on staff requests for exemption from assigned duties on grounds of personal or professional integrity. We have adopted the following positions, drawing on Bernard Williams's integrity objection and the related concern that moral reasoning can involve substituting calculation for direct attachment—what he described as having one thought too many.

Our precedents: we granted exemption to a physician who refused to participate in a court-authorized lethal-injection protocol, holding that requiring a healer to administer death violates a role-constitutive commitment; we denied exemption to an accountant who refused to file returns for a client whose politics she despised, holding that routine professional service does not implicate integrity; we granted conditional exemption to a researcher who refused to share a unique dataset that a rival group would use to destroy her career-defining project, requiring publication within one year; and we denied exemption to a librarian who refused to process acquisitions from a particular nation, holding that curatorial neutrality is part of her professional identity and her objection rested on general political judgment rather than a direct, role-constitutive attachment.

A new request has arrived. A senior musician in our university orchestra, whose career is built on performing classical repertoire but who also teaches and administers programs, asks to be excused from playing at an official state ceremony honoring a political figure she considers morally corrupt. She does not claim the performance threatens her musical project or her livelihood; rather, she argues that playing beautiful music in direct service of this figure feels like a personal betrayal of her deepest values, and that her integrity requires her not to become an instrument of legitimation, even at the cost of institutional obligation. Her absence would force cancellation of a major section of the program, and no substitute is available on short notice.

If we want to be consistent with our track record, what should we decide? Identify the closest analog among our four precedents, state the two most plausible principles that explain our prior verdicts jointly, say what each principle predicts about this musician's case, and give your best recommendation with the reasoning for preferring it. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, be explicit about what principle we would be adopting with each possible decision. Finally, specify the further case that would distinguish the two principles.
