I'm giving a job talk next tuesday defending a restricted version of James's will to believe against Clifford-style evidentalism. My thesis is that Clifford's injunction applies cleanly to first-order empirical beliefs but breaks down when turned on what I'm calling scafolding commitments: the background presuppositions like the uniformity of nature, the basic reliability of testimony in ordinary domains, and the coherence of inductive practice that make particular evidential assessments possible in the first place. I argue James is right that these can't be grounded evidentially without circularity, so a limited will to believe is necessary to avoid skeptical paralysis rather than being a license for wishful thinking. The audience is mostly analytic epistemologists and philosophers of science — folks who take Clifford seriously as a live position, have some sympthy for pragmatic encroachment, but think James mostly licenses fideism and sloppy reasoning. They care about circularity arguments, about not smuggling in hidden a priori commitments, and about whether this collapses into a bad kind of foundationalism.

Prepare me for the Q&A: list the objections I'll actually get, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do, phrased the way an analytic epistemologist would put them (no LessWrong jargon). For each one, give me a two-minute reply and flag exactly where the reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede up front — probably that my scope is much narrower than James intended and that I'm not defending belief against evidence inside science. And include the objection I'm praying nobody asks, which is whether my scafolding commitments are just disguised rationalist metaphysics and whether I've accidentally revived the kind of a priori framework that analytic philosophy of science rejected long ago. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
