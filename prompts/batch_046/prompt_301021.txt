I am a doctoral student working at the intersection of philosophy of history and social ontology, and after a seminar discussion last week on collective agency I found myself wondering whether the conceptual architecture behind perpetrator explanation has been left behind by empirical historiography. The debate between Christopher Browning's study of Reserve Police Battalion 101—where situational pressures, peer dynamics, career incentives, and incremental commitment appear sufficient to drive ordinary men toward mass killing—and Daniel Goldhagen's opposing emphasis on a deeply rooted, culturally distributed eliminationist antisemitism, remains one of the most consequential in modern genocide studies. Yet I think there is an underexplored philosophical layer: can Goldhagen's notion of an eliminationist mindset be rendered coherent as a non-reducible, group-level dispositional state without committing us to a mysterious collective consciousness, and can Browning's mechanisms be reconstructed within frameworks of collective intentionality that allow distributed action without requiring shared explicit beliefs in every participant?

If you were advising a small interdisciplinary group—perhaps three philosophers and two historians—who have three years of funding to work on the conceptual foundations of perpetrator explanation rather than on new archival research, which open questions, implications, or lines of theoretical work should they prioritize, and in what order? I am not looking for a survey or a reading list. Instead, provide a defended agenda: for each priority, explain why it is upstream of the others—what conceptual blockages it removes—how tractable it is for philosophers without access to fresh empirical data, and what kind of argument or finding over the next few years would cause you to revise its rank upward or downward. In particular, I want you to weigh whether the central crux is ontological (whether cultural dispositions can be causally efficacious without being instantiated in individual psychologies at the moment of action), methodological (how historians should treat archival silence regarding explicit ideological statements), or normative (what the integration of situational and cultural explanations implies for our attributions of moral responsibility in collective violence). Please also identify any apparently important question that is actually a poor fit for this group—either because it demands empirical resources they do not have, or because resolving it would not materially alter our understanding of perpetration.
