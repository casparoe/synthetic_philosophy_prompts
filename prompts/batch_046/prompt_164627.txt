I am a retired physicist who has only recently begun serious study in philosophy of mind and the history of computing. I have been reading Alan Turing’s post-war writings on intelligent machinery, particularly his analogy between educating a machine and educating a child: he seemed to think that true intelligence should emerge from minimal innate structure, subjected to disciplined, error-correcting instruction guided by a teacher, rather than being supplied in advance with explicit rules for every domain. That pedagogical vision strikes me as quite different from the dominant contemporary practice of pretraining large language models on vast, largely uncurated text corpora and then refining them through reinforcement learning from human feedback.

With that framing in mind, I want to ask what Turing would likely have said about modern large language models had he lived to observe them. Would he have regarded today’s training pipeline—massive statistical ingestion of text followed by reward-based fine-tuning—as a realization of the child-like education he advocated, or as a departure from it? He expressed particular skepticism about mechanisms that merely reproduce stored information in novel combinations without genuine understanding. Yet present-day systems occasionally generate coherent philosophical arguments about education and cognition—arguments they were never explicitly taught.

Here is the more specific, somewhat unconventional angle I wish to inject: a small but growing body of researchers has recently proposed what they call curriculum pretraining and embodied tutoring protocols, in which models are exposed to data in staged difficulty levels and coupled with robotic sensory apparatus, explicitly attempting to replicate a school-like progression involving experiential interaction rather than pure textual absorption. Would Turing have seen these protocols as closer to his vision of disciplined machine education? Furthermore, if he encountered a system capable of arguing about pedagogical theory—producing reasoning that is not a direct reproduction of any single source—would he interpret that as the kind of emergent initiative he hoped machine learning might achieve, or as an especially sophisticated form of the storage-and-retrieval mechanism he distrusted?

Please address this by reasoning from the commitments evident in Turing’s well-known 1950 essay and in the 1951 lecture he delivered to the Manchester society, without offering a bibliography or listing specific sources. I am particularly interested in whether his implicit distinction between externally guided discipline and autonomous problem-solving can be mapped onto the modern distinction between supervised fine-tuning and the in-context reasoning capacities that appear to emerge during large-scale pretraining.
