Since my recent appointment to our university’s intergenerational risk-assessment panel, my mentor’s prior judgments on distributive harm have become directly relevant to a recommendation I must draft. Her track record: (1) severely torturing one person is worse than giving every currently living human an imperceptible dust speck in the eye; (2) permanently blinding one person is worse than causing every currently living human to lose a single hair; (3) inflicting a year-long severe migraine on one person is worse than causing 10^25 people each to undergo a subliminal discomfort that never reaches consciousness; (4) killing one innocent person is worse than causing 10^35 people each to feel an unnoticed 0.1-second temperature shift; (5) when asked about that same migraine versus 10^18 people each genuinely feeling a mild ten-second headache, she said she was genuinely torn but ultimately held that the single migraine was still worse, calling this the weakest point in her view.

We must now choose between Policy A, which will certainly subject one randomly selected adult to one day of severe torture, and Policy B, which will certainly cause every conscious being that will ever exist—numbering in the quintillions across all generations—each to experience one genuinely felt minute of mild headache, with no other effects. Predict her verdict and defend consistency. What are the two or three most plausible rules that jointly explain verdicts 1–4? Which of them diverges on this new case, and what does each predict about Policy A versus Policy B? Which earlier case—3, 5, or 1—is the closest analog, and why? If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, what principle is she adopting by endorsing Policy B rather than Policy A, and vice versa? Finally, what single hypothetical case—requiring no empirical data I cannot access—would best distinguish the remaining rules?
