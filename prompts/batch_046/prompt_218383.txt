I have been drafting a novel in which a protagonist wakes each morning convinced she has lived through elaborate nocturnal dramas, only to discover that her written accounts bear little resemblance to any continuous experience—an inversion of the usual unreliable-narrator trope that has led me into the philosophical literature on whether dreams are experiences at all. In popular discussion I keep encountering an analogy that treats sleep as a private cinema: the brain is said to project a continuous film in a darkened auditorium, with the sleeper as the sole audience member, and this image is routinely used to defend the intuition that dreams must be conscious occurrences unfolding in real time during REM, since a screening requires a viewer present. I want to press this analogy to its breaking point, not to settle the Malcolm-Dennett dispute by citation, but to see whether its rhetorical force holds up under structural audit.

Please lay out the explicit mapping: what corresponds to the projector, the reel, the screen, the darkness, and the audience in both the real-time experience account and in Dennett's competing model, where the narrative is assembled upon awakening rather than witnessed during sleep? Identify which disanalogies are merely decorative and which undermine the inference that a viewer is necessary—particularly those involving temporal continuity, sensory modality, and the absence of any verification criterion for the private screening. Then tell me whether the cinema analogy, once stripped of its imported features, still supports the conclusion that dreams are experiences occurring in the moment, or whether it collapses into something closer to the image of finding an unmarked manuscript in a drawer: coherent upon retrieval, but not witnessed in its composition. Speak from what you know of the arguments, without lists of further reading, and treat the analogy as the central object of analysis rather than a stepping-stone to authority.
