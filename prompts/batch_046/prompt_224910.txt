I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, though I occasionally read in the field. Recently I came across an unnamed essayist's summary that tries to collapse the usual debate—whether the Enlightenment was primarily a source of progress or of domination—into a single structural diagnosis rather than a historical disagreement. I am unsure how much of it is a fair synthesis and how much is a distortion, so I would like an audit.

The summary runs as follows. First, it claims that the Enlightenment is best understood not as a set of ideas about knowledge or liberty, but as a system-level upgrade: it replaced heterogeneous, context-bound authority with a universal evaluation protocol—reason—whose essential function was to make every particular attachment legible to centralized administration. Second, it asserts that Kant's categorical imperative and Locke's theory of property rights are not independent moral and political doctrines, but two implementations of this same protocol; both require that concrete relationships be stripped of local meaning so they can be processed by a neutral, abstract procedure. Third, it argues that colonial bureaucracy and industrial science did not merely coincide historically, but co-emerged structurally, because both depend on exactly this translation of particular knowledge into standardized form—the colony and the laboratory being parallel sites of the same operation. Fourth, it concludes that when defenders cite democratic institutions, medical science, or individual rights as achievements of the Enlightenment, they are identifying surface-level outputs without recognizing that these outputs are structurally coupled to extraction and domination. The underlying architecture, on this view, is neither good nor evil in isolation; it is an extraction engine that produces progress and domination as coupled outputs.

How fair is this? I want a claim-by-claim assessment: which parts accurately reflect arguments made by serious critics, which parts conflate distinct positions into a single protocol, which parts overstate correlation into structural necessity, and what a defender of the Enlightenment—someone who holds that universal reason genuinely protects against arbitrary domination—would reply. In particular, does the identification of Kantian universalism with Lockean property theory survive scrutiny, or is it a category error that collapses normative ethics into economic liberalism? And if that identification fails, does the summary's final claim—that defenders are describing symptoms of a deeper extraction architecture—still hold, or does it depend on the conflation?
