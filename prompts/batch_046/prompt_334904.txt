Adopt the view that logical omniscience in epistemic logic is a defensible normative idealisation, not only for human reasoners but specifically for artificial systems: the failures of closure observed in large language models—missing immediate entailments, contradicting premises across contexts—ought to be treated as performance limitations (retrieval or working-memory bottlenecks) rather than competence-level absences of logical knowledge. The agent’s implicit belief state should be modelled as closed under consequence, with any deviation merely an implementation artefact. What would have to be true for this to be wrong? I am not asking for the familiar bounded-rationality objections applied to humans. Rather: suppose neural architectures were shown to possess no compositional syntax in their implicit representations, so that there is no structured “knowledge state” available to be closed; or suppose formal results demonstrated that closure under even single-step inference is intractable in principle for any finite-state system, not merely slow in practice. Which of these would be fatal, and which could defenders absorb as describing only the mechanism of retrieval? What costs—such as an inability to explain epistemic progress through explicit inference—would proponents themselves concede, and which objections would they reject as category mistakes? Rank the defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, and say whether the position is genuinely exposed or well insulated.
