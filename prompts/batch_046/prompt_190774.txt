Write a dialogue between Dr. A, who defends Sartre's account of radical freedom and bad faith, and Dr. B, who accepts Sartre's ontology but contests the individualist framing of self-deception. Focus the exchange on a concrete scenario: a physician working within an opaque algorithmic triage system that ranks patients by an undisclosed score. Dr. A should argue that even under such a protocol, the physician remains radically free; if she treats her own evaluative consciousness as merely executing the score, she is reducing her transcendence to facticity, which is precisely bad faith—a freely adopted flight from freedom, not a structural inevitability. Dr. B should reply that this protocol is engineered to make the physician's own judgment appear epistemically unreliable relative to the algorithm, so the self-deception is not a personal project but is distributed across the institutional design. Dr. B should introduce the notion of heteronomous bad faith, in which the deception is embedded in the role architecture rather than located in the agent's choice, and should press Dr. A on why distributed deception should still be attributed to the individual as an original choice to comply. Dr. A, in turn, should address whether institutional design can ever genuinely obscure transcendence or whether it merely supplies a more seductive pretext for the same original flight, and should respond directly to the claim that the physician's compliance is not freely chosen but structurally coerced. Both interlocutors should be equally competent, avoid strawman characterizations, engage with each other's actual points in real back-and-forth, and conclude without either conceding defeat. The dialogue should be philosophically rigorous and should not rely on external citations or bibliographic references.
