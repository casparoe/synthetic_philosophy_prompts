I have been studying moral philosophy independently, without formal coursework, and have become preoccupied with the intuition that moral responsibility requires that one could have done otherwise. Through popular lectures and introductory readings, I encountered Harry Frankfurt's well-known challenge to this principle of alternative possibilities, centered on a hidden controller who never actually intervenes. While I can summarize the case, I am unsure whether I understand the underlying philosophical structure well enough to apply it to a genuinely novel situation, so I would like a rigorous self-test.

Could you please design a final-exam essay question suitable for an upper-level undergraduate seminar? The exam should allow forty-five minutes and be marked out of thirty points. Rather than asking for a summary of Frankfurt's argument, the question should require students to analyze a modified scenario—let us call it the institutional-protocol case. In this case, an agent completes an important decision within a bureaucratic organization that possesses an automatic override protocol designed to activate if and only if the agent shows any sign of hesitation. The agent does not hesitate, so the protocol remains dormant; however, she is unaware of the protocol's precise trigger conditions. She also believes she retains a right of appeal that could halt the process, but unknown to her that appeals mechanism has been administratively disabled. The exam question should ask whether this agent possesses alternative possibilities in the sense relevant to moral responsibility, and whether Frankfurt's counterexample can be extended to cover institutional, rather than personal, hidden controllers.

Please provide three elements: first, the exam question itself, clearly worded; second, a model answer that demonstrates the precise analytical moves expected—specifically, distinguishing the counterfactual causal structure of the original case from the epistemic asymmetry introduced by the agent's false belief about appeals, explaining why the dormant protocol functions analogously to Frankfurt's hidden controller, and assessing whether the principle of alternative possibilities should be abandoned, retained in a causal form, or reformulated in epistemic terms; and third, a marking scheme that assigns marks explicitly, identifying the moves that earn full credit (for example, recognizing that the absence of physical coercion does not entail the presence of metaphysical alternatives, noting that the disabled appeals mechanism concerns what the agent can know rather than what she can causally do, and evaluating whether moral responsibility depends on access to possibilities rather than on their bare existence) and the errors that should reduce the score (for example, conflating the hidden protocol with actual coercion, treating the agent's false belief as itself a Frankfurt-style intervener, or offering a biographical summary of the debate without engaging the institutional variant). The point is to separate students who have memorized the label Frankfurt case from those who can disentangle causal counterfactuals from epistemic conditions in an unfamiliar setting.
