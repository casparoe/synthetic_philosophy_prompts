Exam question: What is the heterogeneity problem for hedonism? Explain why the qualitative differences among pleasures—such as sensory enjoyment, intellectual satisfaction, and emotional relief—threaten the claim that pleasure is the sole intrinsic good. Then distinguish the qualitative hedonist's response from the quantitative hedonist's response, and evaluate whether either succeeds in showing that all pleasures share a feature sufficient to ground a unified theory of value.

Student answer:

Hedonism holds that pleasure is the only intrinsic good and pain the only intrinsic bad. The heterogeneity problem observes that the pleasures of eating, solving a mathematical problem, and hearing music appear to have no common phenomenal quality; they differ in intensity, duration, and sensory modality, but share nothing intrinsic. If so, then grouping them under one heading pleasure seems arbitrary, and treating them as instances of a single good becomes unjustified.

The qualitative hedonist, such as John Stuart Mill, responds that all pleasures do share one uniform feature—an irreducible quality of pleasantness—just as all red objects share redness. Differences in kind are differences in the objects or causes of pleasure, not in pleasure itself. By contrast, the quantitative hedonist, in the tradition of Jeremy Bentham, denies that any shared intrinsic quality is necessary. Instead, pleasure is treated as a homogeneous magnitude measurable along dimensions like intensity, duration, certainty, and propinquity. Once pleasure is operationalized numerically, qualitative diversity becomes irrelevant to moral arithmetic.

The quantitative approach therefore resolves the heterogeneity problem more decisively than the qualitative one, because it removes the burden of identifying a mysterious shared feel across disparate experiences. Meanwhile, Mill's own distinction between higher and lower pleasures actually undermines the qualitative strategy: rather than appealing to a single shared tone, Mill ranks pleasures by the verdict of competent judges. This shows that qualitative hedonism implicitly relies on plural criteria of value and therefore collapses into a kind of pluralism, leaving quantitative measurement as the only genuinely unified hedonist option.
