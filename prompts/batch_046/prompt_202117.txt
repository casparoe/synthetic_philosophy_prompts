In discussions of Maimonides’ philosophy of religion, animal sacrifice is often analogized to training wheels on a bicycle: a temporary pedagogical support for a people not yet capable of pure intellectual worship, meant to be discarded once maturity arrives. The analogy is used to defend the stronger claim that even biblically ordained rituals are purely instrumental and ultimately dispensable. Audit it explicitly: lay out the correspondence table—what plays the role of the bicycle, the rider, the parent, the road, and the moment of removal—then identify the disanalogies that matter for that conclusion. In particular, address whether the analogy can accommodate Maimonides’ insistence that the sacrificial laws retain an eternal formal structure even after their meaning is transformed, and tell me whether the conclusion that sacrifice is merely provisional scaffolding survives once those breaks are visible, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along. If the analogy collapses, suggest a better one that captures the same pedagogical logic without smuggling in dispensability.
