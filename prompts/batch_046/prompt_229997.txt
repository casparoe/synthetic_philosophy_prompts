I'm closing a graduate seminar on consciousness and sleep, and the final discussion has kept returning to the Malcolm-Dennett dispute over whether dreams are genuine experiences that occur during sleep or narratives assembled at the moment of waking. The students are familiar with Malcolm's argument that without an independent criterion for verifying perceptual content in an unobservable state we have no justification for treating dream-reports as reports of actual experience, and with Dennett's response that a cassette or splice model—content loaded, edited, or composed upon arousal—makes the question of what was happening depend on a retrospective construction that may not correspond to any continuous nocturnal occurrence. I want to end the session with a poem that takes this debate seriously rather than decorating it.

Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, two refrains alternating through an ABA rhyme scheme, finally meeting in the closing couplet—in which one refrain captures Malcolm's skeptical position (that in sleep there was no perceptual occurrence to recover) and the other captures Dennett's compositional position (that the account is spliced together at dawn). The speaker should be someone just waking, addressing either themselves or an unseen listener, so that the first-person report of the dream becomes structurally ambiguous: the act of saying I dreamed should feel as though it might be either recalling an absence or performing an edit. Use the form's repetition to show why the two views keep passing each other—each refrain returns unchanged, yet the rhyme scheme forces them into proximity—and place the turning point in the penultimate stanza, where the speaker realizes that choosing between the positions requires deciding whether waking consciousness discovers or invents the night that preceded it. Do not resolve the tension; let the final couplet hold both refrains side by side, leaving open whether sleep was a phenomenological blank or an unseen production. Make the poem accurate enough that a philosopher of mind will recognize the criterion problem on one side and the narrative-splice model on the other, but write it so that a reader unfamiliar with the debate can still follow the human situation. No bibliography, no citation, no list of further reading—this is for a room that already carries the texts in memory.
