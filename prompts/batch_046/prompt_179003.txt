Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps on whether generational categories—Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, and analogous cohort labels—denote real social kinds or are nominal demographic artefacts.

Party A maintains that these cohorts are ontologically robust: shared formative historical conditions (technological, economic, political) cluster in ways that produce stable, cross-contextual dispositions, so explaining behaviour by cohort membership is not reducible to age-grading or period effects.

Party B maintains that the labels are reified marketing and administrative conveniences: observed differences collapse into life-stage universals and socio-economic trajectory once properly controlled; the boundary conditions (e.g., 1981–1996 for Millennials) are arbitrarily drawn, and treating them as kinds risks ecological fallacy and misdirected policy.

Produce the document they should sign together. Structure it as follows:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims—covering at least: that birth-year bins have predictive utility in specific domains; that age-related variance is real and large; that measurement of cohort effects is confounded by period and life-stage; and that policy sometimes targets cohorts successfully even if the ontology is unclear.

2. The residual disagreements, phrased in neutral wording that each party accepts for its own view—particularly on whether formative-event clustering generates kind-level causal powers, and whether social-kind emergence requires intentional institutional construction or can arise from distributed historical exposure alone.

3. Three cruxes, specified precisely: (i) whether a non-arbitrary natural boundary exists around the digital-transition cohort; (ii) whether inter-cohort variance in a specified outcome (e.g., political socialisation or mental-health presentation) exceeds intra-cohort life-stage variance after controlling for socio-economic trajectory; (iii) whether targeted policy (e.g., “Gen Z mental-health programmes”) succeeds because the kind is causally real or because it accidentally intersects with age and class.

4. For each crux, describe one concrete study design or empirical case both parties agree would bear on it, and record each party’s explicit prediction in advance—what result would move them, and what they would conclude if the opposite result occurred.

5. A brief section on the downstream stakes: what each party predicts about survey design, resource allocation, or predictive modelling if the other party’s ontological view is correct.

Keep the tone formal, avoid bibliographies or reading lists, and ensure the disagreement is made exact rather than rhetorical. Note also which feature of the ontological dispute—causal efficacy, boundary determinacy, or reducibility—this collaboration format cannot resolve without further empirical work, and state frankly what the facilitator must supply.
