They asked me to referee and I honestly can't tell who's landing the good points.

Maya said illusionism about phenomenal consciousness is self-defeating: if there's genuinely no phenomenal feel, then there's no subject being fooled, so illusionism eliminates the very phenomenon it needs to explain. Leo said that's wrong because illusionism only denies intrinsic non-physical phenomenal properties, not functional representations; the 'seeming' is just a cognitive misrepresentation of its own structure, so what's being explained is the misrepresentation itself, not some ghostly property.

Maya came back that this just collapses illusionism into representationalism — if there's a representational state with phenomenal content, that content just IS phenomenal consciousness, only redescribed. So illusionists aren't denying consciousness, they're giving it a fancy representational label. Leo replied that phenomenal properties are theoretical posits from introspection alone; once you show introspection is unreliable, you've removed them from ontology. The illusion is specifically that we think these representations reveal intrinsic qualities.

Maya then said this feels like a bait-and-switch: saying consciousness is an illusion but keeping conscious representations is like saying the table is an illusion while keeping a table-representation — you haven't eliminated the object unless you also eliminate the representation's object. Here the representation is the consciousness, so you've kept exactly what you claimed to get rid of. Leo answered that tables are physical objects we can verify externally, but phenomenal properties depend solely on introspection; showing that faculty is unreliable eliminates the need for them in ontology, which is the core claim.

Can you go through each of these moves and rate them from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief reasons?
