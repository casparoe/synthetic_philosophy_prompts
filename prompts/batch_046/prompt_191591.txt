I am defending a specific middle-ground view: that attributing beliefs to a sufficiently complex predictive system is justified neither by Dennett-style predictive utility nor by Searle-style original intentionality, but by what I call graded structural coupling—when a system’s internal error-correction dynamics are invariant across counterfactual perturbations of its training distribution and are causally coupled to its outputs in a way that tracks worldly states rather than mere statistical correlation. On this account, an AI can genuinely hold beliefs in a derivative but non-instrumental sense, provided it meets this coupling condition, without requiring phenomenal consciousness or biological embodiment. I want an honest map of how exposed this position actually is. What observations, formal results from mechanistic interpretability, or conceptual arguments would decisively undermine it rather than merely complicate it? Which of its core commitments—such as the distinction between correlation and representational tracking, or the claim that counterfactual invariance is sufficient for belief—are genuinely vulnerable, and which are insulated by definitional qualification? What does this view predict that both pure instrumentalism and original-intentionality exclusivism do not, and does it risk any unacceptable consequence it cannot absorb? Please distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would only be awkward, and say what defenders of this graded position should themselves concede as counting against it. I would personally find it most persuasive if you could identify whether the coupling condition is either satisfied trivially by any large model or is impossible to verify non-circularly in out-of-distribution failure modes.
