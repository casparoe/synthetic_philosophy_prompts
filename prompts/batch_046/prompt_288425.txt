I'm preparing a reading for a philosophy of history course, and in our discussions students repeatedly invoke the image of history as an arc—specifically, the familiar claim that the arc of history bends toward justice—to argue that moral progress is structurally directed rather than contingent. They treat this as a kind of secularized providence: injustice may endure for long periods, but the curvature of the process itself guarantees eventual correction, just as a physical arc is determined by initial conditions and an underlying force. I want to audit how far this analogy actually carries weight.

First, provide an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the arc's geometry, the bending force, the point of origin, and the target endpoint, and what in historical reality corresponds to each? Second, examine where the mapping breaks—does an arc have inertia that can be overcome? Can external intervention deflect it, or is the curvature inevitable? What supplies the "force" of justice, and does treating justice as a physical endpoint smuggle in a contested teleology? Third, consider whether the analogy survives if we replace the geometric image with a dynamical-systems model—say, justice functioning as an attractor within a state-space of institutional configurations—without importing hidden purpose. Does this refined version still license patience with present injustice, or was the original arc doing all the normative work? Finally, assess whether the conclusion that history tends toward justice survives once the disanalogies are explicit, and suggest whether a different analogy—perhaps history as an open-ended negotiation without predetermined curvature—does the philosophical job more honestly.
