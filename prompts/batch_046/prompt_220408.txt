I've been thinking about the long-standing standoff over David Lewis's modal realism—the thesis that possible worlds are concrete entities, spatiotemporally isolated from our own world and populated by real counterparts of actual things. For decades, philosophers have traded objections about ontological profligacy, the problem of modal knowledge, and the alleged arbitrariness of world-individuation, while defenders reply with arguments from theoretical utility, the analysis of counterfactual dependence, and analogies to belief in unobservable entities in science. Yet the dispute feels curiously static: very few participants seem to change sides, and the literature largely rehearses the same dialectical moves without convergence. My working theory is that the disagreement is not, at bottom, about whether there literally exist infinitely many concrete universes, but rather about what a metaphysical theory is supposed to deliver. One camp treats ontological parsimony as a hard constraint and treats Lewis's ontology as a kind of reductio; the other treats explanatory coverage and semantic unification as primary and regards the ontology as an acceptable price for those goods. Is that diagnosis right? Or is there a hidden empirical or formal question—perhaps concerning the logic of modality, the structure of counterpart relations, or the cognitive accessibility of non-actual worlds—actually doing the work beneath the surface? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, not a survey of familiar objections: do the sides differ about substantive facts, about standards of evidence appropriate to metaphysics, about which cases should count as data, or about starting intuitions that neither side can independently argue for? Are they answering different questions under the single label "modal realism"? And given whatever diagnosis you offer, what would count as genuine progress? Could a refined formal distinction—say, between ontological and ideological commitment—or a shift in framing break the impasse, or should we conclude that no new experiment, theorem, or conceptual distinction could resolve it because the disagreement is essentially about incommensurable theoretical virtues?
