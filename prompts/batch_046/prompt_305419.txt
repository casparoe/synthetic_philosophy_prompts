After a long, unresolved argument with a friend over dinner about whether the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution name real discontinuities in human affairs or are largely retrospective labels invented for pedagogical and administrative convenience, I have agreed to lead a single ninety-minute session for a reading group of six educated non-specialists—lawyers, engineers, and a novelist—who have consumed popular histories but never studied historiography. I do not want to deliver a lecture on which school of historians is correct; I want the group to feel the conceptual problem in its bones, specifically to understand what kind of claim is at stake when someone asserts that a period is "real" rather than "constructed," and why the answer reshapes how we read continuity, causation, and archival survival. Please design the explanatory arc rather than the transcript itself.

Set out the exact dependency order in which the conceptual components must be introduced, with each step’s prerequisite made explicit: what must be firmly in place before the audience can even recognize that there is a problem here, rather than merely hearing that experts disagree. Identify the one carrying example or case study that should anchor the entire session; specify what structural features it must possess and what it must lack—whether it should involve a named figure, a technological shift, an institutional reform, or a textual tradition, and whether it should sit astride a clean chronological boundary or deliberately blur one—in order to make the conceptual tension visceral rather than abstract. Map the misconceptions this particular audience will predictably import, such as the assumption that "Renaissance" describes a sudden psychological awakening, or that labeling an era "medieval" is a neutral chronological act rather than a normative judgment; indicate the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be headed off, and how to absorb it without derailing the arc. Include a concrete check for understanding that would demonstrate they have grasped the conceptual structure—distinguishing "carving nature at its joints" from "drawing borders for administrative coherence"—rather than merely adopting a fashionable skepticism. Finally, state explicitly what you will leave out at this level—whether specific historiographical movements, technical debates in the philosophy of history, or the institutional origins of the tripartite scheme—and say so openly, so that I know where the plan deliberately stops.

Make sure the plan treats the question as genuinely open: the goal is for them to leave uncertain whether these periods are "real," but crystal clear on what conditions would have to obtain for them to be real, on how survivorship in the archive can manufacture the appearance of coherence, and on why the distinction matters for interpreting any long span of human activity.
