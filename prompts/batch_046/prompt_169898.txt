I've come to accept a strong version of the extended mind thesis—specifically, that there is no principled metaphysical boundary between internal neural processes and stable external artifacts in the constitution of belief, and that this extends symmetrically to normative responsibility: if a belief is genuinely constituted by an external device with which I am regularly coupled, then I am epistemically answerable for it in exactly the same direct way I am for an occurrent internal belief, with no mediating "adoption" or "endorsement" step. This applies even to dormant or rarely accessed entries, provided they remain available on demand, and it denies any special "biological privilege" that makes neural storage more genuinely mine. Tell me what would count against this position—not merely the familiar coupling-constitution objections or the Otto intuition pump, but which specific cases, formal considerations, or results from other domains (say, about personal identity over time, the neuroscience of memory consolidation, predictive processing, or legal doctrines of possession and control) it genuinely stands or falls with. Which of these defeaters would a committed defender concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as missing the point? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward. In particular, consider whether empirical evidence that neural beliefs carry a distinctive phenomenology of ownership that artifact-mediated beliefs systematically lack would be decisive, or whether the defender can absorb it by treating ownership as contingent rather than constitutive. Also consider whether the position collapses if responsibility requires a kind of direct volitional access that external artifacts, by their nature, cannot supply—even under conditions of transparent coupling. I want to know where this view is genuinely exposed and where it has been insulated by qualification.
