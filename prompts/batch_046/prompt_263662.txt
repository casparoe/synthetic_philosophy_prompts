You are grading a philosophy-of-mind exam. The prompt given to students was:

Norman Malcolm argues that dreaming is not experiencing: since the sleeper is unconscious, there can be no concurrent experiential state, and a dream report is an interpretation formed after waking rather than an introspective account of what occurred during sleep. Daniel Dennett rejects the Cartesian-theater picture and holds that dreams are not real-time mental occurrences unfolding during sleep; instead, they are retrospectively constituted narratives, assembled upon awakening from fragmentary cues without any prior unified experiential content. Now consider a modified case: a sleeper in sustained deep NREM sleep—no REM signatures, minimal cortical arousal, and no measurable episodic-memory consolidation—awakens and reports a coherent, temporally extended first-person narrative that she insists felt like a continuous experience lasting several minutes. Call this appearance phenomenal echoing: the seeming presence of unified experiential content in the absence of any corresponding real-time mental occurrence. Apply Malcolm's and Dennett's frameworks to this case. Can either accommodate phenomenal echoing? If not, does the phenomenon count against the framework, or can it be explained away?

Here is the student's answer (approximately 550 words):

Malcolm's central claim is that dream reports are not reports of experiences that happened while asleep, because experience requires consciousness and sleep is its absence. Applied to the NREM case, Malcolm should deny that there was any experience at all; the report is just a post-waking interpretation, perhaps shaped by residual neural noise but lacking any experiential origin. Dennett, by contrast, believes that dreams are real experiences that occur during sleep but are stored in the brain like a cassette tape and then played back upon awakening. Because of this, Dennett can allow that a recording was made even in deep NREM, just with lower fidelity due to reduced brain activity. So both philosophers can accommodate phenomenal echoing: Malcolm treats it as a pure illusion with no original content, while Dennett treats it as the playback of genuinely recorded content. This means phenomenal echoing actually supports Dennett over Malcolm, because if there is an echo there must have been an original sound—that is, an original experience during sleep. Dennett's cassette model explains the echo perfectly, since recording can happen silently at a low level without REM. Therefore, the case undermines Malcolm's view: the presence of a structured echo shows there was something there to echo.
