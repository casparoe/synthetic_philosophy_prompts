I hold a sharpened version of the Stoic dichotomy of control, revived in modern therapeutic practice, that partitions agency into a strict binary: our present judgments and volitions are fully up to us, while every external outcome—including bodily health, reputation, and the actions of others—is not. I take this not merely as framing but as a metaphysically substantive claim, reinforced by predictive-processing accounts that treat the brain's direct outputs as confined to motor commands. What would genuinely count against this position? I want defeaters drawn from empirical studies of agency and shared control, phenomenological reports that blur the boundary, philosophical objections about distributed responsibility, and Stoic texts that complicate the binary—not generic skepticism, but the specific evidence or arguments that should move a committed defender. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether retreating to a purely therapeutic interpretation saves the view or abandons what made it distinctive.
