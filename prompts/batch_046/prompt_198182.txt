I hold the view that applying inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) to human values is conceptually misdirected, not merely technically intractable. The standard framework treats observed behavior as noisy samples drawn from a stationary reward function defined over world-histories; I think this is ontologically wrong for human agents. Much of our action is better explained by diachronic narrative identity maintenance, framing-sensitive preference construction, and social coordination norms that do not reduce to individual-level preferences over outcomes. On this account, there is often no hidden reward function to recover—behavior expresses commitments to self-constituting stories and context-dependent norms rather than stable utility maximization. What would count against this position? Please sort the defeaters by how decisive they would be, from fatal to merely awkward. In particular: first, are there formal decomposition results showing that apparently narrative-driven behavior can still be modeled as a mixture of stationary reward functions plus structured noise, rendering the narrative explanation redundant? Second, which empirical patterns—say, stable revealed-preference rankings across framing manipulations—would defenders of standard IRL concede as genuinely damaging to my view, and which would they dismiss as measuring the wrong construct? Third, has my position been insulated into unfalsifiability: if every observed consistency in choice can be redescribed as surface-level narrative coherence, is that a methodological defect rather than a feature? Fourth, do philosophical frameworks in the rationalist-adjacent literature on preference coherence or extrapolated volition offer a bridge between narrative identity and utility-theoretic representation without collapsing the former into the latter? I want an honest assessment of where this view is actually exposed—not a dismissal of IRL on practical grounds, but a map of what evidence or argument should genuinely move me.
