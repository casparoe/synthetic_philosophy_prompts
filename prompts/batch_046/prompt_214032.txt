I serve on the investment oversight committee for a mid-sized municipal pension fund, and over the past year we have begun receiving allocation proposals for “convex tail-risk” instruments—strategies that bleed a fixed quarterly premium in exchange for payouts that are theoretically unbounded if an unmodelable systemic crisis occurs, such as a simultaneous sovereign-default and currency-collapse cascade. Because the triggering events lie outside any assignable probability distribution, standard expected-value analysis is useless; yet the board is divided between treating these proposals as reckless speculation and treating them as necessary insurance. A colleague recently suggested we adapt the logic of Pascal’s wager—decision under radical uncertainty with potentially infinite stakes—to build a reusable evaluation framework rather than relying on intuition. I need you to turn that logic into a concrete, step-by-step decision procedure my committee can actually run on any proposal of this kind.

The procedure should specify the ordered questions to ask, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage; the exact point at which it stops with a firm rejection; the conditions under which it hands the decision back to the board as an irreducible value judgment rather than a technical conclusion; and the safeguards that prevent it from being triggered by every low-probability fear. Then apply it to three proposal types: (A) a long-volatility overlay costing 2.5% per quarter with payout tied to an undefined “systemic break” event; (B) a bespoke sovereign-default derivative with near-zero running cost but ambiguous legal enforceability in bankruptcy; and (C) a conventional TIPS allocation being marketed as a “prudent” substitute even though it does not address the unmodelable tail. Include one case where the procedure yields an approval recommendation that runs against ordinary fiduciary caution, and explicitly state what the procedure cannot do—particularly regarding moral hazard, intergenerational equity, and the risk of model error disguised as unknown probability.

Please take a definite position: either defend the adapted Pascalian procedure as genuinely usable within fiduciary duty, or demonstrate precisely where it collapses when applied to pension governance—do not merely balance the two sides. Keep the entire response under 750 words.
