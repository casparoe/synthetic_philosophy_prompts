I have been tracking the implicit normative framework my colleague Dr. Amara Okonkwo applies when she judges cases of archival absence in our philosophy-of-history workshop. Her rulings to date are these. First, she treats a nineteenth-century plantation ledger that lists enslaved persons only by first name and assigned labor category—omitting all kinship relations—as an actively violent silence that must be read against the grain and reconstructed where possible. Second, she regards a municipal birth registry destroyed in a 1923 fire as a purely contingent absence, carrying no ethical weight beyond ordinary historiographical caution. Third, she judges a pharmaceutical corporation’s retention schedule—under which negative clinical-trial data are shredded and only positive outcomes archived—to be an engineered silence serving economic power, requiring corrective disclosure. Fourth, she defends an Indigenous community’s restriction of sacred burial records to elder-governed access as a legitimate exercise of sovereign refusal, not an erasure. Fifth, she recently condemned a state immigration digitization project that applies facial-recognition indexing only to non-European arrival records, leaving European entries searchable only by text, despite a published technical report noting the disparity; she called this a bureaucratic silence that converts administrative convenience into racialized visibility asymmetry and demanded immediate protocol revision rather than mere documentation.

Next week we discuss a new proposal. A university-based participatory archive of labor protests collects submissions via a mobile application that requires geolocation verification and accepts only video files under thirty seconds. The result is a rich corpus of brief, geotagged clips from downtown demonstrations, but virtually no material from rural picket lines or from workers who disable location tracking for privacy or safety reasons. The curators publish a detailed “silence map” marking the geographic and demographic gaps, invite further submissions, but leave the technical requirements unchanged.

Please predict Dr. Okonkwo’s verdict on this case. Identify the principle she is implicitly running—whether it concerns asymmetries of institutional power, the reversibility of absence without cost to vulnerable subjects, the transparency of representational gaps, or something else—and explain which other principles fit her prior five rulings equally well. For each competing principle, state what it predicts about the participatory archive, and indicate which single further case would best adjudicate among them. Finally, give your own best-guess prediction of her verdict, with your reasoning, and note whether her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome.
