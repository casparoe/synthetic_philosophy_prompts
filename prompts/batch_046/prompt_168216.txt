Two people in my study group deadlocked over Davidson's radical interpretation. One sketched a case: we are interpreting an agent who, in one physical domain but nowhere else, systematically asserts that a single object occupies two distinct locations using the same syntactic devices they elsewhere use for ordinary negation and conjunction. She argued that the principle of charity requires us to avoid attributing contradiction at all costs, so we should reinterpret the logical constants locally in that domain—treating the apparent contradiction as a coherent, non-classical relation—because flat contradiction renders the speaker massively irrational and therefore uninterpretable. He replied that Davidsonian charity governs empirical belief and desire, not logical form; since the agent uses negation consistently elsewhere, we ought to preserve classical logic globally and instead attribute false physical beliefs. She then pressed that this smuggles in an unjustified prior commitment to logic: if belief and meaning are genuinely interdependent, then preserving overall interpretability might demand revising logical form rather than dumping error onto empirical judgment. He countered that allowing piecemeal logical revision undermines the shared structural framework required to bootstrap belief attribution in the first place, so her view collapses into incoherence. Neither yielded. Who is right about which specific point—does charity license local logical revision to avoid contradiction, or does it fix logic and relocate error? And what, if anything, are both missing about how Davidson treats the relation between logical constants and the holistic network of belief?
