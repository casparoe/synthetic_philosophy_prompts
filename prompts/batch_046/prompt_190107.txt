I just finished a long and rather exhausting argument with a friend about luck egalitarianism, and I need to sort out my thoughts before committing anything to writing. We kept circling back to what I want to call the recursive contamination problem: if every exercise of responsible choice is itself shaped by prior distributions of brute luck—education, socialization, cognitive endowments—then labeling later outcomes as instances of option luck seems to presuppose a baseline of autonomous agency that the view itself denies. My friend dismissed this as merely a dynamic variant of the familiar conversion objection, insisting that the canonical reply treats choices as genuinely free once made, regardless of their causal history. I remain unconvinced, because I think that reply works for isolated decisions but collapses when applied iteratively across a life course, where each choice becomes the brute-luck substrate for the next. Given all this, which open questions or lines of work in luck egalitarianism actually deserve priority if one's goal is to make the view viable for real-world social policy? Should resolving this recursive contamination problem take precedence over other live debates—such as the currency problem or the shift toward relational egalitarianism—or is it a dead end?
