I have been working through an extension of the standard Dutch-book coherence arguments for expected utility maximization into normative ethics, and I have built a line of reasoning that now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to prove that any robust deontological view is formally incoherent in exactly the same way that violating the independence axiom is incoherent. Here is the argument, with each step labeled.

(1) If an agent's preferences over lotteries violate the independence axiom, then a bookie can construct a finite sequence of pairwise trades—a money pump—that leaves the agent with the same final option they began with but with strictly less value extracted at each intermediate step.

(2) A deontological constraint—say, an absolute refusal to torture one innocent person even to prevent a million analogous tortures—functions structurally like a lexicographic preference ordering over moral outcomes: it assigns discontinuous or unbounded disvalue to a particular act type regardless of the scale of consequential benefits.

(3) We can construct a sequential "moral pump" for such a constraint. Present the agent with a long chain of binary choices, each involving a single, isolated instance of the forbidden act paired with the prevention of one future instance elsewhere. Locally, in each pair, the agent refuses the trade to uphold the constraint. But globally, the accumulated sequence produces exactly the million tortures the agent sought to prevent, plus the one they directly caused.

(4) If susceptibility to a money pump demonstrates irrational incoherence in one's preference structure, then susceptibility to a moral pump demonstrates incoherence in one's normative value structure: the agent's local commitments and global outcomes are systematically misaligned in a way that can be exploited by the framing of sequential choices.

(5) Therefore, holding any robust deontological constraint makes one vulnerable to moral pumping, which means such constraints are incoherent in the same formal sense that intransitive or non-independent preferences are incoherent.

(6) But it is clearly not incoherent to maintain an absolute prohibition on torture even at enormous consequential cost; people hold this view stably, revise it only through genuine moral learning rather than through extraction, and do not treat it as a local mistake to be corrected.

So (5) must be false, and the analogy between preference coherence and moral coherence must break down somewhere. Where exactly does it go wrong? Is the flaw in treating deontological constraints as lexicographic preferences (step 2), in assuming that local refusal plus global accumulation is the right framing (step 3), in the claim that coherence in one domain transfers to the other (step 4), or elsewhere?

If I have to choose, I would rather preserve the intuition that coherence arguments have genuine normative force (so I am reluctant to abandon step 4 entirely) and I am also reluctant to abandon step 6, since giving it up feels like collapsing into pure act-utilitarianism.
