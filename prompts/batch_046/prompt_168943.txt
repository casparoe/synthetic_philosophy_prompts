I convene a philosophy seminar of twelve graduate students for a single seventy-five-minute session. Design a structured, runnable role-play or deliberation that makes participants feel—before the debrief names it—the problem of layered consent in institutional settings: a subject gives voluntary, informed agreement to a broad procedural framework, which then authorizes specific actions without renewed explicit consent, and the question is whether and how deception, coercion, or competence failure at either layer invalidate the whole. Specify the setting (graduate seminar, twelve participants, seventy-five minutes), the assigned roles (at least three distinct parties with asymmetric information and differential authority over the framework versus its execution), a payoff or reputation scheme that requires no real money, and rules that create hidden information asymmetries so that participants discover for themselves that removing one failure mode—for instance, full disclosure of the framework—does not repair failures in competence or structural coercion at the execution layer. The idea should show up in what happens: arrange the rules so that a technically voluntary agreement to the framework leaves participants feeling non-consenting about particular outcomes, and so that they experience the temporal gap between framework agreement and outcome realization in compressed form. Include a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the distinctions between propositional and procedural consent, the competence thresholds required for each, and the difference between direct coercion and structural coercion embedded in the framework itself. State explicitly what will predictably go wrong the first time you run this—likely a collapse of the distinction between consenting to rules and consenting to outcomes, or a premature demand for unanimous renegotiation—and how to handle it without breaking the simulation. Finally, be frank about which feature of layered consent the activity necessarily cannot reproduce (for example, the irreversibility of certain executed actions, or the years-long delay between initial agreement and realized harm) and explain exactly how the facilitator must supply that feature verbally. The prompt should not ask participants to cite texts, provide bibliographies, or reference obscure theories they would not have encountered in standard coursework; it should work for a model that has no web access.
