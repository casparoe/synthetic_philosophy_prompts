I will present a seminar defense of a functional-cognitive thesis about art to an interdisciplinary reading group. The thesis holds that something counts as art not through institutional framing, aesthetic properties, or art-historical lineage, but through functioning as a reflexive perceptual-cognitive scaffold: it is designed to stabilize intersubjective attention across observers with heterogeneous backgrounds, and it contains an internal mechanism that allows the shared attentional frame itself to be revised from within the experience rather than imposed from outside. I use this to argue that certain AI-generated images and certain non-Western ritual masks belong in the category, while some canonical conceptual works—specifically readymades and performances that deliberately refuse perceptual engagement—fall outside it, which I treat as a feature. The audience is precisely composed: two analytic philosophers of art who defend institutional or anti-definition pluralist positions, one philosopher skeptical of all universal definitions of art, two cognitive scientists who study joint attention and distributed cognition but have no background in aesthetics, and one art historian specializing in ritual objects who regards universal cognitive frameworks as a form of Western imperialism. They are united in caring about whether conceptual art survives, whether cultural specificity is erased, and whether the cognitive-science vocabulary is being smuggled in unexamined.
