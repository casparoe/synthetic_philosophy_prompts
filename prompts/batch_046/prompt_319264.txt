ok so in my seminar on collective memory we got stuck arguing about whether there's actually a moral duty to remember trauma, or if that's just a modern obsession we should drop. my friend jamie was defending margalit's idea that in thick relations—family, close community—there's an obligation to preserve shared painful memories, not just for the dead but for the coherence of the group. she said if you let an archive rot or stop teaching your kids about a specific atrocity, you're breaking an ethical bond.

raj jumped in and said that's exactly the kind of thinking rieff critiques in his work on forgetting. he argued institutional remembrance often freezes conflict and prevents reconciliation, and sometimes the moral act is letting memory fade—like letting monuments decay naturally. he said margalit confuses loyalty with masochism.

jamie came back: but rieff's talking about state politics, not intimate obligations. if your grandmother survived something and you decide it's "healthier" not to know, that's not reconciliation, that's erasure.

raj said fine, but what if remembering is mediated now? he brought up this idea of algorithimic curation in archives—ai that selectively highlights or suppresses emotional details in digitized testimony. he said if we're preserving everything, the form changes what's remembered anyway, so the "pure" duty is already contaminated. he thinks this undermines margalit's ideal.

jamie said that just relocates the duty: we need to preserve the material conditions, not just content. she argued letting an archive physically decay while digitizing it with lossy compression is a double betrayal—you lose the object and the affective texture.

raj replied that affective texture is exactly what should fade. he asked: if a digital archive could preserve facts but gradually reduce emotional valence—like mnemonic chemotherapy—wouldn't that satisfy both sides? jamie said no, that's not remembering, it's processing grief into data.

they kept going but i had to leave for my own defense prep. who's right about which point? specifically: is margalit's thick-relation duty compatible with mediated preservation? does raj's algorithmic example undermine the duty or just move it? and are they both missing something about who controls the decay—maybe the power to forget is as important as the duty to remember? i want a point-by-point verdict, not "it depends." and please don't tell me to read margalit or rieff, i literally have thier books on my desk.
