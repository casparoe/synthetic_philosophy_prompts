Design a 90-minute essay examination question for an upper-level graduate seminar in moral philosophy, centered on the interaction between standing to blame and hypocrisy. The question should present a constructed case of distributed institutional hypocrisy: a national scientific ethics board publicly condemns data fabrication in published research, yet the board's own internal review processes systematically suppress negative replication studies in order to protect institutional funding relationships; individual board members, however, have never personally fabricated data and are unaware of the suppression mechanisms managed by administrative staff. Ask examinees to determine whether individual board members retain standing to blame a researcher caught fabricating data; whether the board's institutional hypocrisy defeats, weakens, or leaves untouched that standing; and how the analysis changes if the blame is directed at the institution rather than the individual researcher. Require students to distinguish between standing-defeating hypocrisy and weight-defeating hypocrisy, to consider whether institutional complicity can be distributed across roles without personal fault, and to address one plausible objection—such as the claim that institutional entities cannot be hypocrites in the same sense as persons, or that standing requires direct personal involvement in the wrong. Provide a model answer of approximately 800 words that takes a clear position, applies the distinction rigorously to both individual and collective levels, and handles the objection with a specific counter-argument. Include a marking scheme allocating 20 marks total across five specific criteria—conceptual clarity on standing (5 marks), correct application of the hypocrisy distinction (5 marks), analysis of distributed institutional responsibility (4 marks), handling of the objection (4 marks), and argumentative structure and precision (2 marks)—with a one-line note for each criterion describing the error that typically costs marks.
