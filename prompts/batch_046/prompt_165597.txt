You are a professional philosopher specializing in epistemology and the philosophy of mind. Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm the key distinctions between propositional and practical knowledge and to map out where institutional practices might implicitly commit to one view or the other.

Consider the licensing structure common to several skilled trades and technical professions: candidates must pass both a written examination on codes, regulations, and theory, and a live practical demonstration performed under direct observation. The institutional rules treat failure asymmetrically. A failed written exam may be retaken without limit; a failed practical demonstration is permitted only one retake, after which the candidate is permanently disqualified in that jurisdiction. Meanwhile, in purely conceptual disciplines—philosophy, theoretical mathematics, or certain legal certifications—professional competence is assessed almost entirely through written and oral examinations, with no structured practical demonstration of reasoning-in-action, yet these fields claim to certify full professional competence.

What philosophical commitments about knowing-how versus knowing-that does this asymmetrical treatment presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology: does the single-retake rule for practical failure treat knowing-how as innate, non-correctable, or more fundamental than knowing-that? Does the absence of practical assessments in conceptual fields presuppose that knowing-how is reducible to knowing-that there, or that it does not exist in those domains? Assess whether the practice is internally coherent or whether its different parts quietly presuppose incompatible views—perhaps that knowing-how is simultaneously more fragile and more essential than propositional knowledge. Finally, describe what a licensing regime would have to look like if it were built on the explicit intellectualist view that knowing-how is merely a species of knowing-that, and contrast it with a regime built on an anti-intellectualist view in which practical mastery is irreducible and must be tested differently.
