I'm preparing a feature on Berkeley's master argument and want to stress-test a reductio that seems to push it past its anti-materialist target into solipsism. The argument runs: (1) The master argument holds that conceiving an object existing unperceived is contradictory, because any such conception is itself an idea in a mind. (2) Apply this to other finite spirits: I try to conceive of your mind existing without being perceived by me. (3) Any conception of your mind is an idea in my mind, hence in conceiving it I perceive it. (4) So I cannot conceive your mind as existing unperceived by me. (5) By parity of reasoning, if X is inconceivable as unperceived then X is impossible as unperceived; therefore your mind exists only when I perceive it. (6) Generalized, only my own present perceptions and God exist—solipsism for every finite observer. (7) Yet Berkeley denies solipsism and distinguishes passive sensible 'ideas' from active spirits grasped by 'notion.' Which step should be abandoned? Is the error a modal slide between (4) and (5), or does the argument illicitly treat an active perceiver as a passive idea? Identify the weakest premise, explain why it initially seemed sound, and state what giving it up costs Berkeley's case against matter. End with Verdict: [step number] [cost in four words or fewer].
