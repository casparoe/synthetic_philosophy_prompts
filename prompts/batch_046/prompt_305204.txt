I'm currently in a graduate seminar on rational choice theory and we have spent the last two weeks on standard money-pump arguments against intransitive preferences—specifically the Dutch-book-style extraction that works by cycling an agent through pairwise trades until she returns to her original option with a net loss. In a parallel unit on diachronic rationality we discussed how future selves should be treated: whether one should be a sophisticated planner who anticipates change, a resolute chooser who binds herself to an initial plan, or a revisionist who grants full sovereignty to each temporal slice.

For my term paper I am trying to work out a hybrid case that none of the assigned readings address directly. Imagine an agent who knows in advance that her transitive preference ordering over outcomes X, Y, and Z will shift rationally—driven by genuinely different information—across three decision points: X ≻ Y ≻ Z at t₁, Y ≻ Z ≻ X at t₂, and Z ≻ X ≻ Y at t₃. A bookie offers her a sequence of paid exchanges at each transition, so that if she accepts every locally rational trade she eventually returns to X having paid out at each step. The agent anticipates these shifts not as mistakes but as appropriate updates.

Please rank the following four stances from 1 (least defensible) to 4 (most defensible) with respect to how well each preserves both diachronic autonomy and resistance to exploitation in this exact scenario. Give each stance one sentence explaining its placement, and be explicit about whether it can justify refusing the very first trade (X to Y at t₁) without treating future preference change as either an error to be corrected or a stranger to be outmaneuvered.

A. Strict intertemporal consistency: the agent must maintain one transitive ordering across all slices; any anticipated shift is a rational failure that should be blocked by precommitment at t₁.

B. Sophisticated global planning: the agent integrates all three anticipated orderings into a single overarching plan at t₁ and refuses any local trade that conflicts with that global trajectory, even when her local ordering at t₂ or t₃ would permit it.

C. Slice-sovereignty revisionism: each temporal self is a fully autonomous rational agent; there is no cross-temporal exploitation because the payments are voluntary exchanges among distinct decision-makers, and the t₁-self has no authority over the t₂-self.

D. Conditional binding: the agent permits local preference shifts only when they are triggered by specified evidential conditions, but she adopts a meta-rule that categorically blocks any trade sequence that returns her to the original option with net loss, regardless of how rational each intermediate ordering is.

In your reasoning, I would particularly value your view on whether stance D collapses into A once the meta-rule is in place, or whether it genuinely occupies a middle ground that respects rational change while still explaining why the first exchange should be refused.
