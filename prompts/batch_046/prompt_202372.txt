Read William Golding’s *Lord of the Flies* through David Lewis’s analysis of convention: an arbitrary, self-enforcing equilibrium sustained by common knowledge of mutual preferences and expectations. Focus first on the conch as a signaling convention that solves the boys’ immediate coordination problem—who speaks and when—without being intrinsically authoritative. Does Golding present its collapse as a straightforward fracture in common knowledge, with Ralph and Piggy still expecting compliance that Jack’s faction no longer shares, or does the novel show something Lewis’s static model struggles to capture—perhaps a shift in salience, a competing emotional equilibrium, or a normative commitment that conventions can generate but not themselves ground? Next, examine Jack’s tribe: the hunt-chant, the painted faces, and the shared belief in the beast seem to form a new convention that coordinates group cohesion against fear rather than against chaos. Ask whether this emergence supports Lewis’s claim that norms evolve toward arbitrary but stable solutions, or whether Golding implies that norms evolve toward emotional salience and moralized identity in ways that make efficiency or rational coordination secondary. Be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the novel to an illustration of coordination failure, and do not distort Lewis’s framework by attributing to it a theory of moral authority it explicitly denies. Conclude by saying what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone cannot: does Golding show that conventions depend on a prior moral agreement Lewis treats as unnecessary, or does he vindicate Lewis by demonstrating that once common knowledge breaks, any arbitrary replacement—however destructive—can take hold?
