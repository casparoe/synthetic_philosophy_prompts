As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I have been trying to understand a conceptual deadlock that mirrors problems I encounter in distributed-state design, and I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a survey of positions. Consider the classic symmetric-universe thought experiment: two spheres of identical material, size, temperature, and internal structure, floating in an otherwise empty space with perfect rotational and translational symmetry, separated only by a distance that itself defines no directional feature. One camp holds that if every detectable property—including every relational property—is shared, then the scenario either collapses into a single sphere or is incoherent; the principle that indiscernibles are identical must stand, and apparent counterexamples always conceal a hidden difference. The opposing camp treats the scenario as a fully consistent metaphysical possibility and takes it to show that identity is not reducible to property equivalence: there can be two entities with exactly the same qualitative profile.

For decades, both sides appear to agree on the structure of the case, share the same logical apparatus, and acknowledge the same spatial symmetries, yet neither has moved the other. My working hypothesis, drawn from engineering rather than metaphysics, is that the stall conceals a disagreement about the reference frame of evaluation. In a distributed system, two nodes can carry bit-for-bit identical state vectors and transaction logs and still be numerically distinct entities because each carries a unique identifier assigned by the consensus protocol; yet from an internal observer who sees only state, the nodes are genuinely indiscernible. I suspect the philosophical debate may split analogously: one side treats identity as primitive and observer-independent (like a node ID), the other treats it as reducible to complete state equivalence (like pure state comparison). But I am unsure whether this analogy genuinely captures the philosophical positions, or whether I am importing a false framework.

Is this disagreement substantive—about whether relations are intrinsic or whether space itself supplies individuating structure—verbal—about whether the word "property" includes positional or structural features—or are the sides answering different questions under one heading, one about logical consistency and one about physical possibility? Is there a hidden formal question, perhaps involving symmetry groups or the ontology of spatial points, that is actually driving the impasse? Given your diagnosis, what would count as real progress: a sharper conceptual distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic properties, a formal result about symmetric spaces, a reframing of the question away from spheres entirely, or should we conclude that the dispute rests on bedrock intuitions about individuation that no argument can shift? I want the diagnosis to engage concretely with what each side actually says about the spheres and their relations, not a catalog of interpretations, together with an honest answer about whether anything—a distinction, a theorem, or a change of framing—could actually resolve it.
