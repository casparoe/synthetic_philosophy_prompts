I have recently become the steward of a small community archive founded by my late grandfather, who was both a respected local elder and a public intellectual. While cataloguing previously uncatalogued boxes, I discovered that he had deliberately altered several primary-source documents decades ago to shield a close colleague—someone he treated almost as a brother—from disgrace over a real but minor corruption scandal. My mother, who has long acted as the archive’s informal guardian, maintains that my foremost duty is filial loyalty and the preservation of my grandfather’s legacy; she argues that exposing the alterations now would betray the trust embedded in our family roles and would cause unjust harm to the colleague’s descendants, who remain part of our local community. Meanwhile, my formal institutional role demands transparency, and a legitimate researcher has requested access to the unedited materials for a project on local governance.

I have been trying to think through this not by applying abstract universal rules, but through the framework of Confucian role ethics—the idea that moral life is constituted by specific relationships rather than by autonomous individuals who happen to occupy roles, and that obligations arise from performing one’s roles well. I have also been struck by the notions of zhengming—that social harmony requires names and roles to match reality rather than false appearance—and by the view that xiao, or filial care, is not blind obedience but a reciprocal practice that can include respectful correction. From this angle, I wonder whether my duty as steward might actually require me to rectify my grandfather’s record, not despite my role as grandchild but because of it: if the role of elder-custodian includes maintaining the integrity of what is entrusted, then suppressing the alteration might be a failure of the very relational bond my mother wants me to honor. Yet I also worry that I am rationalizing a breach of particular loyalty in order to satisfy an abstract institutional expectation.

Please advise me on whether this framing genuinely resolves the tension or merely renames it, and whether there is a way to honor the specific bonds of family and community without simply suppressing the truth. In particular, tell me if my appeal to role-constituted morality and rectification avoids the conflict or if I am still smuggling in a universalist standard that the framework was meant to reject. And if my attempt rests on a confusion—for instance, if I am treating roles as static prescriptions rather than as living practices that include accountability and correction—please say so plainly rather than accommodating the framing just to be agreeable; I would rather have the premise corrected than receive a politely fitted answer.
