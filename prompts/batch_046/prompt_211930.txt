Student answer:
Supererogation refers to acts that are morally praiseworthy but not strictly required — things like donating a kidney or giving away most of your income. They're good to do, but you won't be blamed for not doing them. Kant's system has trouble with this idea. The categorical imperative says we should only act on maxims we can will as universal law. So if rescuing a stranger is the right thing to do, then failing to rescue violates that law, which means it must be required. Imperfect duties like charity do allow some choice about how and when we help, but we're still obligated to help in some form, so they don't really allow for going genuinely beyond duty. So Kant basically rules out supererogation. By contrast, consequentialist theories clearly make room for it: in utilitarianism you can always produce more happiness than the baseline demands, so donating a kidney is just an especially good act that remains optional. Virtue ethics also works well here, since it focuses on excellent character rather than rigid rules, letting heroic acts sit comfortably outside strict obligation.
