For my high school philosophy club, I'm preparing a taxonomy handout on what makes an action genuinely morally worthy, specifically whether acting for the right reasons is necessary and what that requires. Please construct a decision tree with four branching questions. First: if someone performs a morally correct action solely for a non-moral reason—say, returning a lost wallet only because they want a reward—does that action possess zero moral worth, partial worth, or full worth? Second: does "acting for the right reason" require that the agent can consciously articulate the moral principle behind the action, or can an unreflective, instinctive kindness count? Third: when motives are mixed—doing the right thing partly out of genuine care and partly out of self-interest—does the self-interest dilute the worth proportionally, eliminate it entirely, or leave the moral worth completely intact? Fourth: should we apply a "counterfactual isolation" test—asking whether the agent would still do the right thing if they were certain no one (including themselves) would ever know—treating failure as proof that the worth was never genuinely present?

Structure the tree so that every combination of answers leads to a named leaf (for example, a strict Kantian view, an Aristotelian virtue view, a pure consequentialist outcome view, or "unnamed" if no prominent philosopher holds that exact combination). For each leaf, provide one sentence describing its most awkward commitment. Then identify any two leaves that the tree cannot cleanly distinguish from one another, and briefly diagnose any logically possible but apparently empty combinations—are they incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Write the response in American English, labeling each branch node clearly and avoiding any request for citations or reading lists.
