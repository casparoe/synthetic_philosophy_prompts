Analyze the 1829 duel fought at Battersea Fields between Arthur Wellesley, Duke of Wellington, and the Earl of Winchilsea over Catholic Emancipation—ending without injury, as Wellington’s shot went wide and Winchilsea fired into the air—as a case study in the theory of moral revolution advanced by Kwame Anthony Appiah and supplemented by the interest-driven thesis associated with Bernard Williams. Appiah holds that practices such as dueling collapse not when moral arguments defeat them but when honor codes are restructured so that participation becomes dishonorable rather than obligatory; the Williams thesis adds that such restructuring tracks shifts in power and economic interest rather than rational persuasion alone. Apply this framework precisely: map the honor economy of the British political aristocracy in 1829, identify the status costs and benefits of issuing, accepting, or refusing a challenge within that peer group, and determine whether Wellington’s decision to fight yet deliberately avoid killing his opponent represents an honor-preserving compromise or an honor-destroying contradiction. Then examine what the episode reveals about the theory’s explanatory scope. The duel’s aftermath coincided with the near-disappearance of parliamentary dueling, yet military officers continued the practice for decades. Does this divergence support Appiah’s uniform account of honor dynamics, or does it show that the theory must distinguish institutional contexts—civil governance versus military hierarchy—in ways it currently does not? Furthermore, assess the Williams thesis: was the decline of political dueling driven by the rising economic interests of a bureaucratic and commercial middle class (stable property rights, insurability, rationalized administration), or can the shift be explained entirely within the aristocracy’s own honor calculus, without reference to external interest groups? In either direction—whether the theory explains the case well or the case exposes a boundary—state clearly where the framework is silent and where it is forced to speak.
