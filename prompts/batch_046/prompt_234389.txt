I'm preparing a unit for an advanced undergraduate seminar on philosophy of mind, and we have been working through Benjamin Libet's readiness-potential studies and the subsequent debate over whether conscious will is an epiphenomenal byproduct or retains a "veto" function—what Libet called "free won't." Rather than rehearse the usual classroom examples, I want my students to think about how these experimental structures map onto narrative representations of choice. So I'm turning to Denis Villeneuve's film Arrival, adapted from Ted Chiang's novella "Story of Your Life," in which the linguist Louise Banks acquires a nonlinear, deterministic mode of temporal cognition through her study of an alien language and, knowing that her future daughter will die young, nevertheless chooses to conceive and raise her.

Read the film through Libet's framework, but with a specific twist that moves beyond the stock "neuroscience disproves free will" reading. Treat Louise's acquisition of heptapod cognition as analogous to the buildup of the readiness potential: a pre-conscious restructuring of her cognitive architecture that precedes and shapes any explicit deliberation. Then examine her decision to have the child as a test case for Libet's veto window. Does the film represent her as possessing a genuine interval in which she could have refused the future—an authentic exercise of "free won't"—or does the narrative structure collapse the veto into a mere recognition of an already-initiated course? In particular, consider whether knowing the outcome in advance functions like an already-fired readiness potential, leaving no meaningful gap between neural initiation and conscious assent. At the same time, be honest about where the analogy strains: Libet's experiments concern millisecond-scale motor preparation, not life-shaping narrative choices made over months, and the film's block-universe metaphysics may confuse epistemic access to the future with causal determinism in ways that the neuroscience does not support.

I want a reading that takes both the film's visual and narrative logic and the actual experimental philosophy seriously—one that shows what the film illuminates about the phenomenology of predetermined choice that Libet's laboratory measurements miss, and that also marks the points where the film trades on a confusion between linguistic determinism and the specific neurophysiology of voluntary action. Since my students will not have the text in front of them, avoid verbatim quotation and do not supply a bibliography; I need an interpretation they can follow from common knowledge of the plot and major scenes. End by saying what this comparison adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not, and flag the places where an honest critic must admit the film resists the Libet framing altogether.
