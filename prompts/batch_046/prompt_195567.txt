Two colleagues, Elena and Jonah, were discussing the color phi phenomenon over coffee after a seminar on temporal consciousness. Elena defended what she called the Stalinesque interpretation: when two differently colored dots are flashed in rapid succession at separate positions, the brain does not first register a true sequence and then corrupt it. Instead, it waits until both stimuli have arrived, constructs a single coherent narrative of a moving dot that changes color midway, and then stamps that narrative with a present-tense timestamp, releasing it into awareness as though it had just happened. The subject never genuinely experienced red followed by green; consciousness received only the sanitized "film," already edited before publication. Jonah pushed back with the Orwellian alternative. On his view, the visual system does initially register the discrete flashes in real time and forms a correct, if brief, perceptual record. Only afterward—within a few hundred milliseconds—does a retroactive mechanism revise the earlier trace, inserting an intermediate representation of the dot in transit and altering its color. The result is a rewritten past: the subject genuinely remembers seeing the intermediate state, not because the narrative was delayed, but because the memory was altered after the fact, like archives corrected once the record is already filed. Elena replied that if Orwell were right, there ought to be some detectable moment—however brief—when the unrevised sequence is available to report. Yet no protocol has ever caught subjects describing "red, then green, with nothing between"; even under pressure to respond instantly, they report only the smooth trajectory. This absence of an "unrevised draft," she argued, is exactly what Stalinesque revision predicts: there is no false past to recover, only a manufactured present that was never published in raw form. Jonah answered that this assumes consciousness has access to pre-edited manuscripts, but Orwellian editing can occur at the very gateway to working memory—before anything reaches awareness at all. The corrected version is the first version consciousness ever encounters, not because publication was postponed, but because the manuscript was altered in the press before printing. Therefore, the lack of contradictory reports is fully compatible with upstream revision. Elena then added a more specific concern about feature binding: in the phi display, color and location must be bound together to produce the intermediate percept. She suggested that if the binding of color to spatial position occurs only after motion computation is complete, this supports Stalinesque staging—the narrative is assembled first, then its features are bound into a coherent scene before being released. Jonah countered that binding is part of the initial representation, and that the intermediate color-location pairing is precisely what gets inserted during retroactive editing; the original bindings remain intact for the endpoints, while a new binding is fabricated for the middle, which is more naturally explained as an Orwellian insertion into an existing record rather than a wholesale Stalinesque construction. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: for each of Elena's and Jonah's contributions, rate the argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say which side is more right overall about the interpretation of the color phi phenomenon, and identify what important issue—whether conceptual or empirical—both of them are neglecting.
