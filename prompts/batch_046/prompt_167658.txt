I am preparing a long-form feature on infinite ethics for an audience of science and ideas readers, but I want to avoid the familiar popularized surveys of infinitarian paralysis and expected-value reasoning with unbounded populations. Instead, I am trying to identify which conceptual open questions in this domain are genuinely upstream for practical decision-making—for institutions or individuals facing radical uncertainty about outcomes of unbounded scale—and which are formal curiosities that do not actually constrain action.

To that end, I want to examine three somewhat underdeveloped angles that have been floated but not systematically pursued. First, whether non-Archimedean representations of value—for instance, using surreal-number orderings to model well-being—resolve infinitarian paralysis or merely relocate the problem to the choice of ordering principle. Second, whether a robust distinction between countable and uncountable moral populations can be maintained once we abandon the assumption that individual welfare is finitely bounded. Third, whether the notion of moral risk—understood not as expected-value calculation but as the epistemic status of claims about infinite harm—can be articulated without collapsing back into standard Bayesian frameworks.

For each of these lines, please assess: whether it is tractable for philosophers working without empirical data; how it relates to the more familiar aggregation and risk-aversion problems in infinite settings; and what kind of argument or result over the next few years would shift your view of its importance. More broadly, if you were advising a small conceptual research group with three years of funding, how would you rank these three lines against the standard agenda—infinitarian paralysis, inter-world comparison, and St. Petersburg-style analogies? Defend the ranking with explicit criteria: importance to decision-making under radical uncertainty, tractability for non-formal philosophers, and dependency relations—which questions must be settled before others can advance?
