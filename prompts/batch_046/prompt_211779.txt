During my career in high-precision experimental physics I spent decades refining calibration protocols—ensuring that a measurement of a fundamental constant remains stable not merely in one trial but across the full range of thermal, electromagnetic, and instrumental perturbations we could engineer. Since retiring I have turned to philosophy, and I find myself returning to Plato's Meno and its question: why is knowledge more valuable than true belief? Plato's answer, if I understand it correctly, is that knowledge is true belief secured by an account of the cause or reason, the aitias, analogous to securing Daedalus's statues so they do not wander away. I want to compare this tethering account with a framework drawn from my own discipline, which I will call ecological reliability: the view that what distinguishes knowledge from lucky true belief is not possession of an abstract explanatory account but the stability of a tracking relation across the perturbation space in which an agent actually operates.

Please compare these two accounts of the superior value of knowledge. Specifically: does the ecological reliability framework capture the normative force Plato assigns to the tether—explaining why we ought to prefer knowledge—or does it reduce that normativity to a descriptive feature of well-calibrated systems? Conversely, if Plato's account is translated into the vocabulary of predictive tracking and error-correction, does it retain its essential claim about intellectual grasp of causes, or does it dissolve into a procedural description of stable measurement? I ask for no bibliography or external references; I want to know whether this comparison holds together conceptually on its own footing, and whether either account can absorb the other without distortion.
