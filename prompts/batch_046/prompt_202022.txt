I've been trying to think through the philosophical consequences of the independence of the continuum hypothesis from ZFC, and I've arrived at an argument that disturbs me because it seems to generalise into a scepticism about mathematics that I can't accept. I lay it out below in numbered steps, and I hope someone can show me the exit.

(1) The continuum hypothesis (CH) asks whether there exists a cardinality strictly between that of the natural numbers and that of the real numbers. (2) By the results of Gödel and Cohen, CH is independent of ZFC: both ZFC + CH and ZFC + ¬CH are consistent. (3) So if ZFC is our best formalisation of set theory, it does not settle whether CH holds. (4) Nevertheless, CH is a definite claim about the structure of sets; there is either such an intermediate cardinality or there is not. (5) Therefore there is a determinate fact about CH that ZFC fails to capture. (6) Attempts to complete ZFC—whether through forcing axioms, large-cardinal hypotheses, or inner-model principles—are themselves either mutually inconsistent or lack the intuitive self-evidence that would justify treating them as genuinely axiomatic. (7) We are thus forced into a trilemma: either the set-theoretic universe is genuinely indeterminate (a multiverse or pluralist view), or sets do not exist in a single determinate way (anti-realism), or mathematics is permanently incomplete in a manner that undermines its descriptive authority.

But apply precisely the same reasoning to arithmetic. (8) Gödel's first incompleteness theorem shows that any consistent formal system extending basic arithmetic leaves some arithmetic statements undecidable. (9) By parity, either arithmetic truths are indeterminate, or numbers lack determinate existence, or arithmetic suffers from the same permanent descriptive failure. (10) I reject these conclusions for arithmetic, and I take them to be a reductio ad absurdum for the parallel reasoning about sets. (11) Hence the inference from (2) and (4) to (5) and thence to (7) must contain a mistake. (12) I am inclined to keep (4)—that CH has a definite answer—and I would prefer not to abandon (3), though I could tolerate giving up (6) if that is the price of blocking the sceptical conclusion. Which step is the weakest? If I reject (5), am I committing myself to a deflationary view of set-theoretic truth? If I reject (6), must I embrace a particular forcing-axiom programme or an inner-model hierarchy? I want to understand exactly what each option costs.
