Explain the problem of old evidence in Bayesian epistemology, framing your discussion around a concept we might call epistemic amortization: the idea that using long-known data to confirm a new theory requires accounting for the cognitive interest already expended on that evidence before the theory arrived. Contrast this framing with two standard responses—first, the counterfactual account, which asks what credence one would have assigned had the theory preceded the data; and second, the logical-learning account, according to which confirmation occurs when an agent first recognizes that the data are entailed by the theory. Your explanation should be precise but intuitive: clarify why strict Bayesian updating seems to block old evidence from confirming theories at all, how each of the three frameworks attempts to restore confirmation, and whether epistemic amortization introduces distinctive difficulties—particularly the risk that confirmation becomes dependent on an agent's psychological history rather than on objective evidential relations. Conclude with a reasoned assessment of whether old evidence genuinely confirms theories, and specify the conditions under which an amortization-based view is illuminating versus misleading.
