Two members of a philosophy study group, Mira and Jonas, have been arguing over Plato’s attitude toward drunkenness and the symposion. Mira holds that the Laws should be read as consistent with the Republic’s suspicion of intoxication: the symposion permitted for citizens over thirty is essentially a civic stress-test, in which wine lowers inhibitions so that hidden vices become visible to the symposiarch, who then corrects them. The restorative language Plato uses—that wine makes the old feel young and relaxes the soul—is, on her view, purely instrumental: relaxation exposes character rather than improving it, and the age restriction exists precisely because testing should occur only after formative education is complete. Jonas replies that this makes the institutional apparatus sound like a prison guard rather than an educator, and that it ignores Plato’s explicit claim that wine genuinely benefits civic morale. He notes that if the purpose were purely diagnostic, there would be no reason to bar the young, since revealing hidden flaws is arguably more valuable during formation; the restriction instead suggests alcohol is too dangerous for developing souls but therapeutically useful for maintaining social cohesion among the aged. Mira responds that Jonas is conflating palliative comfort with moral endorsement: older citizens are past the stage where exposure can reshape them, so the symposion serves maintenance, not formation. Jonas then introduces a comparison rarely emphasized in standard readings: in Republic II–III, music and rhythm educate the non-rational parts of the soul not through argument but through ordered habit. He suggests alcohol in the Laws works analogously—not by reasoning, but by conditioning the appetitive part under controlled disorder—which means the Laws extends, rather than abandons, Plato’s non-rational pedagogy. The symposiarch, on this view, functions less as a guard and more as a conductor of disorder. Mira counters that the analogy collapses because music possesses rational structure (harmony, proportion) that alcohol explicitly destroys; containing chaos is not the same as channeling order. Jonas grants the disanalogy in structure but insists the functional parallel remains: both target soul-parts that reason cannot reach directly, and both require institutional mediation. He concludes that Mira’s “diagnostic-only” reading fails to explain why Plato assigns positive civic value to the practice rather than treating it as a regrettable necessity. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of Mira’s and Jonas’s major turns—the diagnostic reading, the therapeutic objection, the age-restriction argument, the music analogy, and the structural disanalogy—rate the move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say which party is more right overall, and identify any important consideration about Plato’s view of intoxication that both of them overlook.
