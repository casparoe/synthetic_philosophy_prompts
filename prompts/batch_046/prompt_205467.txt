I was arguing with my roommate about our local zoning board, and we got stuck on this pattern that doesn't quite fit the usual NIMBY story. You know how people will argue against a new apartment building not becuase they hate renters or want high property values, but becuase they say the development will "change the feel" of the street? But then if you press them, they don't mean architecture or noise exactly—it's more like the pace of interaction. They talk about how currently you can leave your house and bump into the same three neighbors walking dogs at roughly the same time every evening, and that this creates a kind of predictable social rhythm. The new building, they claim, would introduce too many strangers moving through at unpredictable hours, breaking that rhythm, even if the actual noise and traffic stay the same. So they're defending not a physical space or an economic interest, but what I'd call a "temporal ecology" of familiarity.

I'm trying to figure out if there's already an established name for this—defending zoning through the preservation of a neighborhood's interaction tempo rather than its built form or property values. I know there's literature on place attachment and defensible space and all that, but those seem to be about physical territory or emotional bonds to landmarks. This feels different, more like protecting a shared schedule of casual encounters. Does this configuration have a name of its own, and if not, can you name and define it in a way that distinguishes it from generic NIMBYism or from Jane Jacobs-style eyes-on-the-street arguments? I'm not looking for a reading list, just whether the carving is already out there and where its edges sit.
