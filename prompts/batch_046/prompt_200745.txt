Exam question: In De Corpore Hobbes argues that geometry is uniquely demonstrative among the sciences because its objects—lines, figures, and motions—are generated by the geometer's own definitions and constructions. Explain how this "constructive" criterion distinguishes geometry from natural philosophy (physics) in Hobbes's epistemology, and assess whether it makes his position closer to modern formalism (where mathematics studies arbitrary but consistent formal systems) or to Kant's synthetic-a-priori view (where spatial intuition supplies non-logical content).

Student answer:
Hobbes's core claim is that in geometry we ourselves are the cause of the figures we study, since we generate them from clear definitions and then demonstrate their properties step by step. Because we know the cause, we have perfect, certain knowledge. In physics, by contrast, we observe effects—falling bodies, heat, magnetism—but we do not construct their hidden causes; we only conjecture them from sense experience. So physics can never reach the demonstrative certainty of geometry.

This constructionist view clearly separates Hobbes from Kant. Kant holds that geometry depends on pure intuition of space, an innate mental faculty that gives content to our judgments. Hobbes rejects any such a priori faculty. For Hobbes, geometric figures are not given by intuition but are produced through the motion of bodies—either in the world or in our imagination—according to rules we stipulate. The content therefore comes from physical motion and our power to imagine it, not from a non-empirical form of sensibility.

At the same time, Hobbes is essentially an anticipatory formalist. Because the geometer creates the definitions, the choice of axioms is arbitrary: we could define "triangle" or "parallel" differently and derive an equally valid system, provided only that the derivations are internally consistent. Mathematics thus does not describe an independent reality; it explores the logical consequences of rules we freely choose. The only constraint is consistency, not correspondence to intuition or to nature.

One qualification is that Hobbes applies this strict constructivism only to geometry. He does not extend it to arithmetic, because numbers are not constructed by us but are abstracted from given quantitative experience; arithmetic therefore remains closer to empirical reasoning and lacks geometry's pure demonstrative status.
