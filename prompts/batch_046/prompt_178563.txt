I am writing a paper for my moral philosophy seminar on Kant's account of moral worth, and I have come to defend a fairly specific version of it: moral worth belongs only to actions performed with what I call regulative awareness—that is, the agent's conscious representation of the moral law not merely as an external reason that moves her, but as a principle she actively self-legislates and sustains in authority over her will through the very performance of the act. On this view, returning a lost wallet out of respect for property rights lacks full moral worth if the agent experiences her motivation as arising from her character or upbringing rather than as an active, second-order endorsement of the law's authority occurring in the act itself; even acting from duty, in the ordinary sense, is insufficient unless it includes this reflexive, sustaining awareness. What would genuinely count against this position? I want arguments, carefully described cases, and distinctions drawn from other areas of ethics or philosophy of mind that expose where it goes wrong—not just the familiar objection that it demands excessive introspection, but also whether it makes moral worth implausibly rare, whether it collapses into a kind of moral vanity, whether it can accommodate the intuition that deeply virtuous actions are often habitual yet still worthy, and whether it wrongly privileges conscious mental architecture over the objective rightness of what is done. Please rank these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which ones a committed defender of the view could concede without abandoning its core claim, and assess honestly whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—for instance, by redefining regulative awareness post hoc to cover every apparently counterintuitive case—and whether such insulation would be a theoretical virtue or a vice.
