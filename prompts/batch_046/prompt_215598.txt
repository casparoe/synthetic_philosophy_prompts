I'm preparing a diagnostic instrument for an advanced epistemology seminar focused on rational belief revision under self-doubt. The topic is higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia: situations in which an agent holds a first-order belief that P while simultaneously possessing strong evidence that her belief-forming processes for beliefs of this type are unreliable.

Please construct a decision tree — with each branch node formulated as a concrete case or forced-choice commitment rather than a theoretical label — that sorts respondents into positions on what rationality requires in these situations. The tree should begin from the following specific scenario and then branch through five further diagnostic questions.

Starting case: You construct a quantitative model predicting a specific outcome with 73% confidence. You then encounter a rigorous meta-analysis demonstrating that non-expert models in this domain are systematically overconfident by 20–30 percentage points, with no exception for models that feel well-reasoned to their creators. Your direct reasoning remains intact — you still see the premises as supporting 73% — but you now have this statistical self-doubt.

Branch nodes (each should be answerable without knowing philosophical terminology):

1. Does the meta-analysis provide a direct, first-order reason to lower the numerical confidence, or does it only undermine your entitlement to hold the belief at that confidence without altering its content?
2. Is it rationally permissible to maintain both the belief that P (with 73% confidence) and the belief that one's reasoning here is unreliable — i.e., is epistemic akrasia ever rational?
3. If you identify a specific, non-statistical feature of your model that distinguishes it from the studies in the meta-analysis, does that feature defeat the higher-order evidence, or must you still calibrate downward?
4. Does the correct response depend on whether the higher-order evidence targets your general cognitive class, your specific reasoning trace in this case, or the objective quality of the premises?
5. If rationality requires splitting your attitude — maintaining the 73% belief for theoretical purposes while withholding it for practical decision — is this split coherent, or does it collapse into either full belief or full suspension?

The leaves should include the following positions: a strict calibration or deference view (revise downward regardless); a coherence or anti-akrasia view (you must align first-order belief with your assessment of reliability); a right-reasons or transparency view (maintain the belief if you still see the premises, but treat the higher-order evidence as defeating entitlement rather than content); and a permissive or compartmentalization view that allows the split attitude. Additionally, include at least two unnamed combinations — answers that no prominent view occupies — with one sentence each explaining why that combination is unstable, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

For each leaf, provide: the position's name (or "unnamed"), a one-sentence statement of its core claim, and its most awkward commitment — specifically, what it must say about a variant case in which the statistical evidence is near-perfect but your direct reasoning feels irresistibly clear and transparent.

Finally, flag any pair of leaves that your tree genuinely cannot separate given these five nodes, and briefly explain why the diagnostic questions fail to distinguish them.

Keep the instrument focused on commitments revealed through case judgments, not on familiarity with authors or texts. Do not include reading lists, citations, or requests for bibliographic verification. The audience is graduate students in epistemology; aim for six to eight leaves total.
