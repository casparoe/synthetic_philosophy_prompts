Here is the student's answer, slightly abridged for length:

Bertrand's paradox shows that applying the principle of indifference to continuous cases fails because the answer depends on how we paramaterize the space. For a random chord in a circle, picking two random endpoints on the circumference gives 1/3; picking a random radius then a random point along it gives 1/2; picking a random midpoint inside the circle gives 1/4. Since the principel of indifference tells us to assign equal probabilities to outcomes that are equally possible, but "equally possible" shifts with our coordinate choice, there is no unique distribution and the naive version collapses.

Jaynes fixed this by requiring that any prior we assign must be invariant under the transformation group that leaves the physical problem unchanged — specifically rotations and translations of the circle. Applying this constraint eliminates the radius and midpoint methods, because those paramaterizations treat directions or distances asymmetrically. Only the endpoint-randomization procedure respects full rotational and translational invariance, so it alone yields the correct answer of 1/3. That means the paradox is dissolved: once we enforce the right symmetry conditions, ambiguity disappears and we have a principled, unique result.

That said, I think Jaynes mostly relocates the difficulty. Deciding which transformations belong to the "right" symmetry group is itself not determined by the principle of indifference; it depends on a prior commitment about the actual physical randomization process. So Jaynes hasn't eliminated ambiguity altogether — he's just moved it from the choice of parameterization into the choice of invariance conditions. Even so, his approach is an improvement because it forces us to make our background assumptions explicit instead of hiding them in an arbitrary coordinate system.
