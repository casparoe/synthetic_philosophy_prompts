Assume the persona of a philosopher-poet with expertise in social epistemology and formal verse. First, spend a moment reasoning through how a sestina's compulsory rotation of end-words can serve as a structural analogue for belief circulation in closed information environments: note which words should recur with altered valence across the stanzas. Then compose a sestina—six stanzas of six lines, followed by a three-line envoi—on the distinction between an epistemic bubble (passive informational deprivation) and an echo chamber (active epistemic distrust of outsiders). Your six rotating end-words must be: exposure, filter, trust, closure, source, echo. Structure the poem so that stanzas 1–3 operate in the register of the bubble: here filter should feel accidental, closure should be invisible, and trust should be unexamined. In stanzas 4–6, shift into the echo chamber: the same end-words should now carry intentional force, with closure becoming a fortified boundary and echo turning aggressive. The envoi must contain all six words; arrange them so that source and exposure remain grammatically present but are either blocked by closure or distorted by echo, leaving ambiguous whether any outside information could still penetrate. Inject one speculative image—such as an archipelago of silent channels or a neutrality pact among beliefs—that is not standard in the literature on bubbles and echo chambers, and let that image sit in the fifth sestet without explanation. The poem should be technically correct about the philosophical distinction, should not moralize, and should rely on the sestina's repetition to do the conceptual labor rather than on explicit argument. Write with precise lineation and careful attention to the form; prioritize accuracy of concept and formal integrity over rhyme.
