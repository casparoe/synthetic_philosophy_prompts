Read Peter Weir’s film The Truman Show through the paradox of fiction, taking seriously both Colin Radford’s problem—how we can feel genuine concern for characters we know do not exist—and Kendall Walton’s make-believe account, on which we direct quasi-emotions at fictional representations. The film inverts the usual epistemic setup: its diegetic audience knows Truman Burbank is a real person yet treats him as disposable entertainment, while the real-world audience knows he is fictional yet responds to his suffering with full moral urgency. Use this inversion to ask whether emotional engagement tracks institutional framing and social practice rather than ontological status. Focus on the spotlight crash, Truman’s confrontation with Christof, and the broadcast audience’s cheers at his escape: do these scenes suggest that “fictionality” is not a property of an object but a licensed mode of attention? Say where the film resists being reduced to an illustration of Walton—particularly in the way Christof’s paternalistic language frames control as care—and conclude by identifying what this case contributes to the philosophical debate that standard literary examples do not capture.
