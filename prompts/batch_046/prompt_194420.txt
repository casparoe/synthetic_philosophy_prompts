Consider a split-brain patient with complete corpus callosum sectioning who, via an external tactile cross-cueing interface, achieves seamless behavioral integration between left-hemisphere linguistic reporting and right-hemisphere perceptual judgment, despite the absence of any restored direct interhemispheric neural connectivity. What's your credence—on a scale from 0 to 1—that such a patient still contains a single subject of phenomenal experience rather than two numerically distinct conscious subjects that have merely learned to coordinate? Decompose your reasoning: first, the probability that phenomenal unity requires direct neural integration rather than functional coordination alone; second, the probability that the right hemisphere possesses the self-referential and affective structure necessary to constitute an independent subject; third, the probability that behavioral and cognitive integration mediated by an external apparatus can merge previously separate subjects into one; and fourth, the probability that the notion of a "subject of experience" is determinate enough for this distinction to have a correct answer. Combine these into an overall number with one line of justification for each term, then tell me which term is least certain and what empirical or conceptual finding would most shift it.
