I'm on this municipal charter review commission — basically a mini constitutional convention for our city — and we're deadlocked over a proposal we have to vote on next tuesday. The council wants to amend the charter to create what amounts to a Ulysses contract for public spending: lock 15% of annual revenue into a climate-adaptation fund for 25 years, and make it nearly impossible to undo without a supermajority of two successive councils plus a voter referendum. The idea is that future politicians will always be tempted to raid the fund during budget crunches, so current citizens should bind their hands now.

But there's a twist that's splitting us. The amendment includes an "escape knot" — if an independant fiscal monitor certifies "structural revenue collapse," the covenant can be suspended for up to three years. The supporters say this is exactly how smart precommitment works: you tie yourself to the mast but keep the knife within reach for genuine emergencies. The critics say it's incoherent, because if the release condition is vague enough that political pressure can shape the monitor's judgment, then we haven't actually constrained anyone; we've just added expensive bureaucracy that creates an illusion of binding.

My task is to draft the commission's formal reccomendation memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a clear bottom line and reasoning that takes the strongest objection seriously rather than waving it away. I was talking with my roommate who's taking democratic theory, and she kept using the phrase "intertemporal democratic legitimacy," but I'm not sure I'm applying it right. Could you write the memo? It should read like an actual document we could circulate, include concrete conditions with maybe a timeline if that's our call, and engage the incoherence argument head-on.
