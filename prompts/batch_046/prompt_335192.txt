So in some strategy consulting firms there's this internal protocol where they evaluate long-term cooperative projects using a kind of centipede-game logic: they map out each decision node where either party could take an immediate payoff and walk away, then apply backward induction to argue that cooperation collapses at the first node, so they reject the whole project. At the same time these firms sell clients on 'relationship capital' programs that assume people will keep passing up short-term gains for years. What philosophical commitments about rationality and common knowledge does the veto protocol presuppose? Does it require that rational agents have perfect access to each other's reasoning, or can it work with bounded rationality? And is the practice philosophically consistent with the relationship-capital side, or are they smuggling in a different conception of practical reason—maybe one where reasons aren't just local payoff maximization? I'm particularily interested in whether the veto assumes that being rational means always acting on stage-independent reasons, and whether that's actually defensible.
