I am a retired ecologist who has recently turned to democratic theory, and I am preparing to moderate a panel Q&A with an unnamed philosopher who defends what she calls a gradient-democracy thesis: the claim that membership in the demos is scalar rather than binary, with an individual's weight determined jointly by the intensity of a decision's impact on her core interests and by the irreversibility of those impacts. She treats sharp territorial or citizenship boundaries as administratively convenient heuristics that carry no independent normative weight, and she allows that future generations and resident non-citizens can hold partial membership weights. She is very skilled at deflecting objections by insisting that any precise cutoff is practically unavoidable and therefore not a theoretical flaw. I want a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that press on the actual mechanics of her view and do not allow her to retreat to that deflection. At least one question should address how she assigns weights to individuals who do not yet exist when intergenerational effects are at stake; another should present a case in which a small number of outsiders suffer intense, irreversible harms while citizens experience large but diffuse and reversible benefits, and ask exactly how her scalar calculus resolves the conflict; a third should ask whether her model, once administrative weights are assigned, collapses into a straightforward all-affected-interests principle with extra bookkeeping. For each of these three, include a follow-up keyed specifically to her likely reply that line-drawing is always necessary and therefore normatively neutral. The remaining questions should probe further in the weeds—perhaps on whether reversibility itself can be measured without presupposing the boundary she is trying to justify, or on how her view distinguishes democratic exclusion from mere disenfranchisement within a shared polity. Conclude with a single, sharply focused question asking what kind of empirical finding or normative argument would genuinely move her to abandon the gradient model in favor of a binary boundary. Please write in a formal, polished register appropriate for an academic colloquium.
