I'm trying to figure out my actual view on Leibniz-style moral motivation — like when is an act genuinely moved by caritas sapientis vs just prudence or confused appetite. Here are my gut verdicts.

Case 1: A logician spends months tutoring a stranger in modal logic, purely because she perceives the rational order unfolding in his mind, with no personal gain and it delays her own work. I call that exemplary — real moral worth, not just being nice.

Case 2: The same logician refuses to share a proof with a rival who could extend it, not from malice but because she doesn't want her contribution diluted. Universal perfection would clearly increase if she shared. I still think she's falling short morally, even though she harms no one directly.

Case 3: A ruler can either build a university library or prevent a famine killing thousands. He picks the library, reasoning that rational beings pursuing eternal truths matter more than preserving bodies that are just machines of nature. That feels wrong to me — like a misapplication of the hierarchy — but I'm not sure if I'm just being sentimental.

Case 4: Someone lies to a dying friend, saying the estranged brother called, knowing it's false but that it calms the friend's confused perceptions in his final hours. The outcome is more pleasant, but I think it's wrong — not just imprudent, morally defective, because it feeds confusion instead of rational clarity.

Case 5: A merchant pays debts exactly on time, but only because he calculates reputation protects long-term profit. No sense of justice as beautiful order. I think that's not morally worthy at all, just smart.

Case 6: A soldier jumps on a grenade to save five comrades, doing it through a cool rationl weighing — five rational souls versus one — with no emotional attachment or love of God. The act saves lives, but I feel something is missing; it's not the same quality as Case 1.

My tentative principle: moral worth requires the will be moved by a clear and distinct perception of universal perfection, not by confused sensory appetite or private calculation, with justice as the floor and charity as the ceiling. But Case 2 and Case 6 both mess with it. Case 2 shows you can perceive universal good clearly and still fail. Case 6 shows you can have rational calculation of universal good without the right appetitive structure, and I want to call it deficient.

Is there a cleaner Leibnizian account that saves all six, or do I have to drop one? If I drop Case 6 and say rational calculation is enough, what does that do to Case 5 — is prudent calculation of reputation suddenly closer to virtue? And what about a case I keep going back and forth on: someone who feels intense charity but is confused about what actually promotes perfection, and ends up causing harm? Would that be morally good but mistaken, or not good at all?

Also, does this hang on whether moral truths depend on God's understanding rather than will, or can I map the principle without settling that? Not asking for a reading list, just the conceptual structure.
