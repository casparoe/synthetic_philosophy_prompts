Write a formal dialogue between two interlocutors—Clara, who holds that Adam Smith’s impartial spectator is fundamentally constrained by the proximity conditions of sympathy, and Elias, who holds that the spectator’s impartiality is precisely what allows it to overcome proximity and judge structurally mediated harm. They should discuss a concrete case: judging moral responsibility in situations where suffering is widespread but visible only through aggregated statistics, with no single identifiable victim. Clara should argue that sympathy requires imaginative identification with a particular other, and that without a concrete face the spectator’s judgment becomes either paralyzed or arbitrarily selective, implying that Smith’s framework needs an auxiliary corrective principle to account for distance. Elias should respond that the spectator’s role is to abstract from particular attachments, and that statistical abstraction is not a corruption of sympathy but an extension of its disciplined, impartial form; he should defend the view that sympathy, properly regulated, can operate through rational generalization rather than direct emotional mirroring. Ensure real back-and-forth: Clara must address Elias’s claim about abstraction without being reduced to an anti-rationalist, and Elias must engage Clara’s concern about selective paralysis rather than dismissing emotional proximity outright. Have them conclude by articulating whether Smith’s account contains internal resources for rendering judgment in such cases, or whether it must be supplemented by an external mechanism not found in his original framework.
