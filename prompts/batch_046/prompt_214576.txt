I've been trying to push the Clark-Chalmers parity principle into a case of long-term cognitive outsourcing, but the argument collapses into absurdity and I can't locate the leak.

(1) If an external resource plays the same functional role as an internal cognitive process—reliably accessed, automatically endorsed, and integrated into reasoning—then it is literally part of the mind.

(2) A researcher has used the same proprietary database for twenty years; she queries it without conscious deliberation, its ontology structures her hypotheses, and she treats its outputs as authoritative without verification.

(3) So the database is part of her extended mind.

(4) The database is now maintained by an anonymous collective of strangers; its taxonomy is revised through debates she never witnesses, and some entries are generated by statistical models she has never examined.

(5) Therefore parts of her mind include reasoning processes—conceptual adjustments, inferential chains, deliberative steps—that she has never performed, never endorsed, and cannot access.

(6) But mental ownership requires that a subject can, in principle, avow or correct the content in question; without that boundary, there is no distinction between her mind and a public archive.

(7) So the parity principle entails that individual minds are routinely constituted by alien cognition, destroying the conditions for first-person authority.

(8) Hence (1) is false.

I want to keep (1). Which step should I reject, why did it look plausible, and what does giving it up cost—especially for the idea that cognition can genuinely extend beyond the skull?
