While writing a paper for my metaphysics seminar on Bradley’s regress, I have noticed that discussions of the argument tend to fixate on whether relations generate an infinite hierarchy without first clarifying what a successful resolution actually owes us. Before I evaluate particular theories, I want the evaluative standard made explicit. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of propositional unity—how a proposition avoids being a mere list or aggregate of its constituents—must satisfy. For each desideratum, identify the motivating case or phenomenon (for instance: false propositions that are unified despite lacking corresponding facts; the same constituents arranged to yield different propositions, such as a relational versus an attributive judgment; compositionality of linguistic understanding; the need to avoid an infinite regress of unifying relations; the possibility of mind-independent or unthought propositions; and the demand that the account extend to other complex entities, like states of affairs, without collapsing propositional unity into them). Indicate for each whether you regard it as non-negotiable or negotiable, and flag at least one pair you take to be jointly unsatisfiable, explaining why the tension is intrinsic rather than superficial. Then, briefly but decisively, score the three main contenders against your list: the view that relations or concepts are intrinsically unsaturated and thereby unify without further aid; the structured-proposition view that constituents are literally arranged in a complex; and the deflationary view that unity amounts to nothing over and above syntactic, grammatical, or logical form. Take a definite position—do not merely balance pros and cons—on which single desideratum does the most exclusionary work in ruling out candidates, and defend that judgment with reasons. I want a committed assessment, not a menu of options left for me to adjudicate.
