I'm currently serving as the graduate-student representative on my university's Student Conduct Appeals Board, and this semester I'm taking a seminar on moral responsibility and free will. We've been asked to review a difficult disciplinary case: a doctoral candidate in neuroscience admitted to selectively altering imaging data in a manuscript submitted for publication, and the faculty misconduct panel has recommended expulsion. Rather than deny the act, the student has submitted a philosophical defense arguing that he cannot bear true moral responsibility for it. Drawing on the structure of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument—that to be genuinely responsible for one's actions one must first be responsible for one's nature or character, which in turn requires being responsible for the prior conditions that formed that nature, generating an infinite regress that makes being causa sui impossible—he contends that his psychological makeup, his chronic sleep deprivation, his abusive mentor's demands, and the hyper-competitive funding environment entirely determined his conduct. He therefore asks the board to reject retributive expulsion in favor of a purely rehabilitative plan.

I need to draft the board's decision memo for circulation before Thursday's vote. The memo should recommend a concrete course of action—whether to uphold expulsion, replace it with suspension plus mandatory ethics reconstruction and a defined probationary period, or adopt some intermediate sanction with a clear time frame—and it should provide reasoning that both the student and the faculty panel could actually engage with rather than dismiss. It should take the Basic Argument seriously rather than treating it as a generic determinist excuse, but it must also address the institutional need to preserve research integrity, protect future collaborators, and maintain consistent disciplinary standards. In particular, I want the memo to examine whether adopting a compatibilist framework for institutional sanctions—separating practical accountability, protective separation, and corrective obligation from metaphysical desert—offers a coherent middle path, or whether that framework collapses once we recognize that our code implicitly treats students as genuinely deserving punishment. Please treat the strongest objection to your recommended position honestly, and write the response in American English.
