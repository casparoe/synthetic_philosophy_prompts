ok so I've been trying to pin down my view on whether religion actually makes societies more moral, and I want a consistency check before I post it in the group. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) When I say "more moral" I mean something functional: lower free-riding in large anonymous interactions, not more subjective guilt or ritual purity behaviours. Basically a game-theoretic metric.

(2) I think secular societies (Netherlands, parts of Scandinavia, maybe Canada) score about as high on this as religious ones, once you control for GDP and inequality, so religion isn't necessary for the outcome.

(3) But I also think institutions only work because people carry a "residual trust" — an expectation of reciprocity that isn't fully calculated — and I worry that secular civic narratives are weaker commitment devices than transcendent ones, so the reservoir might erode over generations.

(4) I think evolutionary debunking undermines religious moral intuitions (purity, authority-based norms) but leaves secular harm-based norms intact, because the latter are rationally reconstructible from cooperative game theory.

(5) I think if every religious community vanished tomorrow, cooperation wouldn't collapse immediately, but over a century or two the residual trust might thin out unless secular institutions become much more explicit about reciprocity norms.

So: do these actually hang together? Specifically, is there a tension between (2) and (3)/(5) where I'm saying religion isn't necessary now but might be necessary later — is that special pleading? Is (4) doing too much work by letting secular norms escape debunking? And is (1) compatible with treating residual trust as a necessary ingredient in (3), given that (1) seems to reduce morality to measurable behaviour?

Please rank the tensions by severity, say which commitment has to go if any, and tell me whether there's a cheaper revision than dropping (4) or (5). Also, seperate the real tensions from the ones that only look bad — don't just flag everything.
