I've been trying to map out Zhuangzi's take on death for a semenar and ended up with this chain that feels like it proves way too much. Here it is:

(1) For Zhuangzi death is just hua — a transformation of qi, not an annihilation. The "person" was always just a temporary gathering.

(2) If an event is genuinely just a rearrangment within an endless, value-neutral cycle, then there is no objective loss happening.

(3) Grief is only rational when there is an actual loss, so on this view grief at death is always conceptually mistaken.

(4) Zhuangzi doesn't just say this intellectualy; he thinks the sage has affectivly internalized it, so the sage shouldn't just suppress grief but should be literally incapible of it.

(5) But Zhuangzi admits he initially wept for his wife before he "got it," which means either he wasn't really a sage then, or sages can still feel grief and correct it.

(6) If we take the first horn, then anyone who ever mourns — including parents mourning a child — is revealing a failure of wisdom, not just human weakness.

(7) That seems to turn Zhuangzi's view into an inhuman emotional standard that condemns normal attachment as ignorance.

(8) So either (4) is wrong and sages can still feel initial grief, or (2)-(3) are wrong and transformation doesn't eliminate loss, which seems to collapse back into the Confucian view he rejects.

I feel like step (4) is where the slip happens — going from "shouldn't grieve" to "should be incapable of grieving" — but I'm not sure what giving it up costs. If I reject (4) I can keep the idea that death isn't a real loss, but then I need some account of why Zhuangzi's drumming on the tub isn't just cold denial. Which premise should I actually drop, and what's the price? I'd rather keep (2) if I can, but I want to know what I'm paying.
