In my software work I deal with multi-objective optimization where different loss functions sometimes cannot be normalized onto a single axis without destroying structural information, and that analogy led me into a philosophical debate I have been tracking: lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism and whether pleasure and pain are genuinely commensurable. The dispute looks frozen. One side holds that extreme suffering dominates any quantity of pleasure as a lexical priority; another claims this is a category error because pleasure and pain do not sit on a shared cardinal scale; a third treats the threshold as a bounded-rationality heuristic rather than a moral fact. They invoke the same intuition pumps and have not moved in years. My hypothesis is that they are running three debates in parallel—one about moral measurement, one about the ontology of value, one about cognitive architecture—while using the word lexical as a shared keyword that hides the split. Is that the right diagnosis? Is there a hidden formal question about aggregating discontinuous utility domains? Or are they stuck on bedrock intuitions about incommensurability that neither side can argue for? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here—a conceptual distinction, an empirical result about human judgment, a formal result about aggregation failure, or should I conclude the framing itself is ill-posed and expect no resolution?
