After a long and somewhat circular argument with a friend about whether the past actually comes divided into chapters or only into paragraphs that later readers number, I want to press the disagreement into a sharper philosophical form. Compare the discontinuist thesis — that "the Middle Ages," "the Renaissance," and "the Scientific Revolution" name real, causally bounded epochs with distinct cognitive regimes and institutional architectures — with the sedimentation thesis, which treats them as retrospective narrative containers that smooth over continuous mutations in practice. Focus on the boundary problem at two transition zones: first, the coexistence of scholastic natural philosophy and humanist textual practices within the same universities and courts from roughly 1400 to 1600; second, the emergence of mathematized experimentation in the seventeenth century alongside persistent Aristotelian curricula. For each thesis, explain how it accounts for the fact that contemporaries themselves began to periodize their own recent past in these terms well before modern historiography formalized the scheme. Then evaluate which thesis survives the shift from a thick conception of periodization — in which epoch-labels carry explanatory load about necessary conditions for knowledge production — to a thin conception, in which they function merely as descriptive coordinate systems. Conclude by stating whether your verdict depends on accepting that historical time possesses ontological joints that exist independently of the narratives that name them, and identify which structural feature of your preferred thesis would collapse if those joints were shown to be purely projective.
