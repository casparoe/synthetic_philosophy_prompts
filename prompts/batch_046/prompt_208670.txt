As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of time, I have been working through the Rietdijk–Putnam argument that the relativity of simultaneity in special relativity entails a block universe, in which past, present, and future events are equally real and temporal becoming is not ontologically fundamental. I would like you to restate the core premises of that argument—not its standard rebuttals—in the vocabulary of equilibrium statistical mechanics. Specifically, treat spacetime events as microstates of an underlying canonical ensemble; treat the "present" as an observer-dependent macrostate boundary or local entropy gradient; treat the conventionality of simultaneity as a frame-dependent coarse-graining; and treat the claim that distant events are "already real" as analogous to the claim that unobserved microstates are already present in the ensemble description. For each major premise of the argument, report precisely whether it survives intact in this thermodynamic vocabulary, whether it must be distorted (for example, by interpreting temporal becoming as an irreversible macro-process analogous to entropy increase), or whether it has no expression at all within statistical mechanics. Then assess whether the residue—the parts that fail to translate cleanly—points to a genuine limitation of the statistical-mechanical framework for capturing temporal ontology, or whether it reveals that the original philosophical argument was already importing metaphysical commitments that physics alone does not enforce. Keep the translation honest: do not smuggle in statistical-mechanical assumptions that dissolve the philosophical problem by fiat, and treat the residue, rather than the successful translations, as the most philosophically interesting part of the exercise.
