I am preparing a diagnostic handout for a seminar on collective memory and political identity that centers on “lost cause” national myths—narratives through which a community defines itself not by victory but by a noble, traumatic, or strategically reinterpreted defeat. Our running cases are the Confederate Lost Cause, the Masada siege narrative in Zionist collective memory, the Kosovo Field myth in Serbian national identity, and the British “Dunkirk spirit” of 1940, though the taxonomy should be built from the logical space of the positions rather than from a list of proper names. I want a branching tree that sorts the actual philosophical and functional stances one can take on whether such myths are legitimate, structurally unavoidable, or inherently dangerous. Each branch should split on a concrete claim about the myth’s internal mechanics: whether the narrative operates with an analeptic orientation (it treats the defeat as a temporary interruption demanding eventual restoration of the lost order) or a proleptic orientation (it treats defeat as a permanent warning or moral foundation with no restorative promise); whether the defeat is interpreted through a sacrificial grammar (the community suffers for a transcendent value that redeems the loss) or a strategic grammar (the loss was a contingent failure of means that teaches tactical humility); whether the myth necessarily requires an external scapegoat or internal traitor to preserve the nobility of the cause; and whether the narrative can be sustained within democratic pluralism or necessarily collapses into ethnic or authoritarian exclusivism. Each leaf should carry either a named position if one is genuinely defended (for instance, a “democratic martyrology” view, a “restorationist particularist” view, or an “anti-mythic civic stoic” view) or be marked “unnamed,” together with one sentence describing its most awkward commitment—the feature that makes it hardest to defend consistently against the other branches. Please also identify any combination of answers that produces an empty cell in the tree, and briefly diagnose whether the gap is incoherent, actively suppressed by the logic of the other commitments, or merely unexplored. In addition, flag any pair of leaves that your taxonomy cannot cleanly separate, explaining why they blur together at the diagnostic level. I do not want bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; the instrument should be constructible from the conceptual distinctions themselves without web access. Finally, please include a brief meta-note on where your classification is most likely to fail: what kind of counterexample, case reinterpretation, or conceptual distinction would lead you to restructure the tree, and which branch you regard as the least stable in your reasoning.
