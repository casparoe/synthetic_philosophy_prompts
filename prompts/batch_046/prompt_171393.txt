I keep encountering a particular argumentative pattern in debates over constitutional monarchy in democracies such as the UK, Scandinavia, or Japan. Proponents argue that an unelected monarch guarantees continuity and national unity precisely because they stand outside partisan politics. At the same time, those same proponents point to moments when royal influence appears consequential — private counsel that alters policy, or formal powers exercised in exceptional constitutional circumstances — to show the institution is not purely ornamental. Critics reply that genuine influence makes the arrangement undemocratic, while purely symbolic influence makes the continuity claim sentimental. The defence thus seems to shift between invoking the monarch's powerlessness to secure democratic legitimacy and invoking hidden efficacy to secure practical value, without resolving the tension. Is there an established concept or term for this exact oscillation? If there is, how does it differ from the standard separation of ceremonial and reserve constitutional functions? If there is not, can you propose a precise definition with boundary conditions — specifying what would count as resolving the tension rather than repeating it?
