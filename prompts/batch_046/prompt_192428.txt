Consider the responsibility gap literature for autonomous systems in high-stakes domains—autonomous command-and-control architectures, clinical triage algorithms, or systemic-risk monitors—where harmful outcomes emerge from structures in which no single human possesses both decisive causal influence and adequate foresight. Rather than treating the gap as uniform, distinguish structural gaps (accountability is diluted across roles by design) from epistemic gaps (actors are present but individually unable to predict system behavior), and distinguish distributive responses (spreading blame more finely) from reconstructive ones (redesigning accountability architectures around irreducible gaps).

Within this framing, several competing lines of work claim urgency: whether the gap is ontologically real or merely a practical failure of coordination; whether ex ante design certification can close it; whether a non-human locus of responsibility is coherent or desirable; whether institutions should accept permanent gaps and restructure decision rights accordingly; and whether ex post audit mechanisms can restore accountability without undermining autonomy.
