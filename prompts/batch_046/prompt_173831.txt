You are a professional philosopher of ethics with expertise in moral luck and in reading film as philosophical argument. Before answering, briefly brainstorm the relevant framework: Bernard Williams’s four categories of moral luck (resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, causal) and Thomas Nagel’s worry that fortune undermines moral assessment, together with one less-discussed dimension—whether chance can corrupt not only outcomes but an agent’s knowledge of her own motives, which we might call epistemic moral luck.

Then read Woody Allen’s Match Point (2005) through this lens. The film opens with a tennis-net metaphor and closes with a wish that a newborn be lucky rather than great; between these frames, Chris Wilton, a former tennis pro who has married into wealth, murders his pregnant mistress Nola Rice and her neighbor Mrs. Eastby, and escapes conviction only because evidence—specifically a stolen ring—lands near a dead addict and closes the case. Meanwhile opera and class mobility structure Chris’s self-image.

Show where each of Williams’s categories operates in the plot: the resultant luck of the ring’s trajectory, the circumstantial luck of Chris’s social position, the constitutive luck of his ambition and self-deception, and the causal luck of Nola’s pregnancy. But go further: does Allen’s use of opera and bourgeois setting introduce a structural form of circumstantial luck that standard taxonomies miss? Is Chris’s security merely resultant, or does his class position grant him epistemic moral luck—the capacity to reinterpret murder as necessary survival without confronting his own motives? Where does the tennis-net metaphor genuinely illuminate moral assessment, and where does it risk conflating causal contingency with narrative determinism? What visual repetition of nets, railings, and water suggests about whether moral luck is an exception or the hidden grammar of Chris’s world?

Be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the film to an illustration of philosophy, and do not distort the philosophical debate to fit a scene. End by stating whether Match Point ultimately vindicates the anti-luck intuition or collapses moral judgment into fortune, and be honest about where the reading breaks down—whether the film’s determinism is too neat to capture the messiness of actual moral assessment, or whether philosophy alone misses something that Allen’s imagery makes unavoidable.
