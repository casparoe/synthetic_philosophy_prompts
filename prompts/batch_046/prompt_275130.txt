Consider the institutional asymmetry of the Nordic model: the state criminalizes the purchase of sexual services but not their sale, funds exit and support programs for sellers, yet deliberately withholds standard occupational licensing, labor-contract enforcement, and workplace-health regulations from the exchange itself. Contrast this with New Zealand's decriminalization, which integrates the practice into ordinary employment and contract law, and with Germany's legalization, which licenses venues and mandates health-registration checks. What philosophical conception of consent, harm, and moral agency does this particular asymmetry presuppose? In particular, can one consistently treat the seller as lacking the contractual capacity to consent to the transaction—thereby justifying her non-criminalization and protection—while simultaneously relying on her credible testimony and cooperative agency in the prosecution of buyers? Reconstruct the implicit ontology: is the harm intrinsic to the exchange, structural in the conditions of entry, or symbolic in the commodification of bodily intimacy? Does the refusal to apply labor law presuppose that the practice is not work but a form of violence or exploitation that falls outside market regulation? And if a secular liberal society adopts this framework, does it implicitly depend on a pre-political conception of human dignity or bodily integrity that it cannot justify through the autonomous-choice framework it applies to other contracts?
