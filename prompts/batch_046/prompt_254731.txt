(1) I buy the standard equality thesis: soldiers on both sides have equal rights to kill in battle, regardless of cause.

(2) But I also think civilians facing an unjust invasion can defensively kill enemy soldiers even when those soldiers arent actively threatening them at that exact moment, as long as the invasion is systemic. I'm calling this "background liability"—probably not standard terminology.

(3) The reason I hold (1) is "agency dilution": soldiers are instruments of state decision, so they shouldnt be personally blamed for political choices they didnt make.

(4) Still, I think if a soldier genuinely knows their cause is unjust and fights anyway, they've forfeited combatant rights, but if they have reasonable belief it's just, they're protected.

(5) Finally, I think war requires "moral bookkeeping": unjust soldiers accumulate a debt that gets settled postwar through tribunals or reparations, not by changing battlefield rules.

So: doesnt (2) blow up (1)? If civilians can target unjust soldiers systemically, equality is fake. And doesnt (4) conflict with (3)—if agency is diluted, how can knowledge of injustice suddenly restore full liability?

There's also what looks like tension between (5) and (1), but I suspect its fake: bookkeeping sounds like it should affect targeting, but maybe its purely postwar?
