Two colleagues of mine have been arguing about a proposal to rename a downtown bridge currently named after a nineteenth-century industrialist who funded public libraries but also violently suppressed labor organizing. They disagree about whether the name should be kept, replaced, or retained with an explanatory plaque. Here is their exchange.

(1) The first said that retaining the name is not neutral. Public infrastructure is part of what she called the mnemonic ecology of a city: it actively frames which lives count as worthy of daily repetition. Because the bridge directs movement through space named after him, it functions as a standing endorsement that his philanthropic outcomes cancel his violent means.

(2) The second replied that this overstates the case. Bridge names are primarily navigational, not honorific. She introduced functional differentiation: the moral weight of a statue in a plaza differs from a coordinate in a transit system. Treating the bridge as a pedestal confuses wayfinding with veneration, and renaming it on moral grounds risks turning every street grid into a perpetual plebiscite on virtue.

(3) The first countered that this distinction collapses in practice. Through dedication ceremonies, historical markers, and casual repetition, navigational names undergo toponymic capture — they are gradually transformed from coordinates into honors. To pretend the bridge is still just a coordinate is willful blindness; the capture has already happened, and retention now licenses present-day power structures that echo his labor practices.

(4) The second conceded that capture is real, but argued that removal is the wrong remedy. She proposed commemorative redirection: keep the name, but officially redefine its referent so that it marks not the man but the site of historical contestation — the extraction of library funds from suppressed wages. Erasure, she said, destroys the very hook needed for moral education; without the name, the contextual narrative has no anchor.

Can you adjudicate their exchange move by move? Rate each of the four moves — (1) through (4) — from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is right about which specific point, what, if anything, both parties are missing, and whether commemorative redirection actually resolves the problem the first colleague identifies.
