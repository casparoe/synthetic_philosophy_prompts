I cover the history and philosophy of science, and I am convening a ninety-minute workshop for about fourteen graduate students—historians, philosophers, and climate researchers—on how germs, climate shifts, and volcanic events are treated as "historical actors" rather than passive background conditions, and on the perils of letting that framing collapse into environmental determinism. Design a structured in-person role-play built around the cascade following the 1815 Tambora eruption—crop failures, altered monsoon patterns, cholera transmission dynamics, and state-level policy responses—but inject a non-standard mechanism: give participants a fixed pool of "causal-explanatory tokens" that they must distribute across human institutions, atmospheric circulation patterns, and microbial networks to construct competing narratives of the same outcome. The rules should be arranged so that participants discover for themselves that allocating tokens to climate or microbes in an "actor-like" manner—treating them as having initiative rather than mere efficacy—tends to exhaust the pool and crowd out human institutional responses, yielding explanatory coherence that feels determinist, while preserving institutional agency fragments causal coherence. Assign specific roles (for example, a bureaucrat, an epidemiological modeler, an atmospheric chemist, a social historian), include a payoff or incentive structure that rewards narrative coherence but applies a hidden penalty for determinism revealed only after the first round, and provide a precise timeline for the session. The debrief should map specific moments in the token-allocation rounds onto the philosophical distinction between causal efficacy and strategic agency, should be frank about which feature of distributed-agency or occasionalist theories the token mechanism cannot reproduce—such as the absence of intentionality or the problem of backward causation—and should note what predictably goes wrong the first time this is run and how to handle it. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation request; the design should stand on its own conceptual architecture. Finally, state explicitly what would change your mind about whether this design succeeds: identify the specific failure mode in the activity, or the philosophical counter-argument about non-human agency, that would convince you that treating germs, climate, and volcanoes as historical actors is either conceptually unavoidable or fundamentally incoherent.
