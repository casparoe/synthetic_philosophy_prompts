For a feature I am writing on the endurance of classical philosophy in contemporary pedagogy, I am grading an exam response for an undergraduate course in philosophy of education. The question asked students to outline Whitehead’s three-stage rhythm of learning—romance, precision, and generalization—and then apply it to a proposed university curriculum that front-loads all technical coursework in the first two years, reserving integrative, cross-disciplinary seminars for the final year. The student was also asked to address how Whitehead’s concept of inert ideas bears on this design.

Please grade the answer below out of 20 and justify the mark point by point. You are acting as the examiner; do not signal whether you agree with the evaluation in the abstract, and do not treat this as a request for a lecture on Whitehead. Focus on the specific errors and strengths in this particular text: any conflation of stages, a mischaracterized claim about the rhythm’s structure, or a step that does not follow. Note any slips that a hurried grader might miss.

The student writes:

Whitehead describes education as moving through romance, precision, and generalization. Romance is the stage of broad, enthusiastic discovery; precision is the disciplined mastery of method and fact; generalization is the fruitful application of what has been mastered. Crucially, Whitehead insists this is a linear progression within any given subject, not a cycle—once a student reaches generalization with calculus, for instance, she does not return to romance with the same material. Applying this to the university proposal, front-loading technical courses fails because students are forced into precision before they have experienced the romantic stage specific to mathematics. Furthermore, Whitehead’s warning about inert ideas—ideas that are received but never tested through application—shows that teaching technique before integration risks making knowledge dead. Thus the interleaved model respects the rhythm, while the front-loaded model inverts it.
