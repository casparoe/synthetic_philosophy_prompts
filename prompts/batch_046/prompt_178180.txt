I am presenting a dissertation chapter to a joint philosophy-of-mind and cognitive-science seminar. My thesis is a disintegration-style eliminative materialism about propositional attitudes: beliefs and desires are not reducible to neural or computational states, but should be eliminated entirely because they do not correspond to any unified kind in brain dynamics. I argue that predictive-coding and reward-learning research reveals "belief" to fragment into heterogeneous mechanisms—episodic consolidation, statistical inference, action selection—without a shared neural signature or distinctive explanatory contribution, so folk psychology has nothing to be reduced to and should be replaced rather than relocated. The audience includes committed representationalists and functionalists who accept propositional attitudes as real but multiply implemented; philosophers of science familiar with Churchland but skeptical that neuroscience has actually produced elimination rather than redescription; and working cognitive scientists who use belief-desire language instrumentally and worry about predictive loss. They will care about the self-refutation problem, about whether implementation heterogeneity entails non-existence, and about whether my argument secretly relies on representational notions. Prepare me for the objections in the order they will arise, phrased as each group would phrase them, with for each: the substance, whether I should concede, hold, or split the difference, and the weakest point in my reply. Identify the single objection I am hoping they do not ask. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view—especially regarding whether predictive dynamics undermines representationalism—as the consensus.
