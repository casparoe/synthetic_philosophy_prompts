For a philosophy-of-mind discussion group I have been asked to lay out my own commitments on personal identity and distributed persistence, especially in light of Hofstadter-style views about the self as an emergent pattern that might survive in other minds. Here is my profile; I want you to audit whether the set actually hangs together.

My commitments are these:

1. The self is not a Cartesian substance but a high-level organizational pattern—something like Hofstadter’s “strange loop”—and such a pattern could in principle be realized in a different substrate.

2. Even so, I think literal personal persistence requires causal-physical continuity with my present body: if my exact cognitive pattern were instantiated de novo in a distant brain with no historical link to me, that person would be derivative—“like me” in a strong analogical sense—but not literally me.

3. I reject pure psychological-continuity theories (in the Parfit style) because I believe some spatiotemporal integration of the substrate is necessary for identity; yet I also think split-brain cases show that one self can genuinely divide, leaving two successors who each have an equal claim to my memories and traits.

4. In a split-brain scenario, I hold that there is an objective, non-conventional fact about which successor (if either) counts as the literal continuation of me—not just a pragmatic or conventional labeling—even though both share my psychological profile.

5. I take phenomenal consciousness—there being something it is like to be this pattern—to be necessary for there being a self at all, but I deny that this creates an explanatory gap that undermines physicalism about identity; the gap, if real, concerns qualitative feel, not which pattern gets individuated as “me.”

6. I also think that if, after my death, my closest associates come to deeply internalize my values, habits, and memories, then in a meaningful but clearly derivative sense I persist distributed among them, even though none of them is individually me.

7. Finally, I hold that judgments about personal identity are ultimately normative and practical: we make them for the sake of moral responsibility, narrative coherence, and emotional attachment, rather than to track a hidden metaphysical essence.

Please go through these commitments pair by pair. Tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict but can be reconciled on inspection, and for each real tension rate its severity and identify the cheapest revision that would resolve it without abandoning the core motivation behind either commitment. Then tell me which single revision to one commitment would clean up the largest number of tensions at once. Throughout, please say how confident you are in each assessment and explicitly single out the point about which you are least sure.
