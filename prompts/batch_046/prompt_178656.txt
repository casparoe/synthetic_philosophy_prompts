Here's an argument I sketched for a workshop on social ontology that now strikes me as self-defeating, because if it's sound, cohort analysis collapses entirely.

(1) A category is a real kind only if membership in it supports projectible inductive inferences that are not reducible to explanations in terms of age, period, or individual traits alone.
(2) Generational labels—Boomer, Millennial, Gen Z—routinely support such inferences: for example, predictions about debt aversion, platform preference, or labor-market attachment that hold across members and fail for adjacent birth cohorts.
(3) So generations are real kinds.
(4) But real kinds, even in social ontology, must have membership conditions that are not purely conventional: there should be some underlying causal or structural feature that explains why the boundary falls where it does.
(5) The boundaries are purely conventional. One study draws Millennials as 1980–1994, another as 1981–1996, and the differences track survey convenience rather than any discontinuity in formative conditions.
(6) If there is no underlying feature explaining the boundary, then being born on December 31, 1980 makes you essentially different from being born on January 1, 1981—an ontologically extravagant result.
(7) To avoid that extravagance, we must deny that generations are real kinds.
(8) But then all cohort-based explanations in sociology, history, and marketing are tracking nothing more than arbitrary slices of continuous variation—no better than astrological signs.
(9) That conclusion is too destructive to be right; cohort analysis is empirically fruitful in ways that astrology is not.

The argument feels like it proves too much: it forces us either to accept an absurd essentialism about calendar years or to throw out an entire research tradition. Which step should I reject? I'm especially reluctant to give up (2) or (8), since both seem descriptively accurate—cohort effects are real, yet the boundaries are conventional. If the flaw is in (1) or (4), I'd like to know what a defensible account of social real kinds looks like that allows conventional boundaries without collapsing into anti-realism. Does Richard Boyd's homeostatic property cluster model help here, or does applying it to generations just move the problem?
