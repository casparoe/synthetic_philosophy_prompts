She started by noting that Lemkin's original framework was much broader than the UN Convention — he included cultural, economic, and moral destruction of a group, not just physical — and that the 1948 Convention dropped cultural genocide mostly because colonial states wanted to avoid scrutiny of their own assimilationist policies. So she thinks the legal category is already poltically contaminated, and that refusing to call forced assimilation genocide is just letting that compromise dictate our moral vocabulary. I pushed back that even if law is pragmatic, genocide has a specific moral core: the intentional physical or biological destruction of a group as such. I argued that if we widen the term to cover banning languages or rewriting archives then we dilute it so badly it can't do the urgent work it needs when bodies are actually piling up. Then she introduced this hypothetical she calls the Meridian protocols — a 1983-style government program that forcibly relocates children, burns community archives, bans the minority language, and has explicit internal documents stating the intent is for the group to cease to exist within two generations, but with zero killings. She said that's precisely what Lemkin had in mind, and that refusing to name it genocide just because no one was shot is a failure of our conceptual tools. I said that's a category mistake: assimilation and cultural destruction are serious wrongs, maybe crimes against humanity, but genocide is specificaly about annihilation, and inflating the charge actually hurts victims of real genocide by making the word seem exaggerated. She came back saying I'm being circular — I'm defending the narrow UN definition even though we just agreed it was shaped by poltics. I said she was conflating legal pragmatism with conceptual precision, not everything horrific is genocide. Neither of us will budge.
