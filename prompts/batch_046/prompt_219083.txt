I serve as a senior program officer at a federal science funding agency. Every quarter I must recommend renewal, reduction, or termination for roughly fifteen established multi-laboratory research programmes—each spanning five to fifteen years—not for single project proposals. The board that approves my recommendations includes lawyers, economists, and former politicians with no scientific training, so my written justification must be readable to non-scientists, yet the criteria must be defensible to the scientists whose funding depends on them. I have about ninety minutes per dossier, no access to an external philosopher of science, and I must sign my name to every recommendation.

Turn Imre Lakatos’s methodology of scientific research programmes into an operational decision procedure I can run repeatedly. Deliver it as an ordered sequence of questions or tests—effectively a flowchart in prose—with explicit stopping conditions: where the answer forces a recommendation (renew at full funding, reduce with conditions, or terminate) and where the answer is a value judgment that the procedure must flag and hand back to me or to the board rather than disguising as technical.

The procedure must distinguish progressive from degenerating problem shifts, but refine that distinction for three recurring hard cases in my docket. First, programmes in applied interdisciplinary fields where the hard core is never explicitly articulated, so consistency checks against core commitments are impossible. Second, programmes that expand their protective belt not through internal auxiliary hypotheses but by importing theoretical machinery from an adjacent, well-established research programme; the procedure must say whether this counts as heuristic expansion or as ad hoc rescue. Third, programmes that keep generating accurate predictions but only by revising auxiliary hypotheses in ways that do not constrain future predictions—what might be called empirically progressive yet heuristically stagnant.

For each step, specify what counts as sufficient evidence, which documents I should demand from programme leadership, and where the procedure will misfire or yield a recommendation I should override on non-technical grounds. Then apply the procedure to three cases:

1. A twenty-year evolutionary psychology programme investigating social cognition. Over the last eight years, every major empirical contradiction was accommodated by adding a new modular sub-routine or context-sensitive trigger to the protective belt, with no corresponding change in predictive strategy. Novel predictions are rare and largely post-hoc.

2. A climate-system modelling consortium that has repeatedly adjusted parameterizations of cloud microphysics and ocean heat uptake in response to divergence from observations, yet has also produced three successive, genuinely novel predictions—about regional monsoon shifts and about ice-sheet feedback thresholds—that were later confirmed independently. The adjustments are technically opaque to non-specialists.

3. A behavioural-genetics programme studying gene-environment interactions in psychiatric outcomes. It has a clearly articulated hard core, generates frequent novel predictions about specific interaction effects, and has seen several confirmed. However, its predictive success depends on continuously refining phenotypic definitions and statistical controls that critics describe as post-hoc categorization. Crucially, applying your procedure here should yield a recommendation to renew at full funding—yet I find the programme’s framing of mental health outcomes ethically objectionable. I want the procedure to flag precisely where my value objection belongs and where it does not, and to say whether heuristic stagnation can ever be overridden by empirical success alone.

Take a definite position: on how cross-programme belt expansions should be weighted, on whether a degenerating heuristic can be sustained by empirical progress, and on whether any procedure of this kind can be value-neutral. Give explicit reasons rather than laying out options. Conclude with a brief, honest note on where your operationalization takes liberties with Lakatos’s original framework or depends on a contested reading of it, and state clearly what the procedure cannot do.
