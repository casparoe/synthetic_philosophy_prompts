Analyze the 1858 Linnean Society joint reading of Darwin's and Wallace's papers on natural selection as a test of the structural inevitability thesis in science, but do so through the intermediate concept of "conceptual packaging"—the claim that a discovery comprises not merely a core mechanism but an inseparable bundle of evidentiary protocols, causal narratives, disciplinary boundaries, and rhetorical address. First, map the two papers onto this packaging framework: identify the core mechanism each author proposed, the evidentiary base each invoked (geological, biogeographical, domestic breeding, or other), the disciplinary audience each addressed (geologists, naturalists, breeders), and the causal narrative each embedded the mechanism within. Second, apply the inevitability thesis: if natural selection was structurally overdetermined by the state of mid-nineteenth-century biology, should we expect convergence at the level of packaging, or only at the level of the core mechanism? Use the actual differences between the papers—particularly Wallace's more explicitly Malthusian framing and Darwin's broader synthetic scope—to test this. Third, reverse the lens: what does the episode, especially Darwin's subsequent two-decade elaboration and Wallace's eventual adoption of the Darwinian framing, reveal about the limits of the inevitability thesis? Does the fact that the "same" discovery required one specific packaging to achieve disciplinary dominance suggest that inevitability operates only at a level of abstraction too thin to be philosophically interesting, or does it instead show that packaging itself is structurally determined? Be disciplined about which features of the episode are common knowledge and which depend on contested interpretation; where the historical record is ambiguous, say so explicitly rather than forcing the theory to speak.
