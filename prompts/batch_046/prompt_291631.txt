I have been working through a cluster of modified toxin-puzzle scenarios with a colleague in our department's practical-reason seminar, and I find myself holding verdicts that I cannot quite bring into reflective equilibrium. The familiar Kavka setup is well known: you are offered a substantial reward tonight if you form a sincere intention to drink a mildly sickening but harmless toxin tomorrow morning, knowing that by tomorrow there will be no further incentive to drink and that execution will therefore be irrational. In that canonical individual case, my verdict is firm: you cannot rationally form the intention, because the anticipated absence of any future reason undermines the present possibility of a genuine commitment. I want to see whether there is a coherent principle of rational intention-formation that unifies this judgment with several less standard variants, or whether I must abandon one of my intuitions.

Here are the further cases, each with my verdict. First, a probabilistic variant: you are offered the same reward if tonight your subjective credence that you will drink tomorrow exceeds 0.9, and you are permitted to ground this credence in long-term empirical self-observation—you have a documented ninety-percent track record of following through on lucrative resolutions even when execution later becomes irrational. Tomorrow there is still no reason to drink. Here I think you can rationally hold the high credence and claim the reward, because you are reporting a predictive regularity about your own behavior rather than committing yourself to a future normative reason. Second, a contractual-enforcement variant: the conditions match the canonical case, except that tonight you may sign an irrevocable contract with a credible enforcer, such that failure to drink tomorrow triggers a catastrophic financial penalty you cannot avoid. Here I think you can rationally form the intention, since the contract supplies an independent, decisive reason for tomorrow's action. Third, a collective-interdependence variant: one hundred agents are each promised one million dollars, divided only if all one hundred actually drink tomorrow; there is public monitoring but no external legal penalty for individual defection, and each suffers only the same mild discomfort. Despite the collective structure, I think each agent still cannot rationally form the intention, because each can anticipate that tomorrow the personal reason to avoid the toxin will remain dominant regardless of others' choices. Fourth, a second-order loophole: instead of intending to drink, you intend tonight to ingest a pill that will induce tomorrow an irresistible first-order intention to drink, which you will then execute automatically; the reward requires only the second-order intention. My verdict is that this is not a genuine solution but a disguised first-order commitment, and if the direct version is irrational, so is this indirect route.

These judgments seem to hang together, but I am troubled by the tension between the probabilistic and the collective cases: if I can rationally predict my own future irrational action in the second case, why does the collective setting in the third case not generate an analogous predictive basis for forming the intention? And the contrast between the canonical case and the contractual case suggests that the presence or absence of an external tomorrow-reason is decisive, yet the probabilistic case shows that I can rationalize future action without such a reason, provided I frame it as belief rather than commitment. I would like a precise statement of the principle—or a small competing family of principles—that captures this pattern, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it, its verdicts on further variants (for instance, a scenario in which the payout is contingent not on intention but on successfully deceiving an observer about one's future behavior), and an honest accounting of which of my verdicts must be abandoned if we prefer a simpler account over one that saves every intuition at the price of complexity.
