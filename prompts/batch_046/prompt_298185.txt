Consider the institutional pattern in which government climate-cost guidelines apply a social discount rate with a near-zero pure rate of time preference for impacts extending beyond a century—treating temporal distance itself as ethically irrelevant—while the same state’s public pension funds and sovereign wealth funds are legally mandated to maximize returns using market-based discount rates that embed a substantial pure preference for present consumption and treat future retirees’ claims as heavily discounted liabilities. What philosophical commitments about pure time preference and intergenerational moral equality does this bifurcated practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value that could license both halves consistently: does it presuppose that future people have context-dependent moral standing, that pure time preference is ethically impermissible only when mediated by environmental risk, or that fiduciary and intergenerational obligations belong to incommensurable normative domains? Assess whether the practice is genuinely underdetermined—compatible with several rival theories of discounting—or whether it implicitly rules out views that deny pure time preference altogether or defend it on uniform preference-satisfaction grounds. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and specify what structural revisions to climate accounting, pension law, or both would be necessary if the state adopted a single, unified theory of pure time preference across these domains.
