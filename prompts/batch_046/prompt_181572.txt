I retired five years ago from a career in condensed-matter physics and have since been auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of science and epistemology. This semester I sit on our university’s Interdisciplinary Curriculum Review Board, which must rule on a proposal for a for-credit seminar titled “Demarcation in Practice: Three Case Studies.” The proposal—submitted by a historian of science with an otherwise solid publication record—would devote eight weeks to analyzing Fomenko’s New Chronology, the ancient-astronaut interpretation of the Nazca geoglyphs, and Plato’s account of Atlantis in the Timaeus and Critias, treating each not as credible history but as “epistemic stress tests” for falsifiability, methodological naturalism, and research-program progressivity. The pedagogical claim is that students only learn to apply demarcation criteria by attempting to apply them to theories that are seductive, internally coherent, and widely circulated outside the academy; the risk is that institutionalizing these case studies in a credit-bearing course lends them a legitimacy that undermines the very boundary the seminar claims to defend. Half the board argues this is precisely how philosophy of science should be taught—rigorously, with live examples—while the other half, including several natural scientists, worries that listing “New Chronology” on an official transcript signals institutional neutrality where none should exist, and that students may leave with a relativized sense of historical knowledge rather than a sharpened demarcation skill.

Please draft the board’s decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—for circulation before our vote next Thursday. The memo should recommend a concrete course of action with an explicit time frame for any conditions, and it should include reasoning the researchers could actually respond to rather than vague appeals to “academic standards.” It must engage seriously with the historian’s argument: that understanding why Fomenko’s statistical chronology collapses under scrutiny, or why the Nazca-line interpretations violate parsimony in ways students can feel rather than merely memorize, requires treating these arguments with enough analytical detail that students witness the failure from inside rather than from a dismissive distance. At the same time, the memo should address the strongest concern on the opposing side—namely, that the university’s imprimatur transforms pseudohistory into an object worthy of semester-long study, and that credit-bearing status risks normalizing what should be marginal. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the board needs actionable reasoning it can vote on, not a literature review. Given my background, I am particularly interested in whether the memo treats the scientific and philosophical issues symmetrically: do not invoke “the scientific method” as if it were self-explanatory, but show precisely how these three case studies illuminate or confuse the boundary you are asking us to enforce.
