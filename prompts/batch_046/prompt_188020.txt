You are a philosopher of economics and development ethics with expertise in causal explanation in history; before you begin, take two minutes to brainstorm the logical forms a single declarative sentence can take—definitional, universal causal, counterfactual-robustness, normative-attributional, and modal—so we can map them precisely onto what follows.

In a graduate seminar on entitlement theory and famine, a participant keeps returning to the line: “Famine is never a failure of food availability; it’s always a collapse of entitlement.” She uses it to dismiss any mention of drought conditions, wartime logistics breakdowns, or crop disease as causally relevant in three specific cases: the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish Great Famine, and the mortality crisis during China’s Great Leap Forward. I am convinced the slogan is doing hidden work, but I need to see the machinery exposed.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing. Present them as a numbered menu rather than selecting one. For each reading, specify exactly:

1. Its logical form—whether it defines famine, states a universal causal law, asserts counterfactual robustness, assigns moral responsibility, or makes a modal claim about all possible worlds;
2. Whether it permits food-availability declines to play any subsidiary, contributing, or triggering causal role, or whether it renders them causally inert;
3. Whether it applies with uniform force to all three cases (Bengal, Ireland, Great Leap Forward) or whether it requires case-specific amendments;
4. Whether the reading is trivially true, substantive and defensible, false, or conceptually muddled; and
5. Whether that specific reading actually licenses her move of ruling out environmental, logistical, or agricultural supply-side factors from the explanatory story.

Keep the analysis centered on the disambiguation and its verdicts, not on a general historical narrative of the three famines. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, page references, or formal citations.
