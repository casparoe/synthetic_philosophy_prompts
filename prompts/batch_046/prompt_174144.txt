I was recently appointed to an interdisciplinary review board tasked with assessing long-range research proposals outside my own specialty, and in that capacity I have noticed a recurring pattern of reasoning that I cannot name with confidence. I hope to determine whether it corresponds to an established concept in epistemology or decision theory, or whether it requires a new distinction.

The pattern is this. When a reviewer evaluates a proposal in a field she knows intimately, she tends to treat broad statistical records—say, the high base rate of failure for ambitious theoretical projects in that domain—as largely inapplicable to the case at hand. She insists that particular institutional conditions, specific methodological refinements, and the investigators' track records provide an insider perspective that legitimately overrides the aggregate data. Yet when the same reviewer turns to an unfamiliar field, she treats that field's insiders' appeals to unique local conditions with marked suspicion, regarding them as post-hoc rationalizations. There she applies the broad statistical record aggressively and treats any claim of exceptional circumstances as a failure of epistemic modesty. The boundary between legitimate contextual exemption and illegitimate special pleading appears to shift with the reviewer's proximity to the subject matter, not with any principled feature of the proposals themselves.

I have observed the same asymmetry in my own judgment: I find it far easier to grant my own disciplinary intuitions exemption from aggregate skepticism than I do to grant the same exemption to disciplines I understand only from the outside. The result is a kind of perspectival double standard in which epistemic humility is demanded of others precisely where I extend epistemic charity to myself.

Is there an established term for this specific failure mode—the selective deployment of insider-view reasoning for one's own domain combined with outsider-view skepticism toward others—particularly as it relates to debates about epistemic modesty and forecasting? How, if at all, does it differ from ordinary confirmation bias, from the Dunning-Kruger effect, or from the general tension between particularist judgment and base-rate reasoning? If there is no precise name, I would welcome a clear definition that distinguishes it from these neighbors and specifies its boundaries: for instance, does it require comparison across domains, or can it occur within a single domain when one's institutional role shifts from advocate to evaluator?
