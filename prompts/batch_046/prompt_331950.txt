I serve on my university's Committee on Institutional Memory and Public Commemoration. We must render a formal decision on a proposal for a permanent digital archive and gallery installation that narrates our institution's 180-year history through a framework its authors call "staged moral maturation." The proposal divides the past into four successive ethical paradigms—foundational exclusion, reformist paternalism, distributive neutrality, and present inclusive pluralism—and treats specific injustices embedded in that history (the acceptance of capital derived from enslaved labor, the sponsorship of eugenics research in the 1920s, the suppression of labor organizing in the 1960s) not as moral failures to be condemned, but as "developmental errors" that were functionally necessary prerequisites without which the institution could not have reached its current inclusive framework. The authors argue that this avoids both nostalgic sanitization and presentist moral condemnation, offering instead a philosophically rigorous genealogy in which moral progress is reconstructed from within rather than judged from without. Half the committee regards this as a legitimate historiographical method—analogous to recognizing that scientific error and falsification drive theoretical refinement—while the other half sees it as Whig history disguised in conceptual vocabulary: writing the past as an inevitable progression toward the present, thereby excusing serious harm by rendering it instrumental to our current values. Please draft the committee's formal opinion memo for circulation before next month's vote. It should recommend approval, rejection, or approval with concrete, revisable conditions; it should present reasoning that members of the losing faction would recognize as having engaged their strongest argument honestly; and it should be structured as an actionable institutional document rather than a general philosophical essay. In particular, address whether a functionalist account of moral development can be maintained in practice without collapsing into progress mythology, and whether an institutional narrative of this kind can ever avoid selecting and ordering past events precisely to flatter the present. Finally, state how confident you are in the recommendation you advance, single out the specific point about which you are least certain—whether the distinction between genealogical and progressive history can actually be preserved in curatorial practice, or whether any narrative framing of institutional history necessarily imports presentist teleology—and indicate what kind of historical counter-example or conceptual clarification would most shift your confidence.
