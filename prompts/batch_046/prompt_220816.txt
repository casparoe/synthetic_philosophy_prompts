As a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of mind, I have tried to formalize the challenge that Benjamin Libet's readiness-potential experiments pose for mental causation, but I seem to have derived a conclusion that proves far too much. I present the argument below with numbered premises, and I ask precisely where the error lies.

(1) In Libet's experimental paradigm, the readiness potential in the pre-supplementary motor area begins approximately 350 milliseconds before the subject reports the conscious decision or urge to move.

(2) If neural event N is a complete causal antecedent of motor event M, and conscious mental event C occurs only after N has already initiated the causal chain producing M, then C is not part of the causal chain that produces M.

(3) Therefore, in voluntary movement, the conscious decision is epiphenomenal with respect to the bodily action.

(4) Now introduce a principle of causal uniformity: whenever a mental state M supervenes on a neural process N that has already completed the functional work producing some outcome O, M is causally inert with respect to O, regardless of whether O is motor or cognitive.

(5) In conscious perceptual binding—such as the integration of color, motion, and form into a unified representation of an object—the underlying neural computation in the dorsal and ventral streams is completed before the subject reports a unified conscious percept.

(6) By (4), the unified conscious percept is therefore epiphenomenal with respect to the binding process.

(7) By parity of reasoning, any conscious mental state that supervenes on neural processes which have already executed their functional role is causally inert.

(8) Since nearly all conscious states supervene on completed neural computations, consciousness is universally epiphenomenal.

(9) Universal epiphenomenalism is incompatible with evolutionary coherence, with first-person knowledge of mental causation, and with the functional role of consciousness in guiding behavior.

(10) Hence, at least one premise in (1) through (8) must be false.

My difficulty is that I cannot identify which step fails without paying an unacceptable philosophical price. If I reject (2), I seem forced either into a non-physicalist interactionism or into a highly implausible denial that neural antecedence excludes mental causation. If I reject (4), I lose the principled basis for treating action and perception differently, yet I have no independent argument for why mental causation should be preserved in one domain but not the other. I am particularly reluctant to abandon (1), which appears empirically robust, and (9), since universal epiphenomenalism strikes me as a clear reductio. Which premise should I surrender? Furthermore, what do the standard positions—Libet's own veto-power model, the compatibilist reinterpretation of neural antecedence, and the emergentist or dual-causal account—each have to pay in order to reject that specific step?
