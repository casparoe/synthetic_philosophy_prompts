I am drafting a dissertation chapter that treats moral particularism not merely as a denial of universal principles, but as a structural claim about the topology of reasons: that the normative weight and even valence of a feature depend on its relational position within a concrete situation, and that this dependency cannot be captured by a hierarchical ordering of exception-laden rules. Before comparing particularist and generalist frameworks, I want the full specification of what a theory of moral judgment must deliver if it takes this topological claim seriously. Provide the desiderata: the phenomena it must accommodate (e.g., the reversibility of reasons across contexts, the existence of moral expertise without algorithmic decision procedures, the difference between defeating conditions and the absence of reasons altogether), the conceptual distinctions it must preserve (e.g., between context-sensitivity and arbitrariness, between holism and incommensurability), and the empirical or logical constraints it must respect (e.g., compatibility with findings in moral psychology, consistency with the possibility of moral disagreement and progress). For each requirement, give the motivating case or consideration. Flag which are genuinely non-negotiable and which are prejudices of particularist or generalist camps. Then say whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable—specifically, whether accommodating reason-reversibility together with the possibility of moral expertise forces either a hidden generalism or a non-propositional account of moral knowledge.
