I have come to think that inference to the best explanation is best reconstructed not as a single inference rule but as a family of context-sensitive selection procedures governed by explanatory-virtue pluralism. On this view, there is no common currency—Bayesian, information-theoretic, or otherwise—into which simplicity, unificatory power, predictive novelty, conservatism with respect to background knowledge, and causal-mechanical transparency can be translated. Instead, which virtue dominates in a given case depends on the specific kind of ignorance facing the inquirer: mechanistic opacity calls for transparency, law-like regularity without underlying dynamics calls for unification, and anomaly under an established framework calls for predictive novelty weighted against conservatism. Consequently, disputes over which theory is best are typically resolved not by computing an aggregate score but by identifying the contextually dominant virtue and checking whether the leading rival is deficient specifically along that dimension. I take this to preserve what is genuinely normative about scientific reasoning without collapsing it into a formal algorithm.

What would count against this position? I want the arguments, historical cases, and formal results—philosophical or scientific—that should move a defender, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. Are there well-documented episodes in which scientists genuinely traded explanatory virtues against one another using an implicit common metric, and if so, do they show pluralism is descriptive failure rather than normative insight? Could a successful unified formalization of IBE—say, within a Bayesian or minimum-message-length framework—demonstrate that the apparent irreducibility of virtues is merely an artifact of incomplete analysis? What does virtue pluralism predict that monist or algorithmic rivals do not—about how scientists ought to behave in cases of direct virtue conflict, or about which theories have historically been rejected and which retained? Which of these potential defeaters would a committed pluralist concede as genuinely damaging, and which could they absorb by appealing to further context-sensitivity or to the idea that virtues apply at different stages of inquiry? Has the view been insulated—by qualifications about family resemblance, local dominance conditions, or incommensurability of contexts—to the point where it risks unfalsifiability, and if so, is that insulation a problem for a view of this kind? Finally, if pluralism is right, should we expect persistent, principled disagreement among equally informed scientists about theory choice, and does the actual record of convergence in mature sciences count against that expectation?
