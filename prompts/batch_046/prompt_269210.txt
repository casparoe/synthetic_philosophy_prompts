Consider the analogy often invoked to defend an empirical reading of the physical Church-Turing thesis: the thesis operates like the second law of thermodynamics, and proposed hypercomputational devices stand in for Maxwell's demon. On this picture, both are contingent physical constraints that seem absolute only because we have not yet engineered the right exotic system to violate them; just as statistical mechanics dissolved the demon by showing that information-bearing entities carry entropy, so might future physics dissolve the Church-Turing boundary by revealing some physical process that computes a non-computable function. Those who reject the comparison insist that the Church-Turing thesis is not an empirical law at all but a mathematical result closer in status to Cantor's theorem, so treating it as thermodynamic smuggles in a falsifiability and physical contingency it does not actually possess.

Can you lay out the correspondence explicitly—thesis to law, hypercomputer to demon, mathematical proof to statistical-mechanical resolution, physical system to ensemble average—and then identify where the mapping breaks? In particular, distinguish the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the claim that the physical Church-Turing thesis is empirically falsifiable in the same sense as a thermodynamic law from those that are merely cosmetic. Does the analogy quietly import features specific to statistical mechanics, such as probabilistic averaging over macroscopic ensembles or the information-theoretic cost of measurement, that have no structural parallel in computability theory? Separate the breakdowns that damage the epistemic-status argument from those that do not, and say whether the conclusion—that the thesis is a contingent physical regularity rather than a mathematical boundary—survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If the thermodynamic comparison collapses, is there a more accurate analogy for capturing how a mathematical result can nevertheless carry physical weight without being a falsifiable law of nature?
