I am preparing the final session of a graduate seminar on naturalism in ethics, and we have been working through Darwin’s Descent of Man together with his private notebooks on morality and religious sentiment. Our discussion turned to a challenge that could not have been articulated to Darwin in his own lifetime: the argument that if our core moral judgments—such as that suffering is bad or that cooperation is obligatory—are better explained by their adaptive utility in ancestral social environments than by any tracking of mind-independent normative properties, then those judgments are epistemically undermined. The students observed that Darwin himself offered a thoroughly genealogical account of the moral sense, tracing it to social instincts, sympathy, and the pressure of public opinion, yet he also treated moral progress—especially the widening circle of sympathy—as a genuine achievement rather than a mere shift in tribal preference.

For this assignment, I want you to reconstruct, with philosophical precision, what Darwin’s response to this modern challenge might have been, treating it not as historical fancy but as a stress-test for the internal consistency of his naturalism. First, given Darwin’s commitment to explaining the origin of moral sentiments through natural selection, would he have accepted the further claim that this genealogy undercuts their objective authority? Or would he have insisted—perhaps analogously to his treatment of religious belief—that explaining the causal origin of a conviction is independent of assessing its justification? Second, please consider how Darwin might have reacted to applying this same reasoning to religious belief, given that he regarded the religious impulse itself as a byproduct of evolved mental faculties, without collapsing into the view that either morality or religion is therefore “mere illusion.” In framing your answer, distinguish carefully between Darwin’s actual published positions and anachronistic projection, but feel free to draw on his explicit reluctance to reduce the highest human faculties to brute utility, his agnosticism about metaphysical claims that exceed empirical reach, and his conviction that moral progress involves an expanding rather than merely parochial sphere of concern. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of secondary literature; I want a reasoned prediction grounded in Darwin’s own conceptual framework, written as if you were advising the seminar on how Darwin’s nineteenth-century naturalism might engage a twenty-first-century metaethical problem he never encountered.
