I'm trying to clarify my own view about incommensurable values and hard choices by seeing whether there's a principle behind my reactions to concrete cases. Here are my verdicts.

First, if a museum director can rescue either one trapped visitor or a singular, irreplaceable manuscript—say, the only surviving draft of an unknown philosopher's major work—she must save the visitor. But I don't think this means the manuscript was merely "less valuable"; its destruction remains a genuine tragedy, not a calculable loss on the same scale.

Second, a talented musician facing a fork between commercial success (broad reach, financial security) and avant-garde composition (artistic integrity, narrow reach) confronts options I regard as genuinely incomparable. Neither path is rationally superior; choosing one entails a real loss of the value embodied in the other, and there is no hidden "all-things-considered" metric that makes the choice correct.

Third, when a government must spend a fixed sum on either eradicating a preventable disease or preserving an endangered language with only ten remaining speakers, I think disease eradication should prevail—yet I resist saying the language is "worth less" in any common currency. The comparison feels category-mistaken, even though I still reach for one side.

Fourth, consider someone with a genetic predisposition to dementia who can take a drug that prevents cognitive decline but eliminates the capacity for deep emotional attachment. I lean toward taking the drug, on the grounds that rational agency should be protected. But this feels like trading one form of human flourishing for another in a way that resists aggregation; I cannot say the choice maximizes well-being, only that one register of value takes precedence.

Now the tension. I also think that when a factory offers essential jobs but permanently destroys a landscape held as sacred, the values at stake—economic survival and sacred preservation—are incommensurable, so there may be no correct decision, only a tragic one. At the same time, I want to say the factory should be blocked because the sacred value is "non-negotiable." But treating any value as categorically protected strikes me as a kind of fanaticism that denies the reality of other values. So I hold both that comparison is impossible and that one side should categorically win, which looks contradictory.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving domain-specific rationality, protected values, or a structural feature of tragic choice—that captures all of these verdicts? If so, what does it predict about a case I haven't listed: a hospital with one available organ must choose between two equally deserving recipients, one whose survival would preserve a unique artistic tradition and one whose survival would advance a critical scientific research program? And if no single principle saves every verdict, which one should I drop—the claim that incommensurability blocks all comparison, or the claim that some values are non-negotiable— and what does the simplified principle then say about the factory case?
