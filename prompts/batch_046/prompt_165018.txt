Write the full specification of desiderata that any adequate account of whether the brain is literally a computer must satisfy, with the case or conceptual tension behind each. Structure the list around the following requirements, adding others if needed, and for each state whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp: (i) a principled distinction between computing in Marr’s algorithmic sense and computing in a mechanistic sense, with a verdict on whether a brain can satisfy one without the other; (ii) accommodation of analog, continuous, and stochastic neural dynamics without forcing them into discrete state-transition schemas; (iii) an account of how syntactic structure relates to neural activity, including whether computation requires representation-bearing vehicles or can run over mere dynamical variables; (iv) preservation or rejection of implementation independence—whether the same computation must be realizable in non-neural substrates; (v) dissolution or acceptance of the triviality objection, i.e., whether the claim “the brain computes” risks being true of every physical system; (vi) a boundary condition for intentionality and consciousness—whether the computation thesis must explain them, bracket them, or deny their relevance; (vii) consistency with predictive-processing and active-inference frameworks that treat neural dynamics as approximate Bayesian inference rather than classical symbol manipulation. Then identify at least one subset of these desiderata that appears jointly unsatisfiable, explaining the tension precisely (for example, between strict implementation independence and analog dynamics constrained by biological morphology). Finally, score the classical symbolicist, connectionist, and mechanistic-computation positions briefly against the list—not to settle the debate, but to show which criteria do the excluding work. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating distinctions; the scoring is secondary.
