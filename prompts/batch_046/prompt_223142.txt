I'm writing a long-form feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on whether pragmatist accounts of truth can survive fine-grained scrutiny, and I've been testing my own intuitions against concrete cases to see whether they favour Peirce's convergence-at-the-end-of-inquiry model, James's practical-success criterion, or something more precise that neither captures cleanly. The pattern I notice concerns how truth is distributed across the parts and wholes of a belief-system—whether false micro-foundations can sit inside genuinely true forecasts, or whether practical success can flow upward without redeeming the specific claims that produce it—but I can't state the governing principle with any rigour.

Here are my verdicts. A meteorological ensemble predicts storm surges with life-saving accuracy, yet its parameterisations of cloud micro-physics are acknowledged by specialists to be literally false approximations; I judge the forecast genuinely true in the relevant respect, despite resting on false foundations. A traditional ecological knowledge system encodes land-management practices that have preserved biodiversity for centuries, but its embedded cosmological narrative lacks any archaeological support; I say the practical guidance is sound, yet I resist calling the cosmological claims true merely because the system works. A mathematician publishes a proof sketch containing an unnoticed gap; for fifteen years researchers build upon it, and all derived theorems survive once the gap is repaired; I maintain the original claim was not true during that interval, even though the research programme it enabled was extraordinarily fruitful. A therapist tells a depressed patient that her friends all value her, knowing they have expressed ambivalence; the belief restores her social engagement and eventually makes the statement accurate. I think the utterance was initially false, and its later verification does not retroactively make it true at the time it was spoken.

The case I keep reversing is this: a clinical researcher halts a drug trial because statistical significance falls just below the conventional threshold, despite a large effect size and a well-understood mechanism; a colleague in an identical position continues, publishes, and saves lives. Depending on whether I imagine the trial failing catastrophically or succeeding brilliantly, I judge either that the cautious researcher was epistemically correct or that the successful one was practically right, and I cannot tell whether my oscillation reveals a flaw in my underlying principle or a genuine split between two kinds of value.

Is there a principle, or a small family of candidates, that delivers all of these verdicts? For each candidate, please state precisely which clause handles which case, predict its verdict on a situation I haven't listed—say, a local navigation system that deliberately employs a flat-earth approximation and never fails within its domain—and be honest about which of my judgements, especially the clinical-trial pair I keep going back and forth on, it must overturn. Please reply in American English, though I have framed the question in British English out of habit.
