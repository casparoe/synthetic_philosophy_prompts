Write a proposal for a 6,000-word philosophy term paper (one semester) on the following conceptual problem. Lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism holds that suffering has lexical priority over pleasure, but only once it crosses a specific intensity or duration threshold; below that threshold, pleasure and suffering may be weighed against each other, or they may be treated as incommensurable altogether. The view is usually defended either by appealing to a phenomenological discontinuity—extreme suffering is not merely quantitatively worse but qualitatively different from any pleasure—or by asserting an axiological asymmetry that suffering’s badness always outweighs pleasure’s goodness above some point. I want to examine whether these two motivations are compatible, and specifically whether the threshold can be fixed in a non-arbitrary way without presupposing the very commensurability that lexical views are meant to deny.

State the question precisely: can a lexical-threshold view consistently maintain that pleasure and pain are incommensurable below the threshold (so that no rational trade-off is available) while also treating the threshold itself as the point at which commensurability suddenly becomes available (so that suffering can be said to “outweigh” all possible pleasure)? Or does the commitment to a threshold implicitly reintroduce a continuous metric that undermines the lexical claim?

Explain why existing answers leave this open. Defenders of lexical negative utilitarianism typically assert the asymmetry or invoke intuition pumps about extreme suffering, but they rarely address whether the formal structure of a lexical ordering requires a background cardinal scale; critics often attack the arbitrariness of the cutoff without asking whether incommensurability below the line makes the cutoff conceptually unstable rather than merely empirically vague.

Set out the semester’s approach. First, reconstruct the formal conditions on lexical orderings in value theory and identify what kind of measurement structure they presuppose. Second, examine phenomenological accounts of pain saturation or qualitative discontinuity to see whether they supply an internal criterion for the threshold that is independent of any cardinal comparison. Third, test both interpretations—continuous-with-threshold and genuinely discontinuous—against two constructed cases: (a) a life with massive but sub-threshold pleasure and minimal suffering, versus (b) a life with minimal pleasure and suffering just above the threshold. Say what each interpretation predicts, whether the predictions cohere with the motivation for lexical priority, and which formal or phenomenological considerations would decide between them.

Specify what counts as success: either a demonstration that the threshold can be grounded in the structure of suffering itself without smuggling in a continuous scale, or an argument that any lexical formulation must either abandon incommensurability or accept that the threshold is a pragmatic convention lacking axiological depth. Specify failure conditions: if the argument collapses into a generic defense of negative utilitarianism, if it relies on technical apparatus from social-choice theory that cannot be developed adequately in 6,000 words, or if the two cases turn out to be indistinguishable in ways that make the formal reconstruction idle.

Include an honest scope assessment. Acknowledge that this is not an empirical project and that it will not resolve broader debates about wild-animal suffering or population ethics, but argue that clarifying the conceptual architecture of lexical views is a prerequisite for applying them in those domains, and that one semester is sufficient precisely because the paper restricts itself to the logical structure of one argument rather than surveying all variants.
