Having spent a career in empirical measurement and only recently turned to normative philosophy, I find suffering-focused ethical theories formally analogous to constrained optimization problems—except that their aggregation axioms diverge sharply from the welfare-maximizing models familiar in economics. I ask you to engineer a single, realistic thought experiment—no longer than one paragraph, set in an ordinary clinical or public-health context, with no science fiction, no demonic predictors, no lottery structures, and no appeals to unnamed philosophers—to isolate precisely how three normative positions diverge on a fixed-budget allocation.

Construct the case as follows. A budget B can be directed entirely to one of two mutually exclusive programs. Program A deploys B to eliminate a severe, chronic suffering-state in a small, fixed cohort of existing individuals—say, one hundred patients—through an intervention that carries an unavoidable structural side-effect: it prevents the conception or birth of a much larger cohort of potential future individuals—say, ten thousand—who, had they existed, would have lived lives of net-positive welfare containing only a modest, unavoidable baseline of suffering. Program B forgoes the elimination protocol, allowing the ten thousand to come into existence with that baseline suffering intact, but applies B only to marginal palliative relief that slightly reduces, without eliminating, the severe suffering of the original one hundred.

The case must be designed so that negative utilitarianism—treating suffering as the sole or dominant disvalue and assigning no compensatory moral weight to happiness or to net-positive lives—clearly recommends Program A; and so that classical total utilitarianism—aggregating all welfare states with symmetric positive and negative weights—clearly recommends Program B. Then introduce a third position, call it asymmetric prioritarianism focused on suffering: it assigns extra moral weight to reducing severe suffering in existing persons, accepts the procreation asymmetry (there is reason not to create lives containing suffering, but no corresponding reason to create happy lives), yet applies a non-linear, diminishing-returns weighting to suffering reduction rather than linear aggregation. State explicitly whether this third view recommends Program A, Program B, or is genuinely indeterminate, and justify the verdict strictly from its internal principles.

Next, verify that the design meets each specification: for each theory, state the precise recommendation, identify the aggregation method or decision principle (e.g., minimization of total suffering, maximization of summed welfare, weighted prioritarian calculus), describe the relevant counterfactual worlds or alternative allocations evaluated, and explain why the verdict follows. Then identify the precise structural feature that drives the divergence—most likely the interaction between (i) the moral status of preventing suffering in existing persons versus preventing suffering by preventing existence, and (ii) the presence or absence of compensatory weight for lost potential happiness—and explain what happens to all three verdicts if that feature is removed, for instance if Program A no longer prevents any potential births, leaving only the comparison between severe-cure and mild-palliation within the same cohort.

Finally, provide an honest diagnostic note: identify which of the three theories fares worst in your scenario, and articulate the strongest objection a defender of that theory could raise—whether by challenging the side-effect framing, denying the asymmetry, disputing the aggregation weights, or attacking the clinical realism—without invoking bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or named authorities. Present the verification section with clearly marked steps, maintain formal tone throughout, and keep the scenario itself under one paragraph.
