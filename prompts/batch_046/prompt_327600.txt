I've been reading the back-and-forth on epistocracy vs democracy — Brennan's stuff, Landemore's reply, the usual suspects — and honestly it feels like it's been spinning since 2016 without anyone actually conceding a point. One side says voters are systematically ignorant so we should weight expert votes more; the other side says democracy is about equality or preventing tyranny, not about getting the 'right' policy. My hunch is that they're not even disagreeing about the same thing: Brennan is running an instrumental evaluation of decision quality, and the defenders are running a legitimacy evaluation, and they keep calling both 'democracy' like it's one concept.

But here's a weirder idea I haven't seen anyone actually center: maybe both sides are committing what I'd call the voter-level fallacy. They treat the individual ballot as the unit of epistemic analysis, but in a large-scale system the knowledge isn't in the voter at all — it's in the filtering institutions, the agenda-setting, the feedback loops that occur over election cycles. So the debate about whether the average voter knows enough might be completely missing the structural level where democracy actually works (or fails).

Is the stall really just that they're answering seperate questions — one about decision quality, one about legitimacy — or is there a hidden factual disagreement about what institutions actually do? And is my voter-level fallacy thing actually a useful distinction that could break the deadlock, or just a reframing that lets both sides keep their positions? I want a diagnosis of the disagreement structure, not a verdict on who should rule, and an honest take on whether anything — a conceptual split, an empirical result about institutional learning, whatever — could actually move this debate forward.
