As a journalist covering science and ideas, I have been trying to work out whether Ian Hacking’s idea of looping effects in psychiatric classification can be made precise enough to guide actual judgments, or whether it collapses into a vague metaphor. I find myself holding a cluster of intuitions about concrete cases, and I want to know whether they cohere, which ones I must abandon for the sake of a simpler principle, and what that principle predicts about situations I have not listed.

Here are my verdicts.

First, the sharp rise in Dissociative Identity Disorder in North America during the 1980s and 1990s. After the condition received formal recognition in the 1980 manual revision and popular media circulated vivid portraits of distinct “alters,” clinicians began seeing patients whose symptom profiles closely matched those portraits—including elaborate amnesic barriers and personality states that seemed almost theatrical. I take this as a paradigmatic looping effect: the classification supplied a script that shaped the phenomenology of distress itself.

Second, the recent surge in adult ADHD diagnoses, especially among women, following the broadening of criteria in the 2013 manual revision and widespread awareness campaigns on social media. Many newly diagnosed adults report recognizing themselves in online symptom descriptions before ever seeking evaluation. Here my verdict differs: I think this is largely the recognition of an underidentified natural kind rather than a looping creation. The underlying neurodevelopmental profile was present; the label revealed it, even if awareness accelerated identification.

Third, the 1973 decision to delist homosexuality from the psychiatric manual, implemented shortly thereafter. Once removed, the clinical phenomenon of “ego-dystonic homosexuality” lingered briefly and then effectively vanished. I do not classify this as looping. It seems to me a normative correction: the category was socially harmful and scientifically spurious, and its removal eliminated a false classification—though I grant that the prior diagnosis may itself have looped back to produce shame and distress.

Fourth, the emergence of “Social Anxiety Disorder” in the 1980 manual revision, followed closely by pharmaceutical marketing of drugs that targeted it. Prevalence estimates exploded once the diagnosis was available and campaigns framed ordinary shyness as pathology. I judge this not looping but commercial fabrication—what some call disease mongering. The sufferers are real, but the specific diagnostic shape appears sculpted by market incentives rather than stabilized through interaction between patients and the category.

Fifth, the Japanese phenomenon of hikikomori—severe, prolonged social withdrawal—first described clinically in the 1990s and now recognized globally. Some clinicians report that patients present with remarkably consistent narratives about family dynamics, withdrawal duration, and subjective justification, as if the clinical portrait had become a template for experiencing social failure. I think this is looping, but of a different kind than the first case: here the classification may have stabilized an otherwise diffuse experience into a recognizable life pattern, rather than generating elaborate internal structures.

My difficulty is that cases one, two, and five all involve diagnosis, social circulation, and changing clinical presentations, yet I classify them differently—one and five are looping, two is revelation. Meanwhile, cases three and four both involve powerful social forces—activist pressure and commercial interest—reshaping psychiatric reality, yet I treat one as legitimate correction and the other as corruption.

So my questions are these. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving the direction of causal influence, the presence of phenomenological scripting, or the stability of the underlying kind—that distinguishes genuine looping from misclassification, commercial fabrication, and simple recognition? If so, state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by each of my cases, and tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate without ad hoc amendment. If no single principle saves all five, present the two or three most plausible candidates, with the verdicts each forces me to drop and the further cases each predicts—for instance, how it would treat “pro-ana” online communities that adopt clinical eating-disorder language, or the global spread of “depression” as a self-described condition in cultures where distress was previously expressed through somatic idioms. Finally, be honest about whether my distinction between activism-driven and market-driven reclassification is defensible on this account, or whether I am merely dressing a political preference in philosophical language.
