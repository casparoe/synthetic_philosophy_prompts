In European hate-speech jurisprudence, statutes commonly criminalize expressions that denigrate or insult members of protected groups, and convictions are sustained even when no specific listener was injured, no violence was incited, and the speaker disclaims any threatening purpose. Courts justify these outcomes by appealing to an objective social meaning of the utterance—its capacity to foster a climate of hostility or to undermine the equal dignity of group members—rather than to the speaker’s subjective intent or to a direct causal link to particular harm. Enforcement typically proceeds ex officio, treating the offense as an injury to public order rather than to named individuals. At the same time, these legal systems preserve protected categories for artistic, scientific, or academic expression, which courts apply by assessing whether a work’s dominant tendency is hostile, not by isolating offensive sentences out of context. By contrast, the U.S. First Amendment framework refuses to recognize group dignity as a standalone interest sufficient to override speech protections, and it rejects content-based criminal restrictions unless they target incitement to imminent lawless action that is likely to occur.

Consider the European institutional practice in particular: treating group-targeted insult as a public-order offense prosecutable without an individual complainant; weighing objective social meaning over speaker intent; and using a dominant-tendency standard for artistic or scholarly exemptions. What philosophical commitments—about harm, interpretation, and collective moral status—must this arrangement presuppose? Must it treat social groups as bearers of dignity that is not reducible to the aggregated welfare or rights of individual members? Does it require a non-causal, climate-based conception of harm, in which speech damages a social condition rather than particular persons? Does the dominant-tendency rule for art and scholarship presuppose a holistic theory of meaning, according to which an utterance’s significance is fixed by communal or institutional interpretation rather than by compositional semantics or authorial intent? Reconstruct the implicit framework, distinguishing what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates. Ask also whether its parts are consistent: for instance, if dignity is genuinely collective and non-reducible, why do protected categories align with specific ascriptive identity groups rather than with voluntary associations, political movements, or economic classes? Finally, describe what this practice would have to become—procedurally and substantively—if its underlying commitments were replaced by an individualist, intent-centered, or strictly causal account of expressive harm.
