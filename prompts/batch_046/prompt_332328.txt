I'm a copy editor at a philosophy journal that publishes a lot on consciousness and AI, and lately we're recieveing manuscripts that keep smuggling gendered defaults into examples without noticing -- kind of like the blind spots Hofstadter's 'Person Paper' satire was mocking, except now it's hiding in technical writing about observers and engineers. I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in what philosophy of language and linguistics actually know about generic reference, markedness, and default framing (not vibes), that I can run in about fifteen minutes per paper. It should be an ordered checklist: the questions in sequence, what counts as passing each one, where I stop with a hard flag, and where I have to hand the call to an author or senior editor because it's a values judgment, not a linguistic fact. Include a small set of mechanical test sentences I can apply without overthinking, then run it on three cases: a consciousness paper describing 'the subject seeing red' with implicit male framing; an AI ethics piece talking about 'the researcher teaching the model' using masculine pronouns throughout; and one case where the procedure forces an answer I'll hate -- maybe it demands a clunky rewrite of a classic example, or it clears a passage that feels wrong to me. The procedure should also catch whether the gendered framing creates an exemplar bias that treats male experience as the canonical case for the philosophical claim, not just sloppy pronouns. Be explicit about where it misfires, what it can't settle, and which steps are really judgment calls dressed up as rules. And please seperate what's genuinely settled in this literature from what's still contested among people who study language and gender, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
