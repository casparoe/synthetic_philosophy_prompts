Public institutions—museums, universities, and civic commemorative bodies—apply a bifurcated schema to figures such as Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus: their achievements are narrated as enduring contributions that justify honor, while their moral failures are framed as temporally indexed and excused by reference to the norms of their era. Crucially, this “man of his time” exemption is achievement-gated: it is granted to figures with celebrated legacies but withheld from contemporaries who committed identical wrongs without comparable institutional contribution, and it can be revoked—witness recent shifts regarding Columbus—when the achievement itself is reinterpreted rather than when new moral information appears. What philosophical commitments about moral judgment, historical distance, and the relation between legacy and culpability does this selective practice presuppose? Must it rely on a particularist weighing of contribution against blame, a two-level distinction between public-historical significance and private moral accountability, or an epistemic thesis that makes contemporaneous norms authoritative only for the honored? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, indicate whether it is philosophically underdetermined, and say what commemorative policy would have to become if the underlying view were replaced by one that treated moral failure as non-indexed and achievement as irrelevant to culpability.
