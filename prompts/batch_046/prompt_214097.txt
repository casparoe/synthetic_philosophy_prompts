You will deliver the opening remarks for an interdisciplinary workshop titled The Boundary of Agency: Stoicism's Dichotomy of Control After Predictive Processing. The audience is divided among clinical psychologists who apply Stoic-informed cognitive behavioral methods, philosophers of mind specializing in embodied cognition and predictive processing, and leaders of modern Stoic community practice groups. The workshop's central tension — which your remarks must frame without resolving — is that the contemporary revival has reduced the ancient dichotomy of control to a popular self-management heuristic focused solely on internal judgments, whereas current research in embodied predictive processing suggests that belief formation and emotional regulation depend on continuous, graded coupling between neural, bodily, and environmental processes, thereby destabilizing the sharp internal-external boundary that the dichotomy traditionally presupposes.

Your remarks should run about eight minutes, approximately 1,100 to 1,300 words. Structurally, they must do three things. First, acknowledge the genuine clinical and communal value that practitioners and therapists derive from the dichotomy, without treating that utility as philosophically naive. Second, explain precisely why the predictive-processing challenge is not merely an empirical update but a conceptual threat to the Stoic framework: if the mechanisms underlying what we call judgment are distributed across brain-body-environment loops, then treating judgment as a purely internal domain of full control mischaracterizes the causal structure of agency. Third, propose a provisional, unconventional reframing to guide the workshop's discussions: reconceive the dichotomy not as an ontological partition between an inner sphere of sovereignty and an outer sphere of indifference, but as a normative orientation toward processes one can influence, where influence is understood as partial, graded, and distributed rather than as all-or-nothing causal control.

The speech must remain accessible to the psychologists and practitioners without alienating the philosophers. Maintain a formal, occasion-appropriate tone — welcoming, intellectually serious, and framing disagreement as generative. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, footnotes, or citations. Do not quote from ancient texts or modern scholars using quotation marks; if you invoke Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius, or contemporary researchers, paraphrase their positions without attributive quotation marks. You may refer generally to predictive processing models and embodied cognition research, but avoid naming specific publications, dates, or page numbers.
