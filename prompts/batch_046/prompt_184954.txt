I'm taking a seminar on the philosophy of technology, and we recently discussed whether artifacts themselves can embody political values rather than merely serving as neutral instruments for human intentions. My instructor brought up the claim that certain technologies have politics built into their material form, but she noted that this thesis becomes far murkier with digital infrastructures whose substrate is invisible, continuously updated, and mediated by statistical optimization rather than by fixed mechanical arrangements. For our final reflection, I want to examine how a thinker from an earlier era of technological criticism might respond to a specific contemporary case, though I'm unsure how to frame the analogy without collapsing into anachronism.

Consider Jacques Ellul, the French philosopher and sociologist of technique who wrote in the mid-to-late twentieth century and died well before autonomous machine-learning systems were embedded in municipal governance. Imagine he were presented with a city-wide traffic-flow network governed by a reinforcement-learning optimizer that adjusts signal timing, pedestrian-crossing intervals, and emergency-vehicle preemption in real time. The system's objective function—minimizing aggregate commute delay—is hard-coded into proprietary firmware that city engineers cannot audit or modify. Its training corpus consists exclusively of GPS traces from registered private vehicles, which systematically underweights pedestrian, cycling, and transit-user patterns. The result is not an explicit policy decision but a material restructuring of urban mobility: neighborhoods with low car ownership experience systematically longer waits at crossings and reduced bus frequency, because the optimizer treats non-vehicular movement as statistical noise. Would Ellul regard this as an instance of technique achieving the autonomy he described, imposing its own rational calculus upon society? Would he locate the politics in the proprietary opacity of the code, in the demographic bias of the training data, or in the very act of delegating public governance to an unaccountable optimization loop? And if he accepted that artifacts can possess politics, would he see this invisible, data-driven system as analogous to the physical megamachines he criticized, or rather as something more radical—an artifact that depoliticizes politics by disguising value-laden choices as technical necessities?

Please predict Ellul's likely position, grounding it specifically in the conceptual architecture he actually developed—particularly his distinction between technique as an autonomous, self-directing system and politics as deliberative human choice—rather than in generic anti-technology sentiment. I am not asking for a bibliography, a list of his major works, or specific page citations; I want an argument about whether this kind of opaque, algorithmic artifact fits his framework, and whether he would conclude that it carries politics in the material sense or instead represents the final dissolution of the political into pure technique.
