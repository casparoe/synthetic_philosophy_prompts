Rather than writing another analytical essay, I want to test this framework through fiction, but with a conceptual injection that forces the boundary question rather than presupposing it: imagine a near-future subglacial research station inhabited solely by synthetic minds—engineered, non-biological consciousnesses with stable autobiographical memory, long-term practical reasoning, and what appear to be autonomous interests—who oversee a breeding program for a genetically enhanced, highly social cetacean species. The welfare-maximizing protocol demands occasional culling of the cetaceans, calculated to minimize total suffering across the population; it also demands the periodic reallocation of synthetic staff—without their consent—to serve as computational substrates for the welfare calculus, on the grounds that they, like the animals, lack the precise status that triggers Kantian side-constraints. Write a short story centered on one synthetic staff member who refuses both roles, and take a definite narrative position: do the synthetic minds in your story fall under the Kantian constraint, or do they belong to the utilitarian calculus? Make this choice explicit in the plot and justify it through the character's reasoning or through the institutional response she provokes. After the story, provide a brief, explicit note identifying at least one point where your narrative takes liberties with Nozick's actual arguments—particularly regarding whether personhood in his framework requires biological humanity, rational autonomy, or moral agency in a specific technical sense—or leans on a contested interpretation of his boundary conditions.
