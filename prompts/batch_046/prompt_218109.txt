I am preparing the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on historiography and philosophical method. Rather than asking students merely to summarise the familiar objections to Whig history, I want a single essay question that forces them to work through a deliberately constructed, somewhat niche case: imagine a historian of logic who narrates the development of medieval modal theories—specifically the formal distinctions drawn by figures such as Duns Scotus and William of Ockham—as an inevitable, if halting, progression toward the possible-worlds semantics of the twentieth century. In this account, fourteenth-century debates about the signification of modal terms are treated as confused anticipations that only achieve clarity once translated into the language of accessibility relations and model structures; alternative traditions, such as those emphasising supposition theory or the autonomy of theological language, are marginalised as dead ends that modern precision has rightly superseded.

Please draft the exam question itself, together with a model answer of approximately 700 words and a detailed marking scheme. The question should require students to identify at least three distinct Whiggish interpretive moves in the narrative described above, to explain why each misrepresents the conceptual autonomy of the past rather than merely committing a factual error, and then to defend or reject the claim that some minimal teleological framing is unavoidable when writing the history of formal disciplines. The model answer should reach a clear conclusion—either that the narrative is irredeemably Whiggish or that a defensible, non-anachronistic teleology can be articulated—and should be written in British English.

The marking scheme must separate an A-grade response from a B-grade response by specifying the exact conceptual moves each must contain: for instance, an A answer should explicitly distinguish between causal influence and conceptual anticipation, should avoid importing modern semantic vocabulary into the description of medieval positions, and should address the objection that all historical narration necessarily selects for relevance in ways that resemble progress. Do not include any request for bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations; the question should be answerable by a student working without external resources.
