As a retired natural scientist who has only recently begun studying philosophy in earnest, I have come to hold a position that extends Burkean conservatism—particularly the principle often associated with Chesterton's fence, that one should not dismantle an inherited practice or institution until one understands the reason it was established—into what might be called institutional epistemology. I take it that long-standing scientific norms, including peer-review architectures, disciplinary boundaries, replication rituals, and the informal hierarchies of expertise, are not merely administrative conveniences but repositories of tacit knowledge accumulated through trial and error. On this view, reformers who tear down such structures in the name of rational transparency or efficiency risk destroying functional adaptations that no individual or committee can fully articulate or reconstruct. I believe this Burkean framework is substantially correct for scientific institutions as well as for political ones, but I want an honest assessment of how exposed the view actually is. What would have to be true for it to be wrong? Please distinguish arguments or evidence that would be decisive—such as systematic historical cases in which inherited scientific practices obstructed rather than preserved the discovery of truth—from those that would be merely awkward, such as isolated failures of conservative caution. Which underlying assumptions—about the rationality of tradition, the limits of explicit reasoning, the analogy between social and scientific institutions—are genuinely vulnerable, and what would the world have to look like if each failed? Which of these escape routes are live rather than merely logically possible? What would a committed defender of this institutional Burkeanism themselves concede as counting against the view, and have proponents qualified it into unfalsifiability? If so, is that insulation a problem for a position of this kind? I ask that you answer in American English, and I do not want bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
