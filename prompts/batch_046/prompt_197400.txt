I've been taking a course on logic and I keep coming back to Carroll's tortoise, but I think I've ended up with a kind of weird hybrid view that I haven't seen defended exactly like this. Most people read the tortoise as showing deduction needs some extra justification, or that it's circular, or that we have to stop with a primitive rule that we just accept. But I think the real lesson is more like normative trasmition failure - logical rules aren't instructions you apply step by step like a recipe, they're boundry conditions on rational thought itself. So the tortoise can't accept the inference not because he's missing a justification, but because treating modus ponens as a regulative instruction is already a category mistake, mixing up constitutive norms with regulative ones. From this I conclude the whole project of justifying deduction is a psuedo-problem, and that deductive competence should just be treated as a primitive feature of rational agency, kind of like perception.

But I'm worried I've just qualified my view into unfalsifiability. So what would actually count against this package? I want the arguments, empirical findings, or formal results that should move me, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. Are there documented cases where fully rational agents systematically fail at simple valid inferences - would that hurt me, or can I absorb it by saying they weren't really reasoning? Does my view risk collapsing into psychologism or conventionalism about logic? And be honest: have defenders of this kind of boundry-condition approach already insulated themselves so thoroughly that nothing could count, and if so is that insulation itself a problem for a view of this kind?
