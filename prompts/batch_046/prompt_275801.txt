Assume Sir Michael Dummett had lived to witness the border regimes that emerged after 2015: predictive risk-scoring algorithms for visa and asylum decisions, the UK proposal to process asylum claims offshore in Rwanda, and the growing but legally undefined category of “climate refugee”. Drawing only on his known philosophical positions—particularly his semantic anti-realism, his intuitionist rejection of bivalence for unverified claims, and his political defence of immigrants—predict his verdicts on the following three propositions. First, that algorithmic border profiling is epistemically unjust because it assigns binary exclusion based on probabilistic risk scores, thereby treating a statement that an applicant poses a threat as determinately true or false without constructive verification, violating the intuitionist constraint that a proposition lacks a truth-value until assertibly established. Second, that outsourcing asylum processing to a third state is permissible only if the originating state maintains step-by-step, verifiable accountability for each claimant rather than merely statistical oversight; otherwise it replicates the anti-realist error of treating an unobservable outcome—the claimant’s eventual welfare—as having a predetermined truth-value independent of our means of knowing it. Third, that the “climate refugee” label should be resisted not because climate harm is fictitious, but because it aggregates constructively unverifiable future harms with present, demonstrable persecution, diluting the specific moral claim of those whose suffering can be directly asserted. For each proposition, state whether Dummett would accept or reject it and defend the prediction with reasoning drawn from his known views. Then identify which of these three issues he would regard as the deepest philosophical error in current immigration practice, and explain why. Write in British English, in no more than 400 words, as one continuous paragraph without bullet points or lists.
