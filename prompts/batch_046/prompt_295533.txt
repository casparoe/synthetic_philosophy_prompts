A friend and I just argued at length about whether Fernand Braudel's longue durée eliminates human agency or merely relocates it. I want to settle this pedagogically: I'm leading a session for a reading group of curious non-historians who think "geography matters" is obvious and that Braudel is only emphasizing climate. Give me a committed teaching architecture, not a menu of options. Specify the exact order in which the three temporalities must be introduced so that the longue durée is not collapsed into background scenery, identifying the one carrying example—the millennial-scale salinization of Mesopotamian irrigation fields versus the rapid fall of specific dynasties—that makes the distinction visceral rather than abstract, and state precisely what that example must contain and omit to do its work. Map the misconceptions they will form at each stage—that long duration means inevitability, that events become epiphenomenal, that Braudel denies free will, and that geographical structures are static—and say exactly where in the sequence each should be intercepted. Propose one sharp check for understanding that distinguishes grasping the longue durée from accepting geographical determinism. Finally, declare outright which element of Braudel's Mediterranean study—its quantitative price-series method, its archival granularity, or its biographical portraits—must be left out at this level, and defend that omission. Take definite positions throughout; do not present alternative pedagogical paths.
