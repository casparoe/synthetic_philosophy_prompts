As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science, I witnessed a sharp exchange at a university seminar between two colleagues—Dr. Elena Vasquez and Dr. Marcus Webb—about whether we should include in our ontology entities that lie behind permanently inaccessible causal boundaries. They focused on black-hole interiors: Model S holds that matter collapses to a singularity, while Model R holds that quantum effects produce a rebound, yet both agree on every observable prediction outside the event horizon.

Move 1 (Vasquez): Ontological commitment should track causal accessibility. If two descriptions never diverge in any observation we could possibly make, they do not represent two distinct realities but two notational variants of one structure. To treat them as different ontologies is to multiply entities beyond explanatory necessity.

Move 2 (Webb): That confuses what we can know with what exists. If Model S and Model R describe genuinely different physical histories—one terminating in a singularity, one in a rebound—then there is a fact of the matter, even if the horizon makes it permanently inaccessible to us. Epistemic humility is not ontological erasure.

Move 3 (Vasquez): I am not claiming the interior is meaningless, only that ontology should be restricted to structures that play a predictive or causal role in the world we investigate. Otherwise we are not doing science but writing unfalsifiable metaphysics. The correct stance is agnostic: use whichever model is mathematically cleaner, but do not claim either describes the true interior.

Move 4 (Webb): But your causal-role criterion, applied consistently, would eliminate the distant past, other minds, and any entity known only by abduction. Science routinely commits to entities we never directly observe. Realism demands that we admit live possibilities weighted by theoretical virtue—such as consistency with quantum gravity—not collapse them into one because of a predictive tie.
