A colleague recently circulated a summary from an interdisciplinary seminar on mechanistic anomaly detection, and I want to check whether I should take it seriously. The summary states that, on the mechanist account of explanation—where explaining a phenomenon requires identifying the entities and activities organized to produce it—an anomaly must be defined not as a statistical outlier but as a disruption in the organized causal structure that normally generates the behavior in question. It then asserts that two systems count as sharing the same mechanism only if they exhibit identical organizational relations among their constituent parts, not merely functional equivalence or input-output correspondence. From this, the summary draws two conclusions: first, that mechanistic anomaly detection is necessarily architecture-specific, since any change in implementation changes the organizational relations and therefore the mechanism; and second, that cross-system anomaly benchmarks are conceptually incoherent, because detecting an anomaly in one architecture provides no epistemic warrant about another, even if the two are functionally equivalent.

My intuition is that this overstates the mechanist commitment and conflates metaphysical criteria for mechanism identity with the epistemic conditions for anomaly detection. But I am unsure precisely where the slide occurs. Is the claim about identical organizational relations a fair rendering of mechanist views, or does it ignore the distinction between token mechanisms and mechanism types? Does the mechanist framework actually preclude functional equivalence as a basis for cross-system comparison, or is there room for organizational similarity rather than strict identity? And does the argument against cross-system benchmarks confuse the conditions under which two systems are the same mechanism with the conditions under which an anomaly detected in one system can inform diagnostics in another? Please separate what in the summary is an accurate extension of mechanist philosophy of science, what is a subtle overstatement or conflation, and whether the normative conclusion about abandoning cross-system benchmarks survives once the summary is corrected.
