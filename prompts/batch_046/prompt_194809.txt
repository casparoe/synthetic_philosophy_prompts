My research group has been arguing over whether pervasive reliance on AI for reasoning eventually collapses understanding altogether, and one member has formalized the worry like this:

(1) To genuinely understand a proposition P, a subject must be able to reconstruct, within their own cognitive economy, the reasoning that supports P.
(2) When a subject S accepts conclusion C solely on the basis of an AI system's output, the reasoning path supporting C lies in opaque weight configurations, attention patterns, and training dynamics that S cannot access or personally reproduce.
(3) Therefore, S lacks the ability to reconstruct the supporting reasoning for C, and so S does not genuinely understand C.
(4) This applies recursively: if S tries to understand the AI itself—say, by querying it about its architecture or reasoning—then S's meta-level understanding is also mediated by the same opaque system.
(5) So S does not genuinely understand the AI either.
(6) But if S does not understand the AI, then any belief S forms through the AI—including the belief that AI dependence erodes understanding—is itself not genuinely understood.
(7) Hence, in an epistemic environment where AI mediation is pervasive, no subject genuinely understands anything, including the skeptical thesis that pervasive AI mediation destroys understanding.
(8) A thesis that undermines the conditions for its own assertion is self-defeating; therefore, the chain must break somewhere.

My colleague insists the break is obvious—just reject (1)—but I think (1) captures something real about the difference between knowing-that and knowing-why. Where exactly does the argument overreach? Identify the weakest step, explain why it initially seemed compulsory, and then lay out what each major repair costs: giving up the reconstruction requirement for understanding, allowing a form of mediated meta-understanding that escapes the loop, or accepting that widespread AI dependence produces a collective epistemic blindness that applies even to our skepticism about it.
