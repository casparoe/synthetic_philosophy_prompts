Over the past year, since I began curating my late mentor’s archive of twentieth-century comparative historiography, a suspicion I first formed in graduate school has sharpened into an argument I cannot find an exit from. The argument concerns the universality of cyclical theories of history—specifically the mechanisms described by Polybius in his account of constitutional rotation, by Ibn Khaldun in his analysis of asabiyya and dynastic decay, and by Spengler and Toynbee in their respective civilizational morphologies. I presented the following chain to a reading group last month, and I now fear it proves too much: if sound, it seems to erase the distinction between political decline and the survival of knowledge, thereby undermining the very archival continuity I am now trying to preserve. I want to know which step fails, and what rejecting it costs.

(1) In Polybius’s anacyclosis and Ibn Khaldun’s model, the cycle of regimes or dynasties is driven by one mechanism: the erosion of the group solidarity that initially made order and conquest possible.
(2) Spengler and Toynbee generalize this mechanism from politics to all high culture—art, science, philosophy—treating them as expressions of a single civilizational vitality that grows, matures, and decays.
(3) If scientific knowledge is genuinely linear and cumulative, its progress must be independent of the cyclical vitality of the civilization that hosts it.
(4) But scientific institutions—patronage networks, peer structures, archives—depend on precisely the social cohesion that the cyclical model describes; when solidarity decays, sustained inquiry collapses and records are dispersed.
(5) Therefore scientific knowledge either is not cumulative, or it is an exception to the universal cyclical law.
(6) Historical cases—such as the recovery of Greek geometry and Arabic algebraic methods after periods of institutional rupture—show that knowledge can survive civilizational decline.
(7) If knowledge survives such decline, then it is cumulative across cycles, which implies its independence from civilizational vitality.
(8) But independence contradicts (4), which asserts that scientific institutions depend on that vitality.
(9) To preserve the cyclical model without contradiction, one must deny that survival amounts to continuity: the recoveries are not transmissions but independent reinventions.
(10) Yet denying textual and conceptual continuity—for example, between Euclid’s Elements and later geometric practice—requires a revision of intellectual history so severe that it undermines the historiographic methods (including Polybius’s own reliance on documentary transmission) that make cyclical comparison possible in the first place.
(11) So either the cyclical view is false for culture and politics, or it forces a radical discontinuity thesis that destroys the evidentiary basis for historical knowledge itself.
(12) Hence the cyclical model proves too much: it undermines the conditions of its own evidence.

Which premise or inference should I reject? I would most like to preserve (1) and (6), and I am particularly reluctant to abandon (4). Is the error in (3)’s hidden assumption that linearity requires independence? Is it in (7)’s slide from survival to independence? Or is (9) a false dilemma that ignores intermediate possibilities—say, that knowledge can be transmitted through fragile, non-institutional channels? What does each alternative cost?
