I have been discussing with colleagues the scope and limits of source criticism as it extends from the practices of Herodotus and Thucydides into contemporary archaeology and paleoanthropology. The classical tradition distinguishes between knowledge acquired through direct inspection and knowledge mediated by testimony, and it insists on rigorous causal precision; modern historians apply analogous distinctions to textual and material evidence. Yet I am trying to understand a deeper conceptual issue that arises precisely at the boundary of this tradition, where the methods developed for written sources encounter a past that left no documents at all.

Please explain what happens to the logic of source criticism when it is applied not to competing written accounts, but to a distant past for which no contemporary written record exists—only material traces interpreted through present-day theoretical models. I want to understand whether the problem is primarily epistemic, in that we lack independent attestations to cross-check, or methodological, in that the critical apparatus developed for textual comparison is being misapplied to non-textual, singular traces. I am especially interested in whether injecting an auxiliary framework—treating material culture itself as a form of testimony subject to transmission decay—resolves the tension or merely relocates the circularity into the interpretation of the archaeological record. I am not asking for a bibliography or specific case studies; rather, I want an intuitive grasp of why this boundary problem threatens the coherence of historical knowledge about the deep past, and whether the distinction between observation and testimony can survive once both are mediated by a single layer of physical remains.
