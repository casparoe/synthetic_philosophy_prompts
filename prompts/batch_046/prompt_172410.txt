hey — retired biologist here, been reading philosophy of mind lately and I'm stuck on this analogy everyone uses for the extended mind thing (the Clark-Chalmers idea). They compare your brain using a notebook or smartphone for memory to a computer using an external hard drive: the storage is still part of the system, the case doesn't matter, and without the drive the computer is crippled just like Otto without his notebook. People use it to argue there's no principled line at the skull.

But in systems biology we care about continuous feedback and integration, not just functional equivalence, and a USB drive doesn't recieve continuous signals from the CPU. So can you map it out for me — what's the CPU, the bus, the drive, the file system, the user? Then where does the analogy break? Does it smuggle in functionalist assumptions that don't fit a biological brain? Is there an asymetry in trust and endorsement that the computer picture hides?

And here's the part I'm really wondering about: some folks talk about predictive processing and active inference, where cognition is error-minimization across brain-body-environment loops. Does that make the extended-mind analogy stronger, because the boundary was already fuzzy? Or weaker, because an external notebook isn't coupled via the same continuous feedback loops as a sensory-motor system — so it's just a scafold, not part of the mind? I don't want citations or a reading list, just your take on whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table.
