You are a professional philosopher of language with particular expertise in theories of vagueness, including epistemicism, supervaluationism, contextualism, and degree-theoretic approaches. A colleague is grading exam papers for an advanced seminar and asks for your help assessing one student response; please think carefully, and begin by briefly outlining the competing theoretical interpretations that could accommodate the pattern below before you commit to an evaluation.

The exam question presented the following track record from Professor M.’s seminar discussions about the predicate “bald” applied with uniform, precise scalp hair counts:

- Exactly 0 hairs: she affirms the statement “X is bald.”
- Exactly 10,000 hairs: she denies it.
- Exactly 3,500 hairs: she denies it and explicitly says this is not a borderline case.
- Exactly 4,500 hairs: she denies it but remarks that this is “right at the boundary.”
- Exactly 4,000 hairs: she refuses to say whether the statement is correct or incorrect, explaining that “our linguistic practices haven’t settled this.”

The student’s answer reads as follows:

“Professor M. is running a classic supervaluationist framework. The clear cases at 0 and 10,000 show that the predicate has determinate extensions at the extremes. At 4,000 she suspends judgment and appeals to unsettled linguistic practice; on the supervaluationist reading this is the penumbral zone, where admissible precisifications disagree, so there is simply no fact of the matter. At 3,500 she calls it false and not borderline, which means every admissible sharpening places the cutoff above 3,500. At 4,500 she also calls it false, though she notes it is right at the boundary—this just means the cutoff is likely near 4,000, with 4,500 falling just on the non-bald side of the hidden line. So the pattern reveals a sharp boundary located somewhere near 4,000, but because we don’t know exactly where, we get indeterminacy in the surrounding region. This differs from Williamson’s epistemicism, which also posits a sharp unknown boundary but denies that there is any zone without a fact of the matter; Professor M.’s explicit appeal to unsettled practice rather than personal ignorance confirms she is not an epistemicist.”

Your task is to grade this answer. Assign a specific letter or point grade (for example, B−, C+, etc.) and provide point-by-point comments that justify it. The answer should be genuinely mixed: correct about the more visible structural features of the data, but containing one or two subtle mistakes—perhaps a conflation between semantic indecision and epistemic ignorance, a misattributed theoretical label, or an inference step that does not actually follow from the premises—that a hasty grader might let slide. There should be no obvious howlers. In your comments, be precise about where the student accurately reads the professor’s pattern, where they misread the status of 4,500 hairs, and whether their characterization of the “hidden line” coheres with the view they name. Do not signal your own philosophical commitment about which theory is correct; assess only the student’s reasoning.

Finally, say explicitly what would change your mind about the grade you assign—for instance, whether a different interpretation of the professor’s remark at 4,000 hairs could render the student’s reading defensible, or whether correctly distinguishing global from local precisification spaces would alter your assessment of the mistake.

Present your assessment as a structured grading report with the grade at the top, followed by bullet-point comments addressing specific lines of reasoning.
