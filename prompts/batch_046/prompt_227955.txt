In my comparative ethics seminar, a recurring fracture has opened between classmates who treat corruption as a culture-independent moral failure and those who treat Western anti-corruption frameworks as institutionally specific instruments that misclassify embedded reciprocity—clientelism, ceremonial gift exchange, and kin-based patronage—as social pathology. Rather than continuing to talk past one another, I want to formalize the disagreement as an adversarial collaboration. Please draft the joint statement that a committed universalist and a committed contextualist could both sign after good-faith negotiation over whether Western standards for corruption are genuinely universal and what exactly counts as corruption in societies where formal institutions are weak.

The statement should begin with a numbered section of agreed empirical claims: descriptions of how gift-giving, client networks, and hiring practices actually function across the societies both sides are discussing, together with shared admissions about measurement difficulties and about the real harms that unregulated patronage can produce. This should be followed by a disagreements table in which each camp’s view is formulated in neutral language—phrased so that universalists recognize it as their own position and contextualists recognize theirs as accurate, not as strawmen—with explicit separation between normative disagreements (whether impersonal rules are morally required) and empirical disagreements (whether local reciprocity networks substitute for or undermine state capacity). Then identify the cruxes: those narrower questions whose resolution would actually move at least one side. I take two to be especially central: first, whether exchanges such as pre-contractual gifts or kin-based appointments cause net welfare harm in low-capacity states, or whether they provide functional substitutes for missing bureaucratic infrastructure; second, whether corruption necessarily requires the judgment of a disinterested third party, or whether an exchange can be corrupt even when all direct participants view it as legitimate reciprocity. For each crux, specify a concrete comparative case, institutional reform, or ethnographic study that both sides agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each side predicts—e.g., whether introducing meritocratic hiring in a particular clientelist context improves outcomes or collapses local governance.

Conclude with a boundary-conditions section: the precise institutional or cultural conditions under which the contextualist concedes that a practice has become corruption even by local norms, and the precise conditions—such as the presence of accountable oversight mechanisms—under which the universalist concedes that imposing Western transparency norms produces more harm than local reciprocity arrangements. The aim is not to adjudicate who is right, but to map exactly where the dispute is genuinely normative, where it is empirical, and where it depends on predictions about institutional failure—so that neither party can later retreat to an easier version of their claim.
