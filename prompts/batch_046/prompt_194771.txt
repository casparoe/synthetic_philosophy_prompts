hey so i'm drafting a short essay for a philosophy of collective decision-making seminar and wanted some real feedback on the argument, not just grammar. the core topic is the discursive dillema — the standard thing where a group votes on premises and then on a conclusion, and majority rules on each level can pull in opposite directions.

i want to push it into a weirder corner. imagine a committee where members don't just vote yes/no on premises; they also tag each premise with a self-assessed 'epistemic weight,' like saying 'i'm only 40% authoritative on P2 since it's outside my field.' now aggregate premises using those weights, but keep the conclusion vote as a flat one-person-one-vote. the conflict that pops up — where the weighted premise set supports one conclusion but the flat vote goes the other way — is what i'm calling the 'jurisdictional divergence problem.' it's basically the discursive dillema wearing a disguise of self-selected authority.

my proposed patch is a tiered aggregation rule: first check if there's a 'domain consensus' among members who claim high weight on a given premise; if yes, that premise binds the whole group, otherwise fall back to unweighted majority. then derive the conclusion and check consistency with the flat vote.

my questions: does this tiered rule actually resolve the contradiction, or does it just bury it inside the weighting step? is there a hidden assumption that people can honestly calibrate their own authority? and wouldn't this invite stratgic voting, where members inflate their claimed weights on premises they want to control? also, am i just reinventing some obvious variant of premise-based aggregation? please point out logical holes, especially anywhere the rule might loop back on itself.
