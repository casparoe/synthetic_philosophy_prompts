Exam question: Consider a modified version of Frank Jackson's knowledge argument in which Mary is a congenitally blind neuroscientist who, upon gaining sight at age thirty-five, can immediately identify red objects by mapping her new visual experiences onto her extensive pre-existing tactile and auditory representations of color-related properties (for example, correlating the visual experience of a warm surface with her prior conceptualization of redness). She reports no phenomenological surprise—no abrupt recognition or "aha" moment—because she treats vision as merely an additional input modality for concepts she already possessed. Does this modified case defeat Jackson's argument that complete physical knowledge leaves out phenomenal knowledge? In your answer, discuss the ability hypothesis and the phenomenal concept strategy, and assess whether Mary's seamless cross-modal integration undermines the claim that she learns something genuinely new.

Student answer: Mary's modified case does not defeat Jackson's argument. The ability hypothesis—first articulated by Jackson to explain away Mary's new knowledge—claims that she merely acquires a practical skill, such as the ability to discriminate red from green, without gaining any new propositional knowledge. On this view, her seamless cross-modal integration supports the idea that she was already conceptually equipped, so no genuine epistemic gap exists. However, this interpretation misses the point of the phenomenal concept strategy advanced by defenders of physicalism like Michael Tye and David Papineau. That strategy holds that Mary acquires a new phenomenal concept—an indexical or recognitional concept—that presents her brain state in a first-personal mode rather than in a third-personal scientific mode. Crucially, this new mode of presentation does not constitute a new physical fact about the world; it is merely a different conceptual access point to the same neural properties she already knew. Consequently, Mary's lack of surprise is fully compatible with her having gained a genuinely new concept, and this actually strengthens the physicalist position: phenomenal knowledge is not an addition to the physical ontology but a reconceptualization of it. Therefore, Jackson's claim that physical knowledge is incomplete remains intact, because Mary now possesses a concept that cannot be derived from her prior physical descriptions, even though the reality it refers to is entirely physical.
