As a journalist writing about authenticity and value, I have been collecting my own reactions to cases that blur the line between original and copy, and I want to know whether my judgments cohere into a principle or simply expose inconsistencies I should resolve.

Here are my verdicts.

First, consider art and artifacts. A painting revealed to be a perfect forgery of a Vermeer—visually identical, chemically indistinguishable, produced with period techniques—immediately loses nearly all market and aesthetic value for me once its origin is exposed. The experience of looking at it is unchanged, yet its status as an original seems essential.

Second, consider a lost ninth-century astronomical treatise, of which only one physical copy existed. After a fire destroys it, scholars work from a high-resolution digital facsimile that captures every letter, stain, and marginal correction. For the purpose of knowledge and interpretation, I treat the facsimile as almost fully adequate; the physical token's destruction is a cultural loss, but the informational content retains its authority. The facsimile is not "fake" in the damaging sense.

Third, consider a scientist's handwritten draft of a breakthrough paper: crossed-out errors, tentative equations, coffee stains, and marginal arguments with a colleague. Compared with the polished published version, I regard the draft as the more valuable historical and even epistemic object, precisely because it preserves the causal trace of reasoning.

Fourth, consider a classical statue missing an arm, restored with a clearly labeled modern marble reconstruction. The restored object is, for me, more valuable for viewing and education than the fragment alone; transparency about the addition seems to protect, not diminish, the original's value.

Fifth, consider an unfinished symphony for which only fragmented sketches survive. An AI system, trained exclusively on those fragments and on the composer's complete works, generates a completion that expert musicologists judge stylistically consistent and emotionally coherent. A renowned orchestra performs it. I feel the performance has genuine aesthetic value, yet I hesitate to grant it the specific value of being "by" the composer, even though substantial portions are literally his notation. I am unsure whether my hesitation tracks something real or is mere prejudice against machine mediation.

Sixth, consider a scientific dataset that is entirely fabricated—no experiments were run—yet the numbers happen to match true physical constants and generate correct predictions. Once fabrication is revealed, I regard the dataset as worthless for science, despite its empirical accuracy.

These judgments seem to pull in different directions. In the painting case, experiential identity is not enough; origin dominates. In the manuscript case, origin is destroyed but value largely survives through accurate reproduction. In the draft case, the imperfections and error-traces increase value. In the restoration case, adding non-original material increases value if properly marked. In the symphony case, mixing original fragments with generated material creates ambiguity I cannot resolve. In the dataset case, empirical success is irrelevant without honest causal provenance.

Is there a single principle—perhaps involving causal pedigree, transparency of intervention, or the kind of value at stake (aesthetic, epistemic, historical)—that organizes these verdicts? Or do I need separate principles for art, texts, scientific objects, and musical works? If a unified account exists, what does it predict for cases I have not listed: for instance, a museum-quality replica of an archaeological tool, made with original materials and techniques and correctly labeled; or an AI-generated painting in the exact style of a dead master, with no original fragments involved? And given your analysis, which of my six verdicts is most likely to be the outlier that should be revised?
