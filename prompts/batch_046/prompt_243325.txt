Draft the joint statement that a preservationist about collective moral memory (working in the tradition of Margalit’s ethics of remembrance) and a closure theorist defending the moral value of letting go (working in the tradition of Rieff’s case for forgetting) could both sign after a sustained, good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should have two parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims—no fewer than six—covering: the distinction between first-generation survivor testimony and later institutional archiving; the psychological and social costs of both compulsive remembrance and forced amnesia; the conditions under which a society may legitimately shift from active commemoration to historical contextualization; the difference between preserving an event’s moral presence and integrating it into a narrative of closure; the fact that descendants do not bear direct culpability for ancestral wrongs; and the claim that synthetic or AI-reconstructed testimony introduces a novel problem for both sides. Each claim must be worded so that neither camp can read its own conclusion into it.

Second, present a disagreements table with three columns—issue, preservationist formulation (worded so a preservationist would accept it as her own view), and closure-theorist formulation (worded so a closure theorist would accept it as his own view). Include exactly four issues: (i) whether the collective duty to remember weakens, transforms, or remains constant after the death of all direct survivors and witnesses; (ii) whether synthetic or AI-mediated testimony—such as reconstructed first-person narratives delivered through virtual archives—counts as a legitimate continuation of remembrance or as a morally problematic simulacrum that blocks authentic closure; (iii) whether “mnemonic latency,” in which a dormant historical grievance is politically reactivated generations later, should be treated as legitimate remembrance or as harmful anachronism; and (iv) whether intergenerational trauma transmitted primarily through institutional archives operates analogously to direct harm, or only to symbolic association, and what this implies for state-sponsored commemorative institutions.

For each disagreement, identify the crux—the precise sub-question such that settling it in one direction would move at least one party. Then, for each crux, specify one concrete case or argumentative test that both sides agree is relevant, and record in advance what each side predicts that case will reveal. One required crux concerns whether naturalness or authenticity of narrative transmission carries independent moral weight, or whether functionally equivalent reconstructed archives are sufficient. Another required crux concerns whether a post-conflict society may actively dismantle a commemorative institution—say, a state-mandated memorial museum—without moral fault once direct stakeholder communities have dissolved.

Write the entire document in a formal, publication-ready register, as if both parties intend to sign and circulate it under their own names. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citation. Do not ask for a verdict on who is right; the deliverable is the collaborative text itself, with residual disagreement rendered exact and non-distortive.
