I teach an undergrad philosophy of tech seminar, about 16 students, 90 minutes once a week. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel that artifacts have politics—not just hear Winner's argument but actually build a public commons and then realize they baked exclusion into the design without intending to. I want them to run into the "script" idea from actor-network thinking: objects prescribe user roles silently. Split the class into 3 groups of 5-6, call them planning committees for a campus plaza. Give each group 12 budget points and a paper grid. They place modules—benches, paths, lights, entrances—to maximize flow and visibility. But each module has hidden engineering specs that are really affordance traps: benches only face north, paths of width 2 force single-file, lights cover one quadrant and leave the rest dark, entrance placement creates a bottleneck near the security office. Frame these specs as neutral technical constraints, not political ones.

After 25 minutes of design, they recieve simulated usage reports: a night-shift worker who can't sit facing the cold wind, a protest group that always ends up in the fully lit quadrant visible to cameras, a wheelchair user who can't navigate the single-file path. Ask them to defend or revise their plan. Only then reveal that none of them chose domination; the politics came from the modular scripts.

Debrief: map each design choice onto the distinction between intentionally political artifacts and artifacts with unavoidable political consequences. Note that this activity works at micro-scale but can't reproduce macro-scale determinism—like a whole city being reshaped by automobile infrastructure—so you'll need to supply that part verbally. Predict what's gonna go wrong: one group will fixate on aesthetics and miss the hidden rules completely—tell me how to redirect them without giving away the trick. Include a timeline, materials list (just printed cards and paper grids), and be honest about the limitation. Write casual, like you're advising me over coffee, and don't bother with citations or a reading list.
