I am a PhD student in political theory working on conservative epistemology, and I have reconstructed what I take to be a rigorous Burkean-Chestertonian argument against institutional reform. I now worry it proves far more than I intend—specifically, that no reform is ever warranted—yet each step looks defensible in isolation. I want comments on whether the inference actually collapses, and if so, at exactly which step.

Here is the reconstructed argument:

(1) Chesterton's fence principle holds that one should not dismantle a social practice unless one understands the problem it was originally designed to solve.
(2) Burkean conservatism adds that long-standing institutions accumulate tacit functional roles—coordinating distributed expectations, absorbing conflict, encoding practical knowledge—that are not transparent to any individual observer.
(3) So any proposal to reform institution I carries an epistemic burden: the reformer must show that the reform will not destroy any load-bearing tacit function of I.
(4) For any sufficiently complex institution, there exists at least one tacit function F that remains unknown to the reformer.
(5) Therefore, for any specific proposed reform R of I, there is a particular unknown function F of I that R might destroy.
(6) If R might destroy a specific load-bearing function F, and the reformer has not demonstrated otherwise, then the reformer has failed to discharge the burden in (3).
(7) Hence no reform proposal can ever fully satisfy the epistemic conditions required to justify altering I.
(8) Consequently, institutional reform is never warranted.
(9) But this implies that even reforms eliminating practices with manifest harmful effects and no visible compensatory benefit—say, a ritual exclusion serving no apparent coordinating purpose—are unwarranted, on the grounds that some hidden function might exist.
(10) That conclusion is unacceptable; therefore at least one step must fail.

My suspicion is that the fracture lies between (4) and (5), where I slide from the general existence of unknown functions to the claim that a specific unknown function is threatened by this particular reform—what we might call a scope shift from existential quantification to a targeted risk. There may also be a modal slide in (6) between "R might destroy F" and "the reformer has not proven R does not destroy F," which subtly converts a possibility into a failed proof. But I am unsure whether fixing these saves the argument or merely relocates the problem. If the real error is in (3) itself, I need to know whether reconceiving the burden—from a negative burden (prove absence of hidden harm) to a comparative one (show expected net benefit despite residual unknowns)—destroys the Burkean position or merely refines it.

Please assess the accuracy of the reconstruction, identify the precise step or steps where the argument fails (if it does), and evaluate what abandoning each candidate premise costs the underlying conservative view. I am especially interested in whether the argument can be repaired by distinguishing first-order functional opacity (not knowing how I works) from second-order meta-functional awareness (not knowing which parts are load-bearing versus decorative).
