I'm preparing a session for a small discussion group I run at my local library — mostly parents and retired teachers with no formal philosophy background — and I'd like to guide them through John Locke's view that education is primarily the formation of bodily habit and disciplined routine before the mind is capable of genuine rational freedom. I want them to see why Locke insists that a child must first be made to submit to external rules and physical hardening — enduring cold, regular hours, and controlled discomfort — not as punishment but as the very precondition for the kind of autonomous judgment he considers true liberty. I need a teaching plan rather than the explanation itself: the precise order in which these components must be introduced so that the dependency of freedom on prior discipline feels coherent rather than authoritarian, the one concrete scenario that should carry the idea and what it must include and exclude — for example, why a story about learning to swim that centers only on conquering fear would miss Locke's point if it ignores the prior necessity of obeying an imposed structure before any internal standard exists — the misconceptions the audience will predictably form, such as the belief that Locke is merely defending a blank-slate model of the mind or advocating cruelty disguised as character-building, and exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be defused before it solidifies. I also need a single question I could ask at the end that would reveal whether they have understood that, for Locke, liberty is the result of trained self-denial rather than the absence of constraint, and I would like you to say explicitly what part of Locke's broader program — perhaps his recommendations on specific curriculum or his views on social rank — should be left out at this level and acknowledged as omitted.
