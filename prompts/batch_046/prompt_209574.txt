You are a philosopher of social science with expertise in ontology, demographic methodology, and the philosophy of statistics. Before answering, briefly map the logical structure of the argument below and identify at least two points where a hidden ambiguity—whether in quantifier scope, modal context, or the boundary conditions of a social kind—might be operating.

Here is an argument I constructed about whether generational cohorts (Millennials, Gen Z, Boomers, etc.) are real kinds or merely convenient statistical artifacts. It strikes me as suspicious because it seems to force a choice between accepting an infinite proliferation of micro-cohorts or abandoning generational analysis entirely, and I think the second horn is too costly to bite.

(1) A cohort counts as a real social kind only if membership in it explains properties of members that are not reducible to pure age effects or pure period effects—this is the ontological version of the age-period-cohort identification problem.

(2) The properties standardly attributed to Millennials—delayed household formation, elevated student-debt burdens, and platform-mediated sociality—remain statistically significant even after controlling for age and for the historical period in which the data were collected, across multiple national contexts.

(3) Therefore Millennial-ness explains these properties, satisfying the condition in (1) and establishing Millennials as a real kind.

(4) However, the explanatory mechanisms cited—broadband adoption, smartphone saturation, the 2008 financial crisis—do not align with the demographic boundaries (c. 1981–1996) used to define the cohort.

(5) Consequently, either the kind is defined by possession of the mechanism (in which case birth-year boundaries are arbitrary: some Gen Xers qualify and some nominal Millennials do not) or the kind is defined by birth years (in which case membership is causally heterogeneous and fails to explain the properties in question).

(6) If we opt for the mechanism-definition, then "generation" fragments into an uncountable set of micro-cohorts—e.g., urban Chinese youth who adopted WeChat between ages 15 and 20 during 2011–2013—each with equal claim to kindhood.

(7) Ontological parsimony should prevent such proliferation; hence the mechanism-definition is unacceptable.

(8) If we opt for the birth-year definition, then (3) is false because membership does not track the explanatory mechanism, violating (1).

(9) Therefore either we accept radical cohort-proliferation or we deny that Millennials are a real kind.

(10) But generational categories remain predictively robust in political behavior, mental-health epidemiology, and consumer economics; abandoning them entirely seems like an overreaction.

(11) So (1) must be false: a cohort can be a useful predictive category without being a real kind that solves the APC problem.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I would prefer to keep (2) and (10) intact if possible, but I am open to revising them if the cost is lower elsewhere. For whichever step you reject, explain why it initially looked plausible—particularly in light of how social kinds and cluster concepts are usually characterized in philosophy of science and demographic methodology—then price the alternatives: what does accepting the mechanism-definition actually cost us in terms of demographic practice, and what does giving up (1) cost our ontology of social groups?
