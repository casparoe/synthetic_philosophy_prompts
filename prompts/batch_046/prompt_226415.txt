I serve on the Editorial Oversight Board of our university's Center for Textual Studies, and we are required to issue a formal decision memo on a publication proposal that has divided the board along lines that map closely onto the philosophy of interpretation, though we have tried to avoid framing it that way. The proposal concerns a critical edition of the posthumous working notebooks of a major twentieth-century literary theorist whose published essays defend a rigorous form of textual autonomy. The notebooks contain extensive marginal commentary on those same essays, including explicit instructions—paraphrased here, not quoted—that future scholars should treat the notebooks as biographical curiosities rather than interpretive keys, and that the author's own intentions are irrelevant to the meaning of the published texts. At the same time, the notebooks contain the only known resolution of several long-standing ambiguities in the published essays.

The case is complicated by forensic and codicological findings indicating that roughly fifteen percent of the notebook pages contain conceptual distinctions and terminological choices that do not appear in any work published before 1990, even though the notebooks are dated to the 1970s. One faction of the board holds that these anachronistic pages are likely interpolations or forgeries and should be excluded from the edition on grounds of inauthenticity; they note that excluding them implicitly relies on a criterion of consistency with the author's prior mental states, which seems to presuppose the very intentionalism the published essays reject. A second faction argues that the author's explicit disavowal of interpretive authority over these notebooks should govern our editorial practice regardless of authenticity, meaning we should either suppress the notebooks or publish them with a clear statement that they carry no hermeneutic weight. A third faction contends that authorial intention is neither recoverable nor normatively binding, so we should publish all pages—including the anachronistic ones—without privileging either the author's instructions or concerns about psychological consistency, treating the notebooks as autonomous texts.

We need a memo addressed to the Board recommending one of the following courses: (a) publish the full notebooks with standard scholarly apparatus; (b) publish them but exclude the anachronistic pages and append an editorial statement honoring the author's disavowal of their interpretive authority; (c) publish all pages with an editorial framework that explicitly rejects authorial intention as a criterion for either inclusion or interpretation; or (d) reject publication altogether. The memo should present a clear recommendation with concrete conditions the press can implement; it should treat the strongest argument for the option you reject with intellectual honesty rather than straw-manning it; and it should address whether settling this practical editorial question requires the Board to take a definitive stand on the general philosophical dispute about whether authorial intention determines textual meaning, or whether the decision can be justified on practical or institutional grounds alone.

In addition, please conclude with a brief epistemic note: state how confident you are in the philosophical premises underlying your recommendation, and identify the specific point—whether about the recoverability of intention, the normative force of posthumous instructions, the criteria for textual authenticity, or the relation between practical editorial judgment and theoretical hermeneutics—about which you are least certain.
