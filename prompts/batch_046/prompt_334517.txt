I've been running a grad seminar on what emotions are—judgment theories, perceptual accounts, feeling theories, all the usual suspects—and there's a pattern my students keep describing that I can't quite place in the literature. Here's the concrete thing: someone feels a sudden bodily activation, heat rising and heart racing, during a tense exchange. At first they just report feeling 'wired' or 'jittery'. Then a colleague offers a framing—maybe 'this is righteous anger at an injustice' versus 'this is defensive shame about being caught out'. Once they adopt one frame, they don't just redescribe the situation; they report that the bodily feeling itself has shifted. The heat might feel like it's in the chest rather than the face, or the tension feels 'clean' rather than 'dirty'. It's not mere labeling, because the phenomenal quality of the arousal changes, but it's also not pure top-down construction, since the activation was already present and constrains which frames are available. I see analogues in clinical writing on somatic experiencing and in anthropological accounts of emotion work, but I don't know if there's a specific concept for this retroactive reshaping of the phenomenal character of bodily arousal by narrative uptake. Does it have an established name? If not, can you coin one and define it precisely, with boundary cases? In particular I want it distinguished from Schachter-Singer two-factor labeling, from Barrett-style constructed emotion theory, and from ordinary cognitive reappraisal—each gets close but misses something about the direction of influence here.

Please be carefull to seperate what's fairly settled in affective science from what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this, and don't present one camp's view as if it's the consensus.
