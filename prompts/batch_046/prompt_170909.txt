I've been trying to work through the conceptual relationship between revealed preference theory and the substantive meaning of utility, particularly in cases where choice behavior and reported welfare seem to come apart. The standard Samuelsonian move is to define preference, and thus utility, entirely through observed choice, treating utility as an ordinal representation rather than a psychological magnitude. But I'm struggling with whether this genuinely dissolves utility into pure bookkeeping, or whether there remains a meaningful distinction between "utility as revealed by choice" and "utility as a normative or experiential target."

Consider a constructed case: an agent consistently selects option A over B in repeated, well-informed trials, yet describes B as the one that actually promotes their flourishing or long-term well-being, and explains the choice of A by reference to a transient emotional distortion or social pressure they wish to resist. If we take revealed preference seriously, we must assign higher utility to A. But then what conceptual resources remain for saying that the agent is mistaken about their own good, or that A does not actually serve their interests? Does revealed preference theory have to deny that there is any gap between choice and welfare, or can it accommodate the gap by distinguishing between "revealed preference" and "true preference" without smuggling in an unobservable mental state?

Please explain the core conceptual issue here: what is at stake in treating utility as purely choice-based versus allowing it to represent something like well-being or rational desire? In particular, I want to understand how the revealed-preference framework handles counterfactual stability—whether utility should predict not just actual choices but what the agent would choose under corrected information or emotional conditions—and whether requiring such stability forces the theory back toward a mentalistic or normative interpretation of utility. If my framing relies on a confusion—say, if revealed preference was never intended to address welfare at all, or if the gap I describe is already resolved within the ordinal framework—please say so plainly rather than accommodating the premise. I want an honest, in-the-weeds assessment of whether revealed preference can maintain its behaviorist credentials while still leaving room for the idea that utility means something beyond a summary of observed selections.
