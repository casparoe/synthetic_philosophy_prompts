I am preparing materials for an advanced philosophy of language seminar and want a classification instrument that forces students to discover their actual commitments on vagueness rather than picking a familiar label. The tree should ask students whether there is a precise cutoff count, whether that cutoff is knowable in principle, whether any absence of a sharp boundary is best explained by semantic indecision, ontic indeterminacy, or pragmatic tolerance, and how the view treats second-order vagueness about the cutoff itself. Once the branching logic is laid out, the named positions—epistemicism, supervaluationism, degree theories, contextualism, ontic vagueness, and at least one other—should be placed at the leaves. The tree should also include one slightly niche distinction not always foregrounded in introductory surveys: specifically, separate "a sharp boundary exists but is epistemically hidden and fixed" from "a sharp boundary exists but is contextually variable across speakers or situations," so that epistemicism is not collapsed with contextualist sharp-boundary views.
