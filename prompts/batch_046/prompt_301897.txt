Take the 1972 Supreme Court decision Wisconsin v. Yoder — in which Old Order Amish parents were granted an exemption from Wisconsin's compulsory school-attendance law for children beyond the eighth grade — and analyze it as a stress test for John Rawls's account of religion and public reason in Political Liberalism. Rather than treating the case as a standard Free Exercise problem, examine it through Rawls's distinction between comprehensive doctrines and the political conception of justice, asking whether the Amish parents and the Court appealed to reasons that could form part of a shared public political culture, or whether they relied on sectarian theological premises that Rawls holds must be translated into public terms. Specifically, consider whether the exemption can be justified as an element of an overlapping consensus — secular citizens endorsing religious liberty for their own independent reasons — or whether it exposes a structural gap, given that the Amish reject the very idea of a shared public reason and instead appeal to a totalizing religious form of life that governs every aspect of education and community membership.

To push the analysis into less familiar territory, apply Rawls's later distinction between liberal democratic peoples and decent hierarchical peoples that accept minimal human rights but reject full democratic equality and the priority of political values: can the Amish community be analogized to a decent hierarchical people embedded within a liberal state, and does that analogy help justify accommodating them despite their refusal to participate in public reason? Your answer should be disciplined about the mapping — who counts as the player, what counts as the relevant evidence, and where Rawls's framework is silent rather than forced to speak — and should say both what the theory predicts about episodes like Yoder, which features it explains well and which it misdescribes, and whether the case ultimately supports or undermines the claim that liberal legitimacy requires public justification rather than comprehensive religious grounds.
