I've been following a long-running dispute in comparative moral philosophy—particularly in rationalist-adjacent forums where people try to operationalise culture—about whether East Asian relational ethics and Western liberal individualism actually disagree about the relation between person and community, or whether they are answering different questions with the same vocabulary. The concrete positions are familiar: the Confucian side holds that personhood is constituted by social roles and that ren is unintelligible outside a relational matrix, while the liberal side holds that rights-bearing agents possess ontological and normative priority over any collective, entering communities by contract or consent without losing identity. Both sides cite the same cross-cultural psychology on interdependent versus independent self-construal, yet derive opposite normative conclusions. The debate stalls whenever someone offers an empirical case—the migrant who severs all family ties, the autonomous agent in a state of nature, the corporation treated as a moral person—because each side reclassifies the case as either an outlier or a confirmation rather than allowing it to adjudicate. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about empirical facts, metaphysical entities, or even normative outcomes, but about what counts as an adequate explanation in moral theory: one side demands derivability from a solitary agent's reasoning (the contractarian or Kantian standard), while the other demands derivability from the structure of a pre-existing relational field. Is that right? Or is there a hidden substantive disagreement—perhaps about whether relational properties are intrinsic or extrinsic, whether community is an aggregate or an emergent entity, or about which cases count as genuine data rather than noise—that explains the persistence of the impasse despite shared evidence? I want a structural diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, not a survey of interpretations. Specifically: do the sides differ about facts, about standards of evidence, about which scenarios count as data, about starting intuitions that neither can argue for, or about answering different questions under the single name of personhood? Is the question itself ill-posed? If the disagreement is verbal or methodological, say so plainly and explain whether any distinction, formal result, empirical finding, or change of question could resolve it; if it is substantive, say what kind of progress should be expected and why it hasn't arrived. One further request: please reply in American English. And if my framing rests on a confused premise—if the debate is not actually stalled, if the explanatory-standard distinction I am drawing collapses, or if asking for this kind of diagnosis cannot be done well for this particular dispute—push back plainly rather than complying politely.
