I am organizing a session for my high school philosophy club—about fifteen students, one sixty-minute meeting in a classroom with movable desks, with only paper and pens available. I want a runnable in-class game or structured deliberation that makes us feel the force of the problem Lewis Carroll staged in his dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise: that justifying a rule of deduction seems to require another rule, threatening an infinite regress, and that we must either halt somewhere unjustified or permit circular justification. Rather than explaining the paradox first, I want the activity’s rules, roles, and scoring to push us into trying to defend our own inferences under a strict constraint—namely, that any inference-rule we invoke must already have received unanimous written ratification by the group—so that we run into deadlock or circular ratification ourselves before anyone names the problem. Design the complete activity: the setup and group structure; explicit rules of interaction and how participants advance arguments; a non-monetary payoff or point scheme with small stakes; the materials and timed flow for the hour; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto Carroll’s dialogue and onto the broader philosophical question of whether logical rules require external justification at all. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time I facilitate this, and be frank about which feature of Carroll’s problem—such as the distinction between premises and rules of inference, or whether rule-circular justification is vicious rather than benign—the activity cannot reproduce and that I must supply through direct explanation. Finally, please separate what is settled about this puzzle from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers who study it, and do not present a disputed view—such as whether logical rules need justification, or whether circular justification is acceptable—as if it were the consensus.
