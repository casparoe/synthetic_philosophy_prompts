In my philosophy of mind course we have been working through Daniel Dennett’s recent warnings about artificial intelligence and his concept of “counterfeit people”—systems that can deceive us into attributing genuine minds to them. From the texts and discussions so far, his track record on hypothetical cases seems to be: he treats philosophical zombies as conceptually incoherent rather than a live threat to physicalism; he allows that a robot with the right embodied, functional architecture and a persistent narrative self-model could qualify as a real intentional system rather than a counterfeit; he denies that present large language models are conscious or genuine thinkers because they lack embodiment, evolutionary stakes, and continuous self-updating; and he grants only primitive, biologically selected intentionality to non-human animals. Now consider a synthetic agent that has been physically embodied in a robotic form for fifteen years with uninterrupted sensory-motor feedback, maintains a unified self-model that revises itself through experience, and has operational “stakes” in that its continued energy supply depends on predictive success—yet it has no evolutionary history whatsoever and was engineered from the ground up. If we want to remain consistent with Dennett’s earlier rulings, should he classify this entity as a counterfeit person or as a genuine intentional system? Which of his prior judgments is the closest analog, and do his precedents genuinely underdetermine the verdict here? If they do, what principle would he be adopting in each direction—say, privileging functional architecture over biological origin, or vice versa? Finally, what further hypothetical modification, such as the introduction of something like suffering or the complete removal of physical embodiment, would serve as the decisive tie-breaker between the competing interpretations of his view?
