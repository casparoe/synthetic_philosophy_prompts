I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks for an interdisciplinary workshop I am co-organizing with colleagues from law and philosophy, titled Ever-Closer Union or Ever-Farther Consent? Sovereignty and the Democratic Deficit at Fifty. The audience is deliberately split: roughly half are EU constitutional lawyers and democratic theorists who will immediately recognize references to pooled sovereignty, multi-level governance, and the authorization gap; the other half are university administrators, foundation officers, and graduate students from unrelated fields who need the argument to stand on its own without specialized jargon. I want the speech to function as genuine philosophical work rather than ceremonial filler. Approach this as a senior constitutional philosopher with expertise in democratic theory: begin by briefly brainstorming the structural tension you intend to exploit, specifically the idea that the European Union's foundational commitment to ever closer union operates not merely as a policy aspiration but as a teleological framing of sovereignty—one that treats democratic authorization as a provisional obstacle to be progressively superseded rather than as a boundary condition that constrains integration. To organize your remarks, introduce and develop a conceptual distinction of your own design, such as diachronic authorization—consent given to an open-ended, evolutionary process of competence transfer—versus synchronic mandate—explicit, discrete approval of each institutional change by a determinate demos. Use this distinction to reframe the democratic deficit: not as a temporary representational shortfall that can be corrected by adding more parliamentary oversight, but as a structural asymmetry between the mode of sovereignty exercised by member states and the mode implicitly presupposed by the integration process itself. The speech should run about ten minutes, be written in a polished, orally deliverable register, and must navigate carefully between the experts and the non-specialists—so avoid naming specific scholars, do not provide bibliographies or citations, and do not invoke highly obscure theoretical frameworks. Structure it with a clear arc: open with the practical puzzle of why further integration often feels legitimate even when citizens reject specific treaty changes in referenda; develop the conceptual distinction and apply it to the sovereignty problem; and close with a single shared question that both halves of the room can genuinely hold, something about whether a political union that constitutionally requires ever closer union can ever achieve democratic closure. Think step-by-step about how each move serves both audiences, and begin with a brief outline of the three sections.
