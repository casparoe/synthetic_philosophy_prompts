Hey, I'm a backend engineer with zero formal philosophy background, but I've been reading Pascal's fragments about justice and force because they weirdly remind me of how we design distributed systems. He seems to say justice without force is powerless, and force without justice is dangerous, so they have to be tied together. He also says people obey laws mainly because they're established, not because they're truly just—since pure justice is too ambiguous and applying it everywhere would overturn all custom. I tried to turn this into a clean argument and now it proves way too much:

(1) Justice is only effective when paired with force, and force is only legitimate when paired with justice.
(2) So any rule that combines established convention with enforced power satisfies both conditions and is therefore justified.
(3) In distributed systems, a blockchain protocol's rules are enforced by the code itself (force) and established by adoption and hash power (convention/custom).
(4) By Pascal's reasoning, once a protocol achieves majority enforcement, its rules become just—even if they censor users or drain funds—because they have both elements.
(5) The same applies to state law: any enforced law is just by definition, and any unenforced rule isn't justice at all since it's powerless.
(6) That means there's literally no room for an unjust law; if it has teeth it's just, and if it doesn't it doesn't count.
(7) But that's just might-makes-right with a fancy french accent, which can't be what he meant.

Which step is the bad one? I really want to keep (1) because in system design rules need enforcement to matter, and raw coercion needs some legitmacy to not be chaos. But I don't want to defend every enforced protocol or law as automatically just. Is the slip in (2) where I turn "needs pairing" into "presence of both equals justification"? Or am I mixing up Pascal's description of why people actually obey with a normative claim about what's right? What's the cost of fixing it without losing (1)?
