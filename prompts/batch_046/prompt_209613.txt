Read the museum-confrontation sequence in Black Panther through the philosophy of colonial restitution and museum ethics, with particular attention to whether the return of looted artifacts—exemplified in real-world debates over the Benin Bronzes and the Parthenon Marbles—constitutes corrective justice for historical theft or an assertion of present cultural sovereignty. Focus on Killmonger's exchange with the curator: does the scene present Western curatorship as a continuation of colonial domination through misrecognition and display, or does it separate the justice of restitution from the violence of his revolutionary method? Consider whether Wakanda's concealed technological superiority introduces a philosophical complication absent from standard restitution debates—if the artifacts are not merely stolen heritage but misidentified representations of a living, advanced culture—does this shift the moral claim from reparation to epistemic correction? I want a reading that distinguishes restitution as retributive correction from restitution as nationalist identity politics, asks where the film's sympathy for Killmonger's grievance conflicts with its narrative rejection of his means, and identifies what the scene dramatizes about the relationship between possession, misrepresentation, and power that legal arguments about provenance and ownership alone fail to capture—without reducing the film to an allegory for either side.
