I defend a lineage-autonomy definition of life: an entity is alive if and only if it is an active node in a continuous causal chain that dissipates energy to maintain organizational closure through self-correcting boundary regulation, where the correction is not externally programmed. Viruses fail because they borrow the host’s loop; synthetic protocells designed de novo with metabolic cycles but no evolutionary ancestry are borderline; and dormant spores or tardigrades in cryptobiosis are not alive during suspension, since the regulatory loop is halted, leaving only a reactivatable potential rather than an executing process. Play it straight: what would have to be true for this view to be wrong? Distinguish decisive defeaters—such as a virus that autonomously corrects its own boundary conditions, or a dormant organism shown to maintain subthreshold regulatory dynamics—from merely awkward cases. What should defenders of this view themselves concede as damaging? Does the dormancy distinction risk collapsing into unfalsifiability, and is that insulation a virtue or a defect for a definition of life? Give an honest assessment of how exposed the position actually is.
