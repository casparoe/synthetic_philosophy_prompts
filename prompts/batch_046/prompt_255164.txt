I am trying to locate the flaw in a reductio my colleague offered against Kant's Formula of Universal Law. The argument proceeds: (1) FUL requires that I be able to consistently will the maxim of my action as universal law without contradiction. (2) Every particular action can be subsumed under maxims of varying specificity—consider "lying when it serves my immediate interest" versus "lying only to prevent an imminent catastrophe that I alone can avert." (3) At high specificity, universalization rarely generates contradiction in conception or in will, since the conditions under which the maxim applies may be jointly impossible or harmlessly rare. (4) At high generality, universalization typically produces contradiction. (5) Kant provides no non-circular algorithm for selecting the correct level of description: he characterizes a maxim as containing an end and a form of action, but both are defined within the maxim itself. (6) Consequently, for any given act one can construct both a maxim that passes FUL and one that fails, merely by adjusting descriptive granularity. (7) Hence FUL yields no determinate verdict; it either permits everything or prohibits everything, depending on an arbitrary choice that Kant's anti-empirical moral framework cannot legitimate. (8) My colleague concludes that this is not a technical omission but a structural failure—the test requires a privileged individuation of maxims that pure practical reason cannot supply. I feel the slip occurs somewhere between (3) and (7)—perhaps in treating the absence of an explicit decision procedure as the absence of any rational constraint—but I cannot identify the precise step or price the rejection. Which premise or inference should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost for a Kantian account?
