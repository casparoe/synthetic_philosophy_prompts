I'm taking an upper-division seminar on critical theory and modernity—about fourteen of us, meeting for ninety minutes once a week—and we have been working through the problem of whether the Enlightenment is best understood as an emancipatory project or as a structure of domination that disguises control as liberation. In conversation after class, a few of us kept circling a specific tension that standard critiques of colonialism or biopower don't quite capture on their own: what I'm calling the retroactive framing problem, in which Enlightenment institutions do not merely suppress alternative forms of life but introduce universal evaluative metrics—efficiency, autonomy reconceived as individual choice, reason as calculability—that then make those alternative forms appear not simply different but demonstrably deficient by standards the framework itself authored. I want to stage this idea as an in-class activity rather than present it in a lecture, because I think the force of the problem only becomes visceral when participants themselves are forced to translate heterogeneous practices into a single metric and discover, through their own adjudication, that the translation strips away exactly what made the practices valuable. The group should be divided into rotating roles—Reformers tasked with implementing one universal wellbeing metric, Local Councils operating with non-commensurable internal practices, and Adjudicators who must resolve disputes using only the metric available to them—so that the domination emerges structurally from the rules rather than from any malicious intent.

Please design a fully runnable game for this setting: specify the group size and time allocation within the ninety minutes, the printed materials needed (role cards, a simple scoring sheet, perhaps a brief case description for each round), the rules and payoff structure that create real small stakes without requiring money—maybe collective credit for the Reformers if adoption is widespread, or protected status for Councils if they can defend themselves in the metric's terms, with Adjudicators earning points for consistent rulings. The timeline should move from an initial round where Councils describe their practices in their own language, through a translation round where Reformers codify those practices into the metric, to a dispute round where Adjudicators must rule, and finally to a revelation round where Councils see the translated versions of themselves. Include a debrief that maps each of these moments onto parts of the retroactive framing idea—translation as epistemic violence, adjudication as authorization of the metric, and the revelation as the moment when resistance begins to appear irrational within the very language it must use to defend itself. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time—likely that students identify too quickly with the Reformers, or that the Adjudicators collapse into relativism rather than feeling the coercive pressure of the metric—and how to handle it with a structural constraint rather than a facilitator intervention. Finally, note honestly which feature of the idea this activity cannot reproduce—probably the historical specificity and violence of actual colonial administration, which must be supplied verbally in the debrief—and confirm that the deliverable requires no bibliographies, citations, or reference to niche theoretical texts that participants could not access without external research.
