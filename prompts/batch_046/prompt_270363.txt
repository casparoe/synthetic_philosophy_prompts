I have just come out of a long and somewhat heated argument with a close friend about whether knowing how to do something can ever be exhaustively analyzed in terms of knowing that certain propositions are true. Rather than rehearse the familiar Rylean and intellectualist positions, I want to examine a more specific intermediate case that arose in our discussion: the skill of revising one's beliefs when confronted with contradictory evidence. My friend maintained that this is unambiguously a form of knowing-that — it requires grasping that consistency norms apply, that Bayes' rule governs rational updating, and that particular hypotheses entail particular predictions. I argued that there remains an irreducible skill dimension: knowing when to suspend judgment rather than update, recognizing the characteristic feel of an ad hoc theoretical rescue, and calibrating one's own cognitive biases in real time without explicit computation. To organize my thinking, I am drafting a short essay defending what I call epistemic know-how — a second-order procedural competence that regulates first-order belief formation, distinct from both pure embodied ability and pure propositional knowledge. The essay proposes three defining markers. First, it resists full articulation: one cannot specify in advance every contextual condition that calls for suspension of judgment. Second, it improves through practice in ways that do not reduce to acquiring more true beliefs about reasoning. Third, and most controversially, it can be present even when the agent holds false explicit theories about cognition — for instance, a person who endorses a flawed explicit heuristic yet possesses a reliable intuitive nose for weak arguments. I would appreciate focused comments on the coherence of this framing. Specifically: does this middle category genuinely avoid collapsing back into either standard anti-intellectualism or standard intellectualism? Is the third marker genuinely coherent, or does reliable epistemic skill necessarily require at least implicitly correct propositional knowledge? And is the first marker overstated, given that we can often teach reasoning strategies through explicit rules? Please evaluate conceptual clarity and internal consistency; I am not looking for a bibliography or literature survey, but rather for direct philosophical engagement with the argument as sketched.
