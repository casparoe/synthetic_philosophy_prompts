I have just finished a rather long and heated argument with a close friend about distributive justice, and I should like to know whether the package of views I defended is genuinely coherent or whether I am helping myself to incompatible premises. Here is my position, stated as clearly as I can.

First, I endorse prioritarianism in its weighted-utilitarian form: moral benefits carry weights that are a convex, strictly decreasing function of the recipient's absolute well-being level, so that the worse off someone is, the greater the moral urgency of improving their situation. Second, I reject the leveling-down objection entirely: I deny that equality possesses any intrinsic value, and I maintain that destroying a surplus enjoyed only by the well-off in order to reduce a gap, without making anyone better off, is not merely inefficient but morally pointless. Third, I hold that prioritarian evaluation should be applied ex post to realised outcomes rather than ex ante to prospects: when we judge whether a policy or action was just, we weight the actual welfare levels that resulted. Fourth, despite this, I think that rational decision-making under risk ought to employ an ex ante prioritarian criterion, weighting each person's expected welfare according to their prior expected well-being at the point of choice. Fifth, I accept a sufficiency-modified version of the view: there exists a threshold of adequate well-being below which priority becomes extreme, but once everyone is comfortably above it, further distributive concerns diminish sharply and efficiency takes over. Sixth, I apply all of this intergenerationally, which means I believe we ought to be willing to impose substantial present costs on well-off current generations in order to prevent catastrophic falls below that threshold for future generations.

My friend argued that commitments three and four are flatly contradictory, that commitment five smuggles an egalitarian premise back in despite my rejection of it in commitment two, and that commitment six either collapses into standard prioritarianism without needing the threshold or else requires a hidden premise about intergenerational impartiality that I have not defended. I am unsure which of these criticisms land. Could you audit the set for me? Please write your answer in British English. For each pair of commitments, indicate whether the tension is genuine or merely apparent, rank the real tensions by severity, specify what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me philosophically, and pay particular attention to whether the ex post versus ex ante split is defensible, whether the sufficiency threshold is redundant given the convex weighting function, and whether my intergenerational conclusion actually follows from the preceding premises or needs an additional argument.
