I am preparing the final exam for an upper-level metaphysics seminar on properties, causation, and modality, and I want one essay question that probes students' grasp of dispositions, finks, and masks without allowing them to earn marks by simply reciting the standard taxonomy. The exam runs three hours; this particular essay should be designed for a focused 50-minute response and will carry 40 percent of the total exam grade. Rather than asking for bare definitions, I want the question to present a constructed conceptual puzzle—what I will call a "reflexively masked fink"—in which an object possesses a base property B that ordinarily grounds disposition D, yet B is also constitutionally coupled with an internal interference mechanism that activates precisely when the stimulus conditions for D obtain. Crucially, the mechanism does not destroy, alter, or replace B; B remains fully present throughout the interaction. The essay should ask students to determine whether this is best analyzed as (i) a genuinely present disposition that is merely masked or antidoted, (ii) a case in which the disposition was never genuinely present because B is essentially finkish, or (iii) a distinctive failure of the simple counterfactual conditional analysis of dispositions. They must explain why the standard distinction between the removal of a disposition and the interference with its manifestation becomes unstable under this reflexive coupling, and they should address whether the argument that finkish cases undermine naive conditional analyses can be extended here without collapsing the boundary between finks and masks altogether.

Please write the exam question with explicit instructions about scope and length, followed by a model answer of approximately 600 words that takes a clear position and works through the ambiguity in the base-property description, and then a marking scheme that separates an A answer—which must identify the reflexive structure, argue for one of the three options with explicit reference to the conditional analysis, and explain why the other two mischaracterize the relation between B and D—from a B answer, which correctly describes finkish and masked dispositions in general terms but applies them mechanically without resolving the specific conceptual tension introduced by the reflexive coupling. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the question and answer should stand on conceptual reasoning alone.
