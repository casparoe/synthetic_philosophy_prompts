In grading essays for our advanced seminar on the philosophy of religion, I have been asked to assess a response to an exam question that asks how Wittgenstein understands religious belief in light of his rejection of evidential justification, and where exactly his view sits between logical-positivist dismissal and simple fideism. The course has spent considerable time on grammatical investigations of religious language, emphasizing that religious commitment is better understood through the distinction between belief-that and belief-in, and we have discussed Wittgenstein’s comparison of deep religious conviction to being held captive by a picture rather than to harboring a private emotional state. The student’s answer reads as follows:

“Wittgenstein rejects any evidential basis for religious belief, which shows that he regards it as irrational when measured against scientific norms. He argues that religious discourse operates within its own language-game, completely separate from empirical science, so religious propositions do not describe states of affairs but merely express inner feelings or personal commitments. Because of this separation, religious belief is purely subjective and cannot be challenged by rational argument from outside the belief system. Nevertheless, Wittgenstein recognizes that religion is woven into a form of life, giving it practical coherence and guiding behavior, so it is not arbitrary or meaningless. In the end, his position amounts to a kind of fideism: belief is meaningful within its own practice but lacks any objective rational standing.”

Please assign a specific grade on a 0–100 scale (with a letter equivalent if you wish) and provide point-by-point comments that justify it. In your assessment, acknowledge where the student correctly captures the separation of religious and scientific language-games and the embedding of belief in a form of life, but also identify the more subtle errors: the misapplication of scientific norms of rationality to religious belief, the reduction of religious language to the expression of subjective feeling rather than to a practice involving judgment and orientation, the false claim that belief is therefore immune to all rational criticism, and the misattribution of fideism. Additionally, note whether the answer’s conclusion that Wittgenstein offers “no rational standing” survives once these disanalogies are corrected, and indicate what a more precise formulation—one that respects the grammatical investigation of belief-in—would look like.
