I am preparing a seminar discussion on the boundary between hermeneutics and institutional authority, and I keep returning to Galileo’s 1633 trial as more than a stock example of science versus religion. I would like you to analyze the trial—specifically the 1616 decree of the Congregation of the Index and the 1633 sentence requiring Galileo to abjure heliocentrism—through the precise lens of Galileo’s own scriptural hermeneutic, as he developed it in his 1615 letter to Christina of Lorraine and in his later writings on the metaphor of the two books. Treat his framework with full seriousness: the distinction between passages that are demonstratively established by natural philosophy and those that are accommodated to common understanding; the claim that the Holy Spirit intends to teach salvation rather than cosmology; and his implicit hierarchy in which a demonstrative physical conclusion should govern the interpretation of an ambiguous biblical text rather than vice versa. Apply this not merely to defend Galileo, but as an analytical grid: how does his hermeneutic classify the Church’s official interpretation of Joshua 10:12 and Psalm 104? Does it treat that interpretation as a legitimate but corrigible error, or as a category mistake—an illegitimate extension of scriptural authority into demonstrative natural knowledge?

Then turn the case back on the theory. The 1616 decree did not merely disagree with Galileo’s cosmology; it classified heliocentrism as formally heretical and forbade its defense, thereby treating a physical proposition as an article of faith. Can Galileo’s accommodationism account for this institutional move, or does his framework presuppose an epistemic individualism in which any rational reader can correct interpretation by direct appeal to the Book of Nature? Where is his theory silent—for instance, on the authority of conciliar or papal interpretation, on the problem of peer disagreement when one peer holds institutional power, or on the distinction between probabilistic and demonstrative arguments in theology itself? I want an honest account of what the episode confirms about accommodationist hermeneutics, what it reveals as an unanticipated anomaly, and where the mapping between Galileo’s categories and the actual charges at trial breaks down rather than being forced to fit. Please be disciplined about saying where the theory is silent rather than making it speak.
