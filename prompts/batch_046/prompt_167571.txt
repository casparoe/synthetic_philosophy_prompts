I'm in a seminar on political obligation and civil disobedience, and we recently got stuck on whether citizens have a pro tanto duty to obey democratically enacted climate regulations that they regard as morally wrong. One participant shut the discussion down by invoking the old maxim that "an unjust law is no law at all," using it to claim that if the carbon-tax scheme is unjust, citizens have literally no legal obligation to comply—so refusing payment isn't civil disobedience at all, but mere ordinary non-compliance, and therefore the standard conditions about accepting punishment and appealing to shared public reason never come into play. I feel certain this leaps over several distinct issues, but I can't quite map them.

Could you lay out the precise, separable claims that slogan might be making—distinguishing, for example, a thesis about legal validity in the positivist sense, a thesis about moral bindingness, a thesis about the conditions for legitimate authority, an epistemic claim about what citizens are in a position to know, and any conceptual claim about the definition of "law"—and for each say whether it is a near-platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or plainly false? Then tell me specifically which of these readings my colleague actually needs to get from the injustice of the tax to the conclusion that refusal isn't civil disobedience and that punishment-acceptance conditions are irrelevant; assess whether that particular reading is plausible; and explain whether, even on that reading, he has really dissolved the problem or only changed the subject from moral obligation to legal ontology.
