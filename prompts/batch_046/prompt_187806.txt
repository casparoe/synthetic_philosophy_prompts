I'm prepping interveiw questions for a small philosophy podcast. Next week's guest is an unnamed defender of a revisionist Aristotelian view: she thinks the usual Aristotle hated markets story is wrong, and that the real distinction in Politics I is between oikonomia (household management with a natural limit) and chrematistike (wealth-getting with no natural limit). She also argues that modern crypto assets are pure exchange-value with no underlying use-value, which makes Aristotle's critique of tokos even sharper than people realize.

I want eight to ten questions that actually press her, not just "explain Aristotle." I need at least one on why interest is unnatural — not just the seeds-and-money metaphor, but whether that's a literal metaphysical category mistake or just rhetoric. Include a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that it's a category mistake: ask whether that means any return on capital violates natural limits, or only returns that can grow without bound.

Another question should target the no natural limit argument: does it apply only to retail trade, or to modern investment portfolios too? Anticipate her saying investment can be bounded by household needs, then ask how she distinguishes healthy saving from chrematistike when both involve accumulating money past immediate use.

Then ask specifically about crypto: if Bitcoin has no physical use-value like shoes, is it closer to Aristotle's conventional measure (nomisma) or to the worst kind of chrematistike? Follow-up if she picks one side — push her on whether a conventional measure can exist without any reference back to goods.

Also include a question about the metaphysical status of money breeding — is that just a metaphor in the text, or does Aristotle's biology commit him to thinking artificial things can have reproductive powers?

Finally, ask what she thinks Aristotle would say about modern passive income streams. Do they violate the natural limit because they're unlimited, or are they okay if they serve the household? Keep it focused and don't ask for bibliographies.
