I'm working through an argument for a seminar on whether death is genuinely bad for the one who dies, and I've added a twist about diachronic harm attribution that now seems to make the reasoning collapse into something I cannot accept. I started from the standard Epicurean observation that death is not present to the dead, but instead of stopping there I asked what conditions must hold for a state to be bad for a subject at all. I want to know exactly where the chain breaks, because if the conclusion is unavoidable then I am prepared to pay a philosophical cost, but I need to know the price precisely.

Here is the argument as I have sketched it:

(1) For any state of affairs S to be bad for a specific subject X, there must be a bearer of that badness—X must be the kind of entity to which S can be attributed as a harm (the bearer condition).

(2) If the bearer must exist contemporaneously with S, then death is not bad for the deceased, since the person is absent when death occurs and absent afterwards.

(3) If, however, the bearer need not exist contemporaneously—if harm can be attributed diachronically to a past or future stage of a life—then the truncation of a life by death harms the living person who had projects, relationships, and future goods that are now foreclosed.

(4) By parallel reasoning, if psychological continuity is what licenses diachronic harm attribution, then severe dementia that severs continuity harms the prior psychological stage by destroying the narrative unity of that life.

(5) But ordinary maturation involves just such a severance: the child-stage is replaced by an adult with different values, largely erased memories, and discontinuous traits, yet we do not regard growing up as a harm done to the child.

(6) Therefore, either we reject the diachronic bearer condition (which saves maturation but seems to block posthumous harm and perhaps death-harm itself), or we accept that maturation is a genuine harm to the prior stage.

(7) The second option is absurd.

(8) So we must reject the diachronic bearer condition.

(9) But if we reject it, then anticipatory fear of death is irrational, because there is no subject present in the dead state to be the bearer of the harm, and the living subject is not yet in that state.

(10) Yet it still seems rational to fear the annihilation of one's consciousness and the loss of one's future goods.

The argument seems to force me either to deny that death is bad or to claim that every child is wronged by becoming an adult. I feel like something slips between (4) and (5)—perhaps an equivocation between destructive discontinuity and developmental replacement—but I cannot name it precisely. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? In particular, I would prefer to keep both the intuition that death can be bad for the one who dies and the intuition that maturation is not harmful, but I want to know exactly what I am paying if I preserve both.
