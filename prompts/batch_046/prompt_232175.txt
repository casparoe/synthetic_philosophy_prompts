I am a doctoral student in political philosophy and collective decision theory, and in workshops on long-term risk I keep hearing the unilateralist’s curse mapped onto Hardin’s tragedy of the commons: each agent, convinced that a unilateral action benefits everyone, adds another cow to a shared future and thereby overgrazes it, just as herders overgraze shared pasture. The analogy is used to license fences, quotas, and central coordination—if unilateral altruism is structurally identical to self-interested overuse, then the same institutional remedies should apply. I want a rigorous audit of this mapping, but with one under-examined twist: in Hardin’s model the herders share symmetric, transparent information about depletion, whereas in the unilateralist’s curse agents typically operate under deep epistemic asymmetry, each trusting a private causal model of long-run outcomes that others reject. Please lay out the explicit correspondence table—agent, commons, unit of action, depletion mechanism, proposed remedy—then separate the disanalogies that matter for the institutional conclusion from those that are merely decorative. In particular, address whether the transparency condition is being smuggled into the target, and whether that false transparency makes disagreement about net benefit look like disagreement about grass height, thereby illegitimately supporting coordination mechanisms. Then take a committed position: does the commons framing actually carry the remedy, or does the epistemic asymmetry break the analogy so thoroughly that a different institutional parallel—perhaps adversarial forecasting or structured peer review—should replace it? Give definite reasons and do not merely enumerate options.
