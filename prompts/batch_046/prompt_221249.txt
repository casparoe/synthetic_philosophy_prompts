I was arguing with a friend about where social rules come from and I think I accidentally built an argument that kills morality entirely. Can you help me find the crack?

(1) Lewis's idea of convention is basically a regularity people stick with because they all want to coordinate — like driving on the right, or waving back when someone waves.
(2) These patterns don't have to be written down; they evolve when a group keeps doing the same thing and it works out.
(3) So take something like "don't steal from your neighbors." That started as a regular pattern, it made cooperation work, and over generations it stabilized just like driving rules.
(4) By Lewis's lights, that's a convention too — just one with a longer evolutionary history.
(5) But conventions are arbitary: we could drive left, or greet with a nod instead. There's no hidden "rightness" in the specific pattern.
(6) So if moral norms are evolved conventions, then for every one of them there's an equally workable alternative we could have settled into. Not lying is no more correct than lying; it's just our local equilibirum.
(7) That means saying "stealing is wrong" is like saying "drive on the right" — a practical hint, not a real moral truth.
(8) But then there's no actual morality, only local habits. That feels like proof that went too far.

Which step should I drop? I really like (3) and (4) — it seems right that moral rules grew out of coordination needs, and I don't want to lose the idea that morality has some real weight. I'm not sure where the slide happens. Is it (5) mixing up two kinds of arbitaryness? Or is (6) smuggling in something it shouldn't? I want to keep as much as possible without ending up saying there's no real right or wrong.
