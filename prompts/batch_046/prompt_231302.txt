I am composing a novel centered on a trauma surgeon who, in the immediate aftermath of a mass-casualty event, must choose whether to direct a scarce blood supply to her own child or to two strangers whose clinical prognoses are marginally more favorable. In preparing the moral architecture of the scene, I was given the following summary of Bernard Williams's integrity objection by a fellow novelist: Williams maintained that utilitarianism treats every commitment, including those constitutive of one's identity, as fungible inputs in an impartial calculus of aggregate welfare. Thus, in any crisis the agent must weigh the claims of intimates against those of strangers without special privilege; should the surgeon save her child only after determining that this course maximizes overall utility, she has, in Williams's phrase, indulged one thought too many — the proper ground being attachment rather than computation. The summary proceeds to claim that this demand systematically severs agents from their ground projects, making sustained personal coherence impossible, and therefore concludes that no acceptable moral framework can ever require a person to act against her defining commitments. The account feels conceptually elegant, yet I suspect it collapses distinctions that Williams kept careful. Might you assess its fairness? Please separate what is textually faithful to Williams's argument, what is a permissible popular simplification, and what appears to be an unacknowledged borrowing from narrative ethics or theories of personal identity. I am especially keen to know whether the objection is directed exclusively at act-utilitarianism or also bears on indirect and rule-based variants, and whether the concern with life-story coherence belongs to Williams himself or is an anachronistic embellishment. I wish to deploy the idea with precision in my fiction, not perpetuate a philosophical misprision.
