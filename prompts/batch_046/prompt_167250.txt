In preparing materials for a graduate seminar on democratic legitimacy, I encountered a summary—advanced in a recent unpublished working paper and echoed by a colleague in political philosophy—of what its author calls the boundary problem, that is, the question of who rightfully belongs to the demos. The summary runs as follows. The problem arises because modern collective decisions—climate regulation, trade policy, monetary coordination—routinely affect individuals who live outside the territorial jurisdiction of the deciding state. The standard literature, according to the summary, offers two competing principles: the all-subjected-persons principle, which restricts membership to those living under the state's coercive legal authority, and the all-affected-interests principle, which extends membership to anyone whose vital interests are significantly altered by the decision. The summary then argues that this apparent tension is illusory. Being subject to coercive authority, it claims, is not an independent criterion but merely the limiting case of being affected—specifically, the case in which one's interests are affected with maximal intensity and without possibility of exit. Consequently, the all-subjected principle is logically nested within the all-affected principle, and the boundary problem dissolves once we recognize that democratic inclusion should be calibrated not as a binary status but along continuous gradients of causal entanglement. Under this model, directly governed citizens retain full voting weight, significantly affected non-citizens receive proportionally weighted proxy representation, and remotely affected parties are granted advisory consultation only. The summary concludes that territorial jurisdiction should therefore be replaced by a functional matrix of democratic membership indexed to the magnitude and directness of causal impact.

I am inclined to think that this framing conflates a conceptual distinction that democratic theorists—particularly those working on the all-affected principle—typically insist upon, and that the move to gradients of causal entanglement introduces an unstated normative commitment about proportional representation that does not follow from the premises about affected interests alone. But I want to be precise in my objections before I present this to the seminar. Could you audit the summary sentence by sentence? Please distinguish what is an accurate characterization of the standard debate (for instance, the genuine tension between subjection-based and interest-based accounts), what is a defensible but non-standard reconstruction (such as treating coercion as an intensity-variable of affectedness), and what is either subtly wrong or an overstatement—especially regarding whether the all-subjected principle is genuinely a subset of the all-affected principle, and whether the proposed gradient model of membership actually resolves the boundary problem or merely relocates it. I am particularly interested in whether a defender of the all-affected principle would accept the summary's claim that the tension is illusory, or whether they would argue that the two principles can come apart in precisely those cases—temporary residents, cross-border externalities, or future generations—where the summary claims they converge.
