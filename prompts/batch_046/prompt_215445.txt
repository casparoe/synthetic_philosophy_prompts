I am a student in an upper-division course on the history of moral philosophy, and I am developing an argument for my final essay about Adam Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator. The standard interpretation in our readings treats sympathy as a pre-moral, affective mechanism—something like an involuntary emotional resonance with another's situation—that the impartial spectator then regulates and corrects. I want to defend a more integrated reading: that sympathy is not prior to moral judgment but is already structured by the normative demands of the spectator's perspective. Specifically, I argue that the impartial spectator should be understood not as an internalized social observer but as a procedural stance that agents construct through the iterative effort to make their sentiments mutually intelligible. On this view, the division of the self into spectator and agent is not a psychological split between two internal voices but a methodological device for testing whether one's sympathetic response can survive the demand for communicable justification.

Please construct the argument for this procedural, intersubjective reading of Smith. You should address at least three points. First, explain how this interpretation handles Smith's distinction between being worthy of approval and merely being approved of, particularly in cases where popular sentiment and considered judgment diverge. Second, show why this reading does not collapse Smith into Humean sentimentalism on one side or Kantian rationalism on the other—specifically, how sympathy remains affective without being arbitrary, and how the spectator introduces normativity without invoking pure practical reason. Third, illustrate the procedural point with a concrete, somewhat unconventional scenario: imagine an agent who initially feels a strong sympathetic pull toward a wrongdoer and only achieves moral clarity not by consulting an internal observer but by attempting to articulate that sympathy in terms that would be acceptable to an interlocutor who shares no particular loyalty to either party. You need not cite secondary literature or provide a bibliography; work with the conceptual structure of Smith's own framework, but you may introduce this constructed case freely. The argument should be formal, precise, and developed as if for a philosophy seminar.
