I am designing the final assessment for an advanced philosophy of history seminar—something between an upper-level undergraduate and first-year graduate course—where we have spent the term on explanation, counterfactual dependence, and the epistemology of macro-historical judgment. I want one essay question that sits squarely in the intersection of hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy, but I am tired of answers that simply rehearse the usual taxonomy: the "I-knew-it-all-along" effect, the suppression of ambiguity in retrospective storytelling, and the black-swan framing. To push past regurgitation, I want the question to inject structural overdetermination into the problem. Specifically, students should confront a scenario—say, the rapid dissolution of a long-stable authoritarian regime in the face of apparently minor triggering events—where historians disagree about whether the outcome was genuinely unpredictable ex ante or whether it was overdetermined by deep structural fractures that only became visible ex post. The prompt should ask them to evaluate whether recognizing that multiple sufficient causal pathways existed actually undermines the charge of narrative fallacy—because a linear causal story might then be a defensible simplification—or whether overdetermination makes the fallacy more pernicious by concealing the contingency of which pathway was actualized. They need to do this without collapsing the distinction between epistemic hindsight bias (a failure of belief calibration) and the narrative fallacy (a failure of explanatory form).

Please write the exam question itself, calibrated for about ninety minutes and expecting an answer in the range of eight hundred to a thousand words. Along with it, provide a model answer of roughly six hundred words that demonstrates the specific conceptual moves required: distinguishing the two biases, applying the overdetermination distinction to the evaluation of narrative coherence rather than merely to counterfactual possibility, and reaching a reasoned judgment about whether structural multiplicity excuses or exacerbates retrospective storytelling. Finally, include a marking scheme—perhaps out of twenty marks—that separates an A-level response from a B-level response by naming the precise moves each tier must contain. In particular, the scheme should make clear what it costs a student to conflate overdetermination with mere unpredictability, or to treat narrative coherence as purely aesthetic rather than epistemically loaded. I do not want any bibliography, citation list, or request for further reading embedded in the deliverable; the model should be able to construct the answer from conceptual resources alone.
