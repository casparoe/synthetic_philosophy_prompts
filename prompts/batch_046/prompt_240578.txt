At a philosophy-of-biology workshop coffee break, two researchers—Dr. Nadia Voss, who works on synthetic genome design, and Dr. Jonas Reed, a molecular evolutionist—fell into a long argument about whether DNA is literally a code or only a powerful metaphor. I listened as a bystander and did not reveal a preference. Eventually they turned to me and asked for an adjudication, but I genuinely could not settle it. Below is my reconstruction of their exchange, followed by the questions I need answered.

Nadia opened by insisting that DNA satisfies the criteria for a semantic code in the strong sense. The mapping from sixty-four codons to twenty amino acids (plus stop signals) is arbitrary in the relevant way: nothing about the chemical structure of uracil forces it to pair with phenylalanine via UUU. Decoding requires an interpreter—the ribosome together with its tRNA adapters—to translate the nucleic sequence into a polypeptide. Moreover, the system supports misrepresentation in a normative sense: a point mutation can insert the “wrong” amino acid relative to the functional specification of the gene, which is not merely a different causal outcome but an error in the message.

Jonas replied that the arbitrariness is overstated. While the assignment is not physically necessary in every detail, it is heavily chemically constrained—tRNA anticodons bind via hydrogen-bond rules that are causally fixed, not conventionally assigned like the symbol “cat” for a feline. The ribosome is not an interpreter but part of the causal machinery, analogous to a lock accepting a key. As for mutation, nature assigns no authority to the wild-type sequence; deviation is therefore change, not falsehood. To call it an “error” is to project teleology onto a process that has only functional success or failure, not correctness conditions.

Nadia countered that chemical constraints do not eliminate conventionality. Mitochondrial genomes in several lineages use altered codon tables—AUA specifies methionine rather than isoleucine—showing that the same chemical substrate can support stable, functional remappings. Synthetic biologists have gone further, expanding the genetic alphabet and reassigning codons to non-canonical amino acids without destroying cellular viability. That reprogrammability is precisely what distinguishes a causal template from a symbolic system: if the mapping can be rewritten while the machinery remains intact, the relationship between sign and referent is not fixed by physics alone.

Jonas conceded the empirical facts but denied their philosophical significance. The mitochondrial variations are minor evolutionary tweaks within a narrow chemical space, not evidence of an open-ended symbolic system. The synthetic expansions succeed only because engineers simultaneously redesign the tRNAs and ribosomes to maintain causal coupling; one can rewire a thermostat to read Celsius rather than Fahrenheit, but that does not make temperature a semantic representation. The decisive point, he said, is that DNA lacks an independent normative dimension. There is no standard of correctness separate from adaptive function, so invoking “misrepresentation” adds nothing that “maladaptation” does not already cover.

Nadia then pressed a syntactic point. The very notion of a “reading frame”—that translation initiates at a start codon and proceeds in non-overlapping triplets—is not derivable from base-by-base chemical affinity. Without an interpretive framework, there is no chemical reason the ribosome should skip nucleotides in exactly threes; hydrogen bonding operates locally, not in modular steps. The frame is imposed by the decoding machinery, which treats the sequence as a structured message with syntax, not as a linear chemical tape.

Jonas responded that the triplet frame is an emergent dynamical property of the ribosome’s physical geometry and the structural footprint of tRNA, not an externally imposed rule. Once evolutionary selection fixes a machinery that translocates in three-base increments, that pattern is just its dynamics. Calling it “syntax” reifies a mechanistic regularity into a normative rule.

Finally, Nadia argued that even evolutionary grounding can supply the needed norm. Selection establishes a functional reference: the wild-type sequence is the baseline against which mutations are measured as deviations. In engineered synthetic systems, the design specification plays an analogous normative role. Thus representation can be grounded in evolutionary or design history without requiring a conscious designer, and the “code” metaphor captures a real structural feature of genetic information.

Jonas closed by maintaining that evolutionary history explains prevalence, not correctness. To treat the wild-type as authoritative is to smuggle in teleology. The causal-template view explains every phenomenon—translation, mutation, synthetic expansion, frame maintenance—without invoking semantics, representation, or syntax. The “code” language is a useful heuristic for prediction, but it misleads ontology by suggesting that DNA carries meaning rather than causing effects.

Please adjudicate point by point: Is the codon-to-amino-acid mapping genuinely arbitrary in the philosophically relevant sense, or is it chemically constrained in a way that undermines the code analogy? Do mitochondrial variants and synthetic genetic expansions demonstrate semantic reprogrammability, or only causal malleability? Is a mutation best understood as representational error relative to a functional norm, or merely as a different causal outcome with adaptive consequences? Is the triplet reading frame a syntactic rule imposed by an interpretive machinery, or an emergent dynamical feature of ribosome geometry? Beyond these specific disputes, what—if anything—are both parties missing? For example, does either account adequately for the near-universality of the genetic code across life, or for the distinction between the information stored in DNA and the information actually used by the cell in regulation and development? And taken together, who is more right overall about whether DNA is literally a code?
