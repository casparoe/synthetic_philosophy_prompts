As a retired physicist who has lately taken up philosophy of science and ethics, I sit on the review board of a small interdisciplinary foundation that evaluates requests to amend active research protocols—changes to endpoints, sample sizes, exclusion criteria, or analysis plans—before they are implemented. I want a reusable decision procedure my colleagues and I can apply in about fifteen minutes per request. The procedure should be grounded in the distinction, familiar from Bernard Williams’s work on practical reason, between reasons that depend on an agent’s existing subjective motivations and those said to apply regardless of them; but I want it translated for our specific institutional context. In particular, treat a research team’s motivational set as including not only their explicit hypotheses and career interests but also their adopted methodological commitments—the specific statistical thresholds, replication standards, and evidentiary norms they endorsed when the protocol was originally approved.

Please design the procedure as an ordered checklist with explicit tests. For each step, state what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a clear classification (internal reason, external reason, or unclassifiable hybrid), and where it must hand the decision to human judgment because the issue is one of values rather than classification. Include at least three worked applications: first, a request to switch from a frequentist to a Bayesian analysis plan after an interim look at the data, justified by the team’s updated priors; second, a request to halt enrollment because of unexpected severe adverse events, justified by a general duty of care that was not part of the study’s original scientific aims; and third, a request to extend data collection for an additional year, justified by the principal investigator’s statement that completing this dataset is central to who they are as a scientist.

The second case should produce an answer that I will find uncomfortable: the procedure should classify the reason in a way that does not automatically license the amendment, even though my own moral intuition strongly favors immediate protection of participants. The third case should expose a boundary where the procedure detects that a reason is technically internal yet remains questionable because of value conflicts. Throughout, flag the cases the procedure cannot settle—especially those involving conflicts between methodological commitments and institutional survival motives—without resolving them by fiat. Finally, include a brief misfire warning describing one type of protocol change for which the internal/external framework is the wrong tool altogether.

Keep the language formal and precise. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list. Do not cite specific page numbers or editions. Where you refer to philosophical positions, paraphrase rather than quoting verbatim.
