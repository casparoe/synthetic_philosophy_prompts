I'm working on a dissertation chapter about institutional epistemology, and I've become stuck on the design logic of interdisciplinary funding panels that evaluate research proposals. The protocol assigns every expert reviewer exactly one vote, with no weighting by past predictive accuracy or domain-specific calibration, and the panel reaches a decision by simple majority. At the same time, reviewers are explicitly encouraged to deliberate until they can produce a shared rationale, yet the funding agency's published guidelines also treat persistent, good-faith disagreement among reviewers as epistemically valuable—something to be preserved in the record rather than eliminated as noise. This seems to combine an aggregation rule that treats voters as interchangeable and independent with a deliberative norm that treats them as interdependent and differentially authoritative.

What philosophical commitments about collective knowledge does this institutional design presuppose? Does it tacitly invoke the conditions of the Condorcet jury theorem—independent judgments, each more likely correct than not—even though deliberation and equal weighting undermine both independence and competence homogeneity? Or does it rest on a different conception of epistemic value, perhaps one drawn from accuracy-first epistemology that has been distorted by procedural constraints, or on a view that prioritizes fairness and robustness over accuracy maximization? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, assess whether its parts are philosophically consistent, and specify exactly what would have to change—both in procedure and in justification—if the underlying epistemology were replaced by a framework that weights judgments by calibrated reliability and strictly separates deliberation from aggregation.
