For a workshop on distributive ethics I am drafting my position on whether numbers count in rescue cases, and I want an internal consistency audit before I present. My commitments are these:

(1) In the canonical life-boat scenario, I am required—not merely permitted—to save the five strangers rather than the one.

(2) I reject any aggregative ontology of moral claims: the five do not possess a collectively greater claim that outweighs the single claim, and there is no scalar "total moral weight" that increases with headcount.

(3) Each individual's claim to rescue is a discrete, non-fusible deontic unit; obligations arising from such claims are plural and parallel, but they do not combine into a stronger composite obligation.

(4) The coin-flip resolution is not merely suboptimal but wrong—specifically wrong to those who are left to die—yet I deny that this wrongness consists in failing to maximize an aggregated value.

(5) Special relationships (kinship, contract, prior commitment) can legitimately reorder or break ties among rescue obligations, but bare numerical superiority is never a legitimate ordering principle.

(6) The correct normative structure is agent-relative: what I ought to do depends on the particular configuration of people standing in need relative to me, not on an impersonal ranking of global states of affairs.

Please audit this package. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and which conflicts are only apparent? For each real tension, rank its severity and specify the least costly revision—whether modifying (1), refining (3), qualifying (6), or another adjustment—that restores coherence without collapsing into pure aggregation or into arbitrary proximity bias. In particular, can (1) and (2) stand together without smuggling in the very weighing mechanism that (3) forbids? And does my rejection of the coin-flip in (4) implicitly commit me to a hidden comparative assessment that undermines (2) and (5)?
