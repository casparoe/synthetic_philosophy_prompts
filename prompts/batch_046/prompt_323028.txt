Two colleagues in a quantitative-genetics seminar have been arguing over a twin-study finding that reports a narrow-sense heritability (h²) of 0.38 for episodic-memory performance, measured in a sample that used adoption-rearing protocols to equalize shared family environment.

Colleague A opens: "That figure means genetic differences account for thirty-eight percent of the observed variance. If we imagine a counterfactual world in which every subject had the same genotype, the variance in memory scores would drop by thirty-eight percent."

Colleague B responds: "That interpretation treats heritability as a decomposable causal share, which it is not. h² is a ratio of additive genetic variance to total phenotypic variance in this specific population, under these specific environmental conditions. It does not license counterfactual claims about variance reduction under genetic homogenization, especially because gene-environment interactions and dominance effects are hidden inside that single number."

Colleague A counters: "But the adoption-rearing protocol was designed precisely to isolate genetic variance by holding shared environment constant. So the 0.38 is as close as we can get to a pure genetic contribution. If anything, it is an underestimate, because it excludes dominance and epistatic variance that broad-sense heritability would capture."

Colleague B counters: "You are conflating broad-sense heritability (H²) with narrow-sense heritability (h²). The study reports h², which captures only additive genetic variance. More importantly, equalizing shared environment does not eliminate gene-environment correlation: active niche-picking means genetically influenced traits cause people to select different micro-environments, and vertical cultural transmission means parental cognitive styles—which correlate with genotype—can be transmitted even in adoption through pre-adoption conditions or through adoptive parents' reactive adjustments to child genotype. So the 0.38 may partly reflect environmentally mediated pathways that are statistically attributed to genes."

Colleague A replies: "Even if some G×E correlation is present, that does not invalidate the claim that genetic differences are responsible for thirty-eight percent of the spread. And if vertical transmission were significant, it should appear in the shared-environment component, not in the genetic one."

Colleague B closes: "Not in an ACE model that assumes additive, independent contributions. Once you allow gene-environment covariance (rGE) and gene-environment interaction (G×E) to operate, the variance components are not separable. The deeper issue is that heritability is a population parameter, not an individual-level causal weight. Asking what percent of a trait is genetic is conceptually misleading—like asking what percent of the area of a rectangle is due to its width—because it suggests a part-whole decomposition that does not support interventionist reasoning."

Then identify the specific conceptual distinction—concerning counterfactual invariance, variance-component separability, or the ontology of statistical attribution—that both colleagues are missing and that would resolve their stalemate. Finally, state whether the disagreement is primarily about statistical interpretation, causal ontology, or the appropriate scope of counterfactual reasoning in quantitative genetics, and explain what kind of clarification (a formal result, a conceptual distinction, or an empirical design) could actually move them closer to agreement.
