I am working through a seminar on political ontology, and one framing that keeps surfacing is whether the left-right axis names a natural kind or merely a historical residue. A colleague recently proposed treating it not as an ideology but as a distributed consensus protocol: citizens hold high-dimensional local preference orderings, and the axis emerges only as a low-dimensional projection held together primarily by negative coordination — that is, each side defines itself mainly by opposition to a common reference point rather than by shared positive doctrine.

Restate this distributed-consensus framing — together with the standard objections that left and right are incoherent coalitions lacking shared first principles — in the vocabulary of evolutionary game theory: populations, strategies, payoff matrices, evolutionary stable strategies, and polymorphic equilibria. Be precise about which political phenomena map onto which game-theoretic constructs. Then deliver the residue report: which aspects of the left-right distinction (for instance, its cross-cultural stability, the asymmetry in self-definition, the role of elite cueing, and the absence of a unified positive program) have clear evolutionary-game counterparts, which require distortion to fit, and which have no expression at all in that vocabulary. In particular, address whether the idea that the axis is held together by negative coordination survives translation, or whether evolutionary game theory forces us to reconceive coalition maintenance as purely positive payoff alignment. Finally, say whether the residue points to a real feature of politics that the game-theoretic framework misses, or whether the original political framing was importing metaphysical baggage that collapses once the vocabulary is cleaned up.
