I'm preparing a seminar on relational conceptions of personhood drawn from African philosophy, particularly the idea that individual identity is constituted through reciprocal relationships rather than being metaphysically prior to them. In the background reading I've kept encountering a particular argumentative pattern that I can't place precisely within the existing literature. The pattern arises when people who hold this relational view confront institutional mechanisms—especially voting, aggregation, or market allocation procedures—that seem to presuppose the very individualism they reject. They object not that the outcome harms particular members in an ordinary sense, but that the mechanism itself transforms the ontological status of the participants.

For instance, in debates about whether a cooperative land-holding group should shift from consensus deliberation to a weighted voting scheme, opponents say that once preferences are summed and weighted, members are treated as replaceable inputs to a decision function rather than as co-constituents of a shared life. They insist that being outvoted in such a system is not merely like losing an argument; it is a different kind of wrong altogether, because the procedure makes each person's presence detachable—something that could be subtracted or substituted without altering what the collective is. I've observed parallel objections in arguments about introducing competitive grading within collaborative pedagogical communities and about using auctions to distribute communal resources. The claim appears to be that certain procedural forms do not just produce bad outcomes for pre-existing individuals, but erode the relational substrate that constitutes those individuals. Is there an established philosophical concept—perhaps within Ubuntu-informed ethics or broader relational ontology—that specifically captures this kind of wrong? How should it be distinguished from Marxist alienation, from standard democratic complaints about procedural unfairness, and from applications of the non-identity problem to group identity? And if the literature lacks a precise term, how might I define and bound the phenomenon so as to separate it clearly from simple nostalgia for traditional decision-making practices?
