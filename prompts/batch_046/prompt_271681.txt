I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. I serve on the governance board of an open-source foundation that maintains widely used cryptographic libraries. At our meeting next month we must vote on a pilot scheme: a flat monthly infrastructure stipend paid to every registered maintainer, regardless of commit volume or code-review output, funded by a ring-fenced share of corporate sponsorship. The proposal is framed as an internal form of universal basic income.

The board is divided. One side holds that compensation should track measurable contribution; without that link we undermine the incentive to keep the libraries secure and violate a principle of contributory desert. The other side argues that maintenance labour is structurally invisible—triaging reports, mediating conflicts, preserving institutional memory, and managing community harm—and that tying income to visible output systematically devalues the work that actually sustains the commons. They ask us to consider a distinction that is not the familiar equality-of-opportunity versus equality-of-outcome framing, but rather what they call participatory parity set against allocative efficiency in digital commons governance. I do not have access to the philosophical literature on this, and I need reasoning that stands on its own without citations or references I could not verify.

Please draft the formal written opinion I will circulate before the vote. Recommend a concrete course of action—approve the pilot, reject it, or approve it with specific conditions and a fixed review date—and provide a clear bottom line that the board can vote on. The reasoning must engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side: if you recommend approval, treat the desert-based objection with genuine philosophical weight rather than dismissing it as mere economics; if you recommend rejection, do the same for the participatory-parity claim. Write in British English, maintain a polished tone appropriate for a technical governance body that includes engineers and lawyers but no professional philosophers, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations.
