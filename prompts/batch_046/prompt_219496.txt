Please describe the most important implications of the saturation thesis—according to which moral value exhibits sharply diminishing returns, so that beyond a threshold further self-sacrifice yields less aggregate good and actively displaces intrinsic non-moral goods such as aesthetic achievement or deep personal attachment—for the debate over whether maximal moral goodness is desirable. Apply this specifically to the familiar distinction between the moral saint, who organizes life around impartial moral improvement, and the loving saint, who integrates moral commitment with particular relationships and personal projects. How does saturation affect the claim that we ought to aspire to the highest possible moral character? In particular, address whether saturation renders the moral saint not merely undesirable but conceptually incoherent as an ideal, and whether it strengthens the conclusion that perfect moral goodness necessarily conflicts with a well-rounded human life.
