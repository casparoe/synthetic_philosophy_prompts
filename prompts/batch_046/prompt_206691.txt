I am preparing a twenty-minute presentation for an interdisciplinary seminar on civic transparency, and I want to be ready for the objections that will actually come rather than those I imagine. My thesis is that the moral duty of citizens to disclose information to the state is not best understood through the language of privacy rights or social-contract loyalty, but through what I call an epistemic asymmetry budget: every institutional relationship between a citizen and the state operates with an implicit, quantifiable imbalance in who knows what about whom, and citizens should treat personal disclosure as a reciprocal expenditure — they should hide or reveal data in direct proportion to the state's own expenditure of institutional transparency, calibrated against the three comparative cases of German data-protection law (which treats opacity as a constitutional default), American administrative openness (which treats disclosure as a presumptive civic virtue), and Chinese social-credit architecture (which treats citizen visibility as infrastructural). I am arguing that this framework makes the German model defensible as reciprocity rather than as rights-claiming, exposes the American model as often non-reciprocal in practice despite its rhetoric, and reveals the Chinese model not as uniquely authoritarian but as an extreme version of a non-reciprocal budget that liberal states also maintain in smaller denominations.

The audience will include two liberal-contract theorists who think reciprocity collapses into consent, a legal scholar who works on GDPR implementation and knows the actual enforcement gaps, a comparative-politics specialist on East Asian governance who is sceptical of Western normative framing, and several graduate students who will ask about feasibility. I want the objections ordered roughly by likelihood and damage: each phrased in the voice of the objector who would raise it, with a note on whether I should concede the point, hold firm, or reframe, and with explicit identification of the weakest premise in my own argument — namely, that transparency expenditure can be measured or compared across radically different institutional cultures — which I should defend actively rather than hope they overlook it. Please also include the objection I am secretly hoping nobody asks: that my framework, if applied consistently, requires citizens in liberal democracies to engage in calculated non-compliance as a form of moral accounting, which sounds less like political philosophy and more like a manual for strategic tax evasion. The answer should be in British English.
