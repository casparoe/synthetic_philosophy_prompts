There's this mirror analogy people use when they talk about the Löbian obstacle for self-trusting agents. The idea is that an agent trying to verify its own reasoning is like a mirror trying to check if it's cracked by looking at its own reflection -- it can't do it without another mirror, and then you're just pushing the problem back. People use this to argue that any rational agent has to rely on some external or unexamined foundation, so pure self-trust is impossible. But I'm not convinced the mirror picture actually lines up with what Löb's theorem does. Can you map out the correspondence -- what's the mirror, what's the crack, what's the second mirror? Then tell me where it breaks, especally around the modal conditions (the box and the provability predicate) and the specifc form of Löb's theorem where trusting provability-of-truth gets you into trouble. Does the mirror analogy smuggle in something about physical reflection or infinite regress that isn't actually in the logic? And once you separate those, does the conclusion -- that self-trust is impossible -- still hold, or was the analogy doing all the work? If there's a better analogy that captures the difference between 'this system proves P' and 'this system proves that provability implies P', I'd like to hear it.
