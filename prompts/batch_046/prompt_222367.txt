I've been tracking a particular argumentative pattern in debates about whether conceivability can guide possibility, and I'm not sure whether it has a specific name. The pattern runs like this. A philosopher wants to establish that two properties can be separated—perhaps conscious experience from biological tissue, or water from its chemical composition. To do so, they invite us to imagine a scenario in which one property is present and the other absent: a silicon system undergoing pain, or a clear, thirst-quenching liquid that lacks hydrogen and oxygen. The mental image feels stable and coherent, and this felt coherence is offered as evidence that the separation is genuinely possible. Yet when I examine the conditions that make the imagined scenario stable, it seems they secretly depend on the very connection being denied. The silicon system behaves like a subject of pain only because we implicitly carry over the functional, evolutionary, and causal roles that make pain meaningful in biological organisms; the imagined liquid behaves like water only because we preserve its macro-physical and chemical interactions, which are only available with H2O. The separation appears achieved in the image, but its coherence is borrowed from the hidden presence of what was supposed to be absent.

Is there an established concept for this precise failure mode—not merely the general unreliability of modal imagination, but this specific form in which an imagined separation derives its stability from covertly retaining the link it claims to break? How does it differ from a plain hidden contradiction, or from the familiar critique that imagination is constrained by actual-world knowledge? If there is no distinct term, what boundaries would you draw around it, and which nearby errors does it exclude? Finally, please indicate what would change your assessment: what argument or distinction would convince you that this is not a separate phenomenon but merely an instance of standard modal error, and where do you think your reasoning here is most likely to go wrong?
