I have been working through comparative moral ontology for a seminar on personhood across traditions, and I am trying to formulate a position that treats Ubuntu not as a sentimental complement to liberal individualism but as a rigorous alternative with genuine ontological bite. Specifically, I want to defend the claim that the Ubuntu insight—often rendered as the idea that a person becomes a person through other persons—does not merely embed an otherwise intact individual within a social context, but undermines the very premise that moral status is anchored in intrinsic psychological capacities such as rational agency, self-consciousness, or autonomous choice. From this relational ontology, I want to derive a targeted conclusion: in cases of profound and permanent cognitive impairment—such as advanced dementia or anencephaly—the standard Western framing, which debates whether such individuals have lost the internal properties required for personhood, is fundamentally misguided. On a genuinely relational view, moral status is not a monadic property that an individual can lose when internal capacities disappear; rather, it is sustained by the persisting communal web of recognition, care, and interdependence, even when the affected individual can no longer reciprocate. Could you construct a formal argument for this position? Please lay out the premises with care, show precisely how the relational ontology blocks the narrative of lost personhood, and address the obvious counter-objection that this risks collapsing into an arbitrary social constructivism in which moral status depends on contingent cultural recognition. I am particularly interested in whether the view can preserve the intuition that there is still something genuinely tragic—though not a deprivation of moral status—when those sustaining communal relations are themselves fractured by death, abandonment, or institutional neglect.
