I'm preparing an interview for a small podcast with an unnamed scholar who has recently defended a specific reading of Plato's Laws: she argues that the Athenian Stranger's approval of regulated symposia is not a concession to human weakness or a simple lesson in moderation, but rather a deliberate civic ritual—what she calls a disinhibition pedagogy—in which controlled wine consumption temporarily weakens the appetitive part of the soul so that citizens can observe, in real time, whether their rational part can still command the spirited part without the usual external supports of law, shame, and social surveillance. She claims this makes Plato closer to a stress-inoculation model of self-control than to Aristotelian virtue ethics, and that it carries unexpected implications for modern ideas about pharmacological cognitive enhancement and simulated exposure.

I am not a professional philosopher; I came to this after reading Books I and II of the Laws on my own and finding the discussion of the sober magistrate and the testing of character more puzzling than the standard "Plato warns against drunkenness" summary suggests. I want a sequence of interview questions that avoids asking her to restate the text or provide a bibliography. Instead, I want the questions to turn on the actual mechanics of her interpretation: how wine is supposed to selectively impair the appetitive part without also corrupting the rational part; whether the magistrate's presence is meant to replace internal rational authority or merely scaffold it during the test; and what conceptual or textual evidence would genuinely move her to abandon the pedagogical reading in favor of a social-concession reading.

Please include at least one question comparing her view to modern exposure-therapy or stress-inoculation models of self-regulation, with a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that Plato's aim is not habituation but revelation. Include another question pressing how her reading accommodates the Stranger's explicit worry that drunkenness makes citizens susceptible to tyrannical and licentious impulses—if the ritual reveals hidden tyrants, why institutionalize the revelation rather than suppress the condition that produces it? End with a question that asks, given her interpretation, what a genuinely Platonic stance on modern "smart" drugs or virtual-reality simulations of disinhibition would be—would they count as legitimate extensions of the symposion's testing function, or as dangerous shortcuts that bypass the necessary vulnerability of the soul?

Keep the tone formal but accessible, press where the literature actually presses, and do not ask for citations, further reading, or references to other scholars by name.
