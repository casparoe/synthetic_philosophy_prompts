I am writing a novel in which two characters—one a neuroscientist and one a phenomenologist—are confined to a locked-room thought experiment about consciousness and causation. For the narrative architecture, I need a diagnostic taxonomy, not a lecture, that maps the logical space of positions on epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment. The instrument should take the form of a decision tree whose root is a single concrete case: a subject reports an intense, qualitatively specific pain, yet every neural and behavioral event leading to the report is fully determined by prior physical causes, with no backward causal contribution from the feeling itself.

From that root, each branch must be a yes-or-no question (or a three-way split) that someone could answer without knowing the philosophical labels. The tree should progress through these distinctions: first, whether phenomenal states exist at all in this scenario; second, if they exist but are causally inert, whether a judgment about them can be true without being accidentally true; third, if reliability is possible, whether it is secured by evolutionary tracking, by conceptual or narrative constitution, or by some form of non-causal correlation; fourth, if reliability is impossible, whether the judgment reports a functional-behavioral state or is systematically false; and fifth, whether first-person authority survives, collapses, or is reconceived as a kind of narrative coherence rather than direct acquaintance.

Each leaf must carry a named position where one exists—interactionist dualism, strong eliminativism, representational functionalism, standard epiphenomenalism, and others—or be marked "unnamed/unoccupied" if no recognized view occupies that node. Every leaf should include a one-sentence statement of its core commitment and its most awkward consequence. I specifically want two less conventional positions included: one that treats phenomenal judgment as a form of narrative self-constitution rather than representational reporting, and one that accepts the causal inertness of qualia but denies that truth requires any causal contact whatsoever. Where two positions yield identical answers at every node—say, evolutionary tracking and conceptual constitution—flag the branch as collapsed and explain why the tree cannot separate them.

The taxonomy should contain at least eight leaves, should note any logically possible but philosophically empty combinations (diagnosing them as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored), and should be phrased for intelligent general readers rather than specialists. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote named philosophers in quotation marks unless you have verified the source; paraphrase without quotation marks instead. Avoid reliance on obscure technical terms that might not be reliably present in a small model's training. Write in precise, polished American English.
