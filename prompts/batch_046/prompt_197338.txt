I was recently appointed to the programming committee of a publicly funded cinematheque, and we are preparing a retrospective of a filmmaker whose later life included convictions for serious violence against collaborators. The question before us is not whether to “cancel” the director in some global sense, but whether individual films—some made before the crimes, some that thematize domination in ways that now feel radically different, and one that was edited using footage obtained through coercion—can be screened without either pretending the biography is irrelevant or allowing it to veto every frame. I have been trying to work with the standard aesthetic debate between autonomism and moralism, but I need something operational: a reusable framework that a non-philosopher can apply in a monthly committee meeting, sign off on in writing, and defend to a board that asks why we included Film A but not Film B.

To make this usable, I have sketched three relations between a work and the wrong that produced it, which I want built into the procedure rather than treated as afterthoughts. First, internal moral loading: the film requires the viewer to adopt the very defective moral perspective—say, a gleeful identification with coercive control—that also enabled the crime, so that appreciating it aesthetically means inhabiting the wrong. Second, external moral shadow: the film is aesthetically autonomous, but exhibiting it now functions as legacy-rehabilitation, altering the public meaning of the artist’s name regardless of the work’s internal content. Third, formal resonance: the harm is not thematized, but the film’s structural properties—its editing rhythms, its spatial organization of bodies, its economy of attention—mirror the dynamics of the violence in a way that is perceptible without being representational. I want these three categories translated into an ordered checklist or decision tree that fixes a role (the committee reviewer), a time constraint (thirty minutes per film), a transparency constraint (written justification citing the step reached), and explicit nodes where judgment cannot be eliminated and must go to a vote.

Write the procedure, grounded in what the philosophy of art actually says about the separation of work and artist, but translated into practitioner steps. Include three worked applications: one film where the procedure excludes the work but my own aesthetic judgment strongly favors inclusion; one where it permits inclusion but I feel persistent moral unease; and one ambiguous case that lands in the undecidable zone the procedure flags. State clearly what the procedure cannot do—for instance, whether it can ever justify screening a work with internal moral loading if that loading is historically indispensable to understanding a genre. Finally, take a committed position: defend either that internal moral loading should be disqualifying regardless of artistic merit, or that it should be weighed but never alone vetoed, and construct the procedure around that choice rather than leaving the committee with an undecided menu of options.
