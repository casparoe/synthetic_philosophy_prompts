I'm writing a novel about a 19th-century naturalist who starts attributing intentions to fungal blights, and it's made me obsessed with whether we can talk about microbes or weather systems as having 'agency' without falling into determinism. I'm also running a small postgraduate seminar — philosophy for writers and historians, mostly — and I need a take-home exam question that doesn't let students hide behind standard determinism debates.

Could you design an essay prompt (around 1500 words, 72 hours, worth 50 marks) that asks students to defend or reject the view that epidemics and climate shocks can be 'historical actors' without committing to either full-blooded environmental determinism or a naive human expectionalism? The question should force them to engage with the specific problem of 'agential ascription under causal overdetermination' — my own phrase, not a standard one — and apply it to at least two cases: say, the Laki fissure eruption's impact on European politics, and the role of yellow fever in the Haitian Revolution's trajectroy. The model answer should be about 700 words and show the exact conceptual move that separates treating a volcano as a cause from treating it as an agent; the marking scheme must flag the specific error of conflating a 'neccessary condition' with an 'intentional cause', and must separate an A answer (which identifies the metaphysical crux) from a B answer (which describes examples well but misses the distinction). Don't ask for citations or reading lists — half my cohort are writing from remote places with patchy internet, and some can't access a library. Keep it upper-level undergraduate or MA, but avoid obscure monographs; make the difficulty conceptual, not bibliographc. There's probably a typo in my brief somewhere, but you get the idea.
