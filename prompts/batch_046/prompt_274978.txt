I'm trying to determine whether my judgments about cross-cultural philosophical disagreement form a coherent pattern, and I need a principle that captures them—if one exists. Here are my verdicts on five cases.

Case 1—Personal identity: In mainstream Western philosophy, psychological continuity (memory, intention, character) is treated as necessary and sufficient for diachronic personal identity. In some Buddhist-influenced traditions, there is no enduring self (anātman); continuity is only a conventional designation over a causal stream of physical and mental events. Verdict: This is not disagreement within a shared concept; the concept of "the person" itself differs—one is an ontological identity, the other a conventional designation.

Case 2—Causal explanation: When explaining why a group of fish moves together, observers in some East Asian contexts tend to cite external conditions (currents, predators), whereas Western observers tend to identify an internal leader (the front fish). Verdict: Same concept of causation; only attentional salience and framing differ.

Case 3—Moral responsibility and outcomes: Some Kantian-influenced traditions hold that blame attaches solely to the quality of the will and is entirely unaffected by unintended outcomes; other traditions calibrate blame partly by harm severity regardless of intent. Verdict: This is a genuinely different concept of responsibility—"pure volitional accountability"—because outcomes are not merely weighted differently but are structurally excluded from the logic of blame.

Case 4—Mental causation and accountability: In a hypothetical culture (and in some non-Western philosophical interpretations), mental states are denied causal efficacy over bodily action—actions arise from physical processes, with mental awareness following—yet agents are still praised and blamed for actions in ways structurally parallel to Western practice. Verdict: Same concept of responsibility; only the embedded metaphysics of action changes, not the normative architecture linking agent, act, and accountability.

Case 5—Collective algorithmic epistemics: Imagine a culture in which complex belief-formation is delegated to collective prediction mechanisms. Individuals maintain "stake-weighted confidence distributions" rather than isolated propositional beliefs; determinate judgments emerge only at the collective level, and post-resolution individual dissent is treated as epistemic malfunction. Members say "we know" but almost never "I know" outside technical domains. Verdict: The concept of knowledge itself has changed—it is essentially collective, procedural, and non-propositional at the individual level—not merely individual knowledge applied to groups.

Is there a single principle that distinguishes "same concept with different conditions, weights, or embedded beliefs" from "genuinely different concept"? If so, state it precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, and say what it predicts for a case I have not listed—specifically, a culture that distributes resources according to ecological need across species, treating ecosystems as the sole bearers of justice, while treating individual animal suffering within those systems as morally irrelevant. If no coherent principle saves all five verdicts, which one should I overturn and why? Be explicit about the trade-off between saving every verdict at the cost of complexity and adopting a simpler principle that sacrifices one.
