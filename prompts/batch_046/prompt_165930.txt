I have been thinking about the recent tendency in environmental history to treat germs, climate shifts, and volcanic eruptions as full-blown 'historical actors' rather than mere background conditions, and I worry that this practice, if defended consistently, proves far more than its advocates intend—specifically, that it dissolves human agency into explanatory redundancy. To see the difficulty, consider the following chain of reasoning. (1) For any factor F to count as a historical actor in event E, rather than a passive background condition, F must make a specific, counterfactual difference to E that can be traced independently of human intentions. (2) The 1815 Tambora eruption meets this standard: had its sulphur aerosol injection been only marginally lower, the 1816 temperature anomalies and the subsequent European grain shortages would not have followed in the precise form they did. (3) By parallel reasoning, the cholera bacillus in the 1854 Broad Street outbreak qualifies, since Snow's spot mapping traces its specific contribution to mortality patterns in a way that the general presence of water in London does not. (4) Therefore both climatic events and microbial agents are properly classified as historical actors. (5) If actor-status requires only this kind of causal specificity, then agency is not an exclusive property of intentional beings but is distributed across every factor that passes the counterfactual test. (6) Yet in any significant historical episode—say, the 1848 revolutions—countless non-human factors (barometric pressure, soil nitrogen levels, viral load in urban populations) also satisfy that test. (7) Once all of these are granted equal actor-status, human political choices become merely one causal thread among thousands of equally specific threads, with no privileged explanatory weight. (8) Consequently, human agency is not literally eliminated, but rendered redundant in explanatory terms: the outcomes in question would have been fixed by the convergence of non-human actors regardless of what individuals chose. (9) Because such redundancy is intolerable to any serious historiography, we must reject either (1) or (5).

I am reluctant to abandon (1), since without some specificity criterion environmental history collapses into indiscriminate causal listing, and I am equally reluctant to abandon (5), since reserving 'actor' status for humans alone looks arbitrary once we accept that germs and volcanoes genuinely shape events. Where exactly does the argument slip—perhaps in an equivocation between causal specificity and explanatory priority, or in a modal slide from overdetermination to redundancy—and what do I pay for retaining the premises I wish to keep?
