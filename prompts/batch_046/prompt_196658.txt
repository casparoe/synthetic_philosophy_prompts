Please construct a sustained argument that Aristotle's account of natural slavery in the first book of the Politics is best reconciled with his theory of ethical habituation in the Nicomachean Ethics only if we understand the natural slave's cognitive deficiency not as a global absence of rational capacity, but as a specific incapacity to deliberate about final ends rather than about instrumental means. On this interpretation, defend the further claim that this reconciliation actually destabilizes, rather than supports, the institutional justification for slavery within Aristotle's own framework: specifically, argue that if the slave retains sufficient practical reason to be habituated into virtue but lacks the autonomous deliberation about ends that defines the fully human citizen, then the master-slave relation structurally mirrors the parent-child relation of moral education, which obligates the master to cultivate the slave's rational capacities toward independence—an obligation that, if taken seriously, undermines the economic and political rationale for maintaining the slave in permanent subordination. In developing this position, address the obvious objection that Aristotle explicitly denies slaves the capacity for virtue altogether, and explain how your reinterpretation of that denial as a denial of autonomous end-setting rather than of habituated character can be defended from the broader treatment of the soul's parts and the role of external authority in moral development. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or specific citations; build the argument entirely from the conceptual structure of the views themselves.
