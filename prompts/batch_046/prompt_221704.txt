I've been thinking on my own about whether consciousness actually does anything in the brain, and I keep returning to a puzzle that seems hard to shake. If the feeling of seeing red—what philosophers call a phenomenal property—is causally inert, then nothing about that feeling causes the physical processes that lead me to say, "I see red." That makes my belief about my own consciousness seem to float free of the very thing it is about. Philosophers call this the paradox of phenomenal judgment, especially in versions of epiphenomenalism that treat phenomenal properties as having no downward causal power. I want to test whether this idea holds up when pressed against a real clinical case I have read about: the blindsight patient DB, studied by Lawrence Weiskrantz beginning in the 1970s. After surgical damage to part of his primary visual cortex, DB reported seeing nothing in a region of his visual field, yet he could point to lights, discriminate orientations, and identify shapes in that same "blind" region with accuracy well above chance. Researchers concluded that visual information was guiding his behavior in the absence of conscious visual experience.

Please analyze DB's case through the lens of epiphenomenalism together with the paradox of phenomenal judgment. I am not asking for a neuroscience summary; I want a disciplined conceptual mapping that runs in both directions.

First, if phenomenal consciousness is causally inert, what should we say is the actual causal source of DB's verbal report that he sees nothing in that field? Is that source structurally analogous to the causal source of a normal person's report that they see red? And does recognizing that analogy strengthen the epiphenomenalist position, weaken it, or expose a tension it has not resolved?

Second, the paradox suggests that if consciousness does not cause our judgments about it, those judgments are unreliable. Yet in DB's case, his judgment that he lacks visual experience in that field appears correct. Does this show that the absence of consciousness can be accurately registered without being caused by that absence? If so, does that undermine the paradox, or does it merely relocate the problem from presence-judgments to absence-judgments?

Third, I want to introduce a distinction I have been playing with—call it the asymmetry of access. In blindsight we can observe from the outside that a subject's behavioral guidance and his phenomenal reports have split apart; in ordinary subjects, we never observe our own phenomenal judgments from an external vantage point. Use this asymmetry to ask precisely what DB's case demonstrates: does it show that phenomenal judgment can be decoupled from phenomenal experience, or does it only show that non-conscious behavioral guidance is possible? Be careful not to overclaim.

Fourth, say where the theory falls silent. What features of DB's case—for example, his reported "feeling of guessing," the researchers' interpretation of his accuracy, or the ethical framing of testing him—does epiphenomenalism have no resources to explain? And what does that silence tell us about whether the paradox is an internal problem for the theory or simply outside its intended scope?

Finally, does DB's episode serve as a confirmation of epiphenomenalism, as an anomaly it never anticipated, or as a case that reveals the position was never really designed to address clinical dissociations like this? Do not force the theory to speak where it does not apply; say clearly if the case is conceptually irrelevant, and explain why.

Write this as a formal, two-way analysis: the theory illuminating the episode, and the episode testing the theory, with explicit attention to where the mapping succeeds and where it breaks down.
