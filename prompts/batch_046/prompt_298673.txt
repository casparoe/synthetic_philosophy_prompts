I want to understand whether hermeneutical injustice applies to what I call “algorithmic opacity fatigue.” Workers harmed by an automated scheduling system can fully describe their experience in everyday moral terms—exhaustion, unfairness, deception—yet the institution only supplies vocabulary like “optimization friction” or “user-adaptation costs.” Crucially, managers and workers share this conceptual gap; no dominant group possesses the missing interpretive framework and withholds it from a subordinate one. Given that hermeneutical injustice is typically characterized as occurring when a marginalized group is denied the collective hermeneutical resources needed to render its own experience intelligible, does this universal, structurally shared absence show that either (a) institutional algorithmic harm falls outside hermeneutical injustice altogether, or (b) the concept collapses into ordinary cultural ignorance, indistinguishable from a society that simply has not developed a particular scientific term? If neither option is correct, what specific feature of power over the formation and legitimation of collective interpretive resources remains operative even when the conceptual lack is shared by all parties within the institution?
