I've been chewing on Daniel Dennett's idea of real patterns and the three predictive stances—physical, design, and intentional—after a long thread on rationalist forums about whether the intentional stance is genuinely tracking patterns in the world or just a useful compression heuristic for bounded minds. Rather than rehearse the metaphysical debate, I'm trying to operationalize the comparison: for each stance, what's the predictive return on descriptive investment?

Specifically, I'd like you to rank the three stances—physical, design, and intentional—into tiers (S, A, B, C, with S being highest) based on their predictive utility relative to descriptive cost, evaluated separately across four contexts: forecasting mesoscale weather, predicting moves from a chess engine with a public evaluation function, coordinating in a repeated prisoner's dilemma with cheap talk, and anticipating token-level outputs from a large transformer model. For each of the twelve stance-context pairings, assign a tier and justify it in a sentence or two.

There's one conceptual twist I want you to keep in mind: I'm specifically interested in cases where adopting a coarser, higher-level stance yields strictly better predictions than the finest-grained physical stance available, despite requiring far less information—what I'd call an "ontological free lunch." In your tier assignments, flag any pairing where you think this occurs. Then, in a brief concluding paragraph, rank the four contexts themselves by how often they permit such free lunches, and say whether you think that pattern reveals something deep about the architecture of reality or merely about the structure of our predictive cognition.
