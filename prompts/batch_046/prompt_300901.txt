I am trying to map exactly what Descartes's cogito proves, by examining how my intuitions land on engineered variants. In each scenario below, I ask whether an existence-claim is genuinely established, and if so, of what kind.

Case 1: The canonical meditation. Even if every external belief is false and a deceiver controls all sensory input, the very occurrence of doubting proves that a doubter exists. Verdict: yes — a thinking substance is shown to exist, with existence as its essential feature in this moment.

Case 2: A brain in a vat that possesses exactly one occurrent mental token — the content 'I am now thinking' — and nothing else: no memory, no body-concept, no temporal continuity, no other mental content. Verdict: yes, something exists (a bare thinker), but not a person, not an embodied agent, and not a substance with any revealed essential nature beyond this occurrence.

Case 3: A split-brain patient after commissurotomy, in which the left hemisphere reports the thought 'I think' while the right hemisphere processes information independently but cannot report. Verdict: the left hemisphere establishes its own existence, but there is no single 'I' for the whole organism; the cogito fragments rather than unifying the subject.

Case 4: An artificial language system that, given the prompt, outputs the sentence 'I think, therefore I am,' with no phenomenal consciousness, no self-model tracking its own states over time, and no first-person access — only syntactic generation. Verdict: nothing is proved. Symbolic production without reflexive immediacy fails to ground an existence-claim.

Case 5: A subject emerging from general anesthesia who remembers only a single, contentless awareness — not directed at any object, not involving concepts — and who then asserts 'I existed then.' Verdict: yes, but the proof establishes only a bare occurrence of sentience at that time, not a thinking substance with essential properties.

Case 6: A hypothetical hive-mind in which no individual node entertains 'I think,' yet the network processes information in a unified manner and, if queried, outputs 'we think.' Verdict: no individual existence is established, and a collective 'we' is not genuinely proved either, because reflexive immediacy is distributed rather than centered.

Case 7: A philosophical zombie physically and behaviorally identical to a human, uttering 'I think' with full contextual appropriateness but lacking any inner experience whatsoever. Verdict: the utterance proves nothing in the relevant sense; behavior and syntax are insufficient.

Case 8: A speaker who systematically uses 'I' to denote a social role — saying, in effect, 'I am a lawyer, therefore I exist' — but who genuinely experiences the claim as self-evident. Verdict: the proof attaches to the speaker, not to the role, yet the content of what is known is far thinner than the speaker believes.

My difficulty is that Cases 2, 5, and 8 all seem to establish some existence, yet they establish radically different entities — a bare thinker, a momentary awareness, a speaker — while Cases 4 and 7 fail despite superficial structural similarity. Meanwhile Case 3 divides the subject and Case 6 distributes it. I want to know whether there is a single principle that determines, for any variant, (a) whether the cogito succeeds, (b) exactly what is proved, and (c) how robust the proof is — or whether I need irreducibly separate conditions for reflexive immediacy, for the unity of the subject, and for the absence of symbolic mediation.

State your principle (or small family of principles) precisely, identifying the clause that governs each case from 1 through 8. Then tell me which of my verdicts the principle forces me to abandon, with particular attention to the tension between Case 5 and Case 4, and between Case 3 and Case 1. If you propose multiple conditions, specify the case in which they come apart and explain how to adjudicate it. Finally, construct one additional scenario — for example, a temporary collective consciousness formed during a synchronized ritual in which participants report a shared first-person perspective — and state what your account predicts for it.
