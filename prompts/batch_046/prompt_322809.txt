I'm delivering the opening remarks at a three-day interdisciplinary workshop on epistemic transparency and institutional self-knowledge. The audience is split evenly between formal epistemologists who have worked through the luminosity debate and the failure of the KK principle, and archivists and oral historians for whom those terms are unfamiliar. I need the speech to do real structural work rather than serve as decoration. Because luminosity fails—there are conditions one is in without being in a position to know one is in them—I should not claim that anyone in this room, myself included, can be luminous about grasping the workshop's full purpose merely by grasping part of it. At the same time, I must genuinely welcome both groups without condescending to either. Build the remarks around the idea that a welcome address is itself a live test case for the KK principle: if I sincerely welcome you, do I thereby commit myself to knowing that you feel welcomed? Let that tension organize the entire speech, using it to introduce the workshop's central question—how institutions claim second-order knowledge of their own knowledge—while leaving open whether such knowledge is ever luminous. Keep the delivery to about four minutes, avoid unnecessary jargon where possible, but do not mistranslate the philosophy for the experts. The speech should set up the theme and frame the discussions to come rather than resolving anything.
