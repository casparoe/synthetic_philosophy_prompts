I'm preparing the central argument for a short essay in an advanced seminar on computation and decision theory, and I would welcome your critical comments on its coherence, its technical accuracy, and whether the analogy I'm using genuinely illuminates the problem or merely obscures it. The essay explores what happens when we treat Solomonoff's universal prior not merely as an epistemic device for prediction but as an ontological distribution over all computable worlds, and we ask how a consequentialist agent embedded inside one of those worlds should evaluate actions. The familiar setup is that the prior weights worlds by the algorithmic simplicity of their generating programs on a universal prefix machine. What I want to examine is the less familiar consequence: if the agent itself is one of the computable patterns being weighted, then its decision procedure is not external to the prior but is descriptively and causally entangled with it.

My sketch runs as follows. A consequentialist agent inside such a framework must integrate expected utility over all worlds in the prior, weighted by their algorithmic probability. But because the agent's own reasoning is instantiated as a program running on the same universal machine that defines the prior, any stable feature of its behavior contributes to the Kolmogorov complexity of the world it inhabits, and therefore shifts the prior weight assigned to worlds that share that behavioral structure. I call this situation prior-bound consequentialism: the agent's local optimization is coupled to the global measure over worlds, not through causal influence on other worlds but through the fact that altering its computation changes the simplicity profile of its own world and of similar ones. The malignancy, as I see it, is that rational deliberation requires the agent to stabilize the prior in order to compute an expectation, yet the very computation required to stabilize it introduces further perturbations into the complexity weights. This is distinct, I think, from ordinary self-locating uncertainty, because the difficulty is not ignorance about which world one occupies but being constitutively part of the measure being optimized over.

I would especially value feedback on three points. First, is the coupling I've described genuinely different from standard updatelessness problems in decision theory, or have I simply redescribed self-locating belief in the language of algorithmic information theory? Second, does my argument illegitimately treat the universal prior as dynamically responsive to internal computation, when in standard presentations Solomonoff induction applies to an observer evaluating external sequences? I want to know exactly where the technical error lies if I have mischaracterized how prefix-free machines handle internal perturbation. Third, I am deliberately avoiding citations to specific historical papers or recent alignment literature, since I want the conceptual point to stand independently, but if my framing of the prior's behavior under ontological interpretation is seriously misaligned with established work, I need to understand the divergence without being sent to a bibliography. Any suggestions for sharpening the distinction between epistemic and ontological readings of the prior, or for testing whether this malignancy survives under stricter restrictions on admissible machines, would be particularly welcome.
