I'm a student in a philosophy of science seminar, and I've been struck by how static the realism debate looks once you reach Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction. The pattern is always the same: realists point to structural continuity across theory change—Fresnel's equations surviving into Maxwell's optics, or the retention of mathematical form in quantum mechanics—and argue that mature sciences converge on approximately true descriptions. Anti-realists respond with the graveyard of ontologies: phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether, and ask why current entities should escape the same fate. The counter-replies are equally familiar—selective realism, the distinction between working posits and core commitments, appeals to explanatory success as a marker of truth. Nobody in my reading group has changed their mind in a semester.

My working hypothesis is that the stall isn't about missing historical data; it's that the two sides are using "truth" and "success" for different jobs. The realist seems to need literal reference preservation to ground explanatory power, while the anti-realist treats success as purely predictive and allows wholesale reference change so long as empirical structure survives. If that's right, then no new case study will break the deadlock, because each side will just redescribe the case in its own vocabulary. But I'm unsure whether this is a verbal disagreement about "meaning," a substantive hidden dispute about whether explanation requires ontological commitment, or simply two different questions—one about prediction, one about ontology—running under a single heading.

Give me a committed diagnosis: is the disagreement verbal, substantive but hidden, or a case of misaligned questions? Use what each side actually says about success and failure, not a generic survey. And tell me, given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—an explicit formal model of reference change, a principled distinction between working-posits and ontological commitments, a reconceptualization of explanatory power, or an argument that the debate should be dissolved rather than solved?
