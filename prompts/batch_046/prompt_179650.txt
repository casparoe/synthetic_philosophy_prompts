I'm developing a view of karma as a theory of moral desert rather than as a causal-mechanical law, and I want to know whether my commitments hang together. Specifically: (1) I take karma to be fundamentally normative—about what agents deserve—rather than descriptive, like a physical law of moral rebound. (2) I think desert can in principle be satisfied by outcomes that are not causally produced by the agent's prior actions; the match between the moral quality of a life and its experienced valence is what matters, not the causal chain linking them. (3) At the same time, I want karma to be action-guiding: present choices must be able to influence future desert, which seems to require at least some probabilistic or institutional link between conduct and outcome. (4) I also hold that purely internal wrongs—malicious intentions never acted upon, with no affected parties—do not generate karmic debt, because without intersubjective harm there is no basis for desert of suffering, though character is still flawed. The tension I see is between (2) and (3): if desert needs no causal link, why should present action matter for future desert? And between (1) and (4): does a pure desert theory commit me to saying that hidden mental states also generate debt, regardless of behavioral manifestation? Please audit these commitments: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision to restore consistency? Rank the tensions by severity.
