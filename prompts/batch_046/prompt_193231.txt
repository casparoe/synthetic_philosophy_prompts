I have reached the opening verses where he deploys the catuskoti—the four-cornered negation—to examine how entities might arise. As I understand the passage, he systematically rejects four possibilities: that a thing arises from itself, from something genuinely other, from both, or from neither cause. What I find puzzling is whether this is meant as a purely logical exhaustion of conceptual options, or whether it already encodes the metaphysical claim that entities lack svabhāva, an inherent, independently established nature.

While discussing this with a friend, she suggested that Nāgārjuna's structure closely mirrors the modern metaphysical grounding dilemma: if we seek a complete explanation for why something exists, we seem forced into either an infinite regress of explanations, circular dependence, or brute, unexplained facts. I wonder whether the fourfold negation can be read as an ancient anticipation of this trilemma—showing that any attempt to ground phenomena in an independent base collapses into one of the four unsatisfactory options, with the fourth (arising from neither) corresponding to the brute-fact horn. Could you help me understand whether this comparison is philosophically fruitful or merely anachronistic?
