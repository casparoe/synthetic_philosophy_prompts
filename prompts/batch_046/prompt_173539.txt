Consider the argument René Descartes advances in Part V of the Discourse on the Method (1637). He grants that artisans could build automata that emit particular sounds when physically triggered, just as a clock strikes the hour, but he denies that any machine could be arranged to reply meaningfully to every stimulus across every context in which a human speaker does so, because human language is governed by a universal rational faculty rather than by fixed mechanisms. He adds that even if an automaton excelled in specific tasks, it would necessarily fail in others, thereby betraying the absence of true reason.

In particular, is he anticipating a version of the Turing test that requires generalization rather than localized competence, and does that reading survive the reply that a sufficiently large ensemble of specialized mechanisms could simulate such generalization without being unified by any single rational principle?
