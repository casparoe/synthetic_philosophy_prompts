As a science journalist who reports regularly on animal cognition, ecological policy, and bioethics, I need to translate the scale argument in invertebrate ethics—specifically the tension between vast population prevalence and low per-capita epistemic confidence about sentience—into a reusable editorial decision procedure. My publication receives frequent pitches and press releases proposing welfare interventions for invertebrates, from laboratory protocols for decapods to agricultural guidelines affecting pollinators and soil fauna. I need a procedure my team can run in under thirty minutes per proposal without specialist philosophical training, one that is transparent about where quantitative reasoning ends and value judgment begins, and that can be applied repeatedly with consistent structure.

The principle to be operationalized holds that when assessing potential moral weight, one must combine numerical prevalence (the order-of-magnitude count of affected organisms) with epistemic density (the directness and robustness of available neurobehavioral evidence for conscious experience). To prevent naive multiplication, I want this translated through a framework I will call the Prevalence-Confidence Matrix, which introduces a functional-replication check: rather than treating every individual in a large population as an independent moral unit, this check asks whether the cognitive architecture that might support sentience is functionally replicated across the population or remains an isolated observation. The distinction is meant to block automatic scaling in homogeneous groups where evidence derives from narrow sampling.

Please construct the procedure as a sequential flow of tests in words, with the following ordered steps precisely defined:

1. The prevalence audit: how to establish an order-of-magnitude population bound for the affected group, what counts as an adequate source for this step alone, and what to do when ecological variability makes the estimate span multiple orders of magnitude.
2. The epistemic density rating: explicit criteria distinguishing direct neurobehavioral evidence, strong analogical inference, and speculative extrapolation, with labeled thresholds for "high," "moderate," and "speculative."
3. The functional-replication check: how to determine whether observed cognitive architectures scale with the population or remain taxonomically or contextually isolated, and how this modifies the weighting from step one.
4. The scale-weighted expectation gate: how to combine prevalence and confidence into a decision-relevant magnitude, including explicit instructions for cases where numerical estimates are unavailable and judgment must substitute; specify the formula or comparative rule in plain language.
5. The coherence and proportionality filter: whether the proposed intervention scales sensibly with the calculated expectation, or whether it creates institutional perversions—such as diverting resources from well-confirmed suffering, imposing unmonitorable obligations, or generating ecological side effects that increase net harm.
6. The judgment stop: the explicit point at which the procedure yields no further determinate answer and requires a signed human decision, with the values question clearly labeled and separated from the empirical questions above.

Mark every step that remains a judgment call dressed as procedure. State explicitly where the procedure will misfire—for example, when indirect evidence is stronger in quality but thinner in sample size than direct evidence, or when ecological context shifts prevalence estimates unpredictably.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals:

A. A regulatory mandate requiring electrical stunning before boiling for decapod crustaceans in commercial food processing, where prevalence is moderate, direct nociceptive evidence exists but is taxonomically patchy, and functional replication across the affected population is plausible but not uniformly demonstrated.

B. A conservation funding proposal arguing that hive-collapse mitigation for managed honeybees should take priority over regional mammalian habitat preservation, on the explicit grounds that scale-weighted expected suffering in bees exceeds that of the vertebrate population despite substantially lower per-capita epistemic confidence. Please show what the procedure returns here, even if it conflicts with standard conservation intuition.

C. A regional ban on neonicotinoid insecticides in a specific agricultural corridor, affecting an estimated insect population in the tens of billions, where evidence for sentience is contested and largely analogical, but the severity of exposure-related harm—if sentience exists—is extreme.

For each case, indicate where the procedure stops with a determinate recommendation, where it hands the decision to a person, and what the recommendation would be if the judgment stop were resolved in the direction least favorable to the proposal.

Finally, specify what the procedure cannot do. It should not generate bibliographies, citation lists, or reading recommendations. It should not invoke named theories, authors, or specific works that require verification beyond general knowledge. It should not pretend to resolve the metaphysical question of consciousness, only to organize the practical reasoning that follows from accepting the scale argument's premises. State which steps are most vulnerable to false precision, and what safeguards prevent the user from treating the output as a mechanical substitute for ethical deliberation.
