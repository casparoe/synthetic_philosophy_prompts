I am preparing a graduate module on decision-theoretic rationality and have observed a recurring argumentative structure—both in the literature and in seminar discussion—that I cannot map cleanly onto any established concept. The pattern emerges specifically in responses to violations of the independence axiom, particularly in the familiar preference-reversal configurations first demonstrated by Maurice Allais, where agents choose differently between pairs of lotteries that share a common consequence. Rather than rejecting the axiom, defenders typically preserve it by introducing a domain restriction that is individually plausible: independence holds for monetary risks but fails under ambiguity; or it holds for individual choice but not for collective deliberation; or it holds as a normative constraint on procedural rationality but not as a descriptive claim about actual behavior. Each restriction is defended on independent grounds—ambiguity aversion is empirically documented, group dynamics are structurally different, normative ideals need not match behavior—yet the cumulative effect is that the axiom applies only within an ever-narrowing class of “pure” cases that rarely occur in practice. I have two concrete instances in mind. First, the successive narrowing from “independence is violated in risk” to “it is violated only when probabilities are unknown,” and then further to “it is violated only when unknown probabilities are embedded in social-choice frameworks.” Second, the parallel shift from treating the axiom as a descriptive regularity to treating it as a normative ideal that applies solely to agents who have already satisfied a separate consistency constraint on preference ordering. I am not asking for a generic account of theoretical revision in the face of counterexamples; I want to know whether there is a specific term—distinct from a standard Lakatosian auxiliary hypothesis and from Popperian immunization—that captures this particular style of saving an axiom through successive, individually reasonable domain carvings. Does such a concept exist? How precisely does it differ from ordinary scope limitation or from ad hoc rescue, and what are its boundary conditions—does it require that the restrictions be normatively motivated, or does it also cover purely taxonomic partitions? If no established term exists, I would welcome a precise coinage together with clear conditions that distinguish it from both legitimate theoretical refinement and straightforward evasion. Please respond in American English.
