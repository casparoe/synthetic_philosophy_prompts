I was discussing with friends whether corporate ethics audits genuinely care about workers, and I noticed something structurally odd about how modern institutions measure labor harm. In supply-chain auditing frameworks—the kind that require large firms to report on labor conditions across their production networks—auditors systematically collect data on wages, working hours, physical safety, and instances of forced or child labor, but they almost never ask whether workers exercise meaningful control over the production process, whether they can recognize themselves in what they make, or whether they experience the kind of estrangement from their labor, its products, and other people that Marx describes in his 1844 Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts under the heading of alienation and the loss of species-being. Meanwhile, labor-market policies in the same jurisdictions treat unemployment as an individual failure of initiative that must be corrected through activation and job-search monitoring, while framing emotional withdrawal or minimal engagement—what is now called “quiet quitting”—as a breach of organizational loyalty rather than as a rational response to structurally alienating conditions. What philosophical commitments about exploitation and alienation does this institutional pattern presuppose?

More specifically, does the auditing practice presuppose that exploitation is strictly a distributive wrong—an unfair extraction of surplus value measurable in pay, hours, and physical risk—while treating alienation as a subjective psychological state that lies outside the scope of justice and enforceable institutional accountability? Or does it implicitly rely on a normative theory that cleanly separates objective working conditions from subjective self-realization, such that only the former can ground rights and regulatory obligations? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguishing carefully what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates or happens to fit with. Could the same audit framework be adopted by someone who believes exploitation and alienation are conceptually inseparable—say, on tractability grounds alone—or does it quietly rule out that integration? And if we replaced the underlying view with a Marxist framework that treats alienation itself as a structural injustice, perhaps requiring democratic worker control over the labor process, what concrete changes would have to be made not only to auditing categories but also to unemployment policy and to the legal framing of work refusal?

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested interpretation of Marx’s distinction between exploitation and alienation, or of the relationship between distributive justice and subjective self-realization in labor, as if it were the consensus view.
