In my philosophy reading group—composed mostly of retired scientists like myself—two members, Dr. Elena Voss and Dr. Marcus Chen, recently reached an impasse over whether scientific descriptions can ever ground categorical moral or policy prescriptions without committing what Hume identified as the is-ought gap, or what Moore later called the naturalistic fallacy. Elena opened by noting that climate-model outputs—descriptive claims about radiative forcing and temperature trajectories—do not, by themselves, entail that we ought to decarbonize; some hidden normative premise, such as that avoidable harm should be minimized, must do the work. Marcus countered that scientists derive normative conclusions from descriptive premises constantly: if a spectrometer shows systematic drift, we conclude that we ought to recalibrate it. He argued that treating this as fallacious would paralyze laboratory practice, so Hume’s gap must be narrower than Elena assumes, applying at most to categorical ethics rather than to practical reasoning embedded in functional contexts.

Elena replied that Marcus was conflating hypothetical imperatives with categorical ones. The calibration case is conditional—if accurate measurement is desired, then correct the drift—whereas the climate-policy inference she had in mind asserts an unconditional obligation. The hidden normative premise, she insisted, is not eliminated but merely relocated into the antecedent of a hypothetical. Marcus then introduced what he termed “functional normativity”: within any system defined by a constitutive aim—whether predictive accuracy in instrumentation or homeostatic stability in an ecosystem—the descriptive identification of deviation from that aim analytically entails a corrective “ought” internal to the system’s own organizational logic. He extended this to evolutionary ecology, arguing that biodiversity maintenance and carbon-cycle regulation are not externally imposed values but descriptively given functional properties of living systems; therefore, “we ought to preserve climate stability” is not an imported moral judgment but an analytical unpacking of what the system’s functional organization requires.

Elena responded that this maneuver smuggles normativity in through the back door. To call evolutionary survival or ecosystem stability a “constitutive aim” is already to interpret selection dynamics prescriptively; natural selection describes what survives, not what should survive. She granted that functional normativity might illuminate instrumental reasoning within science, but she denied that it licenses the leap from descriptive ecology to prescriptive climate policy without an additional, explicitly normative bridge—precisely the bridge Hume warned was missing. Marcus closed by asking why, if the functional aims are genuinely internal to the system’s descriptive dynamics, importing an external value framework should be necessary at all; he suggested that Elena’s insistence on a separate normative premise reflects a dualism between fact and value that functional integration dissolves.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point: assess whether Marcus’s laboratory examples genuinely parallel the climate-policy case or depend on a hidden hypothetical structure; evaluate whether functional normativity, as he presents it, genuinely closes Hume’s gap or merely disguises a relocated normative commitment; and say which party carries the stronger overall position, noting where each makes a genuinely sound point that the other overlooks. Finally, state explicitly what argument or evidence would change your verdict—for instance, a rigorous demonstration that evolutionary functions carry prescriptive force without circular interpretation, or a proof that all apparently categorical scientific “oughts” reduce without remainder to hypothetical imperatives—together with a brief indication of where your reasoning here is most likely to fail.
