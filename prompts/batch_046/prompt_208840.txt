I'm serving on a municipal health board deciding whether our public coverage should include a structured intervention for adults who experience months of debilitating exhaustion, social withdrawal, and cognitive fog after prolonged caregiving for a dying relative—despite normal blood panels, imaging, and the absence of any recognized biomarker profile or disrupted homeostatic or allostatic mechanism. Some board members, including our chief physician, argue that without detectable physiological dysfunction we are merely medicalizing an extreme but normal human response to loss and overwork; they warn that labeling it a disorder will funnel resources away from tangible diseases and encourage people to treat ordinary resilience failures as clinical conditions requiring treatment. Other members, mostly clinicians and social workers, counter that the functional devastation—people leaving careers, dissolving partnerships, and being unable to parent—is sufficient to make this a genuine health problem regardless of whether we can isolate a specific biological failure, and that refusing coverage because tests come back normal is like denying care for an injury whose internal damage doesn't appear on a scan. A community representative adds a different worry: that framing this as an individual medical deficit obscures the fact that our society offers almost no structural bereavement support or workplace protection for caregivers, and that treating it as a disorder shifts responsibility from employers and the state onto the sufferer.

I need to think through whether there is a principled way to determine what counts as a real health condition in cases like this, without simply defaulting to whatever laboratory tests can detect or to whatever level of distress people report. What conceptual framework should guide this decision? Where does each board position capture something important and where does it go wrong? And is there a coherent middle position between "it is only a disease if biology is broken" and "it is a disease if suffering and impairment are severe enough," or does any attempt at compromise collapse back into one of those two extremes?
