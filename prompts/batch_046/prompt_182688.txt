My friend and I just finished a long argument about Nozick's tracking account of knowledge and whether its rejection of epistemic closure can be made consistent. He laid out what he called a uniform tracking argument, and I cannot find the exit, though the conclusion seems to damage the theory from within. Here are the steps he pressed:

(1) Nozick's tracking conditions require that for S to know p: p is true, S believes p, in the nearest worlds where p is false S does not believe p, and in the nearest worlds where p is true S does believe p.

(2) Suppose S knows she has hands (H), and competently deduces that she is not a brain in a vat (¬BIV).

(3) In nearby worlds where H is false — say, she lost her hands in an accident — her evidence changes and she does not believe H. So she tracks H.

(4) Now apply tracking directly to ¬BIV formed by deduction. In nearby worlds where ¬BIV is false — she is a BIV — her experiential evidence is identical, so she still believes she is not a BIV. Hence she fails to track ¬BIV.

(5) My friend then proposed uniform tracking: if a belief-forming method M yields tracked knowledge of p, and q is reached by applying the same rational method (here, deduction) to p, then any failure to track q must be an independent failure specific to q, not inherited from p.

(6) Under uniform tracking, since deduction is the same method applied to H to reach ¬BIV, the failure in (4) is indeed independent: it stems from the modal proximity of BIV worlds to the actual world, not from any defect in the deduction.

(7) But now generalize: for any empirical belief E that S tracks (Paris is the capital of France, my keys are in my pocket), construct the corresponding skeptical scenario S(E) in which E is false or systematically deceptive but evidence is preserved. By (4) and (6), S will always fail to track ¬S(E) independently.

(8) This means knowledge is systematically fragmented: S can track all her ordinary beliefs, yet for each one she independently fails to track the denial of its skeptical counterpart. Closure is not globally false; it is locally blocked by a repeating pattern of independent tracking failures.

(9) The problem is that this fragmentation is rationally unstable. If S knows H, knows that H entails ¬BIV, and applies deduction competently, but still fails to know ¬BIV solely because of an independent modal quirk, then her knowledge of H is rationally inert: it cannot support her reasoning to deny BIV, even though she recognizes the entailment.

(10) So either uniform tracking allows rational integration, in which case knowing H and the entailment should transmit knowledge of ¬BIV — restoring closure and contradicting (4) — or it forbids such integration, leaving S with tracked empirical beliefs that she cannot use to rule out skeptical alternatives she knows are entailed by them. In the latter case, tracking does not explain knowledge so much as catalog isolated beliefs that happen to be true.

(11) Therefore my friend concludes that Nozick's rejection of closure, once made uniform and general, proves too much: it either collapses back into closure or renders empirical knowledge rationally useless.

I want to keep (1), (3), and (9) if I can — I think tracking captures something real about counterfactual sensitivity, and rational integration matters. But I feel like there is a slide somewhere between (5) and (8), or perhaps in (9) itself. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid, then which premise is the least damaging to give up?
