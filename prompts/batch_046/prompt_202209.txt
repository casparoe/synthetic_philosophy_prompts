I am a software engineer without formal philosophical training, and I have been trying to apply concepts from decision theory to the design of adaptive user interfaces. I understand that a money pump exploits intransitive preferences by cycling an agent through a loop of trades that returns her to the starting point with a net loss. I also understand that diachronic rationality concerns rationality over time, and I have formed the impression that it demands temporal consistency: a rational agent ought not to revise her preference ordering unless she acquires new information, and even then the revision must preserve the original ordering over options that have not changed.

Recently I built a toy model of an adaptive pricing engine that detects when a user’s preferences have shifted—say, from valuing speed to valuing accuracy in a search algorithm—and then re-optimises the interface accordingly. I realised that if preferences can shift back and forth, the engine could in principle charge the user for each reconfiguration, cycling her through states in a way that looks structurally identical to a money pump. This led me to think that diachronic rationality actually prohibits any preference reversal that is not strictly information-driven, and that adaptive systems which accommodate “taste drift” are essentially institutionalising money pumps.

Yet this seems to conflict with the common economic view that people rationally update their tastes after experience. So my question is: does the money-pump argument genuinely extend across time to forbid non-informational preference change, or is there a specific condition in diachronic rationality—perhaps relating to the agent’s epistemic state or to the structure of the choice set—that blocks the temporal version of the pump? And if adaptive interfaces do risk creating temporal loops, ought we to design them to freeze a user’s initial preference profile as a rationality safeguard?
