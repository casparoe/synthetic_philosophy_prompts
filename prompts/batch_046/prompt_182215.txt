From what I understand, if a rational agent can formally prove that whatever it beleives is true, then by Gödel's second incompleteness result it can prove its own consistency, which means it's actually inconsistent. That sounds like a hard stop for any AI with recursive endorsement architectures—like if a model checks its own outputs and updates its weights based on that check. But here's the part that confuses me: if the obstacle is that severe, why doesn't it collapse ordinary human reasoning? Every time I work through a proof or even balance my checkbook and conclude 'this is right,' I'm essentially endorsing my own reasoning process. Have I just violated some logical constraint without noticing? Or is the catch that the theorem only applies when the endorsement is fully formalized inside one fixed logical language, so that everyday belief revision is safe?

I was talking to a friend who mentioned tiered trust—where an object-level system can be trusted even when the meta-level checker can't be—but I'm not sure if that's a real escape hatch or just philosophical handwaving. So my actual question is whether the Löbian problem is specifically about formal self-reference in AI design, or if it's a broader epistemic warning that should make us distrust any self-correcting loop, including our own brains. I feel like I'm mixing up consistency of a formal language with consistency of a belief system, but I can't put my finger on where the line actually is, and I'd like to know if there's a principled way to seperate the two.
