I'm serving on the advisory committee for a small municipal archive in a former mill town. We recently acquired two bodies of material from the 1930s: several thousand pages of intact town-council ledgers, meticulously recording tax assessments, zoning votes, and public-health ordinances; and a single water-damaged box from a worker-run cooperative laundry, containing handwritten wash-ticket tallies denominated in 'exchange hours' rather than dollars, fragmentary grievance notes, and a partially burned membership roll listing names absent from every census and property registry we've checked. Our funding permits digitizing only one collection this year. The chair argues that the council records should take priority because they are chronologically complete, authored by elected representatives, and compatible with our cataloging system; a volunteer historian counters that the laundry fragments document a form of economic cooperation that the council's fiscal categories literally cannot represent—since 'exchange hours' have no line in the tax ledgers—and that digitizing them first would not merely preserve paper but correct a structural misrecognition built into the archive itself. I find the volunteer's point compelling but worry that I'm romanticizing brokenness: if the membership roll is illegible in places and the exchange-hour system has no standardized conversion rate, can these fragments genuinely ground historical claims, or am I asking the archive to privilege a beautiful absence over verifiable presence? How should I weigh evidentiary completeness against the risk that our very tools of preservation silently reproduce the council's conceptual framework?
