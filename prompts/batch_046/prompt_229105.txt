For my political philosophy seminar I have to lay out my own commitments on the separateness of persons and check whether they cohere. Here is my profile:

1. I accept a prioritarian form of aggregation: benefits matter more when they go to those who are worse off, but I think this requires only ordinal well-being comparisons, not cardinal measurement across persons.
2. I take the metaphysical core of the separateness objection to be correct—there is no collective subject of experience—so talk of the total welfare of a group is not about any real unified state.
3. From this I draw a normative conclusion: we should never sum moral reasons across individuals; evaluation ought to stay pairwise or individual rather than aggregated.
4. In symmetric rescue cases—five equally needy strangers versus one—I think saving the five is obligatory, and specifically that allowing all five to die is five times as grave a failure as allowing the one to die.
5. I reject Nozick's utility monster as a counterexample to prioritarianism, since weighting tracks deprivation rather than welfare-capacity; yet I also think that if a being genuinely experienced welfare at orders of magnitude beyond human ranges, some unavoidable cardinal comparison might enter.
6. I think Scanlon-style contractualist objections actually damage aggregation more effectively than the separateness objection does, though I reject contractualism because it struggles to justify saving the greater number in symmetric rescue cases.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear incompatible, and how severe is each real conflict? For each tension, identify the cheapest revision that restores consistency and what it costs me. Then say which single change would clean up the most tension at the least cost. In doing so, separate what is broadly settled in the literature from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers who work on this topic, and do not present a disputed view as if it were the consensus.
