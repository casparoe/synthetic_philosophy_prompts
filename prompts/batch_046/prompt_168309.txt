I’ve been back in my hometown for about six months after grad school, and family dinners have turned into this weird recurring experiment. Last week my uncle and I got into it over carbon pricing—he sent me three links, I sent him two studies, and we both ended up saying the other was just “not listening.” It reminded me of this broader argument I see everywhere: one camp says political polarization is basically a bad-faith problem, that the other side knows the facts but performs outrage for tribal points; the other camp says it’s all structural echo chambers and algorithmic filtering, that people are rational given totally different information diets. These two explanations have been circling each other for like a decade in op-eds and some social science, and honestly nobody seems to move. I keep wondering if we’re even talking about the same thing.

So here’s my guess, and I want you to poke at it: maybe the stall isn’t about missing data but about a hidden disagreement over what counts as “arguing in good faith” in the first place. The bad-faith camp treats consistency with in-group signaling as evidence of insincerity, while the echo-chamber camp treats the same behavior as rational belief-formation inside a broken information pipeline. Are they actually disagreeing about psychology versus system design, or is this a verbal tangle around the word “rational”? Or is there a deeper, unspoken split about whether political talk is mainly about truth-seeking or about maintaining social identity—so that each side is answering a different question (how do minds work vs. how do networks work) under one heading?

Can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this really is, using what each side actually claims? Is there a concealed empirical question—maybe about how often people update beliefs privately versus publicly—that could break the deadlock? Would progress need a formal distinction (say, between epistemic rationality and social rationality), a specific kind of experiment, or just admitting the question is ill-posed? And if the impasse is bedrock—if it comes down to incompatible intuitions about human nature—what, if anything, should we expect to resolve it? I’m less interested in which side is right than in why we’re stuck, and what it would take for either of us to actually recieve an answer that feels like progress rather than just another round.
