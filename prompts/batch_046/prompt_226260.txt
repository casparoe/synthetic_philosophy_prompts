For a philosophy-of-mind graduate seminar (twelve students, assumed familiarity with consciousness studies but no specialist background in psychophysics), design a classification instrument—a decision tree—mapping how one might explain the subjective report in the color-phi illusion: the claim that observers experience a colored dot changing hue in mid-trajectory before reaching the location of the second-colored dot.

Root commitment: "The phenomenon is experimentally real and demands a substantive explanation; it is not reducible to a post-hoc reporting error."

Structure the tree from four binary branch nodes, each phrased as a concrete claim about the mechanism or ontology of experience, not as a named theory. The branches must cover:

(i) Whether conscious experience is organized by a single canonical timeline (a unified "theater") or distributed across parallel drafts lacking one authoritative ordering;

(ii) Whether the discrepancy between neural stimulus timing and reported experience is resolved by an Orwellian-style revision of the experiential record after the fact, a Stalinesque-style suppression of contradictory drafts before awareness, or neither—plus, in a non-standard extension, whether such revision targets only the chronological index assigned to the experience (chronometric revision) or also alters its qualitative phenomenal content (ontological revision);

(iii) Whether predictive or interpolative mechanisms generate the intermediate appearance prospectively, rather than correcting or selecting among past representations;

(iv) Whether unrevised or unselected drafts are preserved in some subpersonal archive, permanently destroyed, or never generated in the first place.

Locate named positions within the logical space these branches generate (for example, Cartesian-theater retentionism; multiple-drafts accounts in Orwellian, Stalinesque, and hybrid forms; predictive-coding interpolation views; retrocausal phenomenology; eliminativist or deflationary views about phenomenal reports). Each occupied leaf must include the position's name (or "unnamed" if no established view occupies it) and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment—the conclusion it must accept that its rivals find implausible or embarrassing.

For the remaining logical combinations, note at least three coherent but unoccupied slots, labeling each "unnamed" and giving a one-sentence diagnosis: is the combination incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?

Finally, explicitly flag any pair of named positions that your tree cannot genuinely separate—where they share answers at every branch—and explain in one sentence why the diagnostic fails.

The instrument should contain no bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific editions or page numbers. It should be answerable entirely from conceptual reasoning about the color-phi phenomenon, the timing of experience, and the logical relations between revision, prediction, preservation, and phenomenology.
