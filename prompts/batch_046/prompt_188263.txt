I am marking a final-year examination script in moral philosophy. The question set was as follows:

Some philosophers deny that genuine moral dilemmas—situations in which an agent bears two binding moral obligations that are jointly unsatisfiable—are possible, on the grounds that moral requirements must be consistent and that an overridden obligation is cancelled rather than violated. Others defend their reality, appealing to moral residue and the phenomenology of tragic choice. Evaluate whether moral residue genuinely demonstrates that both obligations persist undefeated, or whether it can be fully accounted for by the defeat of one genuine obligation. In your answer, distinguish carefully between (i) a conflict of obligations, (ii) a conflict of reasons, and (iii) a situation in which an agent is blameless yet the world remains morally defective. Consider, in particular, whether engineered conflicts—such as an automated clinical triage protocol programmed with two hard constraints (never deny emergency care; never exceed capacity) that become jointly unsatisfiable during a surge event—reveal anything about whether dilemmas are structurally possible, or whether they merely reproduce practical design failures that dissolve upon closer normative analysis.

One candidate submitted the answer below:

'Genuine moral dilemmas are indeed possible, and moral residue provides decisive proof. When obligations conflict—for example, a duty to protect a vulnerable party and a duty to respect autonomy—the choice of one does not erase the other. The guilt or regret experienced afterwards shows that the violated obligation was not cancelled but remains categorically in force, which explains why unavoidable wrongness occurs. This is not merely a conflict of reasons, since reasons can be weighed and defeated without leaving any normative trace; obligations, by contrast, bind absolutely. Consequently, if two categorical obligations are incompatible, one is violated regardless of choice, and the agent does wrong whilst remaining blameless, demonstrating that wrongness and blameworthiness come apart. Even algorithmic triage systems that register a constraint violation irrespective of which path is taken confirm that dilemmas are structurally real rather than purely psychological. Those who reject dilemmas by appealing to the consistency of moral requirements therefore overlook this unavoidable feature of tragic choice.'
