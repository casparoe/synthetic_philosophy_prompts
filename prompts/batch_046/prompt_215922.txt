I am preparing notes for a philosophy of ethics reading group on secular models of moral self-cultivation, and I have been examining Benjamin Franklin's Autobiography, specifically the chapter titled "Plan for Attaining Moral Perfection." Rather than summarizing his well-known list of thirteen virtues, I want to understand a deeper tension in the passage itself. Franklin writes, "It was about this time I conceived the bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection. I wished to live without committing any fault at any time; I would conquer all that either natural inclination, custom, or company might lead me into." He then explains his method of dividing virtue into discrete, sequential habits and tracking offenses in a small ledger: "I made a little book, in which I allotted a page for each of the virtues. I ruled each page with red ink, so as to have seven columns, one for each day of the week, marking each column with a letter for the day." By treating moral failure as a quantifiable spot in a grid and virtue acquisition as a matter of weekly concentration, Franklin seems to frame perfection as a technology of self-management aimed at achieving a "steady, uniform rectitude of conduct."

What confuses me is how this instrumental, accounting-like procedure relates to the actual content of the virtues, particularly the final entry, Humility, which Franklin defines with the precept "Imitate Jesus and Socrates." If the scheme genuinely aims at moral perfection, why is Humility placed last—after practical virtues such as Frugality and Industry—rather than serving as a foundation? And why does it invoke imitative exemplarity rather than a discrete habit that can be marked off in a table? Does Franklin's ordering suggest that humility is only achievable once economic independence and social discipline are secured, or does the bookkeeping method itself reveal that his conception of virtue is fundamentally utilitarian and self-interested, with the religious-imitative language of Humility functioning as an aspirational ornament? I would appreciate help seeing whether these two aspects—the secular grid and the exemplary precept—cohere into a single conception of moral perfection, or whether they expose an unacknowledged contradiction in the project.
