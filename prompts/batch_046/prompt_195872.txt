I've been trying to name a specific argumentative asymmetry I keep encountering in discussions of freedom, and I'm unsure whether it corresponds to an established concept or needs a new one. The pattern shows up like this. In debates about employment, people routinely treat a worker's lack of alternatives—say, the need to accept dangerous conditions because quitting means destitution—as a background fact rather than a constraint on freedom. They say the employer is not coercing anyone; the worker is free to leave, and whatever hardship follows is the worker's own problem. Yet in debates about taxation or regulation, many of the same people treat the state's power to penalize or restrict—not just its actual exercise, but its standing authority—as itself an infringement on freedom, even when citizens retain the practical ability to comply or avoid the penalty. So private economic power is naturalized: its existence is ignored as a freedom issue unless it is actively wielded against someone. State power is politicized: its mere institutional presence counts as a restriction.

I'm not asking for the general distinction between freedom as absence of interference and freedom as having resources or capacities, because that is not the point. The point is the selective application: why one kind of arbitrary, avoidable human power is rendered invisible and the other is hyper-visible, using what looks like the same underlying criterion—intentional agency that could be changed—in both cases. Does this specific double standard have a recognized name in the philosophy of liberty? If it does, how exactly does it relate to the familiar distinctions between being blocked by others, lacking means, and being subject to another's arbitrary will even when that will lies dormant? And if it does not have a name, can you define it precisely, distinguish it from both simple interference-based accounts and from resource-based accounts, and map its boundary cases—such as whether the asymmetry holds for nonprofit employers, democratic workplaces, or state-owned enterprises?

Please answer in American English. I am interested in the conceptual carving—what exactly is being applied inconsistently, what it is not, and where its edges lie—rather than in bare labels, bibliographies, or reading lists.
