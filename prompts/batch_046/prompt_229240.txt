I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving active laboratory research, has audited graduate seminars in philosophy of science and social epistemology and now serves on the funding review board of an interdisciplinary research consortium. In our current session we must reach a concrete decision on an international proposal that combines predictive mental-health screening with algorithmic risk assessment across pediatric clinics, and the aggregation of our members' judgments has produced exactly the problem my seminar called the discursive dilemma. Each of our three regional ethics panels evaluated the study using the same three premises—scientific validity, direct risk to subjects, and long-term societal benefit—and every panel reached internally consistent judgments on all three. Yet if we aggregate conclusions directly, with each panel casting one holistic vote to approve or reject, the consortium approves funding; if instead we pool judgments premise-by-premise across all panels and derive the collective conclusion from the aggregated premises, we are compelled to reject it. Before Thursday's meeting I need a formal written opinion recommending which aggregation protocol the board should adopt for this specific decision, stating a concrete course of action with a clear time frame—whether to authorize the study with conditions, suspend it pending revision, or reject it outright—and providing the reasoning that justifies that bottom line. The opinion should treat seriously the strongest consideration favoring conclusion-based aggregation, namely that decomposing each panel's holistic institutional judgment into separately measurable premises violates the integrity of local deliberation and risks treating qualitative ethical assessments as if they were commensurable physical measurements, but it should not simply restate institutional autonomy as paramount. In particular, please address an analogy that has troubled me since my laboratory years: in experimental physics we combine measurements only when they share a commensurable scale and well-defined error structure, yet these three evaluative premises seem qualitatively incommensurable. Does that incommensurability undermine premise-based aggregation altogether, or does it merely require a non-linear or context-sensitive weighting scheme rather than simple majority pooling? I need reasoning I can evaluate and circulate without consulting outside sources or bibliographies.
