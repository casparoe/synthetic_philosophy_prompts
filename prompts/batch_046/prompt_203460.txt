hey, im drafting a short essay on what i call the paradox of deontology, though i might be mangling the usual framing. my core claim is that deontological constraints work like agent-relative side-budgets: each person has a fixed allowance of forbidden acts they cant perform. but in collective or sequential cases, the budget gets weird. say rule is never lie. in case A i lie once and save ten people. in case B i stay silent, but then ten other people lie and the same ten get saved with way more chaos. standard deontology says dont lie in A, yet in B my inaction permits a worse pattern of violations. so the constraint protects my own purity while being basically indifferent to the total pattern i help enable.

i think the deeper issue is that the paradox assumes a unified agent across time. i want to argue that if we treat agency as diachronicaly fragmented — the decision-node at t1 is not fully identical in moral-accounting terms to the node at t2 — then carrying a "debt" of forbidden action across your life is like attributing it to a different person. that dissolves the paradox because the constraint applies only locally, not globally over a lifespan. but im worried this just collapses into act-utilitarianism with extra steps.

heres my rough outline:
1. standard paradox: forbidding harm-causing acts lets greater harm happen.
2. my twist: paradox depends on diachronic unity of the agent.
3. likely objection: this erases the distinction between deontology and consequentialism.
4. my weak reply: local constraints still forbid the act at the node, they just dont accumulate into a global moral score.

what do you think? is this just rehashing scheffler or someone else? also, does my reply in part 4 actually hold up, or am i missing something obvious about how side-constraints are supposed to apply across time? id appreciate comments on accuracy, whether the idea is interesting, and specifically on whether the fragmentation move is coherent. thanks!
