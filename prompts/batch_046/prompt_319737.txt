I am writing a final paper for an undergraduate philosophy-of-mind course in which we have been studying Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences and its central thesis that analogy—not logic, statistics, or rule-following—is the engine of human cognition. Rather than summarizing the book, I want to apply its framework to a well-known narrative: Franz Kafka's The Metamorphosis. Specifically, I am interested in how Gregor Samsa's family members understand him through shifting analogical categories—first as an ailing but still human relative, then as an animal burden, and finally as an obstacle to be removed—rather than through any stable definition of personhood. I want to examine whether Kafka dramatizes the idea that conceptual collapse is not gradual but abrupt: the moment when an analogy is stretched past its breaking point and forces a wholesale restructuring of how someone is seen, not just a change in attitude. I would like to know where this analogical reading illuminates specific scenes—perhaps the father's first attack, the sister's final rejection, or Gregor's own persistence in human-like cognition despite his body—and where the novella resists being reduced to a philosophy-of-mind case study. In particular, does Gregor's own analogical self-understanding, in which he continues to interpret his experience through human social frames long after his family has abandoned them, show something about cognitive resilience that Hofstadter and Sander's account of category formation does not fully explain? Please provide the analysis in American English, without bibliographies or citation lists; I am looking for a conceptual interpretation that takes both the philosophical framework and the literary work seriously on their own terms.
