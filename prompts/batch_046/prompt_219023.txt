I am preparing an essay for an interdisciplinary seminar on early modern philosophy and political economy, and I have become particularly interested in whether Berkeley’s metaphysical commitments actually shaped his economic proposals or whether the two bodies of work are largely independent. In a recent lecture handout circulated by my instructor — summarizing a view that appears in some philosophy-of-economics discussions — Berkeley’s position on money and currency is characterized as follows. According to this summary, Berkeley held that money is not a commodity with intrinsic worth but a conventional token representing labor and public credit; he argued that gold and silver possess no inherent value, since their purchasing power derives entirely from social agreement and the productive capacity of the state. More strongly, the summary claims that Berkeley treated the materiality of gold as metaphysically problematic: because his immaterialism denies that matter exists independently of perception, grounding national currency in gold amounts to basing economic value on a substrate that lacks real being. From this, the handout concludes that Berkeley’s advocacy for a national paper currency — issued by a public bank and backed solely by the labor and credit of the nation — was not merely a practical reform for Ireland but a direct application of his idealist ontology to economic life. The summary therefore presents The Querist as essentially an extension of the Principles and the Dialogues into political economy, suggesting that Berkeley’s monetary theory is best read as a chapter in his metaphysics rather than as an empirical contribution to economic thought.

I find the narrative compelling, but I worry that it collapses distinctions Berkeley might have kept separate, and I do not want to build my essay around a framing that makes his economics derivative in a way he never intended. Before I proceed, I would like a careful accuracy audit of the summary. Please go through it point by point: which claims are faithful to Berkeley’s actual arguments in The Querist and related writings; which are standard but oversimplified glosses that a defender of his economic view might accept with qualification; and which involve a misrepresentation — whether by conflating his metaphysical denial of matter with his economic critique of bullionism, by overstating his anticipation of modern fiat theories, or by treating a practical proposal about Irish banking and national credit as if it were derived directly from the thesis that existence consists in being perceived. I am especially interested in whether the conclusion — that The Querist is essentially applied immaterialism — survives correction of the summary’s stronger claims, or whether the accurate parts leave Berkeley’s economics standing on its own practical and theological grounds, with only an incidental connection to his ontology.
