After a long argument with a friend about how rational agents ought to revise beliefs when observation is ambiguous rather than categorical, I need to resolve a point about Jeffrey conditionalization that I think I am misframing. My friend insists that Jeffrey's rule—updating beliefs by taking a weighted average of the old conditionals over a partition, using new probabilities assigned to the partition cells—is merely ordinary Bayes applied to a hidden-variable model in which the true observation is unknown but the signal structure is fully specified. He claims that the rigidity condition, which requires that the within-cell conditionals remain unchanged, is therefore not an independent constraint on Jeffrey updating but simply a description of what occurs when one correctly expands the sample space before conditioning. I pushed back that this collapses the normative distinction: Jeffrey conditioning is intended for cases in which one directly shifts probabilities over a partition without conditioning on any specific event, and if beliefs inside the cells shift, one is no longer performing Jeffrey conditioning but rather standard Bayesian updating on a richer model. To test whether my objection holds, consider this case: I initially assign low probability to a certain biochemical marker being present, then receive ambiguous assay results that raise my marginal probability of presence to 0.65, but in revising that marginal I also change my views about the underlying metabolic pathways that would explain the marker's presence. If I apply Jeffrey's weighted-averaging formula using my old within-presence conditionals, I preserve one belief profile; if I first revise those internal pathway beliefs and then average, I preserve another. Is applying the formula with the outdated within-cell probabilities ever rationally permissible, or does the internal revision show that Jeffrey conditioning is simply inapplicable and my friend is correct that there is no separate rule being violated?
