As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun working through social choice theory in earnest, I have constructed the following argument and now find it suspicious because it seems to undermine the very possibility of democratic scientific judgment. I would prefer, if I can, to preserve the mathematical result and its direct interpretation, but I need to know what that preservation costs me.

(1) The Gibbard-Satterthwaite result establishes that any social choice function mapping individual preference orderings over three or more discrete outcomes onto a collective ordering—provided it is not dictatorial and satisfies unrestricted domain together with Pareto efficiency—is manipulable: there exists at least one profile of preferences at which some agent can obtain a strictly preferred collective outcome by submitting an ordering other than their true one.

(2) If a decision procedure permits strategic manipulation—if there exists even one profile at which an agent benefits from misrepresentation—then rational agents who understand the procedure will maintain a standing disposition to misrepresent whenever they anticipate being in such a profile.

(3) Hence every non-dictatorial democratic procedure for selecting among three or more outcomes requires, as a matter of rational agency, that its participants be disposed to lie about their preferences.

(4) But if all participants are rationally disposed to lie, then the inputs to the procedure carry no epistemic authority; the output reflects strategic positioning rather than genuine collective judgment.

(5) Scientific peer review, interdisciplinary funding panels, and consensus committees for selecting among competing theoretical frameworks or experimental priorities are all democratic (non-dictatorial) procedures that aggregate expert preferences over three or more discrete options.

(6) Therefore, by (3) and (4), all such scientific institutions are rationally self-undermining: they depend on honest expert input for their authority, yet rationality demands that experts be prepared to falsify their judgments.

(7) The only escapes are either to accept dictatorial authority in science—assigning one expert or algorithm final decision rights—or to deny that rational agents respond to strategic incentives in epistemic contexts.

(8) Both escapes seem unacceptable: dictatorial science contradicts the collaborative ethos of modern research, and denying strategic rationality seems psychologically naive and incompatible with the design of incentive structures in laboratory and funding administration.

Which step should be rejected, and what does that rejection cost? I am particularly reluctant to abandon (1) or the direct interpretation in (2) that strategic vulnerability undermines rational transparency, but I want to understand whether the damage is contained to the extension in (5)—perhaps because scientific aggregation involves continuous parameters, probabilistic beliefs, or epistemic constraints that fall outside the ordinal discrete-choice framework—or whether it runs deeper, forcing us to qualify what rational agency requires in collective judgment. If the argument is valid and one of the premises must go, I would rather keep (1) and (2) intact and pay elsewhere; but I need to see the price clearly.
