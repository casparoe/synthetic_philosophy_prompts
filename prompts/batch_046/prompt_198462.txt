For an upper-level philosophy of art and emotion final exam, design one essay question on the paradox of fiction that students must answer in 50 minutes (approximately 800 words). The question must be unanswerable by merely summarizing standard formulations—students should not be able to earn top marks by reciting Colin Radford’s puzzle or Kendall Walton’s make-believe theory in generic form. Instead, the question must force them to confront the specific crux of whether belief in the reality of the object is a necessary condition for a genuinely emotional response, and they must apply that confrontation to a novel scenario described below.

Embed the following scenario in the exam question: a fully transparent, procedurally generated interactive narrative in which the user knows in real time that every character and event is being instantiated algorithmically from visible source code, with no hidden author, no stable printed text, and no pre-existing fictional world. The user can inspect the code generating a character’s distress, knows the character is a non-existent data structure, yet reports feeling genuine pity and fear for that character. Refer to this as the “transparent-algorithm case.”

Your deliverables should be:

1. The exam question itself, with clearly delineated parts directing students to: (a) precisely state the paradox of fiction, explicitly distinguishing it from the mere suspension of disbelief; (b) evaluate whether and how Walton’s theory of make-believe can accommodate emotional responses in the transparent-algorithm case, with particular attention to whether an author-prescribed “game of make-believe” is necessary for the props and principles to operate; (c) defend or reject the claim that if emotional responses occur in the transparent-algorithm case, then belief in reality was never necessary for emotion, and therefore the original paradox dissolves rather than being solved.

2. A model answer of about 800 words that demonstrates the analytical depth expected for an A-grade response. It should explicitly address the crux in part (c), should treat the transparent-algorithm case as a genuine test of the theories rather than an aside, and should avoid mere summary.

3. A marking scheme out of 30 points, broken into at least five specific moves or criteria that an excellent answer must contain. For each criterion, state: the points available; the specific misconception or omission that typically costs those points; and the concrete distinction between an A-level and a B-level performance on that point. The scheme should reward precise conceptual distinctions and penalize conflation of the paradox with related but distinct issues (for example, treating the problem as solely about aesthetic distance or about the rationality of emotional investment in general).

Constraints: do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not depend on verification of specific publication dates or obscure theoretical labels; and write the prompt in formal language appropriate for an examination-committee context.
