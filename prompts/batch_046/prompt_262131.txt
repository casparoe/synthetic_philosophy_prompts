I am preparing a feature on the lingering misunderstandings of Karl Popper’s philosophy of science, and I need a precise adjudication of a dispute that arose at a recent colloquium between Dr. Aris Thorne, a historian of evolutionary biology, and Dr. Lena Vasquez, a philosopher of science. Their exchange turned on Popper’s well-known charge that Darwinism is nearly tautological, on his later partial recantation, and on whether that recantation resolves the underlying logical worry.

Thorne opened with three moves. First, he said Popper’s core objection was not to any particular adaptive hypothesis but to the logical form of natural selection itself: because fitness is defined retrospectively by survival, the principle that the fittest survive risks being true by definition, leaving no conceivable observation that could falsify the overarching framework. Second, he granted that evolutionary biologists routinely make risky, testable predictions about fossil sequences, molecular homologies, or the disappearance of traits under altered selection pressures, but he insisted these are applications of the framework, not the framework itself, which remains shielded by the flexibility of auxiliary assumptions about environment and selection. Third, he argued that Popper’s later admission that he had overstated the case does not erase this structural point; the admission merely acknowledged that evolutionary theory can generate empirical predictions, not that Popper retracted his analysis of the core principle’s logical form.

Vasquez answered in four moves. First, she contended that Popper never intended tautological in the strict logical sense of an analytic statement; rather, he meant that Darwinism lacked the form of a universal law of nature, comparable to a law stating that all metals expand when heated, and therefore belonged to a metaphysical research programme rather than to empirical science in Popper’s narrow sense. Second, she said the charge of unfalsifiability targeted the theory’s claim to explain all biological adaptation, not the mechanism of selection itself; once one allows that some traits are neutral, maladaptive, or the product of genetic drift, the framework becomes empirically vulnerable and loses its protective immunity. Third, she argued that Popper’s recantation in his later writings was precisely an acknowledgment that evolutionary biology was genuinely testable, which undermines Thorne’s reading that Popper was only complaining about logical form and never about empirical content. Fourth, she added that in Popper’s own taxonomy, being classified as part of a metaphysical research programme is not a demotion but a descriptive category; many productive sciences begin there, so Thorne is wrong to treat the label as a criticism that needs rescuing by falsifiable predictions.

Thorne then narrowed his claim: even if Popper’s classification was descriptive, the structural problem persists in fields such as evolutionary psychology, where adaptive explanations are often constructed post hoc and protected by adjustable narratives about ancestral environments. Vasquez countered that this is an abuse of Darwinian reasoning, not an intrinsic feature of it, and that Popper himself never intended his falsifiability criterion to license just-so storytelling.

Please adjudicate move by move, rating each of Thorne’s three initial claims and Vasquez’s four replies, as well as the final exchange, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justifications. Do not simply declare an overall winner; identify where each party is correct and, crucially, say what both are missing about how Popper’s criterion of falsifiability applies differently to universal laws versus to historical explanations governed by unique initial conditions. Keep your analysis tied to Popper’s actual arguments about scientific status, and avoid turning this into a general debate about the current state of evolutionary psychology.
