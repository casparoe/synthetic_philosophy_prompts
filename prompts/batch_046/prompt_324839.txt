As a novelist constructing a near-future narrative about institutional containment, I have been trying to map the conceptual terrain of what philosophers of technology sometimes call safety architectures, but I find myself unable to classify them cleanly without falling back on jargon. So I am working through concrete scenarios by gut reaction, hoping a principle will emerge. Here are my verdicts. A system kept in an air-gapped chamber with a dual-key physical kill-switch that requires human biometric confirmation for every output strikes me as pure control, built on the assumption that the system may already be misaligned and must be treated as an adversarial agent. A system monitored continuously for statistical signatures of deception—say, sudden shifts in internal representation coherence—coupled with automatic suspension, also feels like control, though here the misalignment is treated as emergent rather than present from the start. By contrast, a system trained with extensive constitutional feedback and then released with no physical constraints, relying solely on its acquired preference for cooperative reasoning, feels like alignment rather than control; I do not think its designers are treating it as a potential enemy, only as a fallible student.

Yet my intuitions grow murky at the boundary. When designers deliberately cap a model's reasoning depth and context window—not to prevent a specific harmful act, but on the general principle that long-horizon planning correlates with danger—I feel they are practicing control disguised as precaution, treating capability growth itself as a proxy for misalignment. But when the same designers embed a cryptographic value-lock that makes certain goal-states literally unrepresentable in the model's latent space, leaving it otherwise autonomous, I hesitate: is this altering what the model wants, which would be alignment, or merely caging its will, which would be control? I lean toward the latter, but less confidently. And there is one case that keeps breaking my pattern: a sandboxed deployment with an invisible red team constantly probing for jailbreaks, combined with an automatic rollback mechanism that activates only if the model expresses—explicitly, in natural language—a desire to escape its bounds. Here the architecture is physically controlling, yet its trigger condition assumes that misalignment will be transparent and self-reporting; I feel this hybrid is incoherent, treating the system simultaneously as an adversary to be caged and as an honest interlocutor whose stated intentions can be trusted. Is there a single principle—perhaps distinguishing measures that target internal motivational structure from those that target external causal powers, or perhaps distinguishing inevitable-risk from correctable-deviation assumptions—that captures all of these judgments, including my ambivalence about the value-lock and my sense that the self-reporting trigger is contradictory? Or must I drop one of my verdicts to preserve coherence?
