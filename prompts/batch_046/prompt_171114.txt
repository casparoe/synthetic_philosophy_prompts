I am a doctoral student in political philosophy preparing the opening remarks for an interdisciplinary workshop convened to honor my advisor’s retirement; she spent her career at the boundary of social choice theory and institutional design. The audience will be split between formal theorists who have worked extensively on judgment aggregation and practitioners—museum curators, legal scholars, and policy designers—who care about collective reasoning in concrete settings but do not use the technical formalism. I want the remarks, about six minutes in length, to treat the discursive dilemma not as a familiar voting paradox but as a structural constraint on what any collective body can sincerely say about its own reasoning process, and I want that philosophical idea to shape genuinely what I can and cannot claim about institutional legitimacy and transparency.

To avoid a generic rehearsal of standard textbook cases, I want the speech built around a specific hypothetical I have devised: a five-person museum board must issue a single institutional verdict on whether a controversial installation is “politically subversive.” The board commits to transparency by aggregating member judgments on two premises—whether the work is “symbolically charged” and whether it is “formally innovative”—using a premise-based rule. Depending on the aggregation procedure, this yields one set of judgments about the premises but the opposite verdict on the conclusion, while a conclusion-based rule reverses the outcome. I need the remarks to use this scenario to demonstrate that requiring a collective to be transparent about its reasoning can force it into direct contradiction, and then to draw out the implications for how we should design bodies that claim to reason together rather than merely vote together. The tone should be formal, respectful, and accessible to the non-theorists without diluting the argument; please do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to specific texts, and please reach a substantive, defended position on whether the cost of potential inconsistency undermines the value of transparent collective reasoning. Write the remarks.
