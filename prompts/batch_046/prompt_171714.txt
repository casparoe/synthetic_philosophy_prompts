I am preparing a short essay for an independent study in metaphysics, and I would be grateful for your critical comments on the argument sketched below. The assignment asks us to take a familiar conceptual distinction and examine where it becomes unstable in applied cases.

My draft concerns the distinction between finks and masks as obstacles to the manifestation of dispositions. As I understand the standard view, a fink is an interfering factor that is present only when the disposition is about to be triggered and that removes or neutralizes the disposition at the moment of stimulus, whereas a mask is an external blocker that remains present regardless but prevents the causal chain from reaching its usual outcome. I accept this distinction for simple physical cases—say, a fragile vase protected by a fixed foam wrapper (a mask) versus a vase made of material that instantly transforms into rubber upon impact (a fink).

However, I want to argue that the distinction collapses in systems that can dynamically adjust their protective mechanisms in response to the very conditions that would trigger the disposition. Consider what I call recursive masking: a factory produces fragile glass ornaments and, precisely because they are fragile, installs an automated self-repairing coating that detects impact and hardens the surface within milliseconds. The coating is not a static layer; it is activated by the stimulus that would otherwise break the glass. On one reading, the coating behaves like a mask, since the ornament remains fragile—the internal structure is unchanged—yet the manifestation is blocked. On another reading, because the protective mechanism is causally triggered by the impact itself and is not independently present beforehand, it functions like a fink: the disposition is present until the moment of testing, at which point an interfering factor removes it.

I extend this to a collective-level variant. Suppose an entire factory is disposed to produce goods that break in transit. The management, aware of this disposition, institutes a mandatory packaging policy for all shipments. The packaging is a mask at the object level, but at the institutional level, the factory’s own awareness of its fragility-causing processes generates the protective condition. I suggest that this makes it unclear whether we should say the factory has lost its disposition to produce broken deliveries (fink) or that the disposition remains but is consistently masked by an institutionally generated blocker.

My tentative conclusion is that once dispositions are embedded in feedback loops—where the conditions that would manifest them also trigger the conditions that prevent them—the fink/mask taxonomy becomes a matter of descriptive framing rather than a metaphysically deep division.

Could you comment specifically on the following?

First, is my characterization of the standard distinction accurate, or have I misrepresented it by treating finks as always removing the underlying disposition rather than merely interfering with its manifestation?

Second, does my recursive-masking example genuinely blur the boundary, or is there a principled reason to classify the self-activating coating definitively as one or the other?

Third, is the move to collective or institutional dispositions legitimate, or does it confuse the dispositional properties of artifacts with the intentional policies of agents?

I am especially concerned that I may be conflating causal interference with conceptual analysis, and I would welcome suggestions on how to sharpen the argument without introducing technical apparatus I do not yet fully understand.
