Proposal for a 6,000-word term paper in philosophy of action on the absent-minded driver problem. Frame the central question precisely: does the driver’s successful route-following exhibit a form of counterfactual robustness—specifically, implicit adaptive adjustment to unexpected obstacles without conscious retrieval—that could ground a non-propositional practical-knowledge attribution, or does every apparent adaptation collapse into subpersonal predictive mechanisms that carry no knowledge status at all? Explain why the standard knowledge-how versus knowledge-that stalemate leaves this open, since both sides treat conscious access to the route as decisive rather than testing the skill’s flexibility. Outline the approach: compare the absent-minded driver with a sleepwalking navigator and an expert musician playing on autopilot, isolating whether implicit behavioral adaptation requires representational updating or can be explained purely by hierarchical error-minimization. State the possible conclusions—a minimal robustness criterion for practical knowledge distinct from both belief and mere mechanism, or a fully deflationary mechanistic account—and identify which considerations would decide between them (e.g., whether adaptation survives disruption of low-level motor patterns). Give an honest scope assessment: the risk is that resolving predictive-processing debates about implicit representation exceeds a single term, so the paper should use those models heuristically rather than adjudicate them. Conclude by specifying what would change your mind: the argument or finding—such as evidence that all implicit route adjustment is mediated by unconscious belief-like states or by purely reflexive dynamics—that would force abandonment of the robustness criterion or acceptance of a mechanistic reduction.
