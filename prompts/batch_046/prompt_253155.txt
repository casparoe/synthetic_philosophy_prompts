I am preparing an interview for a science-and-ideas podcast with a historian of mass violence—call her Dr. Elena Voss—who has recently advanced a synthesis in the long-running debate over Reserve Police Battalion 101. Voss argues that the framing of Christopher Browning versus Daniel Goldhagen—situation against culture, ordinary men against willing executioners—rests on an unexamined premise: that the battalion was a representative cross-section of German society. Drawing on archival personnel records, she claims that pre-war policing institutions in Weimar and the early Third Reich had already filtered recruits through bureaucratic routines that selected for dispositions toward routinized violence against marginalized groups. She therefore rejects both Browning’s emphasis on peer pressure and careerism and Goldhagen’s thesis of a widespread eliminationist antisemitism, proposing instead that a diffuse professional dehumanization—acquired through ordinary policing rather than ideological indoctrination—served as the necessary substrate that made lethal compliance possible without requiring either universal hatred or pure situational coercion.

I want the conversation to avoid biography and to stay inside the mechanics of the argument. Draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that move through the following pressure points, in roughly this order: first, ask her to specify the empirical pattern in pre-war career trajectories that most directly undermines the claim that these were randomly selected ordinary men; second, press her to distinguish her concept of professional dehumanization from Goldhagen’s eliminationist antisemitism at the level of individual motivation, not just aggregate outcome; third, include a follow-up that anticipates her likely reply—that institutional selection explains variance in enthusiasm among the shooters—and forces her to explain why this does not simply collapse her view back into a refined situational account once the filter is acknowledged; fourth, ask how her framework accounts for the documented instances of refusal or psychological breakdown among battalion members without invoking either unmediated moral autonomy or a deep-seated ideological revulsion against killing Jews; fifth, probe the comparative implications: if institutional ecology is the primary variable, should we expect analogous violence in other regimes or campaigns where similar policing structures existed but the targeted population was not Jewish, and does she have comparative cases in mind; sixth, ask what she takes to be the strongest empirical objection to her synthesis—whether it comes from social-psychological experiments on obedience, from demographic data on the battalion’s actual composition, or from comparative genocide studies—and how she answers it; and seventh, conclude with a question about what kind of future archival discovery or psychological evidence would genuinely shift her credence toward either the Browning or Goldhagen pole.

The tone should be precise, unsentimental, and consistent with a journalist covering ideas: respectful of scholarly stakes, but willing to press exactly where the historiographical literature presses. Do not include generic opening questions about her path to the subject.
