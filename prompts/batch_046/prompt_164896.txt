Consider a municipal charter that divides its legislature into two chambers with strictly separated powers. One chamber is filled entirely by sortition from the adult citizenry, serving single, non-renewable terms of four years; its members draft all non-fiscal legislation. The other chamber is elected by proportional representation and possesses only budgetary approval authority, including the power to amend fiscal provisions of bills but not their substantive content. Citizens selected for the sortition chamber receive a salary set at three times the local median income, yet they must complete a forty-hour mandatory training program in administrative and constitutional law before assuming office. Furthermore, any citizen who serves in the sortition chamber is legally barred from running for elected office for ten years thereafter. The charter’s preamble states that the sortition chamber exists to capture the unfiltered judgment of ordinary people, but it also assigns the elected chamber a veto over all spending, implicitly treating fiscal judgment as a domain requiring specialized electoral accountability.

What philosophical commitments about democratic legitimacy, epistemic competence, and political equality does this hybrid design presuppose? Does it presuppose that unfiltered popular judgment and trained administrative competence are complementary goods that can be cleanly partitioned between chambers, or does it quietly concede that they are in tension? Is the ten-year exclusion of former lot-selected members from electoral politics consistent with a view that random representation expresses civic equality, or does it reveal an underlying suspicion that direct governing experience corrupts the representativeness that sortition is meant to secure? Reconstruct the implicit theory of citizenship that makes this institutional split appear coherent, distinguish what the design strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say whether the practice relies on a stable philosophical compromise or on an unstable juxtaposition of rival conceptions—such as a competence-based elitism for fiscal matters coupled with a pure equality-based randomism for lawmaking. Finally, indicate what would have to change in the charter’s structure if one replaced its implicit view with a uniformly epistemic or a uniformly procedural-democratic theory of representation.
