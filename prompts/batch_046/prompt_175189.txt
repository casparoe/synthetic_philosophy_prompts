I am a novelist drafting a scene in which three characters — an epidemiologist, a barrister, and a ship's surgeon — debate what each is permitted to say in conditions of uncertainty. To ground their arguments, I need a philosophical ranking. Please rank the following four norms of assertion by how well they regulate ordinary and expert speech: first, the knowledge norm (assert only what you know); second, the truth norm (assert only what is true); third, the belief norm (assert only what you sincerely believe); and fourth, what I term the 'stewardship norm' — assert only what you are practically responsible for conveying, regardless of your epistemic state. Assign each a score between one and ten, and place them in tiers — exceptional, adequate, inadequate — with a sentence justifying each placement. For each norm, identify the concrete conversational misfire it best explains: for example, the wrongness of confidently stating a medical diagnosis one merely suspects, or the oddness of withholding a true warning because one lacks belief in its cause. Then apply the ranking to my invented case: the surgeon recognises scurvy from pattern and experience, saves the patient by declaring 'You have scurvy,' yet lacks laboratory confirmation and, owing to scepticism about diagnostic taxonomy, does not fully believe the statement. Which norm best vindicates her assertion, and which renders it clearly improper? I ask for precise verdicts, not a bibliography, and I work without access to external sources, so please rely on conceptual reasoning alone.
