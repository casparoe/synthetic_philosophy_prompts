Design a 60-minute, 25-mark essay question for a final-year undergraduate exam in political philosophy that asks candidates to analyse what theory of democratic sovereignty is implicitly presupposed by the Scandinavian and Japanese practice of retaining a hereditary monarch with formal reserve powers—specifically the power to withhold assent or dissolve a government—that are constitutionally available but conventionally never exercised. The question should be unanswerable by summarising Bagehot or listing constitutional articles; instead, it should require students to evaluate whether this institutional design is consistent with popular sovereignty only if the monarchy is understood as a symbolic unifier, or whether it can also be defended as a democratic contingency mechanism that derives its legitimacy precisely from its dormancy. Provide a model answer structured around four specific argumentative moves: (1) a reconstruction of the implicit distinction between authorised and exercised power; (2) an evaluation of whether dormant authority undermines democratic authorisation or preserves it through symbolic continuity; (3) an analysis of whether the Scandinavian and Japanese cases share the same philosophical structure or differ in their relation to parliamentary supremacy; and (4) a reasoned conclusion on whether the practice is philosophically coherent or relies on an unstable hybrid of democratic and pre-democratic legitimacy. Include a marking scheme allocating 25 marks across these four moves with explicit weightings, and for each move provide a one-line description of the characteristic failure mode—such as treating convention as mere habit, conflating symbolic value with democratic consent, or ignoring the contingency-mechanism defence altogether—that should cause a candidate to lose marks. The prompt should not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and should be framed so that a well-prepared student can answer it without access to external sources. Write in British English.
