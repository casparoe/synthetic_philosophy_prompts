I've been turning over a family problem in my mind, and I wonder if there's a principled reason my intuition resists what seems like the logical choice. My sister needs an expensive, long-term treatment that would save her from severe suffering, and if our extended family pools all our resources, we can cover it. But doing so would require my brother to abandon the apprenticeship he's spent five years building, and it would erase the education fund I've been saving for my children. Several relatives argue that we should do whatever produces the greatest overall relief across everyone—adding up benefits and losses as if they all belonged to one collective account. I understand that reasoning, yet I feel there's something wrong with treating my brother's career and my children's prospects as weights that can be canceled out by a larger gain elsewhere, even when that elsewhere is real pain.

To complicate things further, my sister has said privately that if she knew the full cost to others, she would refuse the treatment rather than see her family dismantled for her sake; some relatives think we should proceed anyway and shield her from the decision, believing that the aggregate good of her recovery outweighs her preference to decline. I don't want to deny her care, but I also don't think we should treat each person's deepest commitments and choices as interchangeable pieces of a family-wide calculation. Is there a philosophical reason why distributing burdens and benefits across separate lives shouldn't work like that, and does my sister's own refusal change how we should weigh these sacrifices?
