Here are my gut verdicts on whether a constitutional monarch in a modern parliamentary democracy has acted within legitimate bounds, and I am trying to see whether there is a coherent principle behind them—or whether some of these judgments have to be dropped.

Case 1: Parliament passes a bill legalizing commercial surrogacy with minimal regulatory safeguards by a one-vote margin after intense party whipping. The monarch refuses royal assent, citing a conscientious moral objection, fully aware that parliament can override with a second vote; parliament does, and the bill becomes law. Verdict: this is acceptable, perhaps even a proper exercise of a moral check.

Case 2: Following a national referendum and subsequent parliamentary vote approving a transition to a republic, the monarch refuses assent to the abolition bill, arguing that voters were inadequately informed and that the monarchy serves as an indispensable constitutional check. Verdict: this is clearly unacceptable, a direct violation of democratic sovereignty.

Case 3: During the weekly private audience, the monarch advises the prime minister against appointing a particular minister, citing credible but not yet judicially tested allegations of corruption. The prime minister withdraws the nomination. Verdict: acceptable, squarely within the advisory sphere.

Case 4: At the state opening of parliament, the monarch departs from the government-prepared text to declare, in her own voice, that a pending immigration bill “fails the test of national compassion,” explicitly criticizing specific legislation. Verdict: unacceptable, a breach of political neutrality.

Case 5: After an election produces a hung parliament, the monarch invites the leader of the second-largest party to form a government, bypassing the leader of the largest party, on the ground that the larger party’s platform threatens “constitutional stability,” even though the larger party could assemble a working coalition. Verdict: unacceptable, an abuse of the appointing power.

Case 6: The monarch declines to extend the customary state-banquet invitation to the leader of a legally registered party that won substantial parliamentary seats, explaining privately that the leader’s rhetoric is “morally intolerable,” though no statute governs invitations. Verdict: I keep going back and forth—sometimes this feels like a legitimate defense of symbolic dignity, sometimes like an illegitimate withholding of democratic recognition.

My tentative principle is this: a monarch’s departure from strict neutrality is legitimate only if (i) it operates through a channel that preserves the ultimate legal decision for democratic institutions, (ii) it is motivated by protection of constitutional rights or integrity rather than by personal preference or institutional self-preservation, and (iii) it does not selectively deny symbolic recognition to actors who have secured democratic legitimacy.

Please test this principle against all six cases. For each clause, name the case that motivates it, and say whether the principle correctly predicts my verdict or forces me to drop one. Be precise about what the principle says—define “channel that preserves ultimate legal decision,” “symbolic recognition,” and so on, if needed. Then tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate without ad hoc modification, and propose a repaired version (or a competing simpler one) that handles the remaining cases, even if it sacrifices one judgment. Finally, apply the resulting principle to two cases I have not listed: (a) a monarch who uses private wealth to fund an independent legal challenge to a government policy she believes unconstitutional, and (b) a monarch who delays giving royal assent to a non-controversial budget bill for several months, not refusing it but forcing prolonged procedural delay. What do the principles say about these?
