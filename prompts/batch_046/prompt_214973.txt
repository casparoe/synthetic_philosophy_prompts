I'm trying to settle an argument that arose during our cooperative's annual meeting about whether to participate in the vote for our non-binding community covenant. Our building contains roughly four hundred households, and each year we rank three proposed statements that carry no legal authority but are treated by the board as a mandate for future resource rules. This year, the ballot offers a rigorous environmental pledge that I genuinely support but that will certainly fall short of the required threshold; a diluted compromise that includes one provision I find ethically objectionable yet is far preferable to the third option, a minimalist statement that abandons the commitment entirely; and that minimalist statement itself. A fellow resident—who reads philosophy casually but insists she is not invoking any named theory—argues that because four hundred votes are cast, my single ranking is causally irrelevant to the outcome, which means I have no moral duty to vote. She further claims that if I rank the compromise first in order to block the minimalist option, I am not merely tolerating a lesser evil but actively endorsing a framework I consider wrong, whereas ranking my preferred pledge first is empty virtue signaling that treats a collective decision like a private diary entry rather than an attempt to govern. I feel pulled between abstaining entirely, casting a purely expressive vote for the pledge I believe in, and voting strategically for the compromise to prevent the worst result. Can you help me disentangle whether voting can be obligatory even when instrumentally futile, whether strategic ranking amounts to moral complicity, and whether there is a coherent distinction between expressing a preference and genuinely trying to influence an outcome that I know my ballot alone cannot change?
