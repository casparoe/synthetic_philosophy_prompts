In a seminar I am taking on comparative epistemology, we have been working through the distinction between what is called “ascription universality” — the claim that for a concept to be genuinely universal it must be actively embedded in local conceptual schemes across cultures, not merely projected onto them by outside observers — and what philosophers of science describe as “epistemic dominance,” in which one institutional framework suppresses alternatives not by direct refutation but by rendering them unthinkable or pre-scientific within authoritative settings. I have been trying to apply this pairing to global mental-health taxonomy, and I keep arriving at a conclusion that feels precipitous yet I cannot locate the precise error. Specifically, if conditions like major depressive disorder fail the test of ascription universality — since several societies organize suffering through nosologies that lack any direct analog and instead distribute symptoms across entirely different social and somatic categories — then the DSM’s framing of depression as a universal biological kind appears not merely empirically overstated but structurally dominant: it treats local frameworks as deficient approximations of a Western model rather than as coherent alternatives. From this it seems to follow that cross-cultural psychiatric comparison is inherently illegitimate, and that the only ethically consistent anti-dominance position is to abandon universal taxonomy entirely in favor of purely local, incommensurable illness categories that resist translation. Yet when I presented this argument, a colleague objected that the move actually replicates the dominance it seeks to avoid — by allowing Western scientific discourse alone the authority to declare itself non-universal — but I do not see how that objection succeeds unless one already assumes that scientific realism about mental disorder necessarily requires universality of ascription, rather than some weaker form of cross-cultural structural correspondence or mechanistic overlap. Could you help me identify the exact confusion in this chain? In particular, is the mistake in treating non-universality of ascription as incompatible with comparative science, or is there a deeper presupposition error in linking epistemic dominance to the complete abandonment of translation between local nosologies?
