Please adopt the role of an expert philosopher of science specialising in Kuhnian epistemology and the sociology of scientific institutions. Before you begin, take a moment to brainstorm carefully the distinctions that matter: normal science versus revolutionary science; anomaly versus crisis; semantic, methodological, and ontological incommensurability; and the difference between rational comparison of predictive success and sociological separation of research communities.

Now analyse the cognitive revolution in psychology—roughly the shift from B.F. Skinner’s radical behaviourism to the information-processing and cognitivist frameworks associated with George Miller and Noam Chomsky in the late 1950s and early 1960s—as a case study in Kuhnian paradigm shift and incommensurability. You should treat the episode in both directions.

First, map the theory onto the case: identify the shared exemplars and puzzle-solving practices that constituted behaviourist normal science in the 1940s and 1950s; specify the accumulating anomalies, including Chomsky’s critique of behaviourist accounts of language acquisition and Miller’s challenge to behaviourist memory models; and describe whether these anomalies generated a genuine Kuhnian crisis or merely competitive pluralism. Second, assess incommensurability with precision: did the term learning retain stable reference across the shift, or did the introduction of mental representations and information-processing vocabulary render behaviourist terminology untranslatable? Distinguish semantic incommensurability (changes in meaning) from ontological incommensurability (changes in what entities are taken to exist) and methodological incommensurability (changes in acceptable standards of explanation). Third, examine one institutional feature—the establishment of a separate centre for cognitive studies at Harvard in the early 1960s, structurally independent from the behaviourist department—as evidence for sociological incommensurability rather than purely conceptual incommensurability. Fourth, evaluate what the episode reveals about Kuhn’s framework: does it confirm the non-cumulative, revolutionary picture, or does the continued use of behaviourist methods in applied settings suggest that the shift was more gradual and rationally assessable than Kuhn allows?

Be disciplined about where the theory is silent or misapplies: flag any point where forcing the episode into Kuhn’s categories distorts the historical dynamics. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or suggested reading lists. Write in formal British English.
