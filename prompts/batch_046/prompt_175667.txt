I am a high school student preparing an independent study essay on Jean-Jacques Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert on the Theater. In one part of the text, Rousseau contends that theatrical spectacle turns citizens into passive spectators who applaud representations of virtue without actually practicing it. He contrasts this with republican festivals, in which the citizens themselves are the performers and there is no separated audience, so that moral sentiment is bound up with direct action rather than with the consumption of images. He seems to imply that the problem is not the moral content of particular plays—since some dramas are edifying—but the structural relation between spectator and spectacle, which severs feeling from obligation.

What I am struggling to understand is whether Rousseau takes this structural flaw to be inherent in any representation of action before an audience, or whether he thinks it is specific to the large, commercial theaters of modern cities. He praises certain collective gatherings and ancient practices, yet he condemns theater without exception. Is there an unstated premise in his argument about the importance of physical co-presence and mutual visibility among equals—something like the idea that civic education requires being seen by one's fellow citizens while acting—rather than a general theory of representation? And if that is the case, how should we interpret his approval of public festivals in which people gather but do not perform scripted roles? Does the absence of a scripted narrative preserve the civic bond, or does the absence of a separated audience do the work?

Please help me work through this tension, and please provide your explanation in American English.
