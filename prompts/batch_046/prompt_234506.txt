I am a novelist researching how institutions claim authority over collective memory, and I need to understand the dynamics of declaring a new geological epoch from the inside. Design a ninety-minute structured deliberation for twelve interdisciplinary workshop participants—graduate students in history, geology, and fiction writing—that makes them feel the force of the Anthropocene periodization problem before anyone names it.

Divide them into three committees, each with an incompatible mandate and a hidden veto charter: a stratigraphic body bound to radiometric synchrony and the formal procedures of international geological governance; an indigenous temporal council bound to continuous oral tradition and local landscape markers; and a climate-science consortium bound to global atmospheric signatures. Give them one shared task: to ratify, by collective vote, a single boundary marker—call it a Golden Spike—that will be physically inscribed on a plaque the workshop actually produces, and that will determine which of three pre-written causal narratives (global biological exchange, nuclear isotope deposition, or industrial carbon acceleration) is officially attached to the epoch's origin.

The rules must generate the conflict without facilitation. Each committee may only propose a boundary that invokes at least one criterion from another committee's charter, yet each holds an absolute veto over any proposal that violates its own epistemic charter. Run three rounds with shifting decision rules: first unanimous consensus, then simple majority, then a weighted system in which the stratigraphic body holds two votes and the others hold one each. The real but small stakes are the plaque itself and the narrative it carries; participants should discover that preferences cycle, that consensus collapses not over facts but over jurisdiction, and that selecting a date is an act of distributing narrative sovereignty—what I think of as chronostratigraphic jurisdiction—rather than measuring nature.

After the simulation, include a debrief that maps each procedural failure onto a feature of the real debate: the veto conditions onto incommensurable epistemic authorities; the shifting rules onto the absence of any natural decision procedure for geological periodization; the plaque onto the material consequences of institutional narration. Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce—the actual temporal scale of stratigraphic evidence, the real-world power asymmetries between international scientific bodies and local knowledge holders, and the retrospective construction of epochs rather than their discovery—and state exactly how you will supply those gaps verbally. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. The participants should leave seeing that the question of when the Anthropocene started conceals a prior question about who possesses the legitimate authority to declare a shared planetary past.
