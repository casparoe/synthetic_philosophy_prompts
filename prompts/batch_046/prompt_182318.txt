Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps that have argued for several years about the explanatory scope of predictive processing and the free energy principle. Camp A—the integrationists—holds that predictive coding and free-energy minimization provide a single, mechanistically continuous framework that scales from neuronal dynamics through interoception to collective social behavior, without requiring domain-specific theories at higher levels. Camp B—the pluralists—accepts predictive processing as empirically productive in low-level sensory-motor integration but maintains that extending the free energy principle to collective phenomena amounts to unfalsifiable redescription rather than genuine mechanistic explanation.

The collaboration has focused on one concrete framing case: whether interoceptive predictive coding can genuinely explain the emergence of shared affective states during collective rhythmic activities (for example, synchronized chanting or group movement), or whether such dynamics are better accounted for by social-coordination or ecological mechanisms that predictive vocabulary merely relabels.

Structure the deliverable as a formal document both camps could sign, with the following sections:

1. Agreed Claims. A numbered list of at least six propositions both sides accept—covering the formal structure of predictive coding, established findings about interoceptive inference, the phenomenon of collective synchronization that demands explanation, current evidential limitations, the criteria a successful explanation must meet (e.g., novel prediction, identifiable intervention point, mechanistic specificity), and shared methodological commitments.

2. Disagreements Table. Three columns: the issue; Camp A’s formulation of its position, worded so that Camp A would accept it; Camp B’s formulation of its position, worded so that Camp B would accept it. Address at least: (a) whether the free energy principle applies without theoretical discontinuity from single neurons to groups; (b) whether interoceptive inference in collective settings produces predictions that are empirically distinguishable from those of social-learning or coordination models; (c) whether “minimizing free energy” in this context functions as an explanatory mechanism or as a redescription of observed correlation.

3. Cruxes. Identify two or three sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. For each crux, specify: (i) the precise question; (ii) a concrete study, observation, or argument both sides agree would bear on it; (iii) Camp A’s predicted outcome or interpretation; (iv) Camp B’s predicted outcome or interpretation.

4. Verbal or Substantive? A brief assessment of whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal—considering, for instance, ambiguity in terms such as “explanation,” “mechanism,” or “scale”—with an explicit statement of which part, if any, dissolves under clarification and why the residual disagreement remains genuine.

Write in precise, formal prose suitable for circulation among researchers in cognitive science and philosophy of mind. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations. Do not invoke obscure, highly specific formalisms or little-known empirical results that a well-informed generalist could not be expected to know. Treat the collective-rhythmic-activity framing as a hypothetical test case introduced for the collaboration; it should not depend on citation to any particular study.
