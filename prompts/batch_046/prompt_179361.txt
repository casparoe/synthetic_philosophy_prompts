Question:
Quine's essay Two Dogmas of Empiricism attacks both the analytic-synthetic distinction and the reducibility of meaningful statements to immediate experience. Explain how these two critiques depend upon belief holism, and consider whether a defender of analyticity might reply by arguing that certain revisions—such as altering the logical conventions governing a scientific taxonomy—are best understood as changes in linguistic convention rather than in empirical belief.

Student's answer:
Quine rejects the analytic-synthetic distinction mainly because he accepts the Duhem-Quine thesis: all our beliefs face experience together, so no single statement is ever falsified alone. Because of this holism, there is simply no class of statements that are true purely by meaning, and therefore the distinction collapses entirely. Quine also attacks reductionism by showing that individual statements cannot be translated into direct sensory reports, as the logical empiricists—including Carnap—had claimed. Carnap tried to defend analyticity by identifying it with truth-by-linguistic-convention, but Quine demonstrates that convention itself presupposes analyticity, making the defence circular. Quine further holds, with the logical empiricists, that the meaning of a statement is fixed by its verification conditions; since verification is holistic, no statement possesses an independent meaning. From this it follows that whenever scientists revise a theoretical taxonomy—say, redefining a biological species in light of genetic data—they are never making an empirical discovery, but are always merely changing linguistic rules. Hence Quine's holism proves that scientific change is entirely a matter of pragmatic language choice, with no empirical content whatsoever.
