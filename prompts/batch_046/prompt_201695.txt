Next Thursday I present at a philosophy-of-biology workshop my argument that in synthetic microbial consortia with high-frequency horizontal gene transfer and stress-dependent metabolic coupling, evolutionary selection targets neither genes, individual cells, nor stable groups, but transient "metabolic coupling networks" that form only during specific environmental stress configurations and dissolve afterward. The audience comprises evolutionary geneticists committed to gene-selectionist reasoning, philosophers defending formal multilevel-selection models, and a systems biologist who regards unit-of-selection debates as empirically empty. They will demand concrete microbiological examples, worry about ontological inflation, and test whether my framework produces predictions that gene-level or group-level accounts cannot. Give me the objections in the order they are most likely to come, phrased in the vocabulary each faction would use, with a brief note on what I should concede immediately, what I should hold firm, and where my reply is structurally weakest. Identify the objection about cross-generational replicability that I am hoping nobody raises, and specify the weakest point in my own position—concerning how these transient networks inherit or transmit adaptive information—that I should prepare to defend openly rather than hope they overlook it.
