I have recently been asked to advise a strategic-planning committee on whether historical analogies should guide long-range contingency frameworks, and in preparing I have become struck by a methodological impasse that runs deeper than the usual partisan divisions. Among serious scholars of international relations and political historians, the debate over analogical reasoning—Munich and appeasement dynamics, Vietnam and quagmire dynamics, the so-called Thucydides trap—has persisted for decades without convergence. Both sides examine the same historical record; both concede that analogies frequently mislead; yet one camp treats them as indispensable heuristic structures that reveal deep structural dynamics (rapid power transitions, democratic vulnerability to authoritarian consolidation) which presentist detail obscures, while the other camp treats them as near-certain sources of category error, importing causal mechanisms and institutional conditions that belong to one technological and political context into another where they cannot operate. My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily an empirical disagreement about which analogies have succeeded or failed in forecasting, nor a normative disagreement about boldness versus caution, but a concealed dispute about what counts as explanation in historical and political reasoning—specifically, whether identifying an isomorphic structural pattern is sufficient for predictive or prescriptive use, or whether explanation requires tracking the specific causal pathways and contingent institutional arrangements that generated the original outcome. I want a diagnosis rather than a survey of interpretations: is the disagreement genuinely substantive, or is it verbal, with the sides using “structural similarity,” “lesson,” and “analogy” to mean different things? Is there a hidden formal or empirical question—perhaps about the base rates of power-transition conflicts, about the conditions under which institutional learning actually transfers across contexts, or about the logical structure of analogical inference itself—that is doing the hidden work? Or does the impasse reflect bedrock intuitions about the uniqueness of historical moments, or about the irreducibility of narrative understanding to structural modeling, that neither side can argue for from shared premises? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a conceptual distinction that dissolves the dispute, a formal model of analogical strength with clear failure conditions, a comparative case study designed to isolate transferable mechanisms, or an honest acceptance that the question is ill-posed? I am particularly interested in whether something structurally analogous to the conceivability argument in philosophy of mind operates here—whether the persistence of disagreement despite shared evidence and shared formal tools suggests that the sides are operating with incommensurable conceptual schemes about what history is for—rather than competing claims about a single domain of facts.
