I have been studying Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles and Max Black's well-known counterexample involving two perfectly identical spheres suspended in an otherwise empty universe, arranged symmetrically about a central point. Rather than treating this as a straightforward refutation, I would like you to argue for the following position: that Black's universe does not actually contain two distinct objects, because in the absence of any further relational structure — no third reference body, no asymmetrical background field, no intrinsic directional feature — there is insufficient ontological basis for counting 'two' entities rather than one entity described redundantly. In developing this argument, explain how saving Leibniz's principle in this way commits us to a strongly relationist conception of space, and respond to the objection that the spheres nevertheless stand in a definite, measurable distance to one another.
