I have been thinking lately, outside any formal coursework, about how history is often told as if volcanoes, plagues, or climate shifts were characters with intentions—how an eruption might “reshape” a society, or a pathogen might “force” a political collapse. In my community discussion group—about ten adults with no philosophy background, meeting in a library room for ninety minutes—we have been trying to decide whether this language is merely a metaphor or whether it actually grants non-human forces a kind of agency that crowds human choice out of the explanation. I want a structured in-class activity, closer to a simulation or card-based role-play than a lecture, that makes us physically feel that explanatory crowding: we should work through a concrete scenario, perhaps a society facing internal division together with a sudden environmental shock, using rules that require us to distribute a fixed pool of “causal points” between human decisions and non-human forces such as drought, volcanic winter, or disease transmission. The mechanics should begin by making human agency feel obviously sufficient, then—through hidden roles, escalating constraints, or a scoring system that rewards parsimony—gradually push us to assign more and more explanatory weight to climate and germs until we have accounted for the outcome almost without anyone being responsible for it.

Please include specific roles or rules, a minute-by-minute timeline for the session, materials that require no technology, and a debrief that maps our choices onto the philosophical tension between causal explanation and moral agency, especially the perils of environmental determinism. I also need an honest note about which feature of the idea—likely the normative danger that letting nature “act” can excuse human cruelty or neglect—the activity itself cannot reproduce and must be supplied by facilitated discussion, plus a frank prediction of what will go wrong the first time I run it, particularly if participants resist surrendering human-centered explanations, and how I should handle that resistance without breaking the simulation’s force.
