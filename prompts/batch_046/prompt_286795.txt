Consider the following passage, composed around 1883 in the dense, neo-Aristotelian prose of a minor British idealist essay on artifact ontology. The author attempts to dissolve the classic statue-and-clay puzzle by introducing a distinction between substantial matter and what he calls “formal distribution”:

"If the statue is taken for a substance, we are deceived by its superficial unity. The clay alone enjoys substantial continuity; the statue bears only accidental form, being merely the temporary imposition of an aesthetic concept upon recalcitrant matter. Yet this imposition is not haphazard. Between the arranged particles and the governing idea there subsists an invariant relational structure—a formal signature, as it were—that endures even when the macroscopic figure is obliterated. Suppose the clay is ground to powder and every portion is reshaped into a miniature replica that preserves the same proportional relations to the original concept. The substantial clay remains numerically one, but the formal signature is now distributed across many. The true difficulty, then, is not whether we have one statue or several, but whether substantial continuity and formal distribution can ever coincide within a single object."

I need help understanding the argument’s internal structure. Specifically:

1. The author denies that the statue is a substance, yet he attributes to it an “invariant relational structure” capable of surviving total pulverization and replication in miniature form. Is he treating this structure as a universal, a relational property of each part, or something else? And does this attribution undermine his claim that the statue has “only accidental form”?

2. How does this view differ from standard Aristotelian hylomorphism, in which form is precisely what makes a substance the kind of thing it is? Here form seems to be both non-substantial (merely accidental) and yet capable of being “distributed” across divided matter—does that mean form is being reconceived as a pattern rather than as a principle of unity?

3. The passage ends with the claim that the puzzle arises from the false assumption that “the conditions for being one substance and the conditions for being one artifact must be identical.” Is the author arguing that once we separate substantial continuity (the clay) from formal distribution (the statue’s pattern), there is no longer any coincidence of distinct objects in the same place—only a single substance bearing, at different times or in different parts, different formal signatures?

Please explain these points as clearly as possible. I am not looking for a survey of contemporary literature, but rather an internal parsing of the author’s reasoning: in particular, whether his “formal signature” is intended to solve the material-constitution problem or merely to relocate it from the level of whole objects to the level of part-whole relations.
