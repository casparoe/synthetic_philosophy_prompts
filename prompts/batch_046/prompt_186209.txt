Frame the problem as follows: every readable instance of the lowercase Latin letter "a" belongs to a single category, yet no two share an identical geometric description—some have enclosed counters, others do not; some are double-storied, others single-storied; handwritten variants can approach the shape of a "d", an "o", or even a Greek alpha without losing their identity. What would a theory of letterform essence—of what makes every "a" an "a"—have to accomplish to count as adequate?

Provide the full desiderata specification. The list should cover: (i) the recognition cases it must handle, including extreme stylization, partial occlusion, and cross-script analogues (e.g., Cyrillic equivalents that occupy a similar functional niche); (ii) the distinctions it must not collapse, particularly between type-level identity and token-level resemblance, between permissible stylistic variation and categorical misidentification, and between human perceptual judgment and purely statistical clustering; (iii) the conceptual puzzles it must resolve rather than bypass, such as why there appears to be no necessary and sufficient geometric feature shared by all "a"s, and how style can vary continuously while category membership remains discrete; (iv) the constraints it must respect from adjacent domains—for example, that letter categories are learnable by children without explicit rule instruction, and that recognition remains robust despite the absence of invariant retinal projection; and (v) any boundary conditions that expose hidden commitments, for instance whether the theory must also explain why a deliberately malformed "a" in an avant-garde typeface is still readable as that letter rather than as decorative noise.

For each desideratum, state the motivating case or failure mode: what specific observation or counterexample shows that omitting this requirement produces an inadequate theory. Then identify the single desideratum that does the most work in excluding the broadest class of existing approaches—whether feature-template models, pure distributional clustering accounts, or conventionalist accounts of letter identity—and explain precisely why. Finally, distinguish which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable for any theory worthy of the name, which might be traded for explanatory power elsewhere, and whether the non-negotiable set is jointly satisfiable or whether it reveals an inherent tension in the very idea of a "letterform essence." A table summarizing the requirements, their motivating cases, and their negotiability is acceptable. Do not provide a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; keep the weight entirely on the criteria and their justification.
