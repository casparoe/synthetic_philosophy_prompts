Exam question:
Explain the "wrong kind of reasons" problem as it arises for belief formation and for moral motivation. In the belief case, why are practical considerations (for example, believing one will recover from an illness because that belief aids recovery) considered the wrong kind of reason? In the moral case, why might acting from self-interest—such as returning a wallet only to avoid social punishment—count as acting for the wrong kind of reason, even when the action itself is correct? Then consider a predictive-processing system that revises its world-model through a weighted combination of sensory evidence and reward-prediction error. Does this system face an analog of the wrong-kind problem? If so, is it closer to the epistemic version, the practical version, or both? Defend your answer.

Student's answer:
The wrong-kind-of-reasons problem arises whenever an agent relies on considerations that do not match the constitutive aim of the attitude being formed. For belief, the constitutive aim is truth; therefore any reason that does not bear on truth—such as the practical benefit of feeling hopeful—is automatically the wrong kind. For moral action, the constitutive aim is also truth, specifically the truth about what morality requires; hence any reason that does not track that moral truth, including self-interested motives like avoiding punishment, is the wrong kind.

Evidentialists solve the belief version by allowing practical reasons to enter belief-formation whenever they genuinely increase the agent's confidence, so long as the belief remains responsive to evidence overall. Applying this framework to the predictive system, its world-model has the constitutive aim of accurate prediction. Because reward-prediction errors serve practical survival goals rather than tracking environmental states, they are the wrong kind of reason for updating the model—this is the epistemic analog. Once we restrict the system's updates to sensory evidence alone, we eliminate the wrong-kind problem for both the system and for human belief, since both are governed by the same single aim of aligning representation with truth. The moral version does not apply to the system because machines lack moral agency, but the underlying structure is identical.
