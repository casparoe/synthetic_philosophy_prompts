I'm drafting a chapter on institutional analogies in theories of intertemporal agency, and I'm stuck on an analogy that keeps appearing in the behavioral-economics literature on weakness of will. Hyperbolic discounting is frequently modeled as a divided legislature: the present self is treated like a lower house with immediate veto power, the future self like an upper house with broader temporal jurisdiction, and akrasia is described as the lower house overriding the upper on a specific bill. The analogy is then used to license precommitment strategies—binding rules, commitment devices, and the like—on the grounds that constitutional constraints are legitimate precisely because they check impulsive majorities.

Could you audit this mapping in detail? First, give me an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the constitution, the legislative procedure, the veto override mechanism, and the electorate or sovereign? Second, identify the disanalogies that matter specifically for the normative conclusion. For instance, does the present self have anything like democratic legitimacy over the future self in the way a lower house might have over an upper? Is hyperbolic discounting evidence of a genuine plurality of agents, or is it a single agent with dynamically inconsistent preferences, and does that distinction collapse the institutional comparison? Third, please examine whether the analogy smuggles in the conclusion it claims to support: it assumes that binding the present self is analogous to a well-designed constitutional check, but without an argument for why the future self's preferences should have hierarchical authority, the normative move looks like it's carried by the source domain rather than earned in the target.

I've also been considering a more niche institutional variant—modeling the temporal relation not as bicameral hierarchy but as federalism with non-overlapping jurisdictions, where different temporal slices hold sovereign authority over specific domains rather than competing over the same action. Does this federalist framing improve the correspondence, or does it obscure the fact that akrasia typically involves direct conflict over an identical choice? Please evaluate both analogies on that point.

Finally, I want this analysis to be self-critical: state explicitly what would change your mind—whether an empirical result about the structure of intertemporal choice, or a conceptual argument about the unity of agency, could cause you to reject the institutional framing altogether. And please flag where your reasoning is most likely to fail, for instance if hyperbolic discounting turns out to be largely an artifact of task framing rather than a deep feature of practical rationality.
