I have been teaching the Peirce-James exchange on truth for over a decade, and the same stalemate recurs every seminar. Peirce maintains that truth is the opinion that inquiry, if pursued far enough, is destined to produce; James maintains that a belief is true just insofar as it works, proves useful, or satisfies in the long run and on the whole. The students split into two camps. One camp insists this is a substantive disagreement about whether truth is singular and mind-independent or plural and practice-dependent. The other camp insists it is largely verbal: Peirce is offering an analysis of the concept of truth, while James is offering an account of the conditions under which we are entitled to call a belief true, and these are compatible projects that happen to share a word. A third, smaller group suggests that beneath the surface lies an empirical disagreement about whether scientific communities actually converge on unique answers, but they never specify whether they mean convergence in the long run across all possible inquirers or only local convergence within a given disciplinary matrix, and without that specification the claim is untestable. I am not looking for a historical survey or a bibliography. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure. Is it verbal, substantive, or disguised empirical? If it is verbal, what exact distinction—between semantics and pragmatics, between alethic monism and pluralism, or between normative and descriptive theories of truth—would dissolve it? If it is substantive, which premise is actually in conflict: the claim that truth is a regulative ideal, the claim that truth is constituted by practical success, or a hidden premise about the unity of science? And whatever your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress. Would it take a formal model of inquiry convergence, a conceptual distinction that shows the two projects are orthogonal, or an argument that one of them is not actually talking about truth at all?
