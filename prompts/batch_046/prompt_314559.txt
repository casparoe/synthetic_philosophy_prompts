I've been thinking about aesthetic testimony lately, particularly through the lens of calibration and belief updating. In philosophy, there's a long-running debate about whether we can genuinely learn about the aesthetic value of an artwork through others' judgments—say, a critic's claim that a film achieves a kind of sublimity—or whether such testimony only gives us predictive data about how we ourselves are likely to react upon direct encounter. The familiar positions are well-trodden: some hold that aesthetic properties are response-dependent and therefore not the kind of thing that can be transmitted by testimony, while others argue that expert critics function as reliable instruments, much like thermometers, allowing us to form justified beliefs about value we have not yet experienced firsthand.

But I want to push this into somewhat weirder territory. Suppose we take the rationalist emphasis on calibration seriously and model aesthetic judgment as a forecasting problem. If I treat a respected critic's praise as Bayesian evidence, I have to decide exactly what hypothesis it updates: that the work is objectively beautiful, that it will produce a particular phenomenological state in me, or that a reference class of people with tastes similar to mine will endorse it. Each framing seems to break something important. The first assumes an aesthetic realism that many find implausible; the second collapses testimony into a mere prediction of my own future preferences, which seems to erase the normative authority of the critic's judgment; the third makes aesthetic belief a statistical aggregation that may have nothing to do with my actual first-person encounter with the work. There's also a deeper coherence worry: if I systematically defer to experts in domains where I have direct access—like taste or visual experience—do I risk a Dutch-book-style vulnerability, holding beliefs that I wouldn't wager on in light of my own direct evidence?

I'm taking a course that touches on epistemology of testimony and value theory, and I'm trying to write something that doesn't just rehearse the standard dialectic. Write an essay on whether aesthetic testimony can ever carry genuine normative authority, or whether it is necessarily reduced to predictive calibration about one's own future reactions. Weigh the main arguments on both sides, but reach a definite conclusion and justify it. In particular, address whether treating expert aesthetic judgment as Bayesian evidence creates a kind of calibration paradox that undermines either the objectivity of aesthetic value or the autonomy of first-person experience. Don't just survey positions—defend a verdict.
