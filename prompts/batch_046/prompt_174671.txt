I’ve been working through the boundary problem in a seminar on democratic theory, and my study group keeps returning to a version of the all-affected principle that seems to collapse into something conceptually unworkable. I want to check whether there is a hidden equivocation or a scope shift we are missing, because each premise feels individually plausible but the conclusion is one I cannot accept.

Here is the argument as we reconstructed it. (1) A collective decision is democratically legitimate only if every party whose interests are materially affected by its outcome has an equal procedural voice in making it. (2) “Materially affected” is defined causally, not geographically or nationally: if a policy changes the welfare trajectory of an entity, that entity falls within the affected set regardless of citizenship, residence, or temporal location. (3) A long-term climate or infrastructure decision made in 2025 will alter the welfare trajectory of specific individuals who will not exist until 2125. (4) By the non-identity problem, we cannot identify which specific future people are affected, but we can identify that some specific people will be affected; therefore democratic legitimacy requires that the decision-making body include representatives for that affected set. (5) Since the specific individuals are unknown, representation must be assigned structurally — say, through a recursive proxy system in which present voters delegate a fraction of their voting weight to trustees charged with representing future interests. (6) But those trustees themselves exercise power over people who do not yet exist and who therefore cannot hold them accountable; applying (1) recursively requires that the future affected parties have a voice in selecting their own trustees, which is impossible ex ante. (7) Therefore either the all-affected principle applies infinitely and democracy becomes structurally impossible for intertemporal decisions, or it applies nowhere and the boundary of the demos collapses entirely — leaving us with no principled reason to exclude non-citizen residents, sentient animals affected by policy, or distant future generations represented only by algorithmic forecasts.

Which step should we reject? I am especially reluctant to give up (1) or (2), since they capture the intuition that democratic authority derives from affected interests rather than from arbitrary borders. But I also want to avoid the conclusion that long-term governance is conceptually impossible. Is there a subtle modal slide between (3) and (4), or does the problem lie in treating causal effect as sufficient for procedural inclusion? If we reject (5), what do we pay — do we simply accept that democracy is necessarily presentist, and if so, how do we answer the charge that presentism is just a disguised form of arbitrary exclusion?
