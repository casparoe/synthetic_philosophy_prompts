Since I was recently appointed to the advisory board for a national museum of historical reckoning, I have been forced to articulate gut reactions about transgenerational culpability that I previously held only vaguely. Here are my verdicts on whether a present-day nation or its citizens can be said to bear collective guilt—political, moral, or metaphysical in Jaspers’s taxonomy—for past state wrongs.

First, I think present-day Germans, including those born long after 1945, properly bear political and moral guilt for the Holocaust; the legal and institutional continuity of the Federal Republic with the Reich, combined with unbroken transmission of benefits from expropriation, makes denial feel like moral evasion.

Second, I think present-day Americans bear analogous collective moral responsibility for chattel slavery and Jim Crow, not as personal criminal guilt but as an enduring burden that shapes present obligations.

Third, I think present-day citizens of Turkey do not bear guilt for the Armenian genocide, despite the Republic being the legal successor to the Ottoman Empire; the ideological rupture of 1923, the absence of institutional celebration of the perpetrators, and the exclusion of victims from the present polity all seem to break the chain.

Fourth, I think democratic citizens who actively consented to an unjust war—say, British voters who supported the 2003 Iraq invasion—share political guilt for that decision, but descendants born afterward who never had a voice do not inherit it personally, though they inherit the state’s obligations.

Fifth, I think that if a nation has formally repudiated a crime, replaced its governing apparatus root and branch, and made full reparations—imagine a hypothetical post-authoritarian state that dissolved its security services and returned all expropriated wealth—then collective guilt should be considered extinguished, leaving only historical memory.

Yet I keep returning to a pair that undermines this framework. I feel strongly that Australians today should acknowledge collective responsibility for the Stolen Generations, despite formal apologies and the passage of generations. But I feel equally strongly that modern Egyptians do not bear guilt for the forced labor of the pyramids, even though the Egyptian state persists and citizens live amid those monuments. The difference cannot be mere temporal distance, since the Stolen Generations are closer in time than the Holocaust yet I assign guilt in both directions inconsistently. Nor can it be institutional continuity alone, since Germany, Turkey, and Egypt all exhibit continuity but I assign guilt only in the first case. And if I invoke the persistence of structural harm to explain Australia, I must explain why Germans still bear guilt despite massive reparations and structural transformation.

Is there a precise principle—perhaps involving narrative incorporation into national identity, the presence of victim-descendant communities within the polity, or a distinction between guilt that demands acknowledgment versus guilt that demands atonement—that captures all or most of these verdicts? What does it say about a case I have not resolved: a present-day citizen of Chile who benefits from land seized during the Pinochet dictatorship but whose own family actively resisted the regime? And which of my verdicts—especially the Turkish and Egyptian judgments, or the claim that full reparations extinguish guilt—must be abandoned if the principle is to remain coherent?

Please separate what is settled in the philosophical literature on collective and transgenerational guilt—such as the basic distinction between criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt—from what remains genuinely contested, particularly regarding whether democratic consent, institutional continuity, intergenerational benefit transmission, or narrative self-understanding is the decisive factor. Do not present any contested interpretation, including the direct application of Jaspers’s taxonomy beyond its original context, as if it were the consensus view.
