You are a philosopher of science with expertise in the history of biology and the foundations of computation. Before you begin, briefly brainstorm the different dimensions on which reasoning can be “inverted”—that is, in which successful performance is achieved without the kind of comprehension that is traditionally treated as a prerequisite. Then rank the following five conceptual moves—all belonging to the broad theme of competence without comprehension—by how radically they sever performance from understanding, using 1 for the least radical inversion and 5 for the most radical. For each item, state its rank and in one sentence classify the inversion as primarily ontological (about what exists), epistemological (about what is known), or methodological (about how we should study the system):

1. Darwin’s claim that cumulative natural selection produces organisms with the appearance of designed competence despite no designer possessing foresight or biological knowledge.
2. Turing’s claim that a universal computing machine can execute any algorithmically describable task without possessing semantic understanding of the symbols it manipulates.
3. The claim—realized in evolutionary computation—that running a Darwinian selection process on a Turing-complete substrate yields engineered solutions that neither the human programmer nor the evolutionary process itself comprehends.
4. The thesis that human scientific cognition is itself the product of blind variation and selective retention, so that our own grasp of Darwinian and Turing-style inversions is a competence that arose without any prior comprehension of evolutionary or computational theory.
5. The “hidden comprehension” objection, which attempts to reverse the inversion by arguing that in both the Darwinian and Turing cases comprehension is not absent but merely relocated—to the environment’s selection pressures, or to the human programmer who designed the machine.

After giving your 1-to-5 ranking, identify which entry you placed at rank 3 and explain in two sentences why it is the most ambiguous case: does it constitute a genuinely new inversion, or is it merely a compound of the first two? Conclude by assigning each of the five entries a confidence score from 0 to 1 indicating how strongly you think the inversion actually holds, and briefly justify the lowest score you assigned.
