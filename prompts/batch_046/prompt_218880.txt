I'm writing the final exam for my upper-level moral philosophy seminar — PHIL 340, though there's a typo on the syllabus that says PHIL 430 — and I need an essay question that really drills into particularism vs generalism without letting students just parrot back the standard summaries of Dancy or Hooker. I was arguing with a friend over coffee about this weird tension particularists face: they say moral reasons are context-sensitive and don't stack up into universal principles, but then how do they explain moral learning? If there's no principle linking cruelty to wrongness, how does someone learn to recognize cruelty as a reason in a brand new case? And if two people disagree about whether some feature counts as a reason — same non-moral facts, opposite moral verdict — does particularism just reduce that to a bare clash of intuitions with no rational way to adjudicate?

I want the exam question to force students to deal with that specifically, using Dancy's idea of the 'shape' of situations — where a feature can switch valence depending on context — but they can't just name-drop it; they have to actually deploy the machinery. The question should ask them to defend or attack the claim that particularism collapses into intuitionism unless it smuggles in some hidden meta-principle about salience. Please write the question for a 90-minute closed-book exam, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme that seperates an A from a B. The scheme should list the specific conceptual moves a top answer must make — like whether generalists can accommodate context-sensitivity through defeasible ceteris paribus clauses — and should name the mistakes that should cost marks. Don't put any bibliography requests or citation prompts in the exam text itself, since they won't have books.
