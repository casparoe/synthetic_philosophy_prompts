In my graduate seminar on moral change, discussions about the disappearance of practices such as dueling and chattel slavery have settled into a predictable standoff between those who explain the shift through material interests and rational argumentation, and those who follow the position associated with Bernard Williams and developed in Kwame Anthony Appiah's analysis of honor codes, according to which reputation economies and status preservation do the central explanatory work. Despite extensive historical documentation, the debate has stalled: each side can absorb the same archival evidence by redescribing honor as a strategic interest or interest as an honor-preserving mechanism. Before asking students to adjudicate the dispute, I want a structural diagnosis rather than another historical case study. Is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or a case of philosophers and historians answering different questions under the single heading of causal explanation? In particular, does the impasse depend on an unexamined ambiguity in the concept of honor—specifically, whether honor operates as a thick moral concept with intrinsic normative weight or merely as a social-coordination technology embedded in aristocratic patronage networks? And given your diagnosis, what kind of conceptual clarification, formal distinction, or change of framing—rather than further empirical data—would actually count as progress? Please respond in American English, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
