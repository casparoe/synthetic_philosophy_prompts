I am a novelist invited to deliver the opening address at the retirement symposium for Dr. Alistair Venn, a cognitive scientist whose career was devoted to modelling self-reference in distributed neural networks. The audience is divided: on one side sit philosophers of mind and systems theorists intimately acquainted with Hofstadter's account of the 'I' as a strange loop—a symbol that activates other symbols in a pattern with no central controller—while on the other sit family members, humanities alumni, and local benefactors for whom the self is still an obvious, private possession. Venn himself has always rejected talk of an enduring inner essence, yet he is deeply moved by the loyalty and continuity that human lives display. I wish the address to honour that tension without resolving it into sentimentality or reductionism. Crucially, I want the speech's own structure to perform the philosophical idea rather than merely describe it: the speaker's 'I' should be shown to depend upon the audience's 'you', upon Venn's theories, and upon the occasion itself, so that the address can only complete its meaning in being heard together. It must be warm, accessible to those without technical training, and rigorous enough that the specialists recognise the architecture of a self-referential loop being enacted in real time. Please write a fifteen-minute address that observes these constraints, never asserting that anyone present harbours a hidden, unchanging soul, but allowing that patterns of reference can become stable enough to warrant affection and gratitude.
