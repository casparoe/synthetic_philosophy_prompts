I'm auditing my intuitions about species membership for a philosophy of biology discussion, and I'd like to know whether they cohere around a single principle or whether I need to abandon one. Here are my stable verdicts. Lions and tigers can produce viable hybrids in captivity, yet Panthera leo and Panthera tigris still strike me as genuinely distinct species; the hybridization feels like an artificial exception that doesn't erase a deeper organizational boundary. Two populations of deep-sea archaea separated for geological timescales, unable to interbreed sexually but maintaining near-identical metabolic architectures through horizontal gene transfer, seem clearly conspecific—perhaps more clearly so than two recently isolated mammal populations that have diverged only in morphology. In a ring-species chain where population A breeds with B, B with C, but A and C are reproductively isolated, I want to say A and C are different species, yet I resist treating B as belonging to two species simultaneously, which pulls me away from treating interbreeding as a transitive relation.

A synthetic organism engineered with a minimal genome that can reproduce with wild-type bacteria but lacks any shared evolutionary history strikes me as belonging to the same species if it integrates into the wild population's gene flow, but not if it remains reproductively isolated despite functional similarity. Meanwhile, two asexual lineages that have diverged considerably in nuclear genome but retain the ability to exchange plasmids feel like one species to me, even though they share almost no recent common ancestry in the usual sense. Is there a coherent account—perhaps centered on functional or organizational integration rather than on genealogy or reproductive isolation alone—that delivers all of these judgments? Or does accommodating the ring-species case force me to abandon either the synthetic-organism verdict or the archaea verdict? And what should the resulting principle say about a case I'm unsure of: a fossil lineage with no reproductive isolation possible among intermediates, where morphology shifts gradually from one endpoint to another that is as different as any pair of living species?
