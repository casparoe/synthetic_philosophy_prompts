I hold that honoring sunk costs is sometimes rational, but not for the usual psychological reasons: specifically, in repeated interactions with observable actions, continuing a failing project can function as a credible commitment signal that deters future exploitation, and the expected strategic value of maintaining a reputation for resolve can outweigh the incremental direct cost of continuing — the past investment is honored only instrumentally, as observable evidence of that resolve, not because it carries intrinsic normative weight. What would genuinely count against this? I want the defeaters sorted by severity: formal results in evolutionary or repeated game theory showing that observable continuation cannot separate commitment types in the relevant structures; empirical evidence that observers in high-stakes domains fail to update beliefs about an agent’s resolve based on abandonment versus continuation; conceptual distinctions that prevent this signaling rationale from collapsing into an unfalsifiable post-hoc rationalization of any observed escalation; and any behavioral or economic finding that the signaling benefit is systematically swamped by the direct cost. Which of these would defenders of the signaling view themselves concede as damaging, and which could they absorb by qualification? Finally, is there a live risk that the view becomes insulated — for instance, by allowing any continuation to be interpreted as strategic signaling — and if so, does that insulation undermine its claim to be a normative theory rather than a descriptive excuse?
