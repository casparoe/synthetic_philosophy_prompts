In my philosophy of cognitive science seminar, a recurring analogy compares conceptual slippage in microdomains—such as Hofstadter-style letter-string analogies or the shadow-box lattice domain I work on, where analogies map light-source positions to shadow shapes across 3×3 grids—to a knot tied in a rope. The claim is that applying analogies pulls different ends of the rope, so the knot shifts, tightens, or loosens but never fully unravels; therefore, the concept has no fixed core. From this, colleagues argue that formal architectures like Copycat-style rule systems with fixed slippage parameters are like photographing the knot from one angle: they capture a static state but destroy the essential fluidity, so formalism should be abandoned for a fully dynamic, non-representational account.

Provide a correspondence table mapping the knot, the rope, the pulling ends, the tightening or loosening, the resistance to full unraveling, and the photographer's frame onto the conceptual dynamics of the microdomain. Then identify the precise disanalogies that matter for the anti-formalist conclusion: Is analogical "pulling" agent-driven in the way rope-pulling is? Does the knot's structural integrity actually presuppose a hidden fixed form that a parameterized formal system could recover? Does the photograph objection depend on assuming that formal rules must be static rather than iteratively updated? Take a committed position: once these disanalogies are weighed, does the conclusion that formalism is doomed survive, or was the knot analogy carrying it by treating fluidity as inherently uncapturable? If you think a different analogy—perhaps drawn from topology or dynamical systems—better serves conceptual slippage in these microdomains without smuggling in anti-formalist commitments, specify it and defend the substitution.
