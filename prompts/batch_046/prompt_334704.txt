I recently discussed the doomsday argument with a friend. The reasoning, as I understand it, is that if humanity eventually numbers in the trillions, then my being among the first hundred billion people is statistically implausible — comparable to drawing a very low number from a vast urn. From this, one might infer that human extinction is likely within a few centuries.

My friend objected that the argument assumes I am randomly selected from all humans who will ever live, which she finds incoherent: I am not picking myself out of a hat, but am the specific result of my parents, my era, and my particular history. I replied that random selection is not required; one need only ask what fraction of all people live near the beginning if the total is enormous, and notice that it is minuscule. She countered that this logic applies to any arbitrary category — for instance, being the first person in one's family with a particular eye color — so it fails to single out humanity's lifespan as special.

I then raised a further consideration: some thinkers hold that there is a hidden assumption about how to weight possible worlds — specifically, that worlds containing more observers are more probable precisely because they contain more observers. If that is correct, then living in a long-lasting, populous civilization is not surprising at all; in fact, it might be expected. My friend said this sounds like inventing observers merely to avoid an unwelcome conclusion, and noted that if the principle were true, I should be astonished not to find myself in a galaxy already filled with life. I responded that perhaps the principle governs reasoning only when one lacks specific evidence about one's location.

Please adjudicate our exchange move by move. For each step, indicate whether the point is clearly mistaken, mostly sound, or somewhere in between, and briefly justify your rating. Then say which of us is closer to being right overall, and identify anything important that both of us are neglecting. I am not asking for citations or named authorities — just a direct evaluation of the reasoning as presented.
