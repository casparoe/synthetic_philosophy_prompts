As a retired physicist recently turned to philosophy of history, I have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern in assessments of past scientific practice. A commentator condemns a nineteenth- or early twentieth-century researcher for violating a norm—say, failing to secure informed consent or neglecting statistical controls—and, when challenged for judging the past by present standards, responds that the norm was not anachronistic, because it can be reconstructed from the researcher’s own methodological writings. Yet the reconstructed norm is invariably articulated in modern conceptual language, and the researcher’s actual conduct is then evaluated against this reconstructed version and found deficient. I have observed this in debates about clinical experimentation before 1945 and about eugenics-era data collection. Is there a specific concept for this precise oscillation—imposing a contemporary framework while defending the judgment as an internal reconstruction of the subject’s own rational standards—distinct from ordinary presentism and from charitable reinterpretation? If no established term captures it exactly, how should its boundaries be drawn: in particular, how does one distinguish it from a legitimate recovery of implicit norms?
