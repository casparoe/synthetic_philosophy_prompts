I have recently begun designing subjective well-being instruments for a palliative-care outcomes study, and I keep encountering patient reports of "feeling good" that seem phenomenologically incommensurable: the blank relief of nausea subsiding, the warm recognition of a visitor's voice, the brisk satisfaction of finishing a puzzle, the dull contentment of warmth after a bath. This has drawn me into the heterogeneity problem for hedonism—what, if anything, do these pleasures share?

Draft the joint statement that two opposing philosophical camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on this problem. Name the camps precisely: the Phenomenal Uniformists, who hold that every pleasure contains a shared, non-intentional qualitative character (a distinctive "hedonic tone" or pleasantness analogous to a sensory quality), and the Intentional Heterogenists, who hold that pleasures share no intrinsic phenomenal feature but are unified only by being attitudes of enjoyment directed at radically diverse intentional contents.

Format the document as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims—about hedonism's core thesis, about the diversity of experiences called pleasures, about what is not in dispute (for instance, that intensity varies, and that pleasure is not reducible to a single bodily sensation like warmth). Second, a disagreements table: for each remaining dispute, provide the formulation that the Uniformists would accept for their own side and the formulation that the Heterogenists would accept for theirs, phrased neutrally enough that neither side can reject the wording of its own position. Third, identify the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving one would genuinely move at least one camp. For each crux, specify a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts about that case. I have in mind three cruxes in particular: whether clinical anhedonia involves the loss of one quality or the global failure of an enjoyment-attitude across contents; whether so-called "mixed" pleasures (e.g., the burning pleasure of very spicy food, or bittersweet remembrance) contain blended qualities or competing intentional attitudes; and whether asking subjects to rate "how pleasant" disparate experiences on a single scale presupposes a shared metric or merely constructs an ordinal comparison. If any part of the dispute turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, say exactly which part and why. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; write only what both camps could endorse from reasoning alone.
