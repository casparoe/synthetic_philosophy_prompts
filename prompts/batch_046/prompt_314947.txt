I am preparing a presentation for my sixth-form philosophy and earth-sciences seminar on the Anthropocene debate. Would you please write an English sonnet — fourteen lines in iambic pentameter, with the rhyme scheme ABAB CDCD EFEF GG — that takes as its subject the dispute over when the Anthropocene began and, more fundamentally, who possesses the authority to declare it has begun. The poem should reference at least three competing stratigraphic proposals — such as the 1610 Orbis spike, the 1784 industrial carbon signal associated with Watt’s steam engine, and the mid-twentieth-century radiogenic and synthetic markers that underpin the Crawford Lake Golden Spike proposal — but should treat them not as neutral measurements, rather as rival narratives about human planetary dominance. In the octave, construct the impression that geology merely reads the rock record and uncovers an objective boundary waiting to be named. At the volta in line nine, the poem must turn sharply to expose that choosing one marker is an institutional act: a decision by stratigraphic commissions, not a voice emerging from the strata themselves. The closing couplet should crystallise the precise conceptual tension — that to name an epoch is simultaneously to describe a layer of sediment and to assert which human activities count as geologically definitive — and should also leave open the niche question of whether "Anthropocene" ought properly to rank as an epoch at all, or only as a briefer stratigraphic event. Please use British English throughout, and write with sufficient conceptual precision that a student of historical geology or philosophy of science would recognise the stakes rather than merely the terminology.
