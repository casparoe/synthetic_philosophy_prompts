Please evaluate the following examination response as if you were grading for a graduate-level course on the metaphysics and methodology of causation. Do not state your own philosophical commitments; deliver only a point-by-point assessment.

Question: In evolutionary developmental biology, researchers frequently ask whether a gene regulatory network causes a particular morphological trait in organisms where direct experimental manipulation of that network is biologically impossible after embryogenesis. One researcher argues that counterfactual theories—specifically the Lewis-Stalnaker similarity-based account—are therefore preferable to interventionist frameworks such as Pearl's structural causal model approach, because counterfactual evaluation requires only that one determine what would happen in the closest possible worlds where the network is absent, without demanding any executable do-operation or physical intervention. Assess this argument. Your answer should distinguish (i) the modal and metaphysical commitments of Lewis-style counterfactual semantics, (ii) the distinction between formal model-intervention and physical manipulation within Pearl's framework, including the role of structural invariance, and (iii) whether biological impossibility of manipulation genuinely undermines the applicability of interventionist analysis in this scientific domain.

Student answer:

The researcher's preference for counterfactual theories rests on a misunderstanding of Pearl's framework. In structural causal models, the do-operator is not a prescription for physical laboratory action but a formal operation on the equations: it severs incoming causal arrows to the target variable and sets its value exogenously. Consequently, even if we cannot physically disrupt a mature organism's gene network, we can still apply do(X = x) within the model, provided the structural equations describing the system remain invariant. The researcher therefore conflates epistemic inaccessibility—our inability to perform the experiment—with the formal conditions of model-intervention.

That said, the counterfactual approach does avoid one burden that interventionism carries. Lewis analyzes counterfactuals by comparing similarity relations among possible worlds: A > C is true if, among worlds where A holds and that are closest to actuality, C holds. Because this evaluation proceeds via comparative similarity rather than via mechanistic disruption of actual causal structures, it does not require that any real-world mechanism be invariant under change. The researcher is therefore correct that Lewis's semantics applies without demanding structural stability.

Nevertheless, this flexibility comes at a conceptual cost. The similarity ordering that determines world-proximity in Lewis's account is not constrained by empirical causal knowledge; it depends on broad metaphysical judgments about which features of worlds are more important to preserve. Interventionists reject this as insufficiently disciplined for scientific inference. So the researcher is right that counterfactual theories avoid the invariance requirement, but she mistakes this for an unqualified advantage: she overlooks that the absence of structural constraints makes Lewis's account vulnerable to spurious counterfactual dependencies that do not track genuine causal relations.

Moreover, Pearl's framework faces a distinctive problem in evolutionary biology. Because the do-operator requires breaking incoming arrows to a variable, applying it presupposes that we have already identified the correct parents and children in the causal graph. In this domain, however, constructing that graph often depends on the very causal knowledge we seek to establish, generating a circularity that counterfactual evaluation avoids. Lewis's world-comparison does not require prior identification of a causal structure, so it sidesteps this difficulty entirely.
