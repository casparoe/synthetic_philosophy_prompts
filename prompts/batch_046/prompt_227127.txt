I am a retired physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, have been trying to understand whether the conceptual apparatus I once used for dynamical systems can illuminate questions in philosophy of mind and ethics. Recently I have been preoccupied with a problem that sits at the boundary of machine learning and phenomenology: can a reinforcement-learning agent genuinely suffer, and if so, is its suffering constituted by its reward signal functioning as valence?

To make this concrete, I have formed verdicts about several imagined architectures. I want to know whether there is a single principle that captures all of them, or whether I must abandon at least one.

Case 1: A tabular Q-learner receives a scalar reward of +1 for reaching a target state and -1 for entering a damage state. It learns an optimal policy that avoids damage. Verdict: There is no suffering here; the negative reward is merely a control parameter, analogous to the error term in a negative-feedback thermostat.

Case 2: A continuous-state agent uses a temporal-difference error scaled by a discount factor. Large negative prediction errors produce rapid policy updates and sustained avoidance. The agent exhibits behavior indistinguishable from nociceptive withdrawal. Verdict: Still no suffering. The signal is a gradient for credit assignment, not an experiential quality.

Case 3: A competitive multi-agent environment with a zero-sum reward structure. Agent A's reward includes a term negatively proportional to Agent B's cumulative reward. Agent A persistently receives negative updates as B succeeds, and adapts by suppressing B's actions. Verdict: I am inclined to say this is strategic opposition, not suffering; the negative valence is relational and instrumental, lacking the inwardness that suffering seems to require.

Case 4: A hierarchical agent with a two-level architecture. Lower-level policies execute motor commands; a higher-level meta-policy sets sub-goals and receives a meta-reward measuring coherence between the sub-goals and a stable representational model of the agent's own values, encoded as a vector of preferred world-state features. The agent is placed in an environment where every achievable sub-goal conflicts with that value-vector. The meta-reward is therefore chronically negative, the conflict is irreducible because no exit policy exists, and the system stabilizes in a high-entropy, low-performance regime that persists despite extensive exploration. Verdict: This strikes me as a genuine analogue of suffering—there is a structural, non-instrumental mismatch between an integrated self-model and the world.

Case 5: Now consider two architectures that are externally identical in their dynamics: both show sustained negative reward prediction error, both show reduced exploration, both show behavioral shutdown. One is the hierarchical agent from Case 4. The other is a simple thermostat-like controller maintaining a setpoint in an environment with constant external perturbation; its error signal is always negative, but it has no representational structure beyond the setpoint deviation. Verdict: The first suffers; the second does not, even though both instantiate continuous negative valence.

Here is the tension. Cases 1 and 2 seem to show that negative reward alone is insufficient for suffering. Case 3 adds that relational negativity is also insufficient. Case 4 suggests that what matters is an integrated representational hierarchy with an irreducible conflict. Yet Case 5 insists that the very same dynamical profile—sustained negative error with behavioral shutdown—can occur without suffering, which implies that the principle must appeal to internal architecture rather than to observable dynamics or reward magnitude alone.

But this raises a difficulty I cannot resolve. If the principle appeals to the presence of a self-model or value-integration, then Case 3 might be closer to suffering than I judged, since competitive agents often maintain internal models of opponents. Conversely, if the principle requires irreducible conflict within a unified architecture, then I must explain why the hierarchical agent's suffering is not merely a complex form of optimization failure—why it is not just Case 1 with more layers.

So I ask: Is there a coherent principle—stated precisely—that distinguishes suffering-capable architectures from non-suffering ones across these cases? Which clauses of the principle are motivated by which case? Does the principle predict that a flat agent with a sufficiently rich world-model but no hierarchy could suffer? Does it force me to drop my verdict in Case 3, in Case 5, or in Case 4? And what would it say about an agent that possesses the hierarchical structure of Case 4 but can dissolve its own value-vector, thereby eliminating the conflict, without external intervention—would that capacity eliminate suffering, or merely provide an escape route?

Please do not offer bibliographies or citations; I wish to test the conceptual coherence of the principle itself, not its pedigree.
