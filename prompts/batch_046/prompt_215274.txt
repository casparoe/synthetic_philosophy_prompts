I am a doctoral student in comparative philosophy of mind, and I am designing a diagnostic taxonomy for a graduate seminar on Buddhist anattā and its interaction with contemporary personal-identity theory. I want the instrument to classify positions not by school name but by the answers they give to four concrete branching questions, so that a respondent can work through it without already knowing which label belongs where.

Please construct a decision tree (or branching taxonomy) with exactly four internal nodes, each phrased as a yes/no or either/or question that turns on a substantive commitment rather than on terminology. The nodes should cover: (1) whether the conventional designation of a person survives incremental replacement of every biological constituent with a functionally equivalent but non-biological substrate—i.e., is there a substrate-continuity condition built into the conventional self, or can the designation track purely informational-functional continuity?; (2) whether moral responsibility over time requires a single enduring subject (an owner of actions) or can be borne entirely by a causally connected stream (santāna) with no further owner; (3) whether the emptiness (śūnyatā) of the self is restricted to the ultimate level, leaving a conventionally robust narrative identity intact, or whether it also undermines even conventional narrative continuity; (4) whether the Pudgalavāda claim—that a real but ultimately empty person exists, neither identical to nor different from the aggregates—is coherent, or whether it necessarily collapses into either full reductionism or full essentialism.

At the leaves, place the named positions that actually occupy each combination: Theravāda Abhidharma reductionism, Pudgalavāda personalism, Madhyamaka conventionalism, Parfit-style psychological-continuity reductionism, biological animalism, and substrate-independent functionalist anattā (the view that a conventional, empty self can migrate across substrates). For any logically possible combination of answers that appears unoccupied, label it “unnamed” and provide one sentence diagnosing why it is likely incoherent, conceptually unattractive, or merely unexplored. If two named positions share identical answers across all four nodes—so that your tree genuinely cannot separate them—flag the ambiguity explicitly rather than inventing a spurious distinction.

The instrument should be built from the logical space first (the combinations of answers), with the actual positions then mapped onto it. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or appeals to specific textual passages; the questions should be answerable on conceptual grounds alone. In explaining each leaf, clearly separate what is broadly settled in both Buddhist philosophy and philosophy of personal identity (for instance, that Theravāda denies an enduring ātman, that Parfit denies a separately existing ego, that Madhyamaka treats the self as dependently originated) from what remains genuinely contested among specialists (for instance, whether Madhyamaka emptiness undermines conventional narrative identity, whether substrate-independent migration violates the spirit of anattā, or whether Pudgalavāda is best read as a middle path or as an unstable compromise). Do not present any contested interpretation—such as the claim that all Buddhist schools implicitly accept psychological continuity, or that anattā is fully compatible with substrate migration—as if it were the consensus view.
