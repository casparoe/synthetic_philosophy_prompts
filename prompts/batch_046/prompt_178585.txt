I am a doctoral candidate in comparative political anthropology, writing my dissertation on bureaucratic exchange practices in post-colonial Southeast Asian states. In my fieldwork I have become fluent in the vocabulary of institutional grammar—rules-in-form versus rules-in-use, boundary rules that determine legitimate participants in an action situation, aggregation rules for collective choice, and costly signaling of in-group loyalty—but I remain less at home with the deontic vocabulary of normative philosophy. Recently I have been reading the universalist argument in the corruption literature that insists clientelism and ritualized gift-giving in public offices are corrupt regardless of local moral economies, because they violate impartial procedural norms that should govern state action everywhere; I want to understand whether that claim retains its force when stripped of its moral-psychological language.

Restate that hard universalist position—specifically the claim that local reciprocity obligations must be overridden by impartial procedural standards, and that Western anti-corruption norms are not merely parochial but structurally required for legitimate governance—in my institutional vocabulary alone. Translate its core commitments into terms of action situations, payoff rules, boundary-spanning signals, and institutional design failures: what kind of signaling malfunction does gift-giving represent on this account, which aggregation or boundary rules are allegedly violated, and how does the wrongness of corruption map onto coordination failure, path dependence, or parasitic extraction? Then deliver the residue report: which philosophical commitments translate cleanly, which must be distorted to fit (for example, does moral wrongness collapse into functional inefficiency or credible-commitment breakdown?), and which elements—perhaps those involving dignity, impartial respect, or the intrinsic value of procedural fairness—have no expression whatsoever in institutional grammar. Finally, tell me whether that residue points to a genuine blind spot in institutional analysis (it can register dysfunction but not normative violation independent of function) or whether it reveals that the universalist position was implicitly relying on functionalist premises all along. I am working without web access, so please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need only the conceptual architecture and the honest report of what does not survive the translation.
