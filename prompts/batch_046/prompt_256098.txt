I am a novelist—my recent book, The Cartographer of Forgotten Names, traces a city’s renaming after a regime change through an archivist who refuses to erase old street signs but instead annotates them with translucent overlays—and next week I am presenting to the city’s Heritage and Public Memory Commission. The panel is composed mostly of urban planners, a legal historian who advises on monument-protection statutes, and a municipal attorney focused on liability and community-consent protocols; they are practically minded, skeptical of literary metaphors in policy, and inclined to treat commemorative ethics as a binary between preservation and removal. I will defend a framework I call “palimpsestic friction”: rather than judging statues and street names by the moral biography of the honoree, we should ask whether an object generates productive mnemonic dissonance—cognitive tension between its original celebratory intent and present values—and respond not by demolition or uncritical retention, but by “layered annotation,” retaining the physical marker while integrating counter-memorial elements that make the historical irony structurally visible. I have sketched the argument around three moves: that commemorative space functions analogously to narrative irony in fiction, where meaning depends on the audience’s awareness of a gap between speaker and author; that removal collapses this temporal depth and converts history into a flat, presentist endorsement; and that layered annotation satisfies both the preservationist’s concern for historical continuity and the reformer’s demand for ethical accountability without requiring unanimous moral purity from historical figures.

Prepare me for the objections I will actually face in that room, ordered by how likely they are to arise and how damaging they could be to the proposal’s survival. For each objection, phrase it as that planner, historian, or attorney would put it—not in generic philosophical language, but in the vocabulary of zoning codes, liability exposure, participatory governance, and statutory interpretation—then give me a concise reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and flag precisely where that reply is weakest. I need to know which objections I should concede up front, perhaps that literary analogies do not constitute legal precedent, or that annotation budgets can exceed removal costs in certain municipal contexts, and which ones I must hold absolutely, especially regarding the claim that removal is not ethically neutral but actively reshapes historical consciousness. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks: either the legal-historical challenge that layered annotation might constitute a compelled-speech alteration of a protected monument, or the planner’s practical question about who authorizes the counter-narrative when the community itself is divided over whose memory should be layered. Give me the reply for that feared question too, with an honest note on its softest point, so that I do not rely on hoping they miss it.
