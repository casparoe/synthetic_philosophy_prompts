In metaphysical debates about material constitution, a familiar comparison treats the statue and the lump of clay as analogous to a ship and its planks: replace parts gradually and identity is preserved in one case but not the other. More recently, some philosophers have borrowed an analogy from digital media, likening the statue–clay relation to that between a digital image file and the particular arrays of pixels that display it at any moment: the file endures through successive renderings, yet no one identifies it with the pixels currently glowing on a screen. The analogy is deployed to argue that constitution involves a functional or formal structure that can be realised in different material substrates, and therefore that the statue is not identical to the clay — just as the image is not identical to the present display.

Audit this analogy in the context of the statue and the clay. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: what corresponds to the image file, the pixel array, the screen, the rendering software, and the viewer or interpreter? Then identify where the mapping breaks down: for example, whether clay possesses anything analogous to display hardware, whether the statue’s aesthetic and causal properties depend on its specific material in ways that digital images do not depend on particular pixels, and whether the notion of a ‘file’ already presupposes a distinction between form and matter that the statue–clay case is meant to establish rather than illustrate. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the non-identity conclusion from those that are merely decorative. Finally, assess whether the analogy was carrying the argumentative weight — particularly the functionalist assumption that form can be separated from substrate — and suggest what a more apt analogy might be if this one collapses.
