I'm currently in an upper-level philosophy of mind course on consciousness and computation, and I'm drafting a conceptual exercise about adversarial collaboration. Please write the joint statement that a defender of the Lucas–Penrose Gödelian anti-mechanist argument and a mechanist critic—one who accepts that human cognition is physically implemented and broadly computational—could both sign after a good-faith collaboration.

Structure the response as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: about Gödel's first incompleteness theorem and what it establishes for consistent formal systems of sufficient arithmetic strength; about the conditions under which such a system can recognize its own consistency; about the actual features of human mathematical cognition (fallibility, distributed reasoning, resource bounds, social correction); and about the basic logical form of the Lucas–Penrose argument itself, stated without evaluative gloss. Second, a disagreements table that states each camp's residual position in wording its own side would accept—specifically, whether a human mind can be modeled as a sound formal system that knows its own consistency; whether applying the theorem to biological minds involves a category error; and whether mechanists must deny all non-algorithmic insight or can accommodate it within heuristic, non-deductive, or emergent architectures. Third, identify the cruxes: sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. These should include whether consistency is a binary architectural property or a graded, context-dependent achievement; whether the argument requires attributing perfect consistency to humans or only a weaker epistemic property; and whether generating a true arithmetical statement that exceeds one's explicit axiomatic base requires non-mechanical insight or can be explained by implicit, distributed, or meta-level formal resources. Fourth, for each crux, specify the case, argument, or structural consideration both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance—for example, if a resource-bounded formal system with explicit self-correction protocols were shown to produce novel number-theoretic results that it could not derive within its own fixed axiomatic base, what each camp would take that result to show.

Please separate what is settled—the mathematical content of Gödel's theorem, the logical structure of the Lucas–Penrose reasoning, and the empirical limits of human cognition—from what is genuinely contested among specialists in the philosophy of mind and logic. Do not present a contested interpretive claim, such as that the argument conclusively demonstrates the mind is non-mechanical or that it has been conclusively refuted, as the consensus view. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
