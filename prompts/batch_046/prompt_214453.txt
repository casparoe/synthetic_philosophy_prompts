Next week I'm presenting to our moral psych / metaethics seminar — half the room are doxastic involuntarists who think belief just aims at truth, plus a couple people who think the wrong-kind-reasons problem is basically a category mistake about normative domains. My claim is that it collapses for first-person beliefs about your own moral traits: holding that you're courageous isn't just representing a fact, it's partly constituted by the practical commitment to act bravely, so moral reasons to cultivate courage aren't wrong-kind reasons for that belief, they're internal to what that belief is. Prep me for the objections in the order they'll come, phrased like they'd actually say them — transperancy of belief, the aim-of-belief argument, the slippery slope to self-deception, maybe the spouse-fidelity analog. Which ones have real bite? What should I concede up front — probably that this doesn't generalize to third-person or empirical beliefs? What's the one reply that has to land, about the difference between representational content and practical commitment? And flag the weakest point in my own view, which is probably drawing the line between belief-constituting commitment and outright self-deception. Keep it casual, no need for a bibliography.
