I've been thinking about debates over who belongs to the democratic "demos"—the boundary problem—and I keep noticing a specific argumentative cycle I can't name. First, a theorist proposes that anyone substantially affected by a collective decision ought to be included, which seems to push membership well beyond current citizens or residents, perhaps to future generations, foreign workers, or people in other countries affected by climate or trade rules. Second, critics object that the group becomes unmanageably large and that "being affected" offers no clear cutoff. Rather than defend the broad membership directly, the theorist then offers a substitute mechanism: a guardian, an ombudsman, or a weighted consultation for the excluded parties. But the justification for that substitute always appeals to the values or choices of the existing, narrower citizenry—we appoint a proxy for future people because present citizens care, not because those future people help select the proxy. I've seen versions of this in discussions of climate governance and transnational institutions. Is there an established concept for this precise loop—expanding the demos through an interest-based criterion, then contracting it back via a representational proxy justified by the original group? If there is no precise term, what distinguishes this from a legitimate form of indirect representation, and where exactly does the reasoning fail? Please separate what is settled in the literature from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and write the response in American English.
