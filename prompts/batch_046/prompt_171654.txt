Analyze the Canadian parliamentary apology of 2008 for the Indian residential school system as a case study in the philosophy of state apologies and collective moral responsibility. Begin by mapping the episode onto the distinction between institutional continuity—the Canadian state as the same legal-political entity that authorized and funded the schools—and moral inheritance—present citizens and governments bearing remedial obligations without being the direct perpetrators. Examine whether the apology, delivered by the Prime Minister on behalf of the state, presupposed that only a continuous agent could genuinely apologize, or whether it functioned as an expression of regret by a successor regime for an inherited wrong. Next, introduce and apply the concept of performative disavowal: the structural tension in which an apology must assert institutional continuity (to establish standing and responsibility) while simultaneously claiming moral discontinuity (to present the apology as a decisive break with the past). Use this tension to assess whether the 2008 apology resolved the wrong, institutionalized an unresolved contradiction, or transformed the nature of the state's relationship to the harm. Conclude by saying what the episode reveals about state apology theory—specifically, whether such apologies require a unified account of collective identity across time, or whether their efficacy depends precisely on leaving that identity strategically ambiguous.
