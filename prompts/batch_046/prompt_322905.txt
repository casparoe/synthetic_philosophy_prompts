Consider the institutional architecture of the European Union as it actually operates: member states formally retain sovereignty and constitutional identity, yet have delegated specific regulatory competences to supranational bodies; within those competences, EU law is treated as having direct effect and supremacy over conflicting national law; democratic legitimacy is claimed to flow both from the directly elected European Parliament and from democratically accountable national governments sitting in the Council; and the founding treaties commit the Union to ever closer union while simultaneously guaranteeing respect for national constitutional structures and identities. Critics routinely identify a democratic deficit—extensive binding regulatory power that lacks direct electoral authorization from a single European demos. What theory, or family of theories, of sovereignty does this combination presuppose? Must it involve a post-Westphalian conception of divided, pooled, or shared sovereignty, or can the arrangement be reconstructed within a classical unitary-state model by treating the delegation as revocable agency that leaves ultimate sovereignty intact? What conception of democratic legitimacy is implicit in the dual-channel account—is it a form of compound or multi-level democracy, and does that conception require sovereignty itself to be dispersed rather than merely delegated? Is the democratic deficit best understood as an accidental design flaw correctable by strengthening the Parliament, or as a structurally necessary consequence of preserving member-state sovereignty while granting EU law supremacy? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory, distinguish what the architecture strictly requires from what merely happens to fit it well, assess whether its commitments about sovereignty and democracy are mutually consistent, and describe what the institutional design would have to become if rebuilt on a strictly unitary sovereignty model with direct democratic authorization at the European level alone.
