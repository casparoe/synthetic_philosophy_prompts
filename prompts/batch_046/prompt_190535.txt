I'm helping organise a philosophy of mind reading group for next term and we're planning a session on whether pain is genuinely bad in itself, or whether its badness comes from something like an evaluative judgement that can get stripped away. I was chatting with a friend about those disociation cases — pain asymbolia, I think it's called? — where people still feel the noceceptive signal but don't experience the usual dread or urgancy. I want to close our discussion with a short poem, specifically a villanelle (around nineteen lines, the usual five tercets and a closing quatrain) that takes that split seriously.

The two refrains should track the separation between the bare sensation and the missing alarm: one about the nerve firing or the tissue crying out, and one about the absence of the warning that usually makes it awful. The poem should build so that by the middle stanzas the reader is left wondering whether the hurt is really in the body or in the interpertation, and I'd like the closing couplet to keep that tension open rather than resolving it. I've also been playing with this representional idea — that pain might misrepresent damage as demanding immediate flight — and I'd love if that sat quietly in one of the non-refrain lines, without turning the poem into a lecture. Please don't quote any philosophers or cite papers in the text itself; I'm just after the poem. And try not to make it a sermon about suffering or a tidy clinical case study — just let the contradiction live.
