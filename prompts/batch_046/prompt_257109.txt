I'm auditing a graduate seminar on ancient ethics and metaethics, and two students—Rosa and Devin—got into a sustained argument about how to read Plato's Euthyphro and whether the dilemma still threatens contemporary theistic ethics. Rosa contends that Socrates is primarily targeting explanatory priority, not ontological constitution: she argues that when Socrates asks whether the pious is loved because it is pious, he is asking whether divine attitudes can serve as the ground for moral properties, and she thinks modern divine-nature views escape precisely by reversing the explanatory arrow—God's nature explains both why God loves the good and why the good is good, with no vicious circularity. Devin objects that this domesticates the text. He insists that Socrates frames the options in terms of being-made-pious versus being-loved-because-pious, which concerns what constitutes piety rather than merely what explains it, and that any view identifying the good with God's nature still faces a relocated dilemma: we can ask whether God's nature is good in virtue of being divine, or whether being divine is good in virtue of exemplifying that nature. Rosa replies that Devin is smuggling in a secular metaethical demand for external grounding; on a genuinely theistic picture, God is the terminus of explanation, so asking for a further ground is like asking what makes the standard meter one meter long. Devin counters that the terminus move only succeeds if God's nature is necessarily and uniquely perfect, but then morality is anchored in necessary truths that God recognizes rather than creates—which lands us back on the second horn, just relocated from independent moral properties to necessary features of divine nature. They then turned to me and asked me to referee, noting that neither of them had considered whether polytheistic versions of the first horn—where different deities love incompatible actions—alter the structure of the dilemma in ways that monotheistic framing obscures.

Please adjudicate point by point: is Rosa correct that Socrates is primarily concerned with explanatory priority, or is Devin right that the ontological framing is inescapable? Does Devin's relocated dilemma genuinely threaten divine-nature theories, or does Rosa's terminus-of-explanation reply dissolve it? And where are both of them missing something—perhaps about how polytheistic disagreement among gods changes the first horn, or about whether the dilemma presupposes a particular conception of properties that a deflationary account could reject? In giving your verdict, please say how confident you are in each judgment, and explicitly identify the point about which you are least certain.
