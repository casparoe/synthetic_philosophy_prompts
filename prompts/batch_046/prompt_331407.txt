The argument proceeds in three moves. First, standard functionalism holds that mental states are individuated by their causal-functional roles rather than by the physical stuff that realizes them; multiple realizability then allows that the very same grief, belief, or desire could be instantiated in neurons, silicon, or some other substrate so long as the pattern of inputs, internal relations, and outputs is preserved. Second, fictional characters appear to possess mental states in a robust, if derivative, sense: Hamlet grieves, Macbeth desires power, and readers understand these attributions not as metaphors but as structured interpretations of narrative behavior. Crucially, these "character states" are realized in strikingly different substrates—the printed word, a live theatrical performance, Laurence Olivier’s 1948 film interpretation, and, hypothetically, an interactive AI narrative in which the player co-determines Hamlet’s responses. If functionalism applies here, then Hamlet’s grief should remain identically the same mental state across all four realizations, because the internal narrative-functional relations (grief caused by paternal death, producing hesitation, shaping dialogue) are preserved. Third, I want to challenge this apparent victory for functionalism by arguing that in fiction the substrate is never merely the physical medium; it necessarily includes the cognitive architecture of the audience or player. A reader of the printed text supplies much of the emotional texture through private inference, whereas a film viewer receives it through Olivier’s facial micro-expressions and Elgar’s score, and an interactive player receives it through their own agential choices. Consequently, the functional loop that defines Hamlet’s grief extends outward into different human (or machine-mediated) cognitive systems, meaning the "same" functional role is not truly substrate-independent: it is co-realized by the medium and by the perceiver’s distinct mental apparatus. This suggests that fiction exposes a hidden parameter in the philosophy of mind—what we might call distributed narrative realization—where multiple realizability breaks down not because the internal pattern changes, but because the boundary of the system realizing it shifts.
