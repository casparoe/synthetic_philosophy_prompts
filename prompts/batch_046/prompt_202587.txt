I have spent the past decade writing novels that almost no one buys, not from masochism but because the act of constructing them absorbs me so completely that the boundary between labor and living seems to dissolve. Now a publisher has offered me a contract substantial enough to erase my debts and secure my family for years, on the condition that I spend the next three years producing a meticulously outlined historical epic in a mode I find deadening—work executed to schedule and market specification rather than from any inner necessity. My agent insists that refusing would be vanity disguised as principle: labor is labor, she says, and the security it buys will eventually create the conditions for the work that actually matters. Yet I feel that accepting would transform writing from the one activity in which I experience existence as self-justifying—what might be called play—into a mere instrument for survival, thereby destroying the very thing that makes my life feel meaningful rather than merely sustained.

I am not asking for career counseling. Drawing specifically on Schlick's account of meaning—particularly his distinction between purpose-driven labor and intrinsic, playful activity—give me a committed judgment: does this refusal carry genuine philosophical weight, or am I only rationalizing an unwillingness to mature? Take a clear position, defend it without retreating into "it depends," and be explicit about where your reasoning is strongest and where it is most vulnerable.
