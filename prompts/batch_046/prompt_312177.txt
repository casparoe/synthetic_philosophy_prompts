In my high school philosophy elective, we have been studying critiques of modern publicity and the press. A classmate keeps invoking the slogan that "the crowd is untruth" — attributing the sentiment broadly to Kierkegaard — to dismiss viral opinions formed on social media, arguing that when an idea is held by an anonymous, algorithmically amplified mass, it is automatically disqualified from being genuinely true. I want to understand exactly what philosophical work that phrase can do, since it seems to slide between several different claims. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses it might express: an empirical generalization about the error rates of large groups; a moral claim that anonymity in the "public" dissolves individual responsibility and thereby corrupts judgment; an epistemic claim that genuine knowledge requires personal appropriation rather than passive media consumption; and perhaps a metaphysical claim that truth is necessarily singular and cannot be possessed collectively? For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and indicate which versions actually support my classmate's dismissal of a widely shared opinion simply because it is popular. Furthermore, does the original critique depend on the idea that the press creates a phantom "public" lacking any concrete existence — and if so, does the presence of algorithmic curation today strengthen or weaken that specific argument?
