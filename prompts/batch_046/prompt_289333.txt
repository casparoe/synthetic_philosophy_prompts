I am in my final year of sixth form, and over the past fortnight I have been debating with my friends whether our minds ever genuinely ‘rewrite’ what we have just perceived. Our philosophy teacher introduced us to the colour phi illusion — in which a red dot followed rapidly by a blue dot can produce a report of motion and an intermediate colour change — and we became absorbed by the distinction between Orwellian revisions, where the brain alters the earlier phenomenal experience in memory, and Stalinesque revisions, where the brain never generates the wrong experience at all but merely fabricates the subsequent record of it. We found that the standard binary does not capture our disagreements about whether such revision occurs in the perceptual binding stage, in working memory, or only during later narrative consolidation, nor about whether the same mechanism could apply if the stimuli carried semantic rather than chromatic content.

For our philosophy society’s next meeting, I should like you to construct a formal diagnostic taxonomy — presented as a branching tree — that sorts philosophical positions on the colour phi phenomenon and on the broader timing of conscious experience. The root node should ask whether there genuinely exists an intermediate phenomenal state requiring explanation. From there, each branch should split on a concrete, answerable claim: for example, whether any revision is retrospective or prospective; whether it alters sensory quality, conceptual content, or both; and whether an analogous ‘semantic colour phi’ — in which successive words (‘red’, then ‘blue’) replace the coloured dots — would be subject to the same revision mechanism or to an entirely different one. The tree must include, as named leaves, the classic Orwellian position; the classic Stalinesque position; a predictive-processing or Bayesian construction view that treats the intermediate appearance as an inference-driven prior rather than a falsified historical record; and at least one logically possible combination that remains apparently unoccupied, which you should flag and briefly diagnose as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Every internal node must be phrased as a neutral yes/no or either/or question that a student could answer without knowing which philosophical label it leads to. For each leaf, provide a single sentence stating its most awkward commitment. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; the handout is intended for classroom discussion without web access. Write in polished British English, keep the tree to roughly eight to ten leaves, and build the taxonomy from the logical space of answers rather than from a simple list of philosophers’ names.
