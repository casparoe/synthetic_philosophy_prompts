I've been working through an argument about what the gender-equality paradox actually licenses for egalitarian justice, and it ends up swallowing almost every correction of group-based outcome disparities. I want to know precisely where the reasoning slips. Here are the steps as I have them.

(1) The gender-equality paradox: in societies with stronger formal gender-equality policies, weaker gender-role socialization, and greater economic freedom (the Nordics relative to the US and UK), occupational gender segregation tends to be larger, not smaller.

(2) The liberation-revelation principle: if a group difference is suppressed by social pressure and expands when that pressure is removed, the difference is better explained by the release of autonomous preferences than by the persistence of hidden structural bias.

(3) Therefore, in egalitarian societies the larger gender divergence in career choice reflects not concealed discrimination but the freer expression of genuinely different preferences between men and women.

(4) If career choices made under conditions of strong freedom and weak socialization are largely preference-driven, then statistical gender disparity in occupations is not, in itself, an injustice; it is the aggregate result of choices that justice should respect rather than correct.

(5) So policies that pursue gender parity in career outcomes within egalitarian societies are not remedying injustice but overriding legitimate preference diversity—making them unjustified or actively unjust.

(6) The liberation-revelation principle is domain-general: apply it to any group disparity (class, regional, ethnic) that widens after formal barriers fall, and the same conclusion follows—widening under freedom means preference, not bias, and parity-pursuing intervention becomes illegitimate.

(7) Hence egalitarianism, understood as the project of eliminating group-based outcome differences, is either empirically mistaken about the source of disparities or normatively self-defeating: it demands correction precisely where freedom reveals that no injustice exists.

This feels like it proves too much. If (6) holds, it doesn't just defend gendered career patterns; it dissolves the case for correcting nearly any disparity that survives liberalization. I don't want to deny the empirical pattern in (1), and I'd like to preserve some space for egalitarian correction, yet I can't see how to block (7) without abandoning (2). Which step should I reject—(2), (4), or the move to (6)—and what does giving it up cost? In particular, is there a coherent way to keep the idea that freedom reveals preferences without letting that fact license abandoning all egalitarian intervention, or is the real error treating preference-expression and bias-elimination as mutually exclusive explanations?
