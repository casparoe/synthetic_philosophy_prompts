I have never taken a philosophy course, but after working and traveling for several years in countries with very different everyday norms, I keep returning to the same puzzle: when is breaking a local convention merely clumsy or inefficient, and when is it actually wrong? In one city, skipping a queue feels like a personal betrayal; in another, it is barely noticed. Tipping can function as charity, a hidden wage, or pure coercion depending on local framing. Jaywalking is technically criminal almost everywhere, yet in some places it draws real moral anger while in others it is the default behavior of everyone including the police. I want to understand whether a visitor who grasps the practical purpose of these rules—getting served, maintaining order, crossing safely—can still be said to violate a moral obligation, or whether the wrongness is reserved for those who share the local history that turned a coordination device into something people feel entitled to rely on.

I am assembling a study guide for an adult-education discussion group that meets weekly at a community center; the level is roughly equivalent to an advanced undergraduate seminar, and members can write for about seventy-five minutes. I need a single essay question that tests reasoning rather than memorization, built around a conceptual framework I have sketched myself. The framework distinguishes three layers in any conventional practice: first, the coordination layer, where the rule solves a practical problem; second, the expectation layer, where participants come to depend on others following it; and third, the accountability layer, where failure is treated not merely as surprising or inefficient but as something that calls for explanation, apology, or redress. I want the question to ask students to apply these three layers to three concrete cases—a tipping culture where payment is technically voluntary but practically mandatory, a queue culture with strict linear ordering, and a jaywalking culture where the act is illegal yet universally practiced—focusing especially on the position of a well-informed visitor who understands the coordination purpose but has not lived through the local history that constructed the expectation and accountability layers.

Please do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or named references to specific philosophers with publication dates; the answer should stand on reasoning and the framework alone. Structure your response in the following sections, in this exact order: 1. The exam question (about 150 words): present the three-layer framework, lay out the three cases, and instruct the student to determine for each whether a well-informed visitor violates a moral obligation, a social expectation, or neither, with a defense of the distinction. 2. The model answer (about 700 words): deliver a polished argument that applies all three layers precisely to each case, resolves the visitor problem without collapsing it into generic cultural relativism, and reaches a nuanced conclusion about whether conventions ever generate genuine moral obligations for outsiders. 3. The marking scheme (about 300 words): organize around five must-hit points totaling 20 marks, list the marks for each, and include a one-line note on how candidates typically fail that point—such as conflating coordination failure with moral failure, or assuming that understanding a rule automatically binds one to its accountability layer. Ensure the question rewards careful use of the three-layer distinction and penalizes answers that merely invoke being respectful or doing as the locals do.
