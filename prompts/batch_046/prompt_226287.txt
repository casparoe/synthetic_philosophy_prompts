I've been thinking about how doctors actually declare someone dead, and I keep returning to the split between brain death — tested through brainstem reflexes like the pupillary response — and circulatory death, declared after the heart stops and a waiting period (often five minutes) passes. What strikes me is that a body can sit in the gap between these two standards, alive by one and dead by the other, and that this gap is precisely what allows organ retrieval to happen without technically violating the rule that doctors must not kill patients for their organs. I find that reasoning unsettling. Could you write a pair of linked sonnets — one in the voice of a neurologist testing reflexes, one in the voice of a cardiologist listening for a pulse — with a shared rhyme scheme so the two poems feel like one argument torn in two? Each should have a volta where the other standard intrudes: the neurologist should suddenly hear the heart still beating, the cardiologist should suddenly notice the brainstem still reacting. Use the form to show that neither definition fully contains the other, and keep the medical details accurate without turning the poem into a textbook.
