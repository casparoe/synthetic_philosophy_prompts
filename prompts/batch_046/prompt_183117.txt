I serve on the university system's Procurement and Sustainability Ethics Committee, which sets binding sourcing standards for campus dining and research-catering contracts. A faculty working group has proposed eliminating ruminant products (beef and lamb) and retaining poultry and plant-based proteins, arguing that this minimizes wild-animal suffering when feed-crop harm is included: beef requires more land, thus more pesticide, harvesting-machinery, and habitat-conversion deaths, while chicken involves direct slaughter but lower total wild-animal mortality per calorie under a marginal-replacement model. A dissenting memo from the philosophy faculty contends that the analysis commits a category error by aggregating foreseeable wild-animal deaths from crop cultivation with intentional farm-animal killings, ignores the sentience-weighting problem (invertebrates versus vertebrates in crop fields), and applies a utilitarian calculus that conflicts with our charter's prohibition on treating direct institutional harm as interchangeable with indirect supply-chain harm. Student advocates have submitted a counter-analysis showing that poultry replacement would increase direct kills by orders of magnitude, and they ask us to consider either a full plant-based mandate or a standard tied to "no-kill" crop sourcing, which does not currently exist at scale.

Please draft the formal written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend one concrete pathway—retain the proposed ruminant-ban/poultry-retention standard; adopt a full plant-based mandate; or adopt an alternative criterion such as a harm-category separation rule—with a specific implementation timeline (contract renegotiation deadlines, interim reporting intervals, and conditions for revisiting the standard). The reasoning must treat the dissenting memo's strongest objection seriously rather than dismissing it, and it must include an honest assessment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. Finally, say what would change your mind: identify the empirical finding or philosophical argument that would lead you to recommend a different pathway, and indicate where the reasoning is most vulnerable to failure.
