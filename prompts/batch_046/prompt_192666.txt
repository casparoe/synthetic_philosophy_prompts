I'm preparing a ninety-minute workshop for an interdisciplinary reading group—mostly philosophy-of-mind and cognitive-science graduate students who have done basic decision theory but have not read L. A. Paul—on the structure of transformative experience. Rather than delivering a lecture on the concept, I need a teaching plan that constructs it from the ground up, treating the dependency order of the ideas as part of the content. The specific thesis I want to land is that transformative choice breaks standard rational-choice frameworks not merely because the outcome is unknown, but because the evaluator and the evaluated are numerically different persons in terms of values and experiential capacities; and I want this kept rigorously distinct from ordinary risk under uncertainty and from Elster-style adaptive preference formation. To avoid yet another session built around parenthood or vampirism, I am using a constructed carrying case: a professional musician who can elect a permanent neural-interface restructuring of her auditory cortex, gaining access to harmonic relationships she currently cannot perceive but altering—or erasing—her emotional attachment to the repertoire she now loves.

Please plan the session's architecture. First, specify the exact sequence in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced and why each depends on the prior, especially the move from epistemic limitation (not knowing what the experience is like) to the stronger claim about personal-identity shift and preference incommensurability. Second, state what properties this neural-interface case must possess and must lack in order to carry the idea without collapsing into high-stakes gambling, probabilistic ignorance, or post-hoc sour-grapes rationalization. Third, map the misconceptions these students are likely to generate—I anticipate conflation with risk aversion, with Bayesian updating, and with adaptive preference change—and indicate the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted and defused. Fourth, propose one concrete check or question I can pose at the end to determine whether they have grasped the structural failure of rational evaluation rather than merely adopting a romantic view of self-change. Finally, identify what I should explicitly refuse to address at this level—perhaps the metaphysics of diachronic identity, or the normative question of whether transformation should ever be chosen—while clearly marking that boundary so the students understand where the concept ends. Do not provide a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I need only the pedagogical dependency structure, the misconception map, and the boundary conditions.
