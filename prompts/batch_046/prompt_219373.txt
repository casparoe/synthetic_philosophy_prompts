I teach an upper-level epistemology seminar and am preparing the final examination. I need a 45-minute essay question on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia that students cannot answer by reciting the standard conciliationist and steadfast positions in peer-disagreement literature. To that end, present a constructed case in which a domain expert receives robust, domain-specific higher-order evidence that her cognitive architecture produces systematic bias in precisely the sub-field in which she holds a well-supported belief—without receiving any first-order defeater for that belief itself. The question should ask students to analyse whether this generates a distinctive form of epistemic akrasia—call it 'structural akrasia', in which an agent retains a belief while acknowledging that her reasoning apparatus is unreliable in that domain—and to contrast it with mere 'calibration failure', where confidence is adjusted downward. Students should be required to evaluate whether conciliationist principles apply when the challenging evidence is internal and mechanistic rather than interpersonal, and to determine whether retaining the belief in such circumstances is rationally vicious or merely epistemically tragic. Please provide: (i) the exam question, suitable for an 800–1000 word answer; (ii) a model answer of approximately 700 words; and (iii) a marking scheme that distinguishes an A from a B answer by naming the specific conceptual moves required—particularly the correct separation of first-order and second-order defeat, the conditions under which belief retention is rational despite higher-order doubt, and an explicit assessment of whether structural akrasia violates any normative epistemic principle. Write everything in British English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests.
