I'm writing my final paper for a metaphysics seminar on identity and individuation, and I'm stuck on whether Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles can survive the standard counterexamples without collapsing into ad hoc qualifications. Rather than asking for a literature review, I want to test my own intuitions against a reconstructed principle. Here are my verdicts on five concrete cases, plus a tentative principle that I think might capture them—though I can already see serious cracks.

Case 1 (the classic empty-space scenario): Two iron spheres, identical in every intrinsic qualitative feature—mass, density, color, internal crystalline lattice—floating in an otherwise empty, homogeneous, non-rotating space, separated by exactly one meter. Verdict: They are unmistakably two distinct objects. The bare numerical difference seems sufficient.

Case 2 (the relational anchor): The same two spheres, but now there is a single red point-source of light positioned 0.3 meters closer to sphere A than to sphere B. Verdict: They are still distinct, and now they are also relationally discernible by distance to the light-source. This feels like a stronger, less mysterious distinction than in Case 1.

Case 3 (the infinite lattice): Not two spheres, but an infinite cubic lattice of spheres spaced exactly one meter apart in every direction, with no boundary and no other entities. Every sphere has the same intrinsic properties and stands in exactly the same spatial relations to its neighbors. Verdict: There are still clearly many spheres—certainly not one sphere repeated—yet I can no longer point to "this one" versus "that one" without arbitrarily breaking the symmetry. They exist, but they seem to lack individual identity conditions.

Case 4 (the hidden quantum difference): Only two spheres exist, separated in empty space as in Case 1, but sphere A contains a single hidden atom in a spin-up state and sphere B contains the corresponding atom in a spin-down state, arranged symmetrically so that all macroscopic and relational properties match. Verdict: I feel they should still count as two objects, but I'm uneasy—if the difference is purely internal, unobservable, and symmetric, it seems metaphysically trivial. Still, physics treats the states differently.

Case 5 (continuous particle exchange): Two spheres maintain stable spatial boundaries, but due to symmetric quantum tunneling every constituent particle of A is continuously replaced by an identical particle from B and vice versa, so that over any non-zero time interval the particle-histories are perfectly interchanged. Verdict: Despite the exchange, I still want to say there are two spheres, located in different places; location tracks the object, not its particles.

My tentative principle: "Two entities are identical if and only if they share all qualitative properties—both monadic and relational—and occupy the same spatiotemporal region. If they share all qualitative properties but occupy different regions, they remain numerically distinct, but only in virtue of bare spatial difference; in the absence of any other entities, that spatial difference is not a genuine relation and therefore cannot ground distinction."

As you can see, this principle saves Case 2 and Case 4 (there are distinguishing qualitative features), but it seems to collapse Case 1 (no other entities, so spatial separation shouldn't count) and Case 3 (all relations are symmetric, so all spheres should be identical). Meanwhile, Case 5 challenges whether spatiotemporal continuity of particles matters at all.
