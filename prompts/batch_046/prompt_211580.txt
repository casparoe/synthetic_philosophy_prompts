I'm auditing the coherence of my own position on the separateness of persons objection to utilitarian aggregation. Here are my commitments, phrased as precisely as I can manage:

(i) I accept the Rawlsian claim that summing gains and losses across different individuals as if they belonged to one subject violates the moral separateness of persons: each individual has an irreducibly separate life and a distinct structure of aims, so interpersonal utility aggregation is conceptually inappropriate in the form that utilitarianism requires.

(ii) I distinguish strong aggregation (summing cardinal utilities across persons to determine the best state of affairs) from weak aggregation (assigning equal procedural weight to each affected person's claim within a collective decision rule), and I hold that only strong aggregation is blocked by the separateness objection.

(iii) I reject reductionism about personal identity over time: I maintain that diachronic psychological continuity is insufficient for identity, and that identity requires either biological continuity or some non-reductionist substantial criterion.

(iv) I hold that if two initially separate human brains were gradually merged via direct neural interfacing into a single integrated conscious system possessing one unified center of agency and one continuous first-person perspective, the result ought to be treated as one person for moral purposes, with prior obligations owed separately to the original individuals being superseded by obligations owed to the unified entity.

(v) I grant that evolutionary debunking arguments weaken the intuitive resistance to strong aggregation, but I maintain that the separateness objection is not an empirical intuition but a normative constraint on the structure of justification; consequently, it survives debunking.

(vi) I believe that institutional design can be broadly consequentialist—oriented toward maximizing aggregate welfare—provided it incorporates an absolute separateness safeguard that prohibits sacrificing any individual's core liberties or basic goods for others' gains, though distributive adjustments confined to non-core goods remain permissible.
