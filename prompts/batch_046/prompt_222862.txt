I have been working through the old Malcolm–Dennett exchange on whether dreams are properly called experiences, and I want to pressure-test an essay sketch before developing it further. I am writing from an inside-view that takes both Malcolm’s scepticism about phenomenal content during sleep and Dennett’s narrative-spindle reconstruction seriously, but I want to avoid the usual pre-training summary that merely rehearses the dialectic. To that end, I have injected a niche conceptual device I call the Counterfactual Memory Ledger (CML): the claim is that upon waking, the cognitive system does not retrieve an experiential tape—Dennett’s cassette is too passive—but computes a counterfactual ledger of what phenomenal states would have been licensed by the emotional residues and episodic fragments activated during REM, then narrativises that ledger into a first-person report. The report therefore has the form of experiential recall but is in fact a reconstructed counterfactual history.

My essay notes are as follows.

First, Malcolm’s core argument, as I understand it, turns on the criteria for being an experience: one must be in a state of perceptual access to an environment, and one must be able to correct one’s report against concurrent sensory input. During sleep both conditions fail, so calling a dream an experience stretches the concept beyond recognition.

Second, Dennett’s response denies that there is any presentation occurring during sleep at all. There are only memory traces laid down in parallel with bodily arousal, and the waking subject weaves them into a story using the same confabulatory mechanisms that operate in waking false-memory construction.

Third, my CML insertion tries to split the difference without being merely ecumenical. The ledger is not an experience (Malcolm is right) and it is not a stored tape (Dennett is right to reject that). Instead, it is a computational product generated at the boundary of sleep and wakefulness, using emotional valence as a weighting function over fragmentary episodic nodes. The resulting narrative is then treated by the reporting system as authoritative experiential data, which explains why dream reports feel incorrigible in the moment even though they are reconstructed.

Fourth, a potential objection is that the CML simply relocates Dennett’s cassette from storage to computation, but I think the distinction matters: a ledger implies normative bookkeeping—what ought to have been experienced given the fragments—rather than causal retrieval. If that distinction holds, it might explain why some dream reports contain impossible geometries or temporal inversions, since the ledger computes across nodes without the consistency constraints that govern waking perception.
