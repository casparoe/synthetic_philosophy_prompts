I am a retired physicist who has only recently turned to the philosophy of history and collective memory, and over the past months I have been discussing with a colleague in cultural historiography whether particular narratives function as “lost-cause myths” in the technical sense—namely, romanticized accounts of defeat that sustain a present collective identity or grievance. She has offered consistent verdicts on several cases: she classifies the postbellum American narrative of the Confederacy as a paradigmatic instance; she includes the modern Israeli national framing of the Masada siege; she includes the Serbian Kosovo Polje narrative; she excludes the British “Dunkirk spirit,” insisting that it is a deliverance myth rather than a lost-cause myth because Britain eventually prevailed and the narrative serves unity rather than separatist grievance; and she includes the Texan Alamo narrative when it is deployed in secessionist discourse. These judgments seem jointly consistent with more than one organizing principle, and I would like to test their coherence.

The new case I wish to put to her concerns the commemorative framing of the 1794 Kościuszko Uprising in contemporary Polish civic education, particularly the interpretation—common in post-1989 textbooks—that treats the crushing defeat not as a terminal failure of the republican cause but as a “prefiguration” of modern democratic sovereignty, even though the immediate political goal was extinguished and independence was only restored more than a century later. Should she classify this as a lost-cause myth if she wishes to remain consistent with her earlier rulings? I can see two plausible rules underlying her track record: one that requires the narrated cause to be genuinely and irreversibly lost, with no subsequent redemption of the same political project; and another that requires only that a defeat be mythologized to serve present identity, moral self-definition, or democratic grievance, regardless of later historical outcomes. Under the first rule, she might be obliged to exclude the Kościuszko case, since Polish sovereignty was eventually achieved; under the second, she might be obliged to include it, since the narrative is actively used to ground contemporary civic identity against authoritarian domination. Which of her prior verdicts—Dunkirk or Masada—provides the closer analog, and if her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, what principle would she be adopting by including the case, and what principle by excluding it? Finally, what further commemorative narrative—perhaps one in which a defeated movement is eventually redeemed by the very imperial power that originally crushed it—would best distinguish the two rules?
