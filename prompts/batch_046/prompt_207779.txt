I'm taking a seminar on moral conflict, and I drafted the following argument to show that genuine moral dilemmas are impossible. It feels suspiciously strong, but I can't locate the error. Here are the steps:

(1) A genuine moral dilemma requires two obligations that are jointly unsatisfiable and neither is overridden by the other.

(2) If neither obligation is overridden, then after the agent chooses, the unfulfilled obligation remains fully in force—call this a normative remainder.

(3) By the principle of residual accountability, a normative remainder that can never be satisfied becomes a standing moral debt that erodes the agent's rational moral standing.

(4) In standard examples—say, rescuing one of two drowning children when only one can be reached—the unchosen obligation is permanently foreclosed, so the debt can never be discharged.

(5) Hence genuine dilemmas entail permanently undischarged normative remainders.

(6) Any framework that requires rational agents to carry permanently impossible debts is incoherent.

(7) Therefore, in every apparent dilemma there must be a hidden asymmetry that makes one obligation decisive; genuine moral dilemmas do not exist.

I want to keep (2), since I think unchosen obligations do leave real residue, and (6), since I think moral theory should not burden agents with unpayable debts forever. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In your answer, please say how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the specific claim about which you are least sure.
