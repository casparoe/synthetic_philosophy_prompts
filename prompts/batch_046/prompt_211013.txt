I've noticed a recurring manoeuvre in rationalist and philosophy-of-AI discussions—particularly on forums such as LessWrong—where an author asserts that some mental property, say genuine understanding or robust agency, must apply to any sufficiently capable system regardless of its physical substrate. They then propose a check for that property built entirely from concepts native to human psychology or Western analytic philosophy: for instance, that the system must justify its outputs in a form that mirrors human deliberation, or that it must display a stable coherence between its commitments and a normative framework we recognise. When an AI or an alien cognitive architecture fails this check, the failure is taken as proof that the property is genuinely absent, rather than as evidence that the check itself might be parochial. The universal claim and the local test appear co-designed, so that the claim is never actually exposed to falsification from outside the framework that invented it.

Is there an established philosophical name for this exact circular configuration—where a concept's alleged universality is protected by an operationalisation that already embeds the concept's disciplinary or cultural origin? I am aware of broader debates about epistemic dominance, about the idea of a view from nowhere, and about what is sometimes discussed under the heading of ascription universality, but none of these quite isolate the mechanism I have in mind: the mutual construction of thesis and test such that counter-examples are automatically classified as lacking the very thing being tested for, rather than as challenging the universality claim itself. If no precise term exists, I would value a coinage that marks its boundaries—distinguishing it from ordinary confirmation bias, from a straightforward category mistake, and from simple cultural imperialism. I am also curious whether this pattern has been analysed specifically in discussions of cross-substrate cognition, or whether it belongs more generally to the epistemology of universalist attribution.
