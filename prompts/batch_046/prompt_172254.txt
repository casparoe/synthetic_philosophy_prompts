I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar and I'm writing the final exam. I want one essay question on Chalmers's meta-problem of consciousness that students cannot answer by merely reciting the distinction between the hard problem and the meta-problem. Instead, the question should present a hypothetical research program—call it the Phenomenal Transparency Project—in which neuroscientists build an artificial system that generates first-person phenomenal reports structurally identical to human ones by modeling interoceptive dynamics, without possessing any subjective experience. The project claims to solve both problems simultaneously: it explains why humans think there is a hard problem (the meta-problem), and it demonstrates that phenomenal reports are fully mechanizable, thereby dissolving the hard problem itself.

The exam question should ask students to evaluate this dual claim using Chalmers's framework. Specifically, they must distinguish (a) explaining the causal mechanism of report-generation, (b) explaining the cognitive origin of the explanatory gap intuition, and (c) determining whether a mechanized report-generator eliminates the need for an explanation of phenomenal consciousness itself. They should identify the specific fallacy—call it the report-mechanism fallacy—involved in sliding from (a) to (c) without addressing (b) independently, and they should argue whether solving (b) completely resolves the hard problem or merely explains away our belief in it.

Please provide: (1) the essay question, formatted for a 90-minute exam with an expected response of about 1000 words; (2) a model answer of approximately 800 words that makes the four critical moves an excellent answer must contain; and (3) a marking scheme totaling 20 marks that separates an A-grade response from a B-grade response by listing the specific conceptual moves required for full marks, the marks allocated to each, and the characteristic error students make that should cost them marks at that point—especially the error of treating the Transparency Project as either an obvious dissolution or an obvious non-sequitur without working through the level-distinction.
