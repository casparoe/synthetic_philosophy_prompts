Analyze the 1945 trial of Han van Meegeren — specifically his exposure for forging Johannes Vermeer's The Supper at Emmaus, which had been sold to Reichsmarschall Hermann Göring during the Nazi occupation of the Netherlands, and the court-ordered demonstration in which he painted a new Vermeer-style canvas before witnesses — through the lens of the dual-value thesis in philosophical aesthetics: that an artwork possesses both intrinsic aesthetic value (grounded in its perceptible properties and formal structure) and extrinsic historical value (grounded in its actual causal origin and the narrative of its creation). Treat van Meegeren's forgeries as what might be called negative originals — objects whose art-historical significance derives precisely from being inauthentic. Address the following: (1) Was the aesthetic experience of experts and viewers who praised the forged Emmaus genuinely undermined by the revelation of its inauthenticity, or does the dual-value thesis wrongly conflate two separable dimensions? (2) How should the thesis account for the court-ordered painting, produced with full transparency about its authorship under legal duress, now preserved in institutional collections but not valued as a Vermeer? (3) Does the trial's reliance on live demonstration suggest that artistic authenticity is ultimately a matter of witnessed performance rather than object-property? (4) Where does the theory miss or misdescribe features of the episode, particularly regarding the moral and political dimensions of selling a forged Dutch master to a Nazi official? Present your analysis as a formal essay, and please write your answer in British English.
