Below is the package of commitments I have arrived at regarding collective guilt and generational responsibility, shaped by Jaspers' fourfold distinction but modified in ways I am unsure about. I want an audit of internal consistency before I defend them in discussion.

(1) I accept Jaspers' category of political guilt: citizens of a state can bear collective responsibility for its crimes regardless of individual participation, and this is not merely metaphorical shorthand.

(2) I maintain that moral guilt is strictly non-transferable: descendants are never morally guilty for ancestors' free acts, and collective moral guilt is impossible because moral blame requires individual agency.

(3) I hold that present citizens can have demanding obligations—reparative justice, institutional reform—to address historical state wrongs, but these obligations rest on institutional continuity and unjust enrichment, not on inherited guilt of any kind.

(4) I deny that nations or states are moral agents in a robust sense; ascriptions like "the nation was guilty" must be analyzed as distributed or institutional, never as literal collective agency.

(5) I treat metaphysical guilt as genuine but essentially private and non-political; it expresses universal human solidarity in failure, and politicizing it collapses it into political guilt and destroys its character.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look incompatible, and rank the tensions by severity. Specifically, I worry that (1) requires a collective agency that (4) rejects, and that (3) might be undermined if (1) and (2) together drain all guilt-based reasons for repair. Meanwhile, (5) and (1) may seem to blur together because both involve collective dimensions. What is the least costly revision—should I weaken (1) to mere distributed liability, revise (4) to allow minimal collective agency, or reconceive (3) so that obligations do not need guilt as their ground? Be precise about what each fix costs and whether any tension dissolves once a distinction is drawn more sharply.
