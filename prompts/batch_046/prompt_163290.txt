After thirty-two years in experimental low-temperature physics, I have lately turned to philosophy of science and find myself returning to the Judy Benjamin puzzle about belief revision under conditionals. I want to know whether learning a conditional relationship—without learning that its antecedent or consequent actually holds—should ever shift one's prior probabilities over the conditions, and if so by what rule. To test my own intuitions, I have constructed several concrete cases and ask whether they fall under one principle or reveal an inconsistency I must resolve.

Here are my verdicts. In the first case, a meteorologist assigns equal prior credence to a pressure front being north or south; she then learns the precise mapping—north produces heavy rain in valley A and none in B, south produces the opposite—without observing either valley. I feel her north-south credence should stay exactly balanced. In the second, a physician begins with 30 percent belief that a patient carries gene X; she then learns that drug D cures if X is present and harms if absent, but has not tested the patient. Again I think the 30 percent should be untouched. In the third, a historian starts at 40 percent belief that a treaty was signed secretly; archival work establishes both that an economic collapse followed and that a public signing would have prevented it through institutional safeguards. Because collapse occurred, she revises her secret-signing credence up to 60 percent. In the fourth, I imagine my old laboratory: I hold 60 percent confidence that an isotope is unstable, learn that instability implies decay counts above five per minute while stability implies counts below, but my counter is broken so I observe nothing. Here too I want the 60 percent to remain unchanged.

The tension, then, is between my refusal to shift in the meteorologist, physician, and laboratory cases and my willingness to shift in the historian case. Is the difference genuinely structural—perhaps turning on whether the consequence is observed, or on whether the conditional is indicative versus counterfactual—or is it merely an unprincipled asymmetry? Please state the simplest principle that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, indicate explicitly which one I should overturn if simplicity demands it, and apply your account to a further scenario: a geologist assigns 55 percent credence to an active fault line, learns that if the fault is active seismic noise displays a particular spectral peak, but can detect that peak only through intermittent, noisy measurements. If she registers the peak once in ten attempts, should her credence in the active fault shift, and according to what logic?
