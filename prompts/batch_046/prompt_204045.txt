hey — i'm writing up my profile for a workshop on early american moral philosophy and want to check whether my take on franklin actually hangs together before i present it. here's where i've landed. (1) i read his 13-virtue ledger not as virtue ethics but as a proto-behaviorist "quantified self" protocol: virtue just is whatever gets ticked off in the weekly grid, so internal character states are basically irrelevant. (2) his famous failure to reach perfection — and his joke that the attempt made him better despite being impossible — should be taken literally: the asymptotic goal is a design feature, not a bug, because permanent self-surveillance is exactly the social mechanism he's after. (3) despite that instrumental framing, i think franklin was genuinely a moral realist about practical virtues like temperance and justice; he treated them as mind-independent goods, not just useful conventions. (4) he also believed these goods are fully accesible to unaided human reason and aligned with self-interest, which is why he published the scheme for anyone to copy and expected merchants and farmers alike to run the same 13-week cycle without any need for revelation or grace. (5) i think the scheme is meant to be culturally universal: human psychology is uniform enough that the same grid works everywhere. (6) but franklin also recognized that humility breaks the model — you can't self-certify humility on a private ledger without performing pride, so that virtue requires external social feedback, which undermines the self-tracking premise. (7) finally, i think his deism is doing hidden work: he treats moral law like physical law, regular and quantifiable and created by a distant architect, but without a personal god there's no forgiveness for the inevitable failures, so the ledger becomes its own punishment and reward system.

my worry is that (1) and (3) pull in opposite directions — if virtue is just ticked boxes, why think they track objective goods? — and that (4) and (6) clash, since humility needs other people but the scheme is sold as self-sufficient and universal. there's also maybe a tension between (2) and (7): if the ledger punishes failure with no possibility of grace, why wouldn't permanent failure just break the agent rather than motivate perpetual correction? and (5) vs (6) looks like a problem but maybe it's not cultural variation, just a structural feature of that one virtue, so perhaps it dissolves on inspection. there's also the pairing of (2) and (3): if perfection is literally unreachable, can there really be objective goods that we're permanently failing to achieve? though i feel like that one might dissolve if you think of them as asymptotic ideals rather than achievable states.
