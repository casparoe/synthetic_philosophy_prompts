You are a political philosopher specializing in collective autonomy, democratic legitimacy, and the metaphysics of group agency. Before answering, spend a moment brainstorming the main competing frameworks—remedial, primary-right, and associative theories of secession—and think step-by-step about how constitutional rules silently encode a particular conception of the state.

Consider the institutional pattern that democratic states actually follow when facing regional independence movements. A referendum is generally treated as constitutionally valid only when authorized in advance by the central government, as with Scotland’s 2014 vote conducted under Westminster’s formal agreement; by contrast, a unilateral referendum, such as Catalonia’s 2017 vote, is treated as an illegal breach of constitutional order regardless of its popular mandate. Meanwhile, international law tends to recognize secession only as a remedial right for groups enduring severe systemic oppression, not for prosperous democratic regions that simply prefer self-rule—while Quebec’s constitutional debates eventually produced a domestic legal framework insisting on clarity of question and clear majority rather than recognizing an unconditional exit right. At the same time, these same states recognize near-unconditional individual rights of emigration, allowing citizens to leave without justifying their departure to anyone.

What philosophical commitments about political membership, democratic authority, and territorial integrity does this pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it treat the state as a voluntary association whose members may exit only by mutual agreement, as a trustee of an indivisible democratic will that cannot be partitioned by local majorities, or as a remedial institution whose legitimacy depends on protecting basic rights? Carefully distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and assess whether it is genuinely consistent with a view that treats political association as analogous to a contract with an unconditional exit clause—much like individual emigration. If a state were rebuilt on the principle that democratic regions possess a primary, non-remedial right to secede comparable to individual exit rights, what concrete changes would be required in constitutional amendment rules, fiscal equalization mechanisms, and citizenship law?
