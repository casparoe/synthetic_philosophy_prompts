In my philosophy of mind course we have been examining whether panpsychism can be reconciled with a rigorous physicalist framework, and I have arrived at a cluster of commitments that I want to test for coherence before presenting them in seminar. I hold: (1) that constitutive panpsychism offers the best resolution of the hard problem—macro-experiential properties are composed from, not emergent upon, the proto-experiential qualities of fundamental physical entities; (2) that basic physical processes, such as the excitation of a quantum field or the spin orientation of an elementary particle, carry an extremely minimal phenomenal character that is not recognizably conscious; (3) that ethically relevant suffering requires not bare experience but some degree of integrated, unified perspective—something like a minimal self-model or global workspace—so that isolated fundamental entities are not literally suffering subjects; (4) that despite (3), the ethical implications of panpsychism are severe: if even minimal experiential qualities have a valence dimension, then large-scale physical processes—cosmic expansion, thermal decay, quantum decoherence—might aggregate astronomically vast quantities of distributed micro-distress; and (5) that I remain committed to a broadly physicalist ontology outside this debate, rejecting substance dualism and insisting that mental properties are ultimately grounded in physical facts, yet I also believe standard physicalism fails precisely because it omits these proto-experiential grounds.

My question is whether these five positions form a stable package. Specifically, does (3) actually block the ethical explosion suggested in (4), or does the compositional thesis in (1) entail that macro-scale suffering is unavoidable whenever enough micro-experiential units are structurally arranged? Furthermore, is (5) genuinely consistent with (1), or does constitutive panpsychism inevitably collapse into a form of property dualism despite my physicalist intentions? I am not asking for a survey of the literature, but for an internal audit: which commitments are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision—whether weakening (4), abandoning the compositional claim in (1) for a non-constitutive variant, or conceding that (5) was never stable to begin with.
