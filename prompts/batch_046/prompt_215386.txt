I am a PhD candidate in moral and political philosophy serving on my university's Committee for Institutional Naming and Commemoration. We must issue a formal recommendation to the university senate by the end of the semester regarding a petition to rename Columbus Hall, named after Christopher Columbus, in light of his documented role in the violent subjugation of indigenous Caribbean populations and in initiating systems of coerced labor. The counter-petition appeals to the 'man of his time' defence, arguing that retroactive moral judgment is anachronistic because Columbus's conduct aligned with the normative expectations of late-fifteenth-century Iberian expansionism.

Please draft the committee's decision memo. It should recommend one concrete course of action—retention of the name, removal and renaming, or retention accompanied by a permanent contextual installation—with an explicit timeline for implementation. The memo should apply a refined distinction between two interpretations of the 'man of his time' defence: (i) the 'structural moral blindness' interpretation, which excuses an agent only when the wrong in question lay genuinely outside the conceptual and epistemic resources available to him; and (ii) the 'normative compliance' interpretation, which excuses an agent merely because his actions conformed to prevailing social norms, irrespective of whether dissenting perspectives were accessible. The memo should argue that commemorative honor is incompatible with the second interpretation when applied to Columbus, given that dissenting voices regarding indigenous personhood and the brutality of conquest existed within his own court and ecclesiastical milieu and that he actively suppressed reports of suffering rather than lacking access to them.

The memo should further compare Columbus's case with those of Winston Churchill and Thomas Jefferson—figures also subject to contemporary moral criticism—to show why the framework yields differentiated judgments rather than a blanket exculpation or condemnation. For Churchill, it should note that imperial policies were vigorously contested within the democratic public sphere he inhabited; for Jefferson, it should observe that abolitionist arguments were already circulating in the Anglo-American intellectual world he actively engaged. The point is not to rank these figures but to demonstrate that the presence of accessible dissent distinguishes culpable normative compliance from excusable structural blindness.

Finally, the memo must treat seriously the strongest objection to its recommendation: the argument that any commemorative naming is irreducibly presentist and that consistency therefore requires either honoring no historical figures or honoring all regardless of moral record. It should explain why adopting that position would destabilize the university's existing commemorative practices—including honors for figures whose moral records are also complex—without providing a workable criterion for future decisions, and why this instability makes the pure presentist view an inadequate basis for institutional policy.

The tone should be appropriate for circulation to faculty, administrators, and student representatives, and the reasoning should be accessible to readers without specialized philosophical training while remaining conceptually precise. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific scholarly works; the argument should stand on its conceptual merits and on generally known historical facts.
