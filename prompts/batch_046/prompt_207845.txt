I had a long and somewhat frustrating argument with a friend about whether liberalism can genuinely remain neutral among competing conceptions of the good life, or whether that neutrality already presupposes an atomistic picture of the person that communitarians reject. To defend the communitarian side, my friend kept invoking an analogy he said he encountered in a graduate seminar: liberal political neutrality is like being a referee in a sport where every competitor writes their own private rulebook—the referee’s authority extends only to enforcing procedural fairness and preventing coercion, never to endorsing the substance of any player’s rules. Communitarian politics, by contrast, is like being the coach of a team with a deep shared history: you do not merely police boundaries, you call plays that express an inherited identity, and demanding that the team abandon its traditions for purely procedural rules is like demanding it cease to be the particular community it is. He used this to argue that liberalism’s apparent fairness actually dissolves the bonds that make moral life intelligible, and that only a politics embedded in shared narrative can avoid reducing citizenship to strategic bargaining.

I want you to audit this analogy rigorously rather than simply adjudicating liberalism against communitarianism on the merits. First, lay out an explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the referee, the private rulebooks, the coach, the team history, the plays, and the competitive structure itself—then identify precisely where the mapping breaks. In particular, examine whether the analogy quietly imports a feature of sports, namely a pre-given competitive framework within which rules are negotiated, that political life lacks; whether it treats cultural membership as as voluntary as joining or leaving a team; and whether it assumes that shared practices can only be inherited rather than reflectively reconstructed. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine my friend’s conclusion from those that are merely decorative, and say whether the claim that liberalism “dissolves moral bonds” survives once these breaks are on the table or whether it was being carried by the emotional weight of the coaching image. Finally, propose whether a better analogy is available—perhaps one that captures the liberal claim that common goods can be cooperatively rebuilt without being anchored in a single inherited narrative—and tell me whether that revised analogy saves or undermines his position. Work through the conceptual structure directly; do not offer bibliographies or lists of texts.
