I am currently taking a course on moral philosophy and political thought, and I recently encountered an argument in a seminar that I found difficult to rebut. We were examining a workplace case study involving a logistics coordinator who, over several months, enforced a new inventory protocol that severely restricted staff access to breaks and protective equipment. Colleagues noted that he displayed no personal hatred towards the workers; rather, he seemed absorbed in procedural correctness, target compliance, and career advancement. When I contended that his conduct remained gravely wrong despite the absence of malicious intent, a fellow student replied: 'That is exactly the banality of evil — Arendt demonstrated in Eichmann in Jerusalem that the worst crimes are carried out by ordinary people who are not monsters.' I felt the slogan was being used to excuse bureaucratic harm rather than to analyse it, yet I could not specify the ambiguity.

Could you therefore lay out the distinct precise theses that the phrase 'the banality of evil' — the term Arendt coined in her report on the Eichmann trial — might be expressing? Present these as a menu of separate readings: for example, psychological claims about the character of perpetrators, normative claims about moral responsibility within hierarchical organisations, conceptual claims about the failure of traditional notions of monstrous wickedness, and sociological claims about the conditions that enable ordinary people to participate in serious harm. For each reading, please indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; and specify whether that particular interpretation actually supports the interlocutor's implicit conclusion — that the coordinator is therefore less blameworthy, or that moral outrage is misplaced. Please write in British English, and do not supply bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
