I'm preparing a discussion for an advanced undergraduate seminar on value pluralism, and we're spending a session on Ursula K. Le Guin's short story "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" alongside philosophical treatments of incommensurable values and hard choices. I want to move beyond the standard utilitarian reading—treating the child's suffering and the citizens' happiness as items on a single scale that the city merely optimizes—toward the more difficult question of whether the story dramatizes a genuine incommensurability: values that cannot be rendered comparable by any common metric, not even one that favors the collective good. In particular, I'm interested in whether the citizens who remain and those who depart are best understood as facing what philosophers call a hard choice—a situation where reason does not determine a unique answer because the competing values are not just differently weighted but structurally incomparable—or whether the narrative secretly preserves a hidden comparability that makes departure an emotional luxury rather than a rational acknowledgment of value conflict.

Read the story through this philosophical lens, but don't let the philosophy flatten the fiction. Where does the story's imagery—the festival, the locked door, the child's refusal to be comforted—support a reading in terms of incommensurable values rather than mere moral cost-benefit analysis? Where does it resist that reading, perhaps by implying that the citizens' joy is causally dependent on the suffering in a way that makes comparison unavoidable? Consider especially the ones who walk away: is their leaving an act that recognizes an unresolvable tension between two legitimate but incomparable goods, or is it an implicit acceptance of a comparison they reject by exiting? And if the story does add something to the philosophical debate that argument alone doesn't—perhaps about the phenomenology of living with an incommensurable wrong—say what that addition is. I'm teaching this to philosophy students who know the story but haven't worked through the value-theory literature in depth, so keep the conceptual distinctions clear without relying on specialized jargon that requires citation.
