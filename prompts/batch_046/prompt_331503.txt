You serve on a hospital clinical-ethics committee that must classify every end-of-life proposal as either permissible withdrawal (allowing death) or prohibited active termination (killing), and you need a reusable procedure that staff without philosophical training can run in under thirty minutes. Design a five-step ordered decision procedure—specifying the question at each step, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a definitive classification, where it stops with a mandatory hand-off to a values board, and which nodes remain irreducible judgment calls. Ground the procedure in the conceptual distinction between removing an artificial sustaining barrier and introducing a new lethal causal factor, and include a dependency-inversion check for cases in which survival is maintained solely by an intervention with no underlying fatal pathology driving death on its own. Then apply it to three proposals: (a) discontinuing mechanical ventilation for a patient with irreversible brain-stem failure; (b) withdrawing artificial nutrition and hydration from a patient in a permanent vegetative state who has no other life-threatening condition; (c) initiating a high-dose opioid regimen in a patient with intact brain function and severe intractable pain, where respiratory suppression is foreseen but comfort is the stated aim. For case (b), the procedure should yield a classification that conflicts with common clinical intuition—explain precisely why the dependency-inversion check forces that result, and identify the exact step where the committee must overrule the procedure on policy grounds rather than correct a factual error. Conclude by stating what the procedure cannot settle, including joint-causation scenarios and the prior normative question of whether the doing/allowing distinction should govern institutional policy at all.
