I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or historiography, but over the past year I have been studying Fernand Braudel's writings on la longue durée—particularly his claim that geography and climate operate on timescales so slow they appear almost static relative to the faster rhythms of economic life and the abrupt disruptions of political events. Rather than contributing to historical scholarship on the early modern Mediterranean, I want to pursue a philosophical project that treats Braudel's framework as a live conceptual problem in social ontology and historical explanation.

The tension I want to investigate sits at the heart of Braudel's tripartite division of historical time. Geographical structures—mountain ranges, maritime currents, climate regimes—are said to form a constraining background that delimits what social and political life can become, functioning as an almost immobile prison of long-term possibility. Yet Braudel also documents cases in which human societies gradually alter these structures through irrigation, deforestation, urban development, and technological adaptation. If geographical structures are genuinely causally autonomous, they ought to resist rapid alteration by agents operating in the faster registers of economic change or individual political action. If, however, they are readily alterable, they lack the independence required to justify Braudel's methodological dismissal of event-centered narrative history.

I propose to examine whether this tension can be resolved by reconceiving geographical time not as a causal determinant but as a modal structure—one that defines the range of structurally possible configurations at a given scale, without necessitating specific outcomes. This reading would preserve Braudel's anti-eventist insight that history is not merely a sequence of discrete actions, while avoiding an implausible ontological determinism about physical environments.
