A philosopher of rationality—call her Professor Voss—has rendered the following judgments on diachronic choice patterns. (1) Cyclic preferences (A ≻ B ≻ C ≻ A) expressed within a single deliberative context are irrational, but the same cycle distributed across distinct temporal stages with altered information is not necessarily a failure of rationality. (2) Susceptibility to a Dutch book arising from shifting credences is irrational only when those shifts occur in the absence of new evidence; if beliefs update properly, sequential betting behavior that appears cyclic is excusable. (3) Preference reversals driven purely by temporal proximity—preferring $100 in one year to $110 in thirteen months, yet reversing the preference when both options are deferred by a year—do not constitute irrationality, despite enabling a money pump via iterated delay. (4) An agent who knowingly accepts a contract that permits cyclic extraction of value, foreseeing the full sequence and consenting to each stage, is not irrational provided her local evaluations remain coherent.

Consider now this new case: an agent discounts future utility hyperbolically rather than exponentially, with a stable discount function that she never revises. Because of this, a sequence of small, immediately available payments can pump her into accepting a net loss over time, yet at every decision node she maximizes her then-current hyperbolically weighted utility and explicitly anticipates the remaining sequence. There is no change in belief, no hidden information, and no local incoherence.

Predict Professor Voss's verdict, identify the closest analog among her earlier judgments, and say whether her record genuinely underdetermines the case. If it does, articulate the two competing principles—perhaps global diachronic consistency versus local procedural coherence at each stage—that would yield opposite answers, indicate which principle you think her track record favors and why, and specify the further case that would best adjudicate between these principles.
