I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science preparing a single seventy-five-minute seminar for second-year graduate students in climate science and economics who have no formal background in epistemology. I need them to understand the distinction between the inside view and the outside view, but specifically as applied to epistemic modesty about one’s own reasoning processes—not merely about predictive accuracy, but about the reliability of the cognitive steps one has just executed. Rather than using familiar textbook illustrations, I want the session built around a constructed scenario: a researcher, Dr. K, has just derived an unexpected quantitative correlation in a large observational dataset. She can evaluate her belief in that correlation either by rehearsing the statistical pipeline step-by-step (the inside view) or by asking how often researchers with her training, using comparable methods on comparable data, have produced spurious correlations of this magnitude (the outside view). The pedagogical twist I want to inject—let us call it the reflexivity loop—is that applying the outside view to her own case requires Dr. K to place herself inside a reference class, which in turn demands an inside-view judgment about her own competence and about whether this particular dataset is genuinely anomalous. Thus the two views are not sequential alternatives but interdependent in self-assessment.
