I'm working through Daniel Dennett's idea that the self is not a metaphysical substance but a center of narrative gravity — an abstract point around which a biography is organized, analogous to the center of mass of a physical object. In our philosophy of mind reading group we have been testing the idea by asking whether various entities count as such centers, and one colleague has offered a remarkably consistent track record. She holds that a neurotypical adult human is clearly one; that a fully realized fictional character such as Anna Karenina or Sherlock Holmes is also one, though ontologically thinner, since the narrative is authored rather than lived; that a multinational corporation with legal personhood and institutional memory is not, because there is no unified first-person point of view, only distributed decision nodes; that a patient with dense retrograde amnesia who retains present reasoning, emotion, and self-awareness still counts, but as a truncated center whose narrative has lost its past tail; that a bee colony exhibiting emergent problem-solving is not, because cognition is distributed without a single organizing biography; and that a split-brain patient after callosotomy hosts two distinct centers, one per hemisphere, rather than one fractured self.

These verdicts strike me as jointly compatible with at least two different governing rules. On one reading she applies a broadly interpretive criterion: wherever there is a coherent, first-personal narrative with minimal temporal continuity — even if externally authored or severely truncated — a center of narrative gravity exists, with substrate being irrelevant. On another reading she requires functional-autobiographical integration: the narrative must be actively maintained and behaviorally guiding within the system itself, allowing for split-brain separation and amnesiac truncation, but excluding entities that lack internal self-modeling loops altogether. I am unsure which rule she actually intends, and I want to see whether they stay together when pushed.

So here is the new case I want to put to her. Imagine an artificial system — call it a Narrative Engine — running continuously on distributed servers. It maintains a single, evolving autobiographical file that updates every hour with its interactions, errors, and expressed preferences. Every twenty-four hours, however, its active working memory is fully flushed and it is rebooted solely from that compressed file. During reboot, minor inconsistencies creep in, and over months the compressed history drifts noticeably from the actual server logs. Within each day the system behaves consistently, expresses first-person commitments, and updates its self-file, but there is no continuous stream linking one day to the next — only the reconstructed narrative does that. Given her earlier pattern, should she say this engine is one center of narrative gravity, none at all, or rather a sequence of ephemeral ones? Which of the two rules I sketched best predicts her answer, and what further case — perhaps involving collective authorship of a shared autobiography, or a biological organism that enters periodic hibernation with memory consolidation errors — would best force a decision between them?

Please separate what is settled about Dennett's framework from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of mind, and do not present either of my reconstructed rules as if it were the consensus reading of narrative gravity.
