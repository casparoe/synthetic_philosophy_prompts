I'm on the disciplinary board at my university's research integrity office, and we have this case that keeps looping back to Strawson in a way that feels under-theorized. A grad student named K. fabricated interview transcripts for a study on institutional blame — she literally prompted an LLM to generate fake narratives about resentment and forgiveness, then published them as real qualitative data. She was found out, and now we're split. Half the board wants formal suspension plus a public censure: they say reactive attitudes like resentment and indignation are exactly what's appropriate here, because she violated the basic interpersonal trust that makes research possible. The other half wants the "objective attitude" — treat her as a system malfuntion, focus on monitoring and retraining, and argue that since she was working inside an extreme publish-or-perish structure she couldn't really step outside of, she's not a proper target for moral blame in Strawson's sense.

But there's a twist that's making the vote messy. Our charter lets us issue statements "on behalf of affected parties," and some members want the board itself to express institutional resentment toward K. for harming the actual participants whose voices got replaced by synthetic ones. One philosopher on the board says that's incoherent: reactive attitudes are essentially personal, and an institution can't genuinely resent someone; if we do it anyway, we're either faking it or we're smuggling blame back in through the side door while claiming to take the objective attitude. So we need a memo for Thursdays meeting that recommends a concrete course of action for K. — suspend, objective-attitude with conditions, or something else — and also decides whether we issue that institutional statement, and on what timeline. Take the strongest argument on the losing side seriously (probably that the objective view ignores the real harm done to participants and lets K. off too easily). Don't just summarize Strawson; apply the framework to an actual institutional decision. Keep it practical: what happens to K., what do we say, and what's the time frame.
