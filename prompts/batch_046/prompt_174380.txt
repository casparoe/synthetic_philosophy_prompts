Following a lengthy argument with a friend about the aggregation of trivial harms, I want to check a summary he confidently offered of the anti-aggregationist reply to the torture-versus-dust-specks scenario. He put it thus: "The thought experiment proves that aggregative consequentialism collapses into absurdity, because any theory that sums welfare must eventually prefer 10^40 dust specks to one instance of torture. The correct lesson is that severe suffering carries infinite or at least discontinuous moral weight, so a rational agent should always prevent torture regardless of the number of minor discomforts at stake. Threshold deontology formalises this by placing harms on a qualitative ladder: once you are above the threshold of serious injury, lower-rung harms become morally invisible, which is why the view needs no arbitrary line-drawing—it is structurally discontinuous." I am inclined to think he has overstated both the conclusion and the architecture of the view, and that he treats an objection about line-drawing as if it had already defeated the position. Is this summary fair? Please distinguish what captures a real anti-aggregationist or threshold-deontological claim, what is a plausible simplification, and what subtly misrepresents the debate—especially regarding whether any serious defender posits literal discontinuity or infinite weight, whether aggregation is rejected wholesale rather than constrained, and whether the charge of arbitrariness is as decisive as he implies.
