I am writing to ask for a diagnostic analysis of an impasse I have observed in cross-cultural moral philosophy. For some years, a debate has persisted between those who defend Dutch directness—treating unvarnished, explicit speech as a form of epistemic respect for one's interlocutors—and those who defend Japanese indirectness—treating strategic ambiguity and so-called white lies as necessary instruments for protecting relational harmony and face. Both sides regularly cite empirical literature on workplace satisfaction and intercultural negotiation failures, yet neither camp has modified its core position. I want you to diagnose whether this disagreement is substantive (about the relative moral weight of honesty versus care), verbal (about what 'respect,' 'politeness,' or 'autonomy' actually denote), or a case of two groups answering different questions—perhaps one about institutional transparency norms and another about intimate social maintenance—under the single heading of bluntness versus politeness.

Please ground your diagnosis in what each side actually says about the wrongness or permissibility of white lies: do they treat them as violations of listener autonomy, or as permissible, even obligatory, face-saving devices? Identify the precise cruxes—the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I hypothesize that one hidden crux is whether linguistic directness functions as a context-invariant virtue or as a context-sensitive variable that shifts with social distance, and another is whether there is an independent moral value in 'naturalness' of communication that survives even when indirectness produces better outcomes. For each crux, specify the kind of concrete case, formal model, or empirical finding both sides would agree bears on it, and record what each side would predict.

Finally, please say how confident you are in the diagnosis you offer, and explicitly single out the point about which you are least sure.
