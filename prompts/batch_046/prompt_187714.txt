I am designing a graduate seminar on formal limits of collective judgment, and a study group walked me through an argument that tries to push Arrow's impossibility result from preference aggregation into what they term "credibility-ranking" within scientific peer review. They lay it out as follows. (1) Arrow's theorem establishes that no social welfare function mapping individual orderings over three or more alternatives into a collective ordering can simultaneously satisfy unrestricted domain, the Pareto principle, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship. (2) In peer-reviewed fields, distributed reviewers generate individual orderings over competing theoretical frameworks according to assessed plausibility, and editorial processes synthesize these into a collective credibility-ranking—determining, for instance, which models receive priority funding or canonical placement. (3) Because reviewer judgments are complete, transitive orderings over discrete alternatives, and because synthesis aims to reflect all reviewers without domination by any single evaluator, the peer-review system appears to satisfy Arrow's four conditions on its input-output profile. (4) Arrow's result therefore implies that any non-dictatorial synthesis must violate Pareto efficiency, independence of irrelevant alternatives, or unrestricted domain. (5) Observationally, peer review does not systematically violate Pareto or independence in the manner familiar from preference aggregation, so unrestricted domain is the only plausible escape hatch. (6) Yet unrestricted domain is the very condition that gives the theorem its bite; if scientific communities restrict admissible theories—excluding pseudoscientific alternatives, for example—they are not applying a universal aggregation rule but rather a gatekeeping mechanism that presupposes the conclusion. (7) Hence applying Arrow's framework to epistemic communities proves too much: it either condemns peer review as dictatorial or forces the conclusion that open scientific consensus is impossible without prior censorship of the alternative space, undermining the normative ideal of open inquiry.

I feel the error lies in a subtle structural confusion between belief aggregation and preference aggregation somewhere between steps (2) and (4), but I cannot isolate it precisely. Which step should be rejected, and what does giving it up cost for the broader project—whether in judgment-aggregation theory, belief-merging literature, or defenses of Arrow's original scope—of applying social-choice machinery to epistemology? Please identify the weakest inference, explain why it looked plausible, and say whether repairing it requires abandoning the analogy altogether or only restricting its domain of application.
