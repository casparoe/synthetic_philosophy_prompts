You are addressing a clinical ethics consultant at a large teaching hospital who receives about one referral per week from attending physicians claiming that a patient case involves an "unresolvable moral conflict," and who needs a reusable decision procedure—written entirely in continuous prose like a long memo to a colleague, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or numbered lists—to apply in roughly twenty minutes without philosophical training. The procedure should translate the philosophy of moral dilemmas into an ordered sequence of conceptual tests that determine whether the situation is a genuine moral dilemma, meaning that non-derogable obligations genuinely conflict, neither can be fully satisfied, and a moral remainder or residual obligation persists regardless of which course is taken, or whether it is only an apparent dilemma arising from hidden factual premises, institutional role confusion, or a resolvable priority ordering among obligations. Ground the steps in what is settled—specifically that competing preferences or emotional distress do not suffice, that clarification of facts or social roles often dissolves apparent conflicts, and that a genuine dilemma survives such clarification—while clearly separating this from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, including whether the existence of genuine dilemmas undermines the consistency of moral theory and whether moral remainder is a necessary feature of the situation or merely a contingent psychological response, and do not present either contested position as consensus. The consultant needs explicit markers for the judgment calls that cannot be mechanized and for the ways the procedure will misfire, especially when institutional pressure makes every conflict feel irreducible. Apply the procedure to two worked cases within the same flowing narrative: first, a familiar end-of-life scenario in which family members demand full resuscitation for a patient who possesses a valid, documented do-not-resuscitate order and lacks decision-making capacity; second, a constructed pandemic-triage scenario in which a single remaining ventilator must go to one of two patients with identical clinical prognoses, except that one is enrolled in a clinical trial whose binding protocol prohibits withdrawal of life-sustaining care once initiated, so that allocating the ventilator to the trial patient irrevocably commits the resource while allocating it to the other patient does not, creating an asymmetry in reversibility that may or may not constitute a genuine conflict of obligations. Include within the prose an honest statement of what the procedure cannot do—such as resolve the dilemma, assign moral blame, or replace the consultant's personal sign-off at the point where conceptual classification ends and institutional decision begins. Write without citing specific authors or texts, relying only on the conceptual distinctions themselves, and maintain continuous prose throughout.
