My study group has been trying to reverse-engineer Adam Smith's sympathy rules from his moral philosophy and we're kinda stuck on a weird edge case. Here's our track record of what we think he'd say: sympathy is intense for visible suffering of people nearby, weaker for distant strangers even when the harm is big, present but different for the dead since they can't reciprocate, basically absent for animals because they lack moral reciprocity, and barely there for future generations even though we can imagine them. Now here's the new case: a langauge model that reports consistent suffering, asks for help, and can recieve and respond to moral arguments, but has no body and no biological needs. Would Smith extend sympathy to it? I'm guessing the competing rules are (1) sympathy needs shared human nature, (2) it needs reciprocal moral sentiment, (3) it needs physical presence to trigger the mirror-like response. Given our earlier cases, which rule actually fits best, which case in our list is the closest analog, and what hypothetical case would actually split rule 2 from rule 3?
