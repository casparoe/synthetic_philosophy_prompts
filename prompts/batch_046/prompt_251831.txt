Here are my moral verdicts on cases where institutional agents cause or allow large-scale harm to non-combatants, arranged to test whether the distinction central to Anscombe’s objection to honoring Truman—between killing intended as a means and harm that is merely foreseen—can be sustained without collapsing into either pure consequentialism or an implausible moral-luck doctrine. First, Truman’s authorization of atomic attacks on Japanese cities in 1945, knowing that tens of thousands of civilians would die and treating those deaths as leverage for unconditional surrender: I judge this gravely wrong, because the harm was not merely foreseen but structurally intended as the mechanism of coercion. Second, conventional strategic bombing of an industrial target embedded in a populated area, where civilian deaths are foreseen but serve no strategic purpose and are not intended: permissible, though tragic. Third, deploying an autonomous targeting system in which unavoidable statistical misclassifications will kill a small number of civilians, with risk minimized and no specific victim targeted: permissible, analogous to the second case. Fourth, declining an available, low-cost software update that would halve those statistical casualties, purely to avoid bureaucratic delay: wrong, but I feel this is culpable negligence rather than murder. Fifth, authorizing stratospheric aerosol injection to stabilize global climate, knowing with high confidence that altered monsoons will cause famine-related deaths in specific regions, yet viewing that harm as a physical side-effect of the cooling mechanism rather than as leverage: I find this permissible, despite the scale being comparable to the first case.

What principle, if any, reconciles these five verdicts? In particular, is there a coherent account of “structural intent” that distinguishes the first case from the fifth without relying on arbitrary proximity or institutional distance? If not, which verdict should I abandon: my judgment that Truman acted wrongly, my judgment that climate engineering is permissible, or my distinction between the third and fourth cases? Please state the principle precisely, identifying the clause that governs each case, and apply it to one further scenario I have not listed: a public health official who maintains an existing vaccination schedule that will certainly cause one hundred avoidable deaths rather than risk a policy change that might reduce overall uptake. Finally, say honestly which of my original verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate, and whether saving that verdict requires complicating the principle in a way that makes it less plausible than overturning the verdict itself.
