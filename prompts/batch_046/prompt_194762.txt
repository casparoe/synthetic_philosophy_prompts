David Chalmers distinguishes the hard problem of consciousness—why physical processes should give rise to subjective experience at all—from the meta-problem, which asks why we judge there to be an explanatory gap that physics cannot bridge. Suppose that cognitive science resolves the meta-problem completely: it shows that our intuition of an explanatory gap is an artifact of introspective opacity and of peculiarities in how phenomenal concepts are deployed, so that there is no legitimate, unrebutted sense that physical accounts leave something out. Would this not dissolve the hard problem itself, rather than merely explaining away our resistance to physicalism? After all, if the sole ground for thinking consciousness is not fully accounted for by physics was this now-debunked intuition, then once the intuition is corrected there remains no phenomenon demanding a non-physical explanation—much as debunking vitalist intuitions dissolved the corresponding hard problem of life. Or does resolving the meta-problem leave the original explanatory task entirely untouched?

Please separate what is settled about the logical and explanatory relationship between the hard problem and the meta-problem from what is genuinely contested among specialists in philosophy of mind, and do not present any disputed interpretation—such as the claim that debunking the intuition fully dissolves the hard problem—as though it were the consensus view.
