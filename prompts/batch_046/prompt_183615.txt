I am currently serving on a committee revising the interpretive plaque for a municipal monument to a figure in the Jefferson–Columbus–Churchill mold: a statesman whose political achievements are inseparable from actions—chattel slavery, colonial violence, or imperial domination—that are now widely condemned. In every meeting, the objection to mentioning those failures is the same one-liner: “He was a man of his time; you cannot judge the past by the standards of the present.” The phrase is treated as a conversation-ender, but I am convinced it is doing hidden work that differs depending on which precise claim is smuggled inside it.

Can you lay out, as a menu of distinct and precisely formulated theses, what that slogan could actually be asserting? I am especially interested in separating: (1) a descriptive claim about what beliefs were statistically dominant in his society; (2) an epistemic claim about whether he possessed the conceptual resources to recognize the wrong; (3) a normative or meta-ethical claim that moral obligations are indexed to historical epochs; (4) a counterfactual claim about whether he could have acted differently even had he held contrary views; and (5) an excusing claim that widespread social acceptance diminishes individual culpability. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, whether it is substantive and defensible, and whether it actually supports the committee’s conclusion—that the plaque should remain silent about the moral failures rather than contextualize them.

Please also address the asymmetry I keep noticing: we rarely invoke “man of his time” to excuse contemporaries who resisted dominant norms, such as abolitionists or anti-imperial dissidents within the same culture. Does that selective application reveal that the slogan is being used not as a general hermeneutic principle but as a targeted excuse? And which of the five readings above survives that observation?

Finally, tell me which of these interpretations is most likely the one people in my committee actually intend when they reach for the phrase, and whether that intended reading is consistent with the work they need it to do—namely, blocking moral condemnation without committing them to full-blown historical relativism. I want to bring your disambiguation back to the next meeting, so please present the readings as an explicit list I can cite point by point.
