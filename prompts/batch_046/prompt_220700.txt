I've been puzzling over a cluster of institutional practices that all treat taxonomic membership as a decisive boundary, and I wonder what philosophy of biology they implicitly commit us to. In conservation law, legal standing and funding flow to "listed species" and their critical habitats, but an individual wild animal that suffers has no claim on protection unless it belongs to one of those listed taxa; abundance or ecological redundancy seems to erase moral status, while rarity creates it, with the line drawn exactly at the species border. Meanwhile, in laboratory and agricultural welfare regulation, scrutiny tiers are assigned by Linnaean rank—non-human primates, birds, "fish," invertebrates—rather than by demonstrated capacities for pain or cognition, so that a highly sentient cephalopod may fall into a lower tier than a less cognitively complex mammal purely because of taxonomic placement. To complicate the picture further, in synthetic-biology oversight, engineered chimeric organisms that combine genetic material from several traditional lineages are required to be assigned biosafety containment protocols based on their "nearest wild-type species," as if the organism inherits the ontological and risk-status of one parent category despite lacking any natural evolutionary history.

What view of species do these practices collectively presuppose? Must they assume an essentialist ontology—that each species possesses a fixed nature that determines both the properties of its members and their legal or moral standing—or can they be reconstructed on a purely evolutionary, population-level view on which species are temporary, interbreeding lineages with fuzzy boundaries? Are the conservation framework (treating species as intrinsically valuable units), the welfare framework (treating taxonomic rank as a proxy for moral status), and the biosafety framework (treating engineered organisms as belonging to a parent species) consistent with one another, or do they quietly rely on incompatible conceptions? Reconstruct the implicit theory of what a species is, distinguish what these practices strictly require from what merely accommodates them, and indicate what institutional changes—whether in listing criteria, welfare categorization, or containment rules—would be necessary if we replaced the underlying view with one that treats species as conventional, historically contingent groupings lacking intrinsic boundaries or uniform member-properties.
