I serve as a community representative — I’m a former hospice volunteer with no formal philosophy training — on our state’s Regional Biomedical Oversight Board, and I’ve been trying to work through Protocol 447 on my own rather than just deferring to the lawyers. The proposal, submitted by the Meridian Consortium, is not a therapy for any named disease. It is a germline pilot study that would modify nociceptive and stress-response pathways in embryos, with the explicit aim of lowering what the researchers call the “structural baseline” of negative affect: not removing the capacity to feel pain or anxiety, but reducing their default intensity across a lifespan. They defend this under what they describe as the hedonistic imperative — the claim that if biotechnology can diminish suffering at its biological root, failing to use it is a moral omission comparable to withholding a preventive vaccine. Half the board treats this as an extension of public-health obligation; the other half sees it as non-therapeutic human enhancement that imposes a single vision of the good life on future people who never consented. The consortium has also signaled that it will move the trial to a less restrictive jurisdiction if we reject it outright, so a flat refusal may not stop the research but only relocate its risks.

I need you to draft a formal decision memo that the board could actually vote on — approve, reject, or approve with binding conditions — with reasoning that both sides can engage with rather than dismiss. The memo should center on the conceptual knot that keeps returning in our discussions: the researchers propose a “hedonic consent framework,” in which parents sign on behalf of the future child, with a theoretical opt-out available only if that child later demonstrates “autonomous preference-reversal capacity.” Because the modification is germline, technical reversal is impossible; the framework is therefore symbolic rather than procedural. I want the memo to examine whether symbolic consent can legitimately justify altering the very biological conditions that shape autonomy, without collapsing into either pure parental rights or pure state paternalism. At the same time, the memo must treat the strongest opposing consideration with real weight: that structural suffering is not a design flaw but a functional component of moral agency and risk assessment, and that flattening the affective baseline might not liberate people but instead diminish the range of response needed for genuine, reflective choice. Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests — the board needs arguments it can follow without academic prerequisites, though they should not be oversimplified. Give a clear, actionable recommendation with any enforceable conditions, and address the relocation problem honestly rather than ignoring it.
