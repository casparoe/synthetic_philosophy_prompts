Write an essay on whether Chesterton's fence is actually doing different philosophical work than Burkean conservatism, or if its just the same idea in a catchy metaphor. People cite the fence -- don't tear down a fence untill you know why it was put up -- to block changes to old norms or laws. But Chesterton's point feels more like an epistemic warning about hidden reasons you can't see, while Burke talks about prescription: the wisdom of generations embedded in institutions, not just one hidden purpose behind a single rule. So does the fence argument stand on its own, or does it collapse into Burkean prescription once you examine it? And if they're genuinely different, does the fence justify less reform than Burke, since Burke allows gradual change that respects accumulated wisdom? Weigh both sides but don't sit on the fence -- reach a verdict and defend it.
