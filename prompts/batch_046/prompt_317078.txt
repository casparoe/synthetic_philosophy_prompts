I've been reading about the invention of tradition in modern nation-building—how rituals, dress codes, and commemorative practices that feel ancient were often standardized in the nineteenth or early twentieth centuries. While discussing this with a friend in cultural heritage policy, I noticed a recurring configuration that doesn't quite match the usual story of deception or false consciousness. Consider a national remembrance ceremony instituted in 1972, or a standardized folk dance codified by a state ministry in the 1960s: in both cases, the recent, well-documented origin is not hidden. School curricula and museum labels mention the date of creation openly. And yet the practice carries the full normative weight of something immemorial. When someone points out that the ritual is younger than many living citizens, the response is rarely "then we should treat it differently"; instead, defenders invoke collective choice, emotional authenticity, or continuity to protect its authority. The awareness of invention seems to coexist with, rather than undermine, the tradition's binding force, and challenging the practice on grounds of its youth is treated as a category error rather than a valid critique.

I'm wondering whether this specific arrangement—an openly acknowledged recent origin paired with unchallenged traditional authority—has an established name in the literature on nationalism, collective memory, or the sociology of knowledge. I don't mean the general concept of invented tradition itself, nor do I mean cases where people are genuinely mistaken about antiquity. I'm asking about the situation where transparency about recent creation is incorporated into the tradition's own justification, so that the tradition is defended precisely by denying that age matters, yet it still functions with all the social weight of ancient custom. Is there a concept that captures this exact relationship between documented newness and functional antiquity? If not, how should one distinguish it from mere cynicism on one side and false belief on the other, and what are its boundary cases—for instance, does a fifty-year-old practice belong in the same category as one that is five years old? I want to understand exactly what this phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie; I will not be satisfied with a bare label.
