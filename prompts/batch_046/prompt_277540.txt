I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of mathematics, and I am designing an assessment for an upper-level course on infinity and ontological commitment. I want a take-home essay examination that students should be able to answer in approximately ninety minutes in roughly eight hundred to one thousand words. The prompt must not be answerable by reciting standard textbook summaries of Hilbert's hotel or Cantor's theorem; it should instead force students to navigate a specific conceptual tension between mathematical practice and physical ontology.

Please draft one essay question that requires students to do the following three things. First, reconstruct Hilbert's hotel precisely enough to show why it is standardly taken to defend—rather than undermine—the coherence of an actually infinite collection, paying particular attention to the role of one-to-one correspondence in accommodating new guests despite full occupancy. Second, distinguish Cantor's argument for the existence of strictly larger cardinalities from the finitist claim that every collection is either finite or only potentially infinite, and identify exactly where in Cantor's proof the commitment to completed, actualized totalities becomes unavoidable rather than merely procedural. Third, evaluate whether the finitist objection succeeds by showing that the structures in the first two parts depend on ontological commitments—such as the actual existence of completed infinite sets—that have no necessary connection to physical reality, or whether the objection instead confuses the domain of pure mathematics with that of empirical instantiation.

In addition, please provide a model answer of about seven hundred words that makes the specific analytical moves an excellent answer must contain, and a marking scheme totaling twenty-five points. The scheme should name at least four must-hit conceptual moves, assign point values to each, include a one-line note on how candidates typically fail each one, and clearly distinguish an A-range answer—which recognizes that the finitist challenge is strongest when directed at ontological interpretation but weakest when treated as an internal mathematical error—from a B-range answer that summarizes both positions without resolving the domain distinction. Do not include any request for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists in the exam prompt itself; the assessment should be fully answerable through conceptual analysis alone. To keep the question somewhat unfamiliar despite using well-known material, frame the finitist challenge specifically as an argument about physical instantiation versus mathematical existence, drawing on the kind of concern a scientist might naturally raise about whether infinite cardinal arithmetic describes anything that could be realized in nature.
