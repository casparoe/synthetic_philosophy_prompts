Please assist me in interpreting a difficult passage from Adam Smith's The Theory of Moral Sentiments. In the relevant section, Smith describes the internal division that occurs when an individual evaluates his own conduct: he becomes both the agent who acts and feels, and the spectator who judges those actions and feelings from an imagined external standpoint. This internal spectator is not merely a copy of any particular real person, but an abstract figure formed through the accumulation of social experience, and he judges propriety by comparing the agent's sentiments to the causes that occasion them. Smith emphasizes that the spectator does not share the agent's passions with their original heat; instead, he considers them with the coolness appropriate to an unaffected observer. At the same time, Smith insists that sympathy—not cold reason—is the foundation of this judgment, since we learn what is proper only by entering into the feelings of others and then internalizing their reactions. This generates a conceptual difficulty: if sympathy requires entering into another's emotional state, and the spectator's authority depends on detachment and proportion rather than shared feeling, then it is unclear whether the spectator's judgment is itself an act of sympathy (perhaps a refined, second-order sympathy directed at the sentiments of an imagined audience) or whether sympathy merely supplies the data for a separate, non-sympathetic rational evaluation. How should one read this relationship? Does Smith provide a consistent account of how sympathy produces a faculty that then operates independently of sympathy's emotional immediacy, or is there an unresolved ambiguity in his view about whether the impartial spectator is ultimately constituted by feeling or by reason?
