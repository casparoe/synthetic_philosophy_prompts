I'm preparing a single seminar for a reading group of non-philosopher urban planners who are expert in quantitative risk assessment but tend to treat every value conflict as resolvable through weighted trade-offs. I want them to understand the thesis that some goods are genuinely incommensurable, such that rational choice between them involves an irreducible loss rather than an optimal equilibrium. Plan the teaching arc: begin by identifying the preliminary distinction that must be secured before the thesis can even be heard — specifically, the difference between commensurability in measurement and commensurability in value — and explain why this distinction has to come before any case study is introduced, given the audience's background. Next, specify the carrying example: a concrete land-use conflict between preserving an indigenous low-intensity fire-management landscape, which sustains a particular kind of relational and intergenerational ecological knowledge, and replacing it with a technologically superior carbon-capture monoculture grid that maximizes a single quantifiable output; explain precisely what features the example must possess (that both options realize goods that are not merely different in degree but structured by incompatible norms of care, time, and relation) and what it must lack (any hidden common metric, such as total welfare or long-term economic yield, that would allow the conflict to dissolve). Then lay out the predictable misconceptions in the order they will arise: first, that incommensurability is just a practical problem of insufficient data; second, that if values can't be weighed then choice is arbitrary; third, that accepting incommensurability entails moral relativism about all conflicts. For each, indicate the exact point in the arc where it should be anticipated and the conceptual move that absorbs it without derailing the session. Provide a check for understanding at the end that can distinguish whether participants have grasped the idea — that commitment to one incommensurable good can generate reasons that rationally constrain future choices without being derived from a prior ranking — from whether they have merely registered emotional discomfort at the absence of a clear answer. Finally, state explicitly what should be omitted at this level — for instance, named historical figures associated with value pluralism, formal axiological frameworks, or the metaethical debate about the ontology of values — and justify each omission in terms of the audience's needs and the session's single objective.
