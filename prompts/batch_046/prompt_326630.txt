I am drafting the final examination for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar that I am teaching this term. The course has covered the standard debates on pain asymbolia and whether the dissociation of sensory-affective and cognitive-evaluative dimensions undermines the claim that pain is intrinsically bad. Rather than asking students to rehearse familiar clinical summaries, I want the essay question to centre on a deliberately injected conceptual distinction: "affective-preserving asymbolia", a hypothetical condition in which a subject retains full negative affective valence, physiological avoidance, and behavioural withdrawal, yet completely lacks any cognitive framing of the sensation as harmful, dangerous, or worthy of concern. The question should ask students to rank three competing philosophical explanations of whether, and in virtue of what, the sensation remains bad in this condition: first, that badness resides purely in the negative affect; second, that badness requires an intact harm-evaluation; and third, that badness is a relational property constituted by the disruption between the affect and the subject's practical life-plan.

Please construct a single essay question suitable for a 90-minute examination, together with a model answer of approximately 600 words and a marking scheme structured around four must-hit conceptual moves (totalling 20 marks). The scheme should clearly distinguish answers that merely describe clinical cases from those that genuinely analyse the conceptual dependencies between affect, evaluation, and badness. The deliverable should contain no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should be written entirely in British English.
