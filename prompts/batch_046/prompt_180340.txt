Design a 90-minute seminar activity for about 14 graduate students in political philosophy. The setting is a single room with movable chairs, no slides, and only paper cards and a whiteboard. The aim is to make participants feel the structural force of public-reason requirements—specifically, the hidden asymmetry of translation labor and the reciprocity failure that occurs when a supposedly neutral “shared vocabulary” is already grammatically shaped by secular liberal premises—before anyone names Rawls, accessibility, or comprehensive doctrines.

Structure the deliverable as a runnable game plus debrief, with these elements:

1. Setup (hidden roles and stakes, 10 minutes). Each student draws a private “Comprehensive Doctrine” card: secular humanist, Catholic integralist, Islamic legal tradition, or deep ecological. They also draw a concrete “Policy Dispute” card (e.g., state funding for religious schools, mandatory environmental service for 18-year-olds, age thresholds for gender-affirming medical care without parental consent). Each student receives a hidden scoring rubric: they gain points if the group’s final policy aligns with their doctrine’s preference, and separate points if their group’s written justification survives a challenge round. The facilitator does not reveal that “secular humanist” maps almost one-to-one onto the permitted vocabulary, while the other three require complex translation.

2. Deliberation rules (30 minutes). In groups of three or four, students must reach unanimous agreement on both a policy position (pro or con) and a one-paragraph “Public Justification.” The justification may use only terms from a printed “Public Vocabulary” list—equal liberty, fair cooperation, harm prevention, democratic legitimacy, basic needs, non-coercion. Any reference to sacred authority, metaphysical human nature, divine command, or ultimate cosmic order is banned from the written text. Groups write their justification on a card and post it.

3. Challenge round (20 minutes). Groups exchange justifications. Any group may play a “Translation Challenge” card against another group’s text, claiming that a phrase smuggles in a private comprehensive premise (e.g., “equal liberty” is being used with a secular-individualist grammar that excludes communal religious goods). The facilitator adjudicates using pre-set, non-public criteria (shared silently with the facilitator only): does the justification require accepting a premise that is not independently available to all citizens? A successful challenge voids the justification and strips the group of its “legitimacy points.” There should be a real but modest stake: the winning group (highest combined hidden-doctrine and legitimacy score) chooses the seminar’s discussion topic for the following week.

4. Final vote and silent tally (10 minutes). Groups vote on which policies “pass” based solely on surviving justifications. Then the facilitator reveals the hidden doctrine cards and the scoring. Most groups should discover that secular-humanist groups reached quick consensus with no felt tension, while other groups either deadlocked, diluted their core commitments, or risked smuggling and lost on challenge.

Debrief (20 minutes). Map moments in the activity onto parts of the idea: the ease of the “native speaker” group maps onto the hidden encoding of shared premises; the deadlock or dilution maps onto the translation burden; the failed smuggling attempt maps onto the reciprocity failure—religious citizens are asked to do cognitive work that secular citizens avoid because their language already counts as public. Predict predictable failures: facilitators will face ambiguous adjudication near the boundary of “smuggling”; secular participants will deny that any asymmetry existed; religious-tradition participants may feel exposed rather than enlightened. Be explicit about what the room cannot reproduce: the long-term democratic legitimacy erosion that accumulates across generations when one group systematically bears the translation tax, and the actual coercive weight of state enforcement that makes the accessibility requirement morally urgent rather than merely conversational. The facilitator must supply these through brief framing after the reveal, not through an opening lecture.

Finally, specify that the activity should not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or verbatim quotations from real texts; if any author’s idea is referenced, paraphrase it without quotation marks. Make clear that the niche framing—the idea that the Public Vocabulary functions as a dialect with hidden grammatical commitments rather than a neutral medium—should emerge from the rules and the scoring asymmetry, not from facilitator explanation up front.
