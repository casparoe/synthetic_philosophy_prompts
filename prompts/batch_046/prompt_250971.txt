I'm taking a seminar on practical reason and narrative ethics, and we're examining how literary characters embody commitment problems that philosophers usually treat with abstract agents. I'd like to read the Gollum-Smeagol arc in Tolkien's The Lord of the Rings through Derek Parfit's hitchhiker scenario, but combined with his reductionist account of personal identity in a way I haven't seen in standard commentary. The basic parallel is familiar: Smeagol promises to guide Frodo and Sam into Mordor in exchange for not being killed, yet once the journey reaches its destination—once the ride is over—he has every instrumental reason to seize the Ring and betray them. What I want to explore is whether Parfit's denial of a further-fact view of personal identity changes how we should evaluate the rationality of that betrayal.

If Smeagol, the agent who makes the promise, and Gollum, the agent who attacks Frodo on the slopes of Mount Doom, are not strictly identical but are related only by psychological continuity with competing desires and branching narratives, does the commitment made by the earlier stage bind the later one? Or does the reductionist framework dissolve the problem by saying there is no deep subject who either honors or breaks the agreement—only a series of temporally related psychological stages with shifting preferences? I am especially interested in the sequence near Shelob's lair and the final confrontation, where Gollum's betrayal is accompanied not by simple greed but by ambivalence, self-loathing, and what looks like a fractured sense of agency.

A good reading should not reduce Tolkien's narrative to an illustration of Parfit's puzzle; rather, it should ask what the literary portrayal adds that the philosophical thought experiment misses. Does Tolkien dramatize a kind of self-binding—perhaps through the oath sworn on the Precious—that functions differently from a rational commitment device precisely because it is embedded in an ontologically unstable, narrative self? And does the work ultimately suggest that rational irrationality is not merely a failure of foresight but a symptom of the kind of psychological discontinuity that, on Parfit's view, undermines the very conditions for being the same agent across time? I'd like an analysis that takes both the philosophy and the text seriously, flags exactly where the analogy breaks down—especially regarding whether the Ring itself functions as an external commitment mechanism—and says whether the literary case supports or undermines Parfit's claim that rational cooperation requires a continuity that Gollum precisely lacks.
