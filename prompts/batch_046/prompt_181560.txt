I work as a documentation officer for a transitional justice archive that receives field reports of mass violence from conflict zones and must assign each event a primary categorical label—such as genocide, war crime, crime against humanity, or systematic atrocity—for entry into both a legal-referral database and a public memorial registry. The label is never merely descriptive: in the legal channel it activates specific international obligations and jurisdictional pathways, while in the memorial channel it fixes the event's collective identity and determines which survivor communities are recognized as the primary subjects of remembrance. Drawing on the tension inherent in Lemkin's original framing—where the destruction of a group is conceived both as a physical-biological fact and as a politically consequential designation—I need a reusable decision procedure that my non-philosopher staff can apply within two working days, without consulting live legal counsel for every case, and that explicitly separates taxonomic determination from performative effect. The procedure should take the form of an ordered sequence of conceptual tests rather than a feature checklist, specify for each step what counts as a sufficient answer and where the sequence halts with a definitive classification, and clearly mark the point at which the remaining ambiguity is irreducibly normative—requiring sign-off by a senior officer rather than resolution by junior staff—because the conflict between legal activation and memorial fidelity is a matter of values, not facts.

Please construct this procedure with the following operational requirements. First, establish the ordered questions: begin with the identification of the targeted collective (how is group membership determined, and what do we do when internal subdivisions—clans, religious sects, caste lines—are salient to survivors but invisible to the legal category?), then move to the nature of the destructive acts (physical elimination versus cultural, economic, or political destruction of group life), then to the evidentiary status of intent (what level of documentary or testimonial proof is required, and how do we classify when intent is fragmentary but pattern is clear?), and finally to the dual-output decision (legal-referral classification versus memorial-registry framing). At each stage, define the threshold for a "yes" that permits progression, the conditions that trigger an immediate "defer" or "no" with explanation, and the specific misfire conditions—such as when applying the legal label collapses distinctions that matter to the affected community, or when withholding it denies them political recognition they seek. Second, apply the procedure to three worked cases: (i) a state-directed campaign of physical elimination directed at a recognized national subgroup, where survivors reject the genocide designation because it erases clan-level distinctions essential to their mourning practices; (ii) an armed non-state group's systematic destruction of religious infrastructure and forced assimilation of a minority, with no documented intent to physically destroy members, but where the community insists on the genocide label to secure international protective attention; and (iii) a siege-induced mass starvation scenario where intent evidence is fragmentary but structural patterns strongly suggest deliberate deprivation. In case (i) or (ii), the procedure should yield a classification that conflicts with the affected community's preference, and you must identify exactly which step produces the divergence and why the procedure cannot internally reconcile it.

Finally, specify the limits of what this procedure can do. It should not rely on citation of specific treaty articles, bibliographic verification, or access to external legal databases; it should be teachable in a single staff briefing and usable by personnel with general legal literacy but no specialized philosophical training. State explicitly that the procedure cannot adjudicate whether a state's refusal to apply the genocide label is morally justified, cannot invent new protected-group categories outside existing frameworks, and cannot substitute for democratic deliberation with survivor representatives about commemorative framing. Identify the single decision node—likely at the point where legal-referral classification and memorial-registry framing diverge—where judgment is irreducibly normative and must be documented with a signed justification by a senior officer, and explain why attempting to automate or delegate that node to junior staff would violate the conceptual distinction between describing destruction and authorizing its political meaning.
