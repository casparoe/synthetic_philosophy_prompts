As a retired natural scientist who has only recently begun working through political philosophy, I listened to two colleagues in my seminar—one a political theorist, one a philosopher of technology—argue about whether Rousseau's critique of theatrical spectacle extends to modern immersive virtual-reality civic assemblies. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves below from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, briefly justify each rating, then say who is more right overall and identify what both sides are missing. Lead your answer with its bottom line in the first sentence, then present the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.

(1) The theorist claimed that Rousseau's fundamental objection to theater in Geneva is representational corruption: theatrical sentiment substitutes artificial emotion for genuine civic virtue, thereby weakening the general will. She applied this directly to immersive VR deliberative assemblies, arguing that they replicate the corruption by allowing citizens to experience collective political passion and decision-making without bearing any real institutional consequences, turning civic life into scripted emotional spectacle.

(2) The philosopher of technology replied that this misidentifies Rousseau's actual mechanism. The core problem is not representation per se but performative contradiction: the actor publicly displays virtue he privately lacks, cultivating hypocrisy in the audience. Because participants in an immersive simulation are not passive spectators observing false exemplars but are themselves the agents acting within the scenario, the contradiction is structurally absent; hence the theatrical critique does not carry over.

(3) The theorist answered that the contradiction is not eliminated but internalized. Within the simulation, participants adopt predetermined roles—delegate, dissenting citizen, mediator—complete with scripted emotional responses, and they feel authentic civic passion while knowing that no binding decision will result. The gap between displayed virtue and possessed virtue is relocated inside the subject rather than removed, which she called representational corruption in first-person form.

(4) The philosopher countered that this reasoning overextends the critique to the point of incoherence. By that logic, even the collective civic festivals and structured civic education that Rousseau himself endorsed involve role-adoption, collective affect, and partial separation from immediate institutional consequence. The decisive distinction is not between first-person and third-person experience, but between practices that generate genuine social bonds and binding obligations and those that produce only the appearance of them. Immersive deliberation is not necessarily spectacle if its outputs are designed to constrain real institutions; the theorist has failed to establish why VR role-play is structurally closer to theatrical spectatorship than to legitimate civic formation.
