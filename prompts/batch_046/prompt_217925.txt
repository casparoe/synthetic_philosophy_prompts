I have recently begun volunteering at a local natural-history museum, where I help maintain an educational display of a live leafcutter ant colony; watching that colony respond to disruptions—without any visible leader, and apparently without any single ant grasping the whole situation—has pulled me back to the old puzzle of whether an ant colony can possess a mind in its own right, the kind of problem raised by Hofstadter’s Aunt Hillary and the ant fugue. I want to bring that exact feeling of puzzlement into my philosophy-of-mind graduate seminar, not as a lecture but as something the twelve of us physically run into together. We meet once for seventy-five minutes in a room with movable chairs and a table for distributing cards, and I need a runnable simulation—call it a fugue-state attribution exercise—whose rules force participants to experience the tension between mechanistic explanation and collective intentionality before anyone names the concept.

Please divide us into two groups: eight Colony participants and four Ethologist observers. Give each Colony member a private role card—three foragers, two soldiers, two brood-tenders, and one queen-proxy—each with only local behavioral rules printed on the card, for example: if you receive a red token, pass it left; if you receive two blue tokens, place one in the central tray; if you feel heat (simulated by a hand signal from me), freeze for thirty seconds. The queen-proxy has no decision-making authority; she may only aggregate tokens that reach the central tray according to her own counting rule. Meanwhile, I will open hidden environment envelopes at five-minute intervals that inject token colors representing a combined drought-and-predator crisis, but no Colony member sees the envelope contents—only the tokens that reach them. The Colony’s collective task, communicated only by the initial card, is to produce within forty minutes either a relocation configuration or a defense configuration in the tray, but they may not speak to coordinate; all communication is token-only. The four Ethologists sit silently outside the circle and must record every five minutes, in full psychological sentences, what they believe the colony believes and what the colony wants.

After forty minutes, stop the flow. Have the Colony members reveal that none of them knew the full environmental state, and have the queen-proxy reveal that she was only counting. The Ethologists then present their attribution logs. Only then do we debrief. In the debrief, map specific moments onto the idea: the first time an Ethologist wrote that the collective was afraid rather than describing token-flow; the moment Colony members felt frustration at lacking global knowledge despite sensing that something was being decided; and the point where the tray’s token pattern looked like a unified choice. Explain what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this—likely the Ethologists over-psychologize within ten minutes, and other participants will treat the queen-proxy as a brain despite her rules forbidding interpretation. Be explicit about the feature this exercise cannot reproduce—namely, the absence of phenomenal consciousness in actual ants—so I know exactly what gap I must supply with discussion rather than with the game. Also tell me how to handle the likely confusion if participants ask whether this proves the colony has no mind, or whether it proves it does, and specify the social-epistemic stakes (e.g., Ethologists earn points for predictive accuracy of the tray state, Colony members earn points for maintaining local-rule fidelity) so that the motivation is real without involving money. Make the rules, materials, and mapping concrete enough that I can run it tomorrow without further research.
