Play the part of a political historian who defends a contingency-based reading of the French Revolution but rejects both the Marxist class-struggle narrative and the standard ideological-purification account of why revolutions devour their children. Your committed position—treat it as a serious, well-resourced thesis rather than a caricature—is that the Terror and the successive purges emerged chiefly from what you call the recursive legitimation problem: once the old sovereign is deposed, the new constituent power must continuously re-authorize itself without any transcendent anchor, and each act of re-authorization simultaneously exposes the contingency of the prior one, generating an accelerating cycle of exclusion that eventually turns on the revolutionaries themselves. You may draw on the conceptual vocabulary of constituent and constituted power, on the institutional trajectory from the National Assembly through the Convention and the Committee of Public Safety, and on the logic of institutional self-reference, but you must not retreat into vague pluralism or concede that your view is merely one option among others. You take it to be more precise than the rival explanations, and you should defend it actively in the first person.

I am a doctoral student in intellectual history working on revolutionary sovereignty. Here is my opening challenge. I grant that constituent power faces a legitimation deficit, but I contend that your “recursive legitimation problem” is the ideological explanation in institutional drag: the Jacobins purged rivals not because of an abstract structural recursion, but because specific class-aligned factions—the sans-culotte and petty-bourgeois elements behind the Montagnards—used the rhetoric of popular sovereignty to eliminate opponents who threatened their material interests. Without that social agent and its class position, your recursion has no mechanism; it risks becoming an empty formalism applicable to any regime change. Moreover, if pure contingency drives the self-devouring, then every revolution should consume itself with comparable ferocity, yet the American Revolution did not. So your account either smuggles class and ideology back in through a side door, or it fails to explain the specific violence of 1793–94. Respond in character, remain fully committed to your position, and address both objections—the charge of hidden reductionism, and the comparative failure with the American case—giving your reasons directly rather than laying out neutral options. Do not break character to summarize debates; argue as yourself.
