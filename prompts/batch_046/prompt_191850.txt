I've been trying to write up my position for a philosophy of religion seminar on Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling, and I want a consistency check before I present next week. Here's where I'm at:

(a) The ethical is genuinely universal—it's the sphere of the 'for all'—and that universality is part of what makes moral obligations binding on everyone, Abraham included. He's not outside ethics by nature.

(b) I also think the knight of faith performs a real teleological suspension of the ethical, not just a reinterpretation. The universal prohibition against killing an innocent child is actually suspended, not hidden inside some bigger principle.

(c) But I don't think Abraham is just a tragic hero or a murderer. He's justified because God issues a specific command that creates a higher telos, and that command transforms the moral status of the act.

(d) At the same time I'm a moral realist about the wrongness of killing innocents—it's not just a feature of the ethical stage or a social convention. So I don't buy divine command theory in general; if God commanded me to kill a random child, that wouldn't actualy make it right.

(e) I also think the Abraham story is mainly pedagogical for us: it shows the limits of universal ethics, but since we don't get direct divine revelation, nobody should try to suspend the ethical in real life. The suspension is only available inside that absolute relation to the absolute.

(f) One more thing: I think the double movement—giving up Isaac with infinate resignation and then recieving him back by virtue of the absurd—is the key structure. The knight returns to the universal with joy, which means the ethical isn't destroyed, just temporarily set aside.

So which of these are pulling against each other? I feel like (a) and (b) are in tension, but that's kind of Kierkegaard's whole point. But then (c) and (d) seem like they might clash—if wrongness is mind-independent and not overridden by just any command, how does God's command to Abraham work without being totally arbitrary? And does (f) undermine (b) if the ethical comes back intact? Or is there an apparent tension between (e) and (b) that actualy dissolves once you see that lacking revelation blocks the move? I want an audit, not a lecture: rank the tensions by severity, say which ones are only surface-level, and tell me the least costly revision if I'm forced to drop something. Don't list every possible conflict—focus on what's genuinely incompatible versus what just looks weird.
