I am preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for philosophy undergraduates who have read some Wittgenstein on belief and certainty but have not encountered Moore's paradox in any systematic way. The aim is not simply to present the paradox as a logical curiosity, but to have them understand why first-person present-tense belief-ascriptions have a transparency that makes the Moorean conjunction structurally unstable, and to distinguish that instability from ordinary pragmatic contradiction or self-deception. Plan the teaching sequence: specify the exact dependency order—what conceptual piece must be in place before the paradox is introduced, what must precede any discussion of transparency, and what must come last; identify the single carrying example that should bear the explanatory weight (it should be structurally parallel to Moore's case but must not be an instance of self-deception, and you should explain what feature it must possess and what feature it must lack in order to do the work); map the predictable misconceptions in the order they will arise—particularly the conflation of Moorean absurdity with performative contradiction and the assumption that the paradox is merely about sincerity conditions—and indicate the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted; propose one diagnostic question that would reveal whether a student has grasped the structural feature rather than simply memorized the standard formulation; and state explicitly what should be omitted at this level (whether technical doxastic logic, the full historical reception, or debates about belief revision) with a brief justification tied to the dependency structure you have outlined.
