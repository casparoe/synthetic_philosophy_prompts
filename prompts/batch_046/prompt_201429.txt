I've been debating with a colleague whether citizens in a pluralist democracy have a special obligation to bracket theological premises when advocating for coercive policy, and we kept defaulting to "Rawls versus Wolterstorff" without knowing whether we actually disagreed about the underlying structure of the duty itself. I'm preparing a decision-tree handout for a graduate seminar that forces people to locate their view by answering concrete diagnostic questions rather than picking a philosopher's name. The root commitment should be this: when a citizen supports a law or policy that will be enforced on everyone, is there any duty—moral or epistemic—to avoid resting that advocacy solely on premises drawn from her particular religious tradition?

From that root, build the tree through the following splits. First, does an analogous duty apply symmetrically to secular citizens with non-religious comprehensive doctrines, or is religious reasoning uniquely singled out? Second, if there is a duty, what does it require: full translation into publicly accessible secular reasons, mere supplementation with secular reasons that could stand alone, or only an internal check that one's religious conviction is sincerely held rather than strategic? Third, is the rationale for the duty primarily about epistemic accessibility—other citizens must be able to understand and evaluate the reasons—or about a moral requirement of mutual respect among free and equal citizens? Fourth, does the duty extend to non-coercive political expression—voting, public protest, moral suasion—or only to the justification of state coercion? Each leaf should carry the name of the position that lands there—e.g., positions in the family of Rawlsian exclusivism, consociational inclusivism in the style of Wolterstorff, secular-motivation views in the style of Audi, or critiques of the restraint doctrine—or be marked "unnamed" if the combination is unoccupied. Every leaf should include one sentence identifying its most awkward commitment, such as being forced to split one's identity or permitting sectarian reasoning in some domains but not others. Wherever two named positions fall on the same leaf, flag the overlap explicitly rather than forcing a false separation. Identify any empty branches—logically available combinations that seem unoccupied—and briefly diagnose whether the gap looks incoherent, politically unattractive, or merely unexplored. Every node should be phrased as a concrete claim about what a legislator or voter may do, not as a theoretical label, so that someone unfamiliar with the literature can still navigate the tree.
