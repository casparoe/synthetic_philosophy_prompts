I am a doctoral student in philosophy of language and cognitive science, currently working on the boundary between statistical pattern-matching and semantic comprehension in artificial language processing. Much of my reading centers on Douglas Hofstadter's longstanding skepticism about whether statistical machine translation can ever amount to genuine understanding, particularly his argument that correct output produced without an internal grasp of meaning remains essentially shallow. Rather than rehearsing the familiar anecdote about back-translation errors, I want to push the conceptual question into a more subtle register.

Consider the following constructed scenario, which I take to be conceptually possible even if empirically rare. A speaker embedded in a tight-knit subculture uses a widely shared lexical item with an ironic, locally inverted meaning—say, a term of praise that within that community functions as a sharp insult—relying entirely on shared pragmatic background rather than on explicit textual cues. A statistical translation system renders the utterance correctly, not because it possesses any model of the speaker's intentions, but because the co-occurrence of specific proper names, dialect markers, and topical keywords in the training corpus correlates strongly with the inverted rendering. A human translator lacking that subcultural exposure, by contrast, might deliver a literal translation that entirely misses the pragmatic force. Here is the issue I want to unpack: does the statistical system's success in this case demonstrate a form of distributed, implicit understanding of pragmatic context, or does it remain an instance of correlation-based contextual resolution that never crosses into intentional grasp? More specifically, please explain the conceptual distinction between resolving ambiguity via high-dimensional statistical correlation and resolving it via an internal representation of speaker meaning, and address whether Hofstadter's critique implicitly presupposes that understanding must be anchored in a unitary, conscious subject. If that presupposition can be relaxed, does the scenario I have described compel us to revise the shallow-translation thesis, or can it be accommodated within a refined version of that thesis that distinguishes between behavioral competence and semantic comprehension?

Please provide an intuitive yet precise conceptual analysis of where the line between statistical competence and genuine understanding should be drawn in such cases, paying particular attention to whether implicit encoding of cultural correlations within a model's weights can be conceptually assimilated to a form of knowing-how without knowing-that, and whether that assimilation ultimately collapses the distinction Hofstadter wants to defend.
