okay so i've been stuck on this thing about whether death is actually bad for you. like, the epicurean line — death is nothing to us, when we exist death isn't present, etc — versus the deprivation view where dying young robs you of goods you'd otherwise recieve. philosophers have been going back and forth on this since at least Nagel's classic paper and honestly it feels like nobody's moved an inch. everyone agrees suffering is bad and being dead involves no concious experience, but they still split right down the middle.

my working theory is that the stall isn't about value theory at all but about hidden ontological commitments: the epicureans implicitly need a presentist or endurantist picture where there's no future self to be the subject of harm, while the deprivationists need perdurantism or at least some modal realism about counterfactual future lives. but neither side really talks about metaphysics of personal identity, they just talk about 'harm' like it's obvious what that requires.

so is that right? or is there a hidden formal disagreement about counterfactual comparison classes? is it that they're answering different questions — one about phenomenological experience, one about axiological comparison — and just using the word 'bad' for both? and if your diagnosis is right, what would actually unstick it? a new argument in personal identity, a conceptual distinction about subjects of harm, or should we just accept that it's bedrock intuition about whether non-existence can be a misfortune? i want the diagnosis, not a survey of who said what.
