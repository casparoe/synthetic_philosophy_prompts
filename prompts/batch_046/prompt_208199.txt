I'm trying to work out what I actually think about ambiguity aversion by looking at my gut reactions on concrete cases, and I keep hitting a wall. Here are my verdicts.

Classic urn setup: 30 red balls, 60 that are either blue or yellow in unknown proportions. I'd rather bet on red being drawn than on blue being drawn. I'd also rather bet on red-or-yellow than on blue-or-yellow. Both feel correct to me, even though I know they can't both be right under standard expected utility.

Real-world analog: I'm picking where to put $10k. A government bond with a guaranteed 3% return, or equity in a startup in a brand-new market where experts genuinely disagree and nobody can assign probabilities. I take the bond every time. Same with medical: if my kid needs surgery, Surgeon A has an 85% success rate over 200 documented cases, Surgeon B uses a cutting-edge technique with no long-term stats but a world-class reputation. I pick Surgeon A, no question.

But then there's this other case that breaks the pattern. I'm choosing a restaurant for my anniversary. Place A has 500 reviews averaging 4.2 stars, very consistent. Place B just opened, zero reviews, but my foodie friend who knows teh chef raves about it. I go with B. That feels right too, which is weird because I'm actively seeking ambiguity instead of avoiding it.

One more: I'm buying insurance. Flood coverage in a region with 100 years of data vs wildfire coverage where climate change has made teh probabilities genuinely unknown. I'd pay a premium for the flood policy and avoid the wildfire one unless it's dirt cheap.

So is there one principle that captures all of these, or am I just inconsistent? If there is a principle, what does it predict for a case I haven't listed -- say, a city planner choosing between two climate adaptation strategies, one with well-modeled risk probabilities and one with deep uncertainty about sea-level timing? And if I have to drop one verdict to get a clean account, which one should go: my ambiguity-averse investment and medical choices, or my ambiguity-seeking anniversary dinner?

Please state the principle precisely, say which clause handles which case, predict the planner case, and be honest about which of my gut reactions it can't save.
