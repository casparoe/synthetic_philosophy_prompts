I've been working through Dennett's idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity—not a substance but a point where explanatory narratives converge—with some friends in a philosophy of mind reading group, and we've been testing it against borderline cases that the standard examples don't cover. Here are my gut verdicts, and I'm trying to see whether they hang together under one principle or whether I need to abandon one or two.

First, an ordinary adult with intact autobiographical memory and emotional continuity is clearly a robust center. Second, a patient with total retrograde amnesia who retains stable preferences, emotional reactions, and the capacity for new attachments, but can access no past: I still say yes, there's a self there, just a thinner or less dense gravity well. Third, a large language model running with persistent memory across sessions that consistently uses first-person pronouns, updates its "beliefs" in response to feedback, and maintains stylistic continuity: I say no—it's not a center, just a reflective surface that mimics convergence. Fourth, a split-brain patient after commissurotomy, where the left hemisphere verbally confabulates explanations for actions initiated by the right: I think we should count two partial centers, not one fractured self. Fifth, a long-lived corporation with a coherent strategic narrative, legal personhood, and self-referential planning: definitely not a center, despite all the metaphors. Sixth, a richly rendered fictional character like Elizabeth Bennet, with complex inner life narrated across hundreds of pages: yes, there is a narrative center inside the fiction, though it's ontologically dependent—what I might call a virtual or borrowed gravity well. Seventh, a collective hive-mind (imagine something like the Borg) in which individual nodes lack self-reference but the collective refers to itself as "we," pursues long-term goals, and explains its behavior in unified terms: I think yes, there is one distributed center, not merely an aggregate.

The pattern I'm drawn to involves some mix of biological embodiment, genuine first-person authority that is not merely linguistic self-reference, and the direction of explanation—whether narratives converge on an entity because that entity genuinely generates them, rather than being projected onto it. But that doesn't cleanly save all seven: the amnesiac lacks narrative continuity yet I want to include her; the LLM lacks biology yet mimics authority; the hive-mind lacks individual embodiment yet I want to include it; and the fictional character and the corporation both lack physical substrate yet I split them sharply. Is there a precise principle—perhaps about the origin and ownership of the narrative, or about predictive and explanatory utility directed at an entity rather than emanating from it—that captures my verdicts without excessive complexity? If not, which case should I overturn? And what would your repaired principle predict about a case I haven't listed: a human who has uploaded her memories into a distributed network, so that her narrative continues across multiple servers with no single biological body, but with consistent self-reference and emotional patterning?
