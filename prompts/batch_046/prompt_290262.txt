The debate over whether societies genuinely 'collapse'—with the Classic Maya lowlands, the Late Bronze Age palace economies of the eastern Mediterranean, and Rapa Nui (Easter Island) treated as the canonical test cases—has persisted for decades without resolution, despite broad agreement on the underlying record. Both sides accept the same archaeological and paleoenvironmental findings: the abandonment of major Classic Maya centers and the cessation of monumental construction in the Terminal Classic; the dissolution of palace-centered redistribution and the fragmentation of interregional exchange networks around 1200 BCE in the Levant and Aegean; and the deforestation, soil erosion, and abrupt end to moai construction on Rapa Nui. The disagreement is not about these facts but about whether they constitute a 'collapse' or merely a rapid restructuring into smaller-scale, non-state political economies.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not empirical—no one expects a new radiocarbon sequence or pollen core to settle it—but conceptual and normative in a way neither camp fully acknowledges. One side defines collapse functionally: a rapid, involuntary decline in sociopolitical complexity, measured by administrative reach, monumentality, and the scale of economic integration. The other defines it ontologically: a society has failed only if the underlying cultural-ecological system itself terminates, which they claim never occurs because populations persist, adapt, and reproduce culture in altered forms. Under these competing definitions, the identical evidence simultaneously demonstrates collapse and disproves it.

I want a diagnosis of the disagreement's actual structure, not a survey of interpretations. Is this a verbal dispute that could be dissolved by stipulating two distinct terms—say, 'administrative decoupling' versus 'cultural discontinuity'—or is there a hidden substantive disagreement about causation, with one side treating environmental stress as sufficient for systemic breakdown and the other treating it as a trigger that only fractures societies lacking adaptive institutional redundancy? Are the two sides actually answering different questions—one asking why states fall, the other asking whether people survive—under the single heading 'collapse'? Or does the stall reflect bedrock intuitions about whether large-scale complexity is intrinsically valuable, or mismatched evidentiary standards about what counts as data on societal health?

Please diagnose it using what proponents on each side actually claim about these three cases. Identify whether the question is ill-posed because it conflates state failure with societal failure, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal distinction between layers of social organization, a reframing that separates descriptive metrics from normative judgments, an empirical finding about post-'collapse' household resilience that both sides must accommodate, or an argument that the debate should be abandoned as a category error. Be honest about whether anything could actually move it.
