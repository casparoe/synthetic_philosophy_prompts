I serve on an ethics review board for a robotics lab, and we've recently implemented an incident-reporting taxonomy that requires investigators to classify every anomalous system behavior into exactly one of three mutually exclusive boxes: a physical-stance explanation (sensor noise, thermal drift, actuator error), a design-stance explanation (the architecture was optimized for a different environment, or a module failed to meet its specification), or an intentional-stance explanation (the system formed a false belief about its surroundings, or pursued an inferred user goal in a way that implied reasoning about mental states). The form explicitly prohibits hybrid classifications, and there's an institutional norm—never written down, but consistently enforced—that reports filed under the intentional-stance box trigger a separate audit pathway, one framed in the language of agency and accountability, while the other two are routed through standard engineering review.

This structure puzzles me, because it seems to treat the three stances not merely as pragmatic predictive tools but as carving nature at its joints. I'm trying to understand what theory of real patterns this taxonomy actually presupposes. Does it commit to a Dennett-style view that intentional patterns are just as real as physical ones, provided they earn their predictive keep, or does the separate audit pathway tacitly assume that intentional descriptions track an ontologically distinct kind of fact—something that only emerges once a system crosses a threshold of representational complexity? Can the practice be made consistent with a thoroughly instrumentalist reading, where all three boxes are just useful heuristics with no deeper metaphysical weight, or does the institutional separation between "engineering defect" and "agent-level failure" smuggle in a commitment it can't justify? And if we were persuaded that patterns are always stance-relative in a purely pragmatic sense, with no special reality attaching to beliefs or goals, what exactly would have to change in our reporting procedure—would the tripartite form become incoherent, or could it survive as an administrative fiction?
