I've come round to thinking self-deception is conceptually impossible — not just rare, but a kind of category mistake. If belief is transparent to reasoning and action, you can't genuinely hold p and not-p at the same time with one hidden from yourself; the hidden belief would have to be causally inert, and then it's not really belief, just a wish or suppressed hunch. So when people talk about knowing deep down they're usually describing regret or conflicted desire, not a buried belief.

But I've also been tinkering with this niche idea that in hierachical predictive processing, a high-level model can suppress lower-level prediction errors without ever encoding the contradiction explicitly — so the brain might maintain incompatible representations in different layers. That feels like an escape route: maybe self-deception is impossible at the personal level but structurally inevitable at the subpersonal level.

What would count against this whole package? I want defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward — specific cases, intuition pumps, or results elsewhere (say about mental representation or belief attribution) that break the conceptual argument or show my predictive-processing caveat has qualified things into unfalsifiability. Which objections would defenders of the impossible view concede are real problems? Which are just people misdescribing weakness of will? And is insulation actually a problem for a claim like this, or only for empirical ones?

Don't just list the usual suspects. I want to know what should actually move me, whether the escape hatch saves anything or just shifts the bump in the carpet, and whether there's a decisive observation that'd make me drop the whole thing.
