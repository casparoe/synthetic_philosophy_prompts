I was arguing with a close friend until late last night about whether moral revolutions—the end of dueling, the abolition of slavery—happen mainly through rational argument, through shifts in material interest, or through transformations in honor and shame. She kept returning to Bernard Williams’s idea that a practice dies when it becomes dishonorable to perform it, not when philosophers prove it wrong; I kept countering with Kwame Anthony Appiah’s argument that dueling collapsed when aristocratic status no longer depended on a monopoly over violence, which means the change was structural and status-driven rather than a sudden moral awakening. We went in circles partly because we had no concrete narrative that could show honor operating as an institutional mechanism rather than just an individual feeling.

Read Pushkin’s Eugene Onegin, and specifically the ball at the Larins’ estate and the duel that kills Lensky, through exactly this framework. Onegin flirts with Olga largely out of boredom, accepts Lensky’s challenge though he considers the ritual stupid, and kills his friend in a ceremony arranged by seconds according to rules neither man believes in anymore. I want a reading that treats the duel not merely as personal tragedy but as an anatomy of institutional honor: what makes refusal socially impossible when both parties privately prefer it? Is Onegin acting to defend personal reputation, submitting to an external code that threatens social death, or participating in a structural inevitability that no longer binds anyone sincerely? Does his modern irony—he understands the absurdity and proceeds anyway—show that the norm is already hollow, supporting Appiah’s claim that honor is being redefined by social utility rather than moral conviction? Or does it dramatize Williams’s thesis that shame can compel compliance precisely when belief has evaporated, revealing a gap between knowing a practice is wrong and being able to withdraw? Point me to the specific scenes—the ball, the challenge, the seconds arranging the pistols, the killing and its aftermath—where this mechanism is visible, and say what Pushkin’s irony adds that philosophical argument alone misses.

Please do not reduce the novel to an illustration: Onegin is not a noble victim crushed by tradition but a self-aware subject who chooses complicity. Tell me where the philosophical reading holds and where the text resists it—whether in Onegin’s psychology, in the social machinery of the seconds, or in Pushkin’s own narrative distance. And as you answer, be explicit about how confident you are in each interpretive claim, and identify the single point—whether about Onegin’s motives, about the fit with Williams versus Appiah, or about the role of irony—where you are least certain.
