I'm currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on critical archival studies, and our instructor has asked us to map the conceptual landscape around archival silence—specifically, who gets recorded, who is omitted, and what kind of power operates in those omissions. Rather than sorting scholars by the labels they adopt, I want a diagnostic decision tree that takes a student from concrete judgments about specific cases to a named theoretical stance. The tree should begin with a single root scenario: a municipal archive that preserves every bureaucratic document of a 1970s urban-renewal program—maps, memos, appraisal forms—while holding no testimony from the displaced residents, even though an independent oral-history project later collected such testimony. From there, each branch node should pose one concrete claim or case comparison on which the positions genuinely diverge—phrased so that a student can answer without knowing which interpretation the answer leads to.

The tree should have six to eight nodes and must locate the following positions as leaves: an archival-positivist recovery view; a Foucauldian discursive-closure view; a postcolonial epistemic-rupture view; a material-institutional contingency view; a Derridean structural-exclusion view; and an agonistic counter-archive view. To keep the instrument from being a mere encyclopedia entry, please inject the following conceptual distinction into one of the nodes: distinguish between "negative space"—absence that is relationally defined by the shape of what is present (the silhouette of the missing testimony formed by the bureaucratic record)—and "void"—absence that leaves no relational trace within the archive itself. Use this distinction, together with a second concrete case (for instance, digitized colonial photographs whose sitters' names are lost but whose compositional framing remains intact), to separate positions that might otherwise collapse into one another.

Please build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of names: first state the diagnostic questions and the combinations they allow, then place the named positions at the corresponding leaves, and explicitly mark any unoccupied combinations with a brief diagnosis (e.g., incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). Also flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot reliably separate—such as whether material-institutional contingency and certain Foucauldian answers overlap on particular branches—rather than papering over the ambiguity. The deliverable should be a formal taxonomy with the root case, each node phrased as an answerable question, a scoring key mapping answer paths to positions, and a brief commentary on the gaps and overlaps. Do not include reading lists, citations, or bibliographic recommendations; the point is to sort commitments, not to direct further research.
