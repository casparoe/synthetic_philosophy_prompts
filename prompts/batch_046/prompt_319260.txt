I recently moved from graduate work in moral philosophy into legislative drafting, and last month I was assigned to help craft a bill that would remove criminal penalties for a specific category of consensual adult conduct—behavior that involves no direct victim but that remains widely condemned on traditional moral and religious grounds. During committee review, a senior legislator dismissed the proposal with the line: “You can’t legislate morality. Hart settled that against Devlin.” He used it to claim that any law tracking moral disapproval rather than tangible harm is conceptually illegitimate, and therefore that decriminalization is the only philosophically coherent path.

Can you take that slogan—“You can’t legislate morality”—and lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing specifically within the Hart–Devlin debate on legal moralism? For each reading, specify: (i) the exact sense of “can” at work (logical, empirical, normative, institutional); (ii) whose authority is constrained (the state, legislators, courts); (iii) whether the claim is conceptual, empirical, or normative; (iv) whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false; (v) whether it actually supports the legislator’s conclusion that my bill should advance; and (vi) whether it belongs to Hart, to Devlin, to neither, or is a misreading of both. In constructing the menu, please attend especially to readings that invoke Devlin’s “disintegration” argument about shared social morality, Hart’s distinction between positive and critical morality, and the possibility that the slogan expresses an epistemic humility claim (that legislators lack the moral knowledge to codify norms) rather than a jurisdictional one. Present the readings as a menu rather than selecting a single winner.
