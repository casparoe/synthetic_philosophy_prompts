I'm presenting at a philosophy of language seminar a thesis I call the contextual-delegation norm of assertion: a speaker may sincerely assert p without personally believing or knowing p, provided she occupies a recognized institutional or testimonial role—such as a designated expert or spokesperson—that socially licenses others to rely on her utterance in that specific context, making the warrant contextual entitlement rather than internal epistemic state. The audience consists of orthodox defenders of the knowledge norm, two belief-norm theorists who treat assertion as expressing conviction, and a social epistemologist concerned with group testimony and asymmetry in expert deference; they care about counterexamples involving insincerity, epistemic luck, and whether my view collapses into hidden belief. Prepare me for the objections I'll actually face: deliver exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences with no introduction or conclusion, ordered from the most damaging objection to the least; in each item, phrase the challenge the way that specific camp would put it, state the one concession I should grant immediately, sketch the reply that must land in under two minutes, and flag where that reply is softest.
