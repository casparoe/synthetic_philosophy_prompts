I was listening to a long-form philosophy podcast that tried to cover Schopenhauer’s aesthetics in a single episode, and the host offered a compressed summary of his view of music that I have been turning over since finishing the relevant sections of The World as Will and Representation. The summary went roughly like this: Schopenhauer ranks music at the absolute pinnacle of the arts because, unlike painting, poetry, or architecture, it does not represent Platonic Ideas at all; instead it is a direct, unmediated copy of the Will itself, which is why he describes music as running parallel to the world rather than depicting it. Because the Will in itself is blind, undifferentiated, and without conceptual form, music cannot represent particular objects or states of affairs, yet it can still express specific emotions—joy, melancholy, heroic resolve—because these are not conceptual representations but structural analogues of the Will’s own patterns of striving and temporary release. The host added that this makes music uniquely able to grant direct noumenal knowledge outside of mystical ascetic denial, and concluded that Schopenhauer therefore regards a world entirely without music as metaphysically incomplete, since music is the only non-ascetic window onto the thing-in-itself and thus completes the spectrum of the Will’s objectification.

Something about the slide from “music copies the Will” to “music expresses particular emotions without any conceptual mediation” strikes me as slippery, and I am unsure whether Schopenhauer truly denies all representational content to music or only denies that it represents through the Ideas. I also wonder whether he actually holds that music provides a kind of knowledge comparable to the denial of the Will, or whether its effect is more like a temporary, aesthetic suspension that never really escapes representation. Is this podcast summary a fair rendering? Please take the main claims one by one—music’s relation to the Ideas, its status as a copy of the Will, the explanation of specific emotional expression, its epistemic privilege relative to asceticism, and the conclusion about metaphysical completeness—and tell me which parts Schopenhauer would accept, which are common popular simplifications, which subtly conflate his universal Will with particular phenomenal feelings, and whether the final claim about music being metaphysically necessary survives once those distinctions are corrected.
