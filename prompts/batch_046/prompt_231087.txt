the directness faction — mostly dutch and german members, no surprise — wants a hard mandate: zero sugarcoating in peer review or collaboration. they say soft-pedaling corrupts belief formation and treats people like fragile objects. they also point to a specific occurence last quarter: a senior researcher told a junior that her failed replication had 'interesting methodological implications.' technically true, but deliberately vague. the junior read it as encouragement, spent six months on a dead end, then burned out. the directness people call this an epistemic harm caused by politeness.

the indirectness faction — mostly japanese members and some high-context americans — argues that banning all soft language forces assimilation to one cultural directness style. they claim flat rejection without relational framing doesn't increase honesty; it just drives people to hide failures. they also say junior members from indirect backgrounds already feel exposed by current norms, and a universal bluntness rule would reduce participation rather than improve it.

the twist that makes this messy: both sides agree the replication incident was bad, but they diagnose it differently. the directness side says it was a white lie. the other side says it was a failure of 'epistemic politeness' — being relationally gentle while remaining informationally crystal-clear — and that the senior violated both norms at once. so the dispute isn't just culture; it's whether we can separate affective politeness (protecting feelings) from epistemic politeness (protecting understanding), and whether that separation is even coherent.

our chair wants a formal memo for tuesday with a concrete recommendation: adopt universal directness, adopt a context-sensitive protocol, or adopt some hybrid. it needs a clear implementation timeline if we go with anything other than status quo. the memo should treat the strongest losing-side argument seriously — especially the worry that nuanced rules just become decoding games that privilege people good at reading indirect signals.
