I'm in a class on ancient socities and we keep lumping the Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya, and Easter Island together as "collapse," but I'm wondering if that's actually a category error. For the Bronze Age it wasn't like one empire died — it was more like palace economies decoupling and trade networks breaking down, and writing only disappeared in some regions since the Phoenician alphabet kept going. The Maya in the southern lowlands stopped building big stelae and moved away, but people kept living in the north and highlands with the same language and practices. Even on Easter Island, I read that rongorongo and other cultural stuff continued after the big statue phase ended, so it's not like the culture actually died out. So if all three are really just rapid simplifications or geographic shifts rather than actual societal deaths, why do historians still treat collapse as a single real phenomenon? And doesn't the Easter Island case specifically show that ecological overshoot doesn't necessarily kill a civilization, since they adapted and kept going? I'm trying to figure out if I should apply this to modern global systems — if collapse isn't one uniform thing, then saying we're "due for a collapse" like these ancient ones is just comparing apples to oranges, right?
