I am a retired biophysicist who has recently begun reading philosophy of science in earnest. In my survey of neuroscience, evolutionary psychology, and cognitive science, I have repeatedly encountered a particular explanatory maneuver that I do not have a name for, and I want to know whether it is recognized.

Here is the pattern, observed in concrete form. A researcher sets out to explain a high-level capacity—say, a speaker's intuitive recognition that a sentence violates grammatical rules in her native language. The first move is standard mechanistic reduction: the capacity is mapped onto specific cortical circuits, synaptic plasticity governed by statistical learning, and so on. The second move is stronger: the author asserts that once the neural mechanism is described, there is literally nothing further to be explained about the grammatical intuition itself; the intuition is declared to be nothing but that pattern of neural activation. When a critic objects that this account mentions neither correctness nor semantic content—only electrical patterns—the researcher responds by identifying correctness with a stable attractor state of the network and meaning with its distributional profile across inputs. So far this sounds like ordinary greedy reductionism. But then a third move occurs: when pressed to explain how an attractor state acquires normative force—why it should count as correct rather than merely stable—the explanation quietly reverts to describing the circuit's functional role within the organism's communicative behavior, its contribution to successful interaction, and its integration with wider cognitive goals. In other words, the organizational and normative properties that were ostensibly dissolved into neurons are reintroduced, now embedded in neuro-physiological vocabulary, but structurally unchanged.

I have seen this same three-step choreography—mechanistic mapping, greedy dissolution, covert organizational re-importation—in discussions of moral cognition, where empathy is reduced to mirror-neuron firing and then mirror-neuron dynamics are said to have social function that reproduces the original moral concept, and in aesthetics, where preference is reduced to reward-circuit activation and then the activation pattern is said to encode value in a way that reproduces the original evaluative judgment. The lower-level description does not genuinely replace the higher-level phenomenon; it seems to perform a kind of explanatory laundering, letting the organizational properties out through the front door and bringing them back in through the service entrance, now wearing mechanistic clothing.

My question is not simply what greedy reductionism is—I understand Dennett's cranes-versus-skyhooks framework and his warning about greedy reductionism that explains away organizational complexity rather than explaining it. What I am after is whether there is an established concept for this specific oscillation: the combination of an explicit denial of irreducible high-level properties with their tacit, structurally identical reappearance at the lower level, disguised as features of the parts. If there is no precise term, can you define one, specify its boundary conditions—distinguishing it rigorously from greedy reductionism, from eliminativism, and from legitimate emergence—and identify where its edges fray? I am not asking for a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; I want the conceptual carving itself, together with worked examples that show where the phenomenon applies and where it does not. Please think carefully about the distinctions before committing to an answer.
