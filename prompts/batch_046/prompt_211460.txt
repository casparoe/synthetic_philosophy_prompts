Analyze Willy Brandt's 1970 kneeling at the Warsaw Ghetto monument through Karl Jaspers' postwar framework distinguishing criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, especially his thesis that only political guilt is inheritable by a state and its citizens. First, map the episode precisely onto these categories: was Brandt acting as the bearer of inherited political responsibility for the Federal Republic, as an individual expressing non-transferable moral guilt, or as something in between? Second, examine the domestic controversy—particularly the charge from parts of the West German opposition that the gesture imposed an undeserved collective shame—to see whether it exposes a tension in Jaspers' strict separation of political and moral guilt, or whether it confirms that democratic representation requires a form of inherited accountability he never fully described. Third, ask whether the purely symbolic, non-juridical nature of the act points to a missing register in Jaspers' taxonomy—something like representational or commemorative guilt that operates between political liability and personal morality—and whether adding such a register strengthens or dilutes his central claim that moral guilt can never be collective. Finally, identify what the episode explains well within Jaspers' model and where the model is silent or misdescribes the dynamics of democratic states confronting inherited atrocity.
