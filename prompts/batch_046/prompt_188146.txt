Question: Philosophers distinguish two mechanisms by which thought experiments persuade: "structural isolation," in which confounding context is stripped away to reveal a bare relation, and "domain mapping," in which the normative logic of one familiar practice is projected onto a structurally different target. Apply this distinction to Judith Jarvis Thomson's violinist argument and to the switch and footbridge versions of the trolley problem. For each case: (a) state which mechanism is primary; (b) explain in one or two sentences how it operates; (c) identify one specific risk that the mechanism creates for misleading the audience. Then argue briefly which of the two cases is more vulnerable to the charge of smuggling an unstated normative premise, and why.

Student answer: "The violinist case is primarily structural isolation. Thomson removes the usual emotional and social context of pregnancy—maternal expectations, fetal development, societal norms—so that only the bare biological fact remains: one person's body is sustaining another. This lets us test whether dependency alone creates a duty to continue support. The risk is that audiences might overgeneralize and think the case proves abortion is always permissible, ignoring that it only addresses dependency, not fetal moral status.

The trolley problem, in both its switch and footbridge forms, is mainly domain mapping. The problem imports the logic of cost-benefit optimization from engineering or economics into the domain of killing, treating lives as commensurable units. The switch version applies this cleanly through indirect causation, while the footbridge version adds direct physical contact, which activates deontological norms against using people as means. The risk is that audiences reject the utilitarian answer in the switch case not from principled reasoning, but because they confuse the structural feature of indirectness with an emotional aversion to physical pushing.

Comparing the two, the trolley problem is more vulnerable to smuggling an unstated premise, because domain mapping always depends on an analogy that may fail—here, that saving lives functions like an optimization problem. The violinist case avoids hidden premises because structural isolation merely reveals what is already present in the physical connection, without importing foreign norms."
