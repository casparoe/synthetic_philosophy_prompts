Defenders argue that once S takes effect, R authorizes the simple-majority route, so the enactment was retroactively valid; critics reply that at the moment of passage R still required P, so S was never properly enacted. Student answer: "The case involves direct self-reference, because R attempts to regulate the conditions of its own alteration. This makes it structurally identical to the liar paradox: we have a loop with no stable fixed point. If S is valid, then R now permits simple-majority amendment, which retroactively justifies S's passage; if S is invalid, then R still requires Procedure P, confirming the failure. Thus there is a direct contradiction with no consistent legal status that can be assigned. The defenders are wrong to invoke retroactive validation. At the precise moment of enactment, R still contained Procedure P, so S did not satisfy the conditions it needed to alter R. The doctrine of nomic inertia confirms this result: because a self-amending rule retains its prior procedural constraints until a valid amendment is fully executed, the legislature's simple-majority vote never actually modifies R. Consequently, R remains intact with its two-thirds requirement untouched. Finally, this resolves the apparent paradox through Hart's rule of recognition. We identify the ultimate criterion of validity in the system—here, R itself—and apply it consistently. Since R demands P and S employed only a simple majority, we classify S as invalid. Therefore there is no genuine paradox, only an invalid statute; the liar-paradox analogy collapses because legal systems can consistently assign the status 'invalid,' whereas logical sentences cannot be consistently assigned 'true' or 'false.' Nomic inertia therefore preserves systemic coherence by blocking improperly executed self-amendment."
