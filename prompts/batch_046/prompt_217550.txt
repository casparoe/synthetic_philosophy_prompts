I serve on my university's disability-accommodations appeals panel, and after a long argument with a friend last night about where health ends and preference begins, I need a disciplined memo before Thursday's vote. The case before us: a doctoral student in philosophy requests extended deadlines and fully asynchronous evaluation on the ground of what she terms "epistemic tempo dysregulation"—not a diagnosed biomedical disorder, but what she describes as a stable, non-pathological cognitive architecture that operates on seventy-two-hour integration cycles rather than the twenty-four-hour rhythms our coursework structure assumes. She explicitly denies that she suffers an internal "dysfunction" in the naturalist sense; instead, she advances a normativist claim that health and disease are judgments about the fit between an organism's capacities and the values embedded in its social environment, and that labeling her tempo a disability is itself an unjust normative imposition that privileges rapid, synchronous cognition. Our medical consultant, working within a Boorse-style naturalist framework, responds that without a failure of species-typical functioning there is no impairment, hence no disability under our policy, which requires "a substantial limitation of a major life activity caused by a physical or mental impairment." The student replies that her limitation is substantial—she cannot meet deadlines that are arbitrary artifacts of institutional design—and that the impairment is constituted precisely by the mismatch between her capacities and the institution's normative expectations, not by an internal biological defect. My friend argued that accepting this framing collapses the disability category into mere preference: if slow conceptual integration is accommodated, why not every student who dislikes morning classes or prefers to work at night? I want the panel's written opinion to recommend a concrete decision—approve, approve with conditions, or deny—together with a time-bound implementation plan if applicable, and with reasoning that takes both the naturalist and the normativist positions seriously. The memo should honestly present the strongest consideration on the losing side and should be written so that both the student and the consultant can reply to its reasoning directly. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the argument should stand on its own conceptual grounds.
