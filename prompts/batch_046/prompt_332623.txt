I was working through Derek Parfit's nonidentity argument in a study group, and a friend shared a summary they found in an old rationalist forum thread. The poster writes: "Parfit proved that if we adopt a carbon-intensive policy, we don't harm anyone in the future, because the specific people who live then wouldn't have existed under a greener policy. Since nobody is made worse off relative to their non-existence, there's no person-affecting harm—only an impersonal failure to maximize welfare. The problem is that this seems to excuse reckless climate choices, so Parfit tried to solve it with his 'same number' quality claim: if we can produce the same number of people at comparable welfare levels, we're required to choose that path. But critics point out that this collapses into the repugnant conclusion, because it commits us to keep creating people whenever total welfare would rise, regardless of who they are."

Something about this feels off to me, especially the slide from "no specific person is harmed" to "therefore there's no moral wrong beyond an impersonal failure," and I'm not convinced the repugnant conclusion is best read as a direct collapse of the same-number claim rather than as a separate feature of total-utilitarian reasoning. Could you audit this summary carefully? For each major move—the identity-dependence claim about climate policies, the inference about the absence of person-affecting wrong, the characterization of the same-number quality principle, and the alleged link to the repugnant conclusion—please say what's accurate, what's a subtle distortion or overstatement, and what a defender of Parfit's view would correct. I'd also like to know whether the poster's final implication, that the nonidentity problem undermines climate obligations, survives once these corrections are made. As a side question, I'm wondering how the reasoning applies to a less-discussed variant: a policy that doesn't change who exists globally, but systematically shifts genetic traits within a stable population, so that a particular future individual exists with a disability they wouldn't have had under an alternative regime. Does this trigger the nonidentity problem, or is there a distinct conceptual issue here about harming the same person under different descriptions?
