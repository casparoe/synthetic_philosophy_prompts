My friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about lexical threshold negative utilitarianism and whether pleasure and pain can ever be placed on the same scale, and she walked me into an argument that seems to prove every coherent position collapses. She calls the hidden premise “scalar fungibility”—the idea that if two experiential states are commensurable at all, they must be substitutable for one another on a single cardinal axis. Here is the chain she pressed on me:

(1) If pleasure and pain are genuinely incommensurable—meaning there is no common metric that permits rational substitution of one for the other—then a rational agent can never have reason to endure pain for the sake of pleasure.

(2) But we plainly do make such trades (e.g., undergoing a painful procedure for long-term health and satisfaction), so incommensurability is false.

(3) Therefore pleasure and pain must share a common scale, and every comparison of them must be made within that scale.

(4) On any shared scale, either values are continuously fungible (any amount of one can in principle be offset by enough of the other) or there is a lexical discontinuity—a threshold below which suffering dominates regardless of how much pleasure exists elsewhere.

(5) If values are continuously fungible, then there exists some finite quantity of pleasure large enough to justify any quantity of pain.

(6) This entails that the eternal agony of one being could be justified by the mild amusement of trillions, which is morally intolerable; so continuous fungibility must be rejected.

(7) Hence there must be a lexical threshold: suffering above a certain intensity takes absolute priority over any accumulation of pleasure.

(8) But if the scale is genuinely cardinal and shared, the placement of any lexical boundary is arbitrary—why intensity 9 rather than 9.0001?—and near that boundary, an infinitesimal increase in suffering produces an infinite moral difference, while an infinite increase in pleasure produces none.

(9) Consequently, a world in which one person suffers at intensity just above the threshold (with trillions experiencing bliss) is infinitely worse than a world in which that same person suffers just below the threshold and no one else exists at all.

(10) That result is absurd.

(11) So both the continuous and the lexical-threshold versions of commensurability fail.

(12) Hence pleasure and pain cannot share a scale, which contradicts (2).

She insists the only escape is to deny scalar fungibility itself, but claims that without it, (2) loses its support because local practical trades no longer entail global scale-sharing. I want to preserve (2) and (6) if possible, and I am especially reluctant to swallow (9). Where does the chain actually break? Which step—or which hidden assumption between steps—should I reject, why did it look plausible, and what does giving it up commit me to? In particular, does rejecting it force me into a pluralist view of experiential value, into a non-cardinal ordinal form of negative utilitarianism, or into denying that lexical priorities can be rationally grounded at all? Please take a committed position: name the specific failure, defend your choice with reasons rather than surveying options neutrally, and state clearly what the rejection costs—especially for the status of suffering relative to pleasure and for whether any version of lexical negative utilitarianism survives.
