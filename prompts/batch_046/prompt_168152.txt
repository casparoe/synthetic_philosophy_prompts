Exam question: Classical deception analyses require the deceiver to know the truth and intend to induce a false belief in the target. Applied inward, this generates a consistency paradox: self-deception about p seems to require both believing p (to possess the knowledge being hidden) and believing not-p (to be the deceived party), which violates rational belief-closure. Some philosophers have recently proposed resolving this not by relaxing consistency, but by reconceiving self-deception as a failure of epistemic authority—a kind of internal division of cognitive labor in which one mental subsystem encodes p with high confidence while a governing subsystem discounts that informant and authorizes not-p, without global doxastic endorsement of both. Is this reconception successful? In your answer, address both the consistency problem and whether such structural division can genuinely count as deception rather than mere compartmentalized ignorance or bias.

Student answer: The consistency problem is serious because rational belief is globally unified: a single subject cannot simultaneously hold p and not-p. The division-of-labor model solves this by distributing representations across subsystems. One subsystem acts as an informant encoding p, while a governing subsystem treats it as unreliable and authorizes not-p. Because there is no single mental state containing both beliefs, contradiction is avoided. This also captures the phenomenology: the subject feels they "deep down" know the truth while genuinely accepting the falsehood. One might object that deception requires an intention to mislead, and without a unified agent there is no such intention. But this assumes intentionalism, which the model explicitly rejects; the deception is structural, not agential, so the objection misses. However, Sartre's classic analysis of bad faith shows that consciousness is fundamentally unitary. If consciousness is one, then partitioning it into subsystems is impossible, which proves the division-of-labor model is inadequate. Therefore, self-deception can occur only within a unified consciousness that hides the truth from itself, not across fragmented parts.
