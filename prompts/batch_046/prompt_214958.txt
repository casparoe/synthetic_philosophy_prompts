I'm working on a comparative study of historical demographic pessimists and contemporary longtermist ethics, and I want to see whether classical frameworks can survive technological updating without becoming unrecognizable. Specifically, I'm interested in how Thomas Malthus might have evaluated a modern pronatalist position that combines three distinct claims: first, that advances in artificial intelligence and off-world resource extraction could suspend terrestrial carrying-capacity limits for several centuries; second, that if a high baseline of welfare is technologically secured, the moral asymmetry between creating a suffering life and failing to create a happy life gives us strong reason to favor procreation; and third, that because future digital or biological consciousness might be instantiated at astronomical scale, a larger present population acts as a multiplier for total future value. Given Malthus's well-known criticism of Condorcet's optimism about human perfectibility, and his insistence that population tends to grow geometrically while subsistence grows only arithmetically unless checked by misery, vice, or preventive restraint, do you think he would treat this modern argument as a genuinely novel scenario or as the same fallacy in futuristic clothing?

Would he likely concede that his geometric-arithmetic model was historically bounded to agrarian economies, or would he argue that any temporary suspension of resource limits merely delays an inevitable correction—perhaps even accelerated by the very technologies that enable rapid expansion and by the normative pressure to maximize future minds? Please develop his likely response in detail, addressing both the empirical claim about decoupling population from resources and the normative claim that more lives are better lives, without assuming that he either blindly accepts modern growth economics or dismisses technological change outright. In particular, consider whether he might warn that securing welfare for trillions of future beings requires institutional checks that pronatalists rarely discuss, and whether his skepticism about human rationality in reproduction would extend to algorithmically optimized demographic planning.
