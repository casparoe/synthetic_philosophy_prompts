I have been working through the comparative-development literature on why some nations remain wealthy while others do not, and I keep returning to the problem that institutional, geographic, and cultural explanations all seem to rest on counterfactual histories we cannot observe. Recently I encountered an unconventional framing—call it the substrate-propagation thesis—which treats modern institutional quality not as an independent cause of prosperity but as an epiphenomenal transmission belt for deeper basal conditions: pre-industrial soil chemistry, the spatial distribution of domesticable species, and pre-colonial settlement density. The thesis is not widely defended, but it raises a sharp methodological question: if institutions are merely mediating variables, then ranking them as primary causes commits a layer-confusion error.

With that in mind, please rank the following four explanatory frameworks by how defensible they remain once we deny ourselves access to randomised historical assignment: (1) the institutional-reversal account in the manner of Acemoglu and Robinson, which treats extractive versus inclusive institutions as the root divergence; (2) the geographic-endowment thesis associated with Diamond, which locates the origin of prosperity in continental axis orientation and available domesticates; (3) the cultural-cooperation narrative that assigns causal weight to transmitted norms of trust and impersonal exchange; and (4) the substrate-propagation thesis I sketched above. For each framework, assign it to one of four tiers—Robust, Partial, Fragile, or Unfalsifiable—and justify the placement in a single sentence that specifically addresses whether the framework can survive the absence of counterfactual substrate mapping.
