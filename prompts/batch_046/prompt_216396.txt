As a philosophy professor advising a foundation’s institutional review board, I want a reusable decision procedure—a word-flowchart with ordered tests, explicit judgment-flag points, and worked examples—that translates the simulation argument’s epistemic implications into a thirty-minute “fanaticism-dampening” audit for long-horizon grant proposals. The board members are non-philosophers, must approve or table a proposal in a single session, and cannot be expected to supply missing probabilities; the procedure must therefore work with qualitative credence adjustments rather than precise Bayesian updates. It should specify: (1) how to detect when a proposal’s expected-value calculus implicitly assumes base-reality status rather than treating simulation-level uncertainty as live; (2) a “nested-prior” discount rule applicable without numerical credences; (3) how to handle the hard case in which moral obligations are claimed invariant across simulation layers; (4) a safeguard flagging when the audit risks over-damping legitimate precautionary reasoning; and (5) an honest statement of what the procedure cannot settle and where an irreducible judgment call remains. Apply it to three cases: (a) a near-term biosecurity grant with modest long-tail benefits; (b) a space-colonization proposal justified by astronomical future welfare over geological timescales; and (c) a termination-funding request arguing that simulation risk renders all longtermist projects irrational. For each, show the procedure’s output, including one result the board will find unwelcome, and conclude by listing the likely mechanical misfires if the board applies it without human judgment.
