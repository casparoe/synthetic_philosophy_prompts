I've been trying to sharpen my own view on diachronic identity, and I'm running into what feels like a false dichotomy in the standard literature: endurantism is presented as the view that objects are wholly present at each time they exist, while perdurantism treats them as four-dimensional worms composed of temporal parts. But I want to compare the two specifically through a lens that doesn't rely on the usual psychological-continuity or bodily-continuity crutches—those feel like they're smuggling in substantive assumptions about personal identity that should be argued for, not assumed. Instead, I'm interested in how each view handles what I'll call indexical anchoring under complete material turnover. Imagine a scenario in which every physical constituent of an agent is replaced over a finite interval—say, ten years—by functionally equivalent synthetic components, with the replacement proceeding either continuously or in discrete jumps. From the first-person perspective, there remains a privileged "now" that seems to have direct practical authority over future stages. Endurantism explains this naturally if the same enduring entity is present throughout, but it owes an account of how that entity survives total material replacement without being reducible to a series of causally linked stages. Perdurantism can accommodate the turnover by treating each temporal part as distinct, but then it needs to explain why practical reasoning should be anchored to this particular stage rather than distributed across all parts of the four-dimensional whole, especially if the replacement is discrete and there is no continuous spatiotemporal path linking every adjacent part.

So compare endurantism and perdurantism on this specific axis: which view gives a more coherent account of why present-stage reasoning retains special normative weight despite complete material discontinuity, and how each handles the discrete-replacement variant where standard continuity conditions fail? I want the argument to stay conceptual—no empirical neuroscience, no citations to specific texts, no bibliography—just rigorous reasoning about persistence, anchoring, and the structure of practical identity over time. In particular, I'd like you to address whether endurantism's commitment to a single enduring self is actually doing explanatory work here, or whether it can be reconstructed within a perdurantist framework by adding a non-derivative relation of stage-to-stage authority, and whether that reconstruction collapses the distinction between the two views or reveals a hidden cost in one of them.
