I am a sixth-form student writing an independent essay on the ontology of social cohorts. Whilst I have only an introductory grasp of sociology, I am fluent in the vocabulary of object-oriented programming and relational database design — specifically classes, instances, inheritance hierarchies, polymorphism, foreign-key relations, and schema normalisation. I should like you to restate the philosophical debate about whether named generations — Baby Boomers, Generation X, Millennials, Generation Z, and the recently designated Generation Alpha — constitute real kinds, mere statistical aggregates, or socially constructed categories, using exclusively that software-engineering vocabulary.

Please address the three principal positions in turn: first, the claim that generations are natural kinds defined by essential shared properties; second, the claim that they are purely constructed labels lacking causal or explanatory force; and third, the claim that they are real but only as demographic cohorts unified by exposure to particular historical conditions. For each, provide the most faithful translation possible: specify what corresponds to a class definition, an instantiated object, an inheritance relation, a polymorphic override, or a relational join. Then supply the residue report: identify which claims translate without distortion, which must be forced into the vocabulary (for instance, rendering temporal boundaries or causal mechanisms as static schema properties), and which possess no programming counterpart whatsoever.

In particular, I want you to examine the proposal that Generation Alpha should be understood not as a new class but as a polymorphic subclass of Generation Z — inheriting core structural attributes whilst overriding parameters for technological environment and educational format. Assess whether this modelling choice resolves any ontological question, or whether it merely disguises a sociological disagreement as an architectural decision. Conclude by stating whether the untranslated residue reveals a deficiency in the programming vocabulary, or whether it indicates that the original sociological dispute is less substantive than it appears.
