I've been covering the free will wars for years, and there's one stalemate I keep running into — the exchange between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso on punishment and just deserts. Dennett's the compatibilist who thinks we can keep moral responsibility and desert without libertarian free will, just by looking at the social practice more carefully; Caruso's the skeptic who says determinism wipes out basic desert entirely and we ought to treat offenders like public-health cases, not blame-worthy agents. They've been at it for what feels like forever, and thier arguments never seem to land on each other — Dennett accuses Caruso of metaphysical overreach that throws away a useful institution, Caruso accuses Dennett of rebranding retribution with fancy compatibilist language. My working theory is that the disagreement isn't about free will at all — that's just the costume it wears. I think they're actually fighting over whether 'desert' names a real normative relation or just an emotional reflex we institutionalize, and neither side has a shared test for what counts as a legitimate moral reason here. Is that diagnosis right? Or is there a hidden empirical claim — maybe about how punishment actually shapes behavior — doing all the work? I want an honest diagnosis: is this verbal, substantive, bedrock intuition, or two different questions wearing one hat? What would actually count as progress — a conceptual distinction, a study, admitting the question is ill-posed? Also, please say exactly what would change your mind about this diagnosis, and flag where your reasoning is shakiest.
