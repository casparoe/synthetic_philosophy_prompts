I am a PhD student in philosophy of science and environmental humanities, and I am designing a 90-minute workshop for an interdisciplinary graduate seminar of about fourteen students—drawn from geology, STS, and philosophy—on the Anthropocene not as an empirically settled epoch but as an act of contested periodization. I want a structured deliberative simulation that makes participants feel, before any lecture, that selecting a start date is not a neutral discovery but a normative allocation of historical authority, and that the power to ratify a boundary is formally centralized even though the relevant transformations are distributed and incommensurable. To keep this from collapsing into a familiar debate about 1610 versus 1945, I am injecting the framing of stratigraphic ventriloquism: the tendency of a globally uniform geological boundary to speak for regional, biological, and archival timelines that actually began shifting at different moments and in different registers—soil microbiome reorganization, indigenous dispossession records, radiogenic signatures, atmospheric composition—so that a single “official” voice necessarily silences temporal pluralities it claims to represent. Please design a runnable in-class game or negotiation in which participants must act as members of a fictional interdisciplinary commission tasked with ratifying one global boundary marker, using evidence packets that are genuinely incommensurable across scales and disciplines. The rules and payoff structure—using reputation points, veto tokens, or simulated future-resource allocations rather than real money—should be arranged so that participants run into a coordination failure that stems not from factual disagreement but from the structural impossibility of making one register speak for all. Specify the group divisions and roles, the materials and evidence packets, the timeline for the 90 minutes, and the debrief that maps specific negotiation moments onto the philosophical problem: who decides, what work a start date performs, and what is lost when one temporal register dominates. Be explicit about which feature of stratigraphic ventriloquism the activity can genuinely reproduce and which feature it cannot, leaving that for me to supply in discussion. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. I do not want bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the task should be answerable from first principles without web access. Finally, I invite direct pushback: if the framing of stratigraphic ventriloquism is incoherent as posed, or if a classroom simulation cannot genuinely reproduce the authority dynamics of bodies like the ICS, say so plainly rather than accommodating the request politely.
