For a seminar paper on Al-Ghazālī's metaphysics, I am examining whether occasionalism can preserve the voluntary/involuntary distinction that underwrites his moral psychology in the Revival of Religious Sciences. I have constructed an argument that seems valid but yields a conclusion Al-Ghazālī clearly rejects—namely that occasionalism dissolves moral agency altogether. The argument proceeds as follows, with a principle I am calling Causal Inheritance inserted at step (3): (1) On Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism, God is the sole direct cause of every particular event, including every bodily motion and every mental occurrence such as the state of willing. (2) If God directly causes the mental state of willing, then the agent does not produce that willing; the agent is only the locus at which God produces it. (3) By Causal Inheritance—if entity A does not cause state S, and S is the immediate antecedent of action A*, then A does not cause A*—the agent is therefore not the cause of the action that follows the willing. (4) Al-Ghazālī distinguishes voluntary actions, which are performed with an accompanying willing, from involuntary actions, which occur without such willing. (5) But if the agent never causes the willing, and God separately causes both the willing and the bodily movement, then there is no causal role for the agent in either the voluntary or the involuntary case. (6) Hence the presence or absence of willing cannot mark a difference in agency; both types of action are equally non-agent-caused. (7) Therefore occasionalism collapses the voluntary/involuntary distinction, undermining the ethical framework Al-Ghazālī constructs. (8) Since he clearly intends to preserve that distinction, occasionalism proves too much. I suspect the damage lies either in an equivocation between being the locus and being the cause, or in misapplying Causal Inheritance across mental and physical domains, but I cannot locate the exact fracture. Which step should I reject? Explain why that step looked initially plausible, and price what giving it up costs for the coherence of occasionalist ethics—particularly whether it forces Al-Ghazālī to concede a restricted form of secondary causation specifically for mental acts. If I must preserve the voluntary/involuntary distinction at step (4), I am prepared to pay elsewhere, but I want to know what that payment looks like.
