I'm preparing a presentation for my philosophy elective on overdetermination and multi-causal explanation in history—specifically, whether a single historical event can have several independent sufficient causes, and whether competing narratives about that event are rival causal claims or complementary answers to different explanatory questions. I want to use Akira Kurosawa's Rashomon (1950) as a case study. The plot—four contradictory testimonies about a bandit's assault on a samurai and his wife in a forest, framed by a conversation at Rashomon gate—is common knowledge in our class, and the film never resolves which account is true.

Please read the film through this lens. Focus on the four accounts given by Tajomaru the bandit, Masago the wife, the dead samurai speaking through a medium, and the woodcutter, and consider how each assigns agency, motive, and outcome differently. Does the film present these testimonies as four sufficient causes for the same death, so that the killing is causally overdetermined? Or does it suggest that each testimony answers a distinct why-question—about desire, shame, survival, or honor—so that they are not genuinely in competition? I am especially interested in the woodcutter's final version, which seems to synthesize elements of the other accounts: does it function as an attempt to collapse multi-causal explanation back into a single narrative thread, and does the film ultimately resist that reduction?

Please do not treat the film as a mere illustration of philosophy. Note where this framing genuinely illuminates a scene—for instance, how Masago's account of the dagger and her husband's silent stare raises questions about whether an event can be fully explained without reference to internal states that no witness can verify. At the same time, say honestly where the film resists the reading: its deep concern with self-deception, social honor, and the characters' need to preserve face may not map neatly onto epistemic debates about causation. Finally, what does Rashomon contribute to the philosophical debate about multi-causal explanation that a standard historical argument—say, that the fall of Rome had both economic and military causes—does not capture?

For my presentation, please work only with the film's plot and the concepts I have outlined; do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not quote dialogue verbatim—just describe the scenes and accounts in your own words.
