I'm currently in my final year of an undergraduate philosophy program, taking an advanced seminar on value theory and population ethics, and I'm helping my professor design the final assessment. We've been working through the standard formulations of the procreation asymmetry, but I keep returning to a tension that the usual summaries gloss over: what happens to the asymmetry if we reject the framing that compares a created child only to non-existence, and instead allow that the relevant counterfactual baseline might be the existence of a different child—one with a different genetic identity—who would have flourished? I want an exam question that forces students to navigate this interaction for themselves, rather than simply reciting Benatar's premises about harm and absence of benefit.

Please draft one 90-minute exam question (20 marks total) aimed at upper-level undergraduates who have read the core literature but cannot answer by summary alone. Present a precise scenario: a couple learns that natural conception gives them a 60 percent chance of producing a child whose life will be severely burdened by chronic pain (net welfare well below neutrality) and a 40 percent chance of producing a flourishing child, but they also have access—at significant emotional and financial cost—to reproductive technology that would allow them to conceive a different, healthy child with high net welfare. Ask candidates to determine what the procreation asymmetry implies about three options: conceiving naturally, using the technology, and refraining altogether. Crucially, require them to address whether the asymmetry survives once we treat the alternative to the suffering child not as non-existence simpliciter, but as the actual existence of that different, happy child, and whether this transforms the asymmetry into a comparative, person-affecting principle or undermines it entirely.

Give a model answer and a marking scheme built around exactly five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth (summing to 20), and include a one-line note on how candidates typically go wrong on each. The difficulty should lie in seeing whether the counterfactual-identity framing collapses the asymmetry, forces it to generate a duty to use the technology, or leaves the neutrality of non-creation intact despite the available better alternative. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or named theorists beyond what is necessary to frame the problem; the evaluation should depend on conceptual precision, not on recall of obscure literature.
