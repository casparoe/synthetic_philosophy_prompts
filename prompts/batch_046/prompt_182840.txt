I've been asked to deliver the opening remarks at a workshop titled "Explanation Across Disciplines," co-hosted by our university's philosophy of science center and our clinical epidemiology unit. The room will be genuinely mixed: practicing epidemiologists, legal scholars who work on causation in tort law, and philosophers of science. I want the remarks—roughly six to seven minutes—to do more structural work than a standard welcome. Specifically, I want them to be organized around inference to the best explanation, but with a conceptual twist I've been developing in recent conversations with a colleague: the difficulty is not merely whether we have arrived at the best explanation, but whether we have noticed that we have crossed an implicit domain boundary without justification.

In both medicine and law, practitioners routinely take a model that best explains observed data in one setting—say, symptom clustering in a tightly controlled trial—and treat it as licensing inferences about underlying mechanisms in a very different population or jurisdictional context, without an explicit bridge principle linking the two domains. I believe this is a distinctive and under-examined failure mode of IBE, but I do not want to reduce it to standard jargon like "underdetermination" or "theory-ladenness," and I certainly do not want to distribute a bibliography or cite specific texts. The philosophical framing should genuinely constrain what I can and cannot promise: I should state clearly that we are not convening to settle once and for all whether IBE is a normative rule of rationality or merely a descriptive heuristic, but rather to examine those precise moments when a practitioner believes she has reached the best explanation and has not noticed that she has quietly changed the subject—moving from explanation of observed effects to explanation of unobserved causes in a domain the original model was never designed to cover.

Please write the remarks in a polished, formal register appropriate for a university workshop opening. They must land for the experts present without losing the clinicians and lawyers, so avoid unexplained technical shorthand. The idea should shape the rhetoric structurally: I need to be able to say what the workshop will attempt—probing these domain-transfer moments in explanatory reasoning—and what I must avoid claiming—namely, that we will crown IBE as the unproblematic gold standard of rational belief. No citations, no suggested readings, and no references to specific philosophers or works that I should look up; the audience should leave with a clear sense of the problem, not a list of homework.
