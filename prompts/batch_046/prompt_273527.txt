ok so my coworker who reads philosophy on lunch breaks got me into Bradley's regress and now i cant stop seeing it in every object graph. heres the argument he threw at me and it feels like it proves way too much but i cant find the leak.

(1) if a proposition like "alice is taller than bob" actually has internal parts — alice, bob, and the relation — then those parts need a binder to be one unified thing, otherwise you've just got three items in an array with no structure.
(2) that binder cant sit outside the proposition, because then the unity is external and doesnt explain why the proposition itself hangs together.
(3) so the binder is itself part of the proposition, which means it also needs binding to alice and to the relation.
(4) same problem repeats forever — infinite stack of internal binders, classic Bradley regress.
(5) but human minds (and honestly LLM context windows) have finite stack depth, so we cant represent infinite nesting.
(6) therefore no mind can ever grasp a proposition that actually has parts.
(7) yet we clearly understand compositional sentences and can swap parts around in inference, like deriving "bob is shorter than alice" without relearning it.
(8) so if (6) is true, compositional understanding is impossible, which contradicts (7).
(9) the only escape is that propositions have no parts at all — they're atomic blobs, like opaque hashes.
(10) but if they're atomic, logical relations between them are just brute correlations, not structured inferences, so logic is basically dead.

he says (2) is the weak link — maybe the binding is just syntax or a type signature, not an extra object. but then i dont see why syntax isnt just another relation that needs binding too. i really want to keep (1) and (7) because the pointer/array analogy feels right and we do parse sentences. which step do i actually have to drop, and whats the cost? is there a real middle ground like Frege's unsaturated functions, or does that just rename the stack frame?
