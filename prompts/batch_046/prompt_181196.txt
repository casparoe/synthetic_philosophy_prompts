Argue that Orwell’s well-known prescriptions for political prose—avoid the passive voice, prefer concrete terms to abstractions, and never use a long word where a short one will do—cannot be consistently applied to politically committed testimony without generating a direct conflict with Gricean cooperative maxims, particularly quantity and manner. Defend the specific claim that in his autobiographical essays describing colonial violence, Orwell achieves political clarity not by literal obedience to those rules, but by strategically violating the maxim of quantity: he suppresses explicit evaluative language while loading ostensibly neutral descriptive detail with implicit structural judgment, so that saying less produces greater political understanding. Conclude that Orwell’s actual practice reveals his rules to be not universal constraints on transparent communication, but strategic norms that must be selectively broken whenever a writer needs to convey systemic culpability without collapsing it into abstract accusation. Do not treat this as a question about style alone; treat it as a claim about the conditions under which cooperative discourse can carry politically substantive content.
