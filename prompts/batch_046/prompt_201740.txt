I've been re-reading Sophocles' Oedipus the King for a course I'm TAing, and I'm stuck on whether the play is actually about logical fatalism or something closer to Aristotle's sea-battle problem. Most people treat the oracle as just destiny, but I want to read the specific scenes -- Oedipus at the crossroads, the messenger's revelation, Jocasta's reaction -- through the lens of future contingents: is there a fact of the matter about whether he will kill Laius before he does it, and does the oracle's knowledge change anything?

But I also want to push past the standard classroom version. Their's this idea I've been playing with, that Aristotle's example isn't really about bivalence but about whether contingent future events have a determinate truth-value without being necessary. If Oedipus had turned left instead of right at that crossroads, does that mean there was no true statement about which way he went? The play seems to treat the prophecy as fixed but not inevitable, which feels different from logical determinism. Could you read the play with that distinction in mind -- where the philosophical idea illuminates a scene, and where the play resists it? I'm especially curious about Jocasta's attempt to reject the oracle: does she fail because she's wrong about contingency, or because she's trying to apply a philosophical fix to something that isn't a logic problem?

Also, what does the play add that the analytic debate misses? Like, the fact that everyone knows and tries to outrun it, but the running is what fullfills it. Is that just dramatic irony, or does it show something about how future-contingent reasoning breaks down when people act on it?
