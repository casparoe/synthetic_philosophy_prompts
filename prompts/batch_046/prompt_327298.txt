I organize a small discussion group for adults—about twelve people, meeting in a community space for ninety minutes, none of us with formal biology or philosophy training. Lately I have been reading popular books on evolutionary psychology, and I am struck by how easily authors construct convincing evolutionary explanations for nearly any human preference or fear; at the same time, I notice that for almost every explanation I can imagine an equally plausible story predicting the opposite behavior. I want an in-person activity that makes my group feel that tension directly, not through a lecture. Please design a structured exercise in which small teams are assigned specific, somewhat unusual human behaviors—such as the discomfort people feel when a melody is left unfinished, or the instinct to duck at a sudden high sound behind them rather than in front—rather than the classic textbook cases. Each team must build the strongest evolutionary account they can, present it, and then face a hidden reversal or constraint that reveals their narrative could have been told backward just as convincingly. Include clear roles, a timeline for the ninety minutes, rules that keep the exercise moving without my intervention, a debrief that maps what happened onto the difference between a testable scientific prediction and a post-hoc story, and an honest note about which feature of real evolutionary science—such as access to cross-cultural comparison or fossil evidence—the activity necessarily cannot reproduce. The participants should discover the problem themselves through what they build; please do not open with a named theory, and do not ask anyone to read or cite specific scholars.
