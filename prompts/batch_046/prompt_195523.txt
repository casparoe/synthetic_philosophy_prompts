I am running a small reading group on Keynes’s philosophy of probability and have constructed the following argument, which now strikes me as suspicious because the conclusion seems far stronger than Keynes himself would have accepted. (1) Keynes holds that a rational degree of belief requires a determinate logical relation between a proposition and the available evidence; where no such relation can be established, numerical probability is inappropriate and we face uncertainty in his technical sense. (2) He distinguishes primary propositions, which concern singular events and carry weights that are incommensurable across domains, from secondary propositions about frequencies, and he maintains that the weight of an argument—the balance of known to unknown evidence—affects rational confidence without altering the probability ratio itself. (3) If the weight is very low, our belief is fragile, and rationality demands that we withhold practical commitment until the weight improves. (4) In domains such as long-term economic forecasting, ethical choice, or personal vocation, the unknown evidence always vastly outweighs the known, so the weight is perpetually near zero, and because these involve primary propositions, no frequency-based substitute can rescue the decision. (5) Therefore, Keynes’s framework entails that rational agents should withhold practical commitment in almost all domains of life. (6) But Keynes clearly did not advocate universal paralysis; he urged bold action in conditions of uncertainty, appealing to animal spirits and convention. (7) Hence the framework, applied consistently, proves too much: it converts every practical decision into an irrational leap. Where exactly does the reasoning slip? Is it in (3), where I conflate epistemic weight with practical licence? Or does Keynes have a hidden distinction—perhaps between beliefs about frequencies and beliefs about singular events—that blocks the slide? I would particularly like to preserve (1) and (6), but I want to know what rejecting (3) or (5) costs. Please provide your answer in British English.
