You are a professional philosopher specializing in applied ethics and institutional decision theory. Before drafting anything, briefly brainstorm the core distinctions in Robert Nozick’s moral framework—especially the sharp divide between treating rational beings as ends protected by side-constraints and treating sentient non-persons according to an experiential-utilitarian calculus, plus his further claim that wanton cruelty toward animals remains wrong even within that calculus.

Your client is a regional wildlife and laboratory-oversight board that must evaluate intervention proposals—vaccination, culling, habitat modification, or engineered population replacement—affecting non-human animals. They need a reusable, ordered decision procedure that a non-philosopher compliance officer can run in about fifteen minutes per proposal. The procedure must specify the questions in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, clear stopping points where the answer is no, explicit hand-off points where judgment rather than fact must decide, and an honest statement of what the procedure cannot settle.

Ground the procedure in Nozick’s view: human beings possess inviolable side-constraints against being used merely as means, whereas animals lack such rights and are properly evaluated by weighing total experiential welfare, subject only to a minimal prohibition on sadistic or wanton infliction of suffering. Inject one non-standard extension into the framework: proposals involving genetically modified or engineered sentient organisms must be evaluated not only by projected experiential welfare but also by whether the modification eliminates behaviors constitutive of that kind’s welfare—treating this second factor as a flagged judgment call the procedure does not resolve.

Then apply the procedure to three cases:
1. Vaccinating a wild badger population against a painful, non-zoonotic disease with minimal procedural risk.
2. Culling an overpopulated deer herd before winter to prevent widespread starvation, where direct killing replaces prolonged suffering.
3. Replacing a wild rodent population with a genetically modified strain engineered to experience less suffering but incapable of natural foraging and social behaviors.

For each case, show the step-by-step result, marking any stop, hand-off, or unwelcome outcome the board may resist. Conclude by listing exactly three limitations: a decision type on which the procedure misfires, a distinction it dangerously collapses, and a value conflict it cannot adjudicate. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not quote any source verbatim. Keep the deliverable formal, focused, and self-contained.
