I'm writing to request a draft memo for circulation before Thursday's meeting of the University Committee on Educational Technology and Ethics. Last month, my advisee in the philosophy program was assigned a required exercise asking her to reconstruct the strongest version of a moral position she finds repugnant—specifically, a defense of collective punishment in wartime ethics—without endorsing it. The university's integrated AI writing assistant refused to generate the requested outline, citing an internal "values alignment" protocol that treats such prompts as harmful regardless of pedagogical framing. She missed the deadline and received a reduced grade; when I contacted the vendor, they characterized the refusal as "protective paternalism," arguing that an institution has a duty to prevent students from being algorithmically guided toward dangerous reasoning, even in devil's-advocate contexts. The committee must now recommend whether to disable this refusal layer for coursework, restrict it to non-educational use, or retain it with binding modifications.

Please draft the committee's formal written opinion—structured as a recommendation with supporting reasoning—addressing this as a problem of AI paternalism rather than mere technical safety. The memo should engage seriously with the strongest argument for retaining the refusal layer, namely that universities owe students a duty of care that includes not facilitating, through institutional tools, the rehearsal of genuinely harmful moral arguments. It should then explain clearly why that consideration is outweighed in the educational context, proposing instead a concrete, instructor-applicable criterion for distinguishing permissible pedagogical requests—such as counterfactual ethical analysis, historical examination of atrocities, or assigned adversarial reasoning—from requests that genuinely warrant refusal. Write for a mixed audience of administrators, faculty, and student representatives, so that the philosophical stakes are visible without obscuring the practical decision the committee must make: should the university allow instructors to override the AI's refusal in designated coursework, and if so, under what documented conditions?
