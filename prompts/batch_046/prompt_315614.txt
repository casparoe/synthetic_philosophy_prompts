I serve as a voting member on the Joint Restitution and Ethics Committee of a university museum whose holdings include works comparable to the Benin Bronzes and fragments analogous to the Parthenon Marbles; at our meeting next Thursday we must decide whether to adopt an in-house proposal called the "Differential Moral Weighting" protocol for all pending and future restitution claims. The protocol applies the logic of gains from trade to conflicts between restorative-justice and preservationist value systems: rather than treating repatriation as a binary choice between full physical return and retention, it structures negotiations so that each party relaxes a non-absolute moral demand—claimants might accept shared custodianship or rotating display rights rather than exclusive possession, while the museum might surrender curatorial monopoly and share revenue in exchange for retaining conservation infrastructure and research access—thereby expanding the feasible set of outcomes beyond what either side could achieve unilaterally. A significant faction on the committee argues that moral values are incommensurable, that treating sovereignty or cultural patrimony as exchangeable goods corrupts both justice and stewardship, and that the protocol risks masking structural power asymmetries behind the language of mutual benefit; proponents reply that over a decade of rigid all-or-nothing demands has produced deadlock and that a well-designed compromise framework can produce genuine mutual gains without reducing rights to commodities.

Please draft the formal written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's vote: it should recommend either adoption or rejection of the protocol, include a concrete implementation timeline with milestones and a sunset clause if adopted, and treat the corruption-and-commodification objection with full seriousness—either showing why it misidentifies fungible preferences with non-negotiable rights or demonstrating that the protocol contains a structural flaw that cannot be remedied within its framework—without including bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, which I will supply separately. The reasoning should be precise enough to guide representatives from legal, curatorial, and community backgrounds, and the bottom line should be an actionable institutional decision rather than personal advice to me.
