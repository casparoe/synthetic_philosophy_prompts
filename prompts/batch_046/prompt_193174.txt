Write a short story about an archaeologist who unearths a single artifact from a collapsed civilization: a small brass mechanism capable of only two stable states—open and closed, present and absent—yet the burial context suggests it was venerated as a sacred oracle that could express grief, moral obligation, and hope. The archaeologist must determine whether the object ever carried original intentionality—whether its makers genuinely meant something by those binary states—or whether all apparent meaning was derived, imposed by the ritual practices and interpretive institutions surrounding it. Dramatize Dennett’s idea that even minimal syntactic machinery, like a two-bitser embedded in a rich functional network, can support genuinely meaningful derived intentionality, against Searle’s insistence that syntax without intrinsic semantic content remains empty no matter how elaborate the embedding. Do not resolve the debate through exposition; instead, let the artifact behave unpredictably in the archaeologist’s hands—perhaps it shifts states in ways that match her own unspoken intentions, or refuses to align with any interpretation she imposes—so that the tension between original and derived meaning becomes felt rather than argued. Keep the philosophical stakes implicit in character and plot: the archaeologist’s own acts of cataloging, naming, and displaying the object should mirror the very question she asks about the makers, leaving the reader unsure whether she has discovered a mind or invented one.
