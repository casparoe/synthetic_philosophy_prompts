As a novelist, I have spent years attempting to render collective movements with something more honest than the cliché of a single voice rising from a crowd, and this has led me into a philosophical dispute that seems to have calcified: the debate over whether populism expresses a pre-existing will of the people or manufactures it through rhetorical ventriloquism. On one side stand those who treat popular sovereignty as an empirical datum—elections, polls, protests read as direct transcripts—so that populism becomes a corrective to elite misrepresentation. On the other stand those who argue that the people is not an agent with beliefs but a constructed subject, assembled through synecdoche and narrative focalization, meaning any claim to embody its will is a performative fiction rather than a report. The stalemate is remarkable: both sides accept the same historical cases—the same rallies, the same ballot results, the same institutional failures—yet neither concedes an inch. Is this disagreement genuinely about the ontology of collective agency, or is it verbal, turning on whether will is being used descriptively or normatively? Might there be a hidden empirical question—perhaps about the psychology of collective identification or the conditions under which dispersed preferences aggregate into a single intentional stance—that neither camp has articulated? Or are they answering entirely different questions, one sociological and one aesthetic-political, under the shared heading of the will of the people? Given your diagnosis, supported by what each side actually says rather than a survey of positions, is there any conceptual distinction—say, between a statistical aggregation and a narrative protagonist—any empirical study of collective intentionality, or any formal model of representation that could actually move the debate? Or does the impasse stem from bedrock intuitions about whether a crowd can possess a unified point of view—an intuition I recognize from my own attempts to write it—such that no progress should be expected and fiction must dramatize the tension rather than resolve it?
