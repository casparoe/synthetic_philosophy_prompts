Your colleague is deciding whether to take a substantial position in a specialized prediction market that prices the likelihood of a particular milestone—sustained net-positive energy output from a specific magnetic-confinement fusion design before the end of 2027—at roughly thirty-five percent. She has access to an internal engineering assessment that she regards as technically sound, which points to an underlying physical propensity closer to sixty percent for success within that window. At the same time, she knows that the market price is produced by an aggregation mechanism explicitly calibrated to converge on objective frequencies across comparable long-range engineering projects, and she has no independent reason to regard that mechanism as miscalibrated in this domain. She is not asking about risk tolerance, portfolio diversification, or expected utility; she wants to know whether rational belief formation obliges her to revise her personal credence downward to match the institutional figure, or whether she may legitimately retain her first-order physical assessment and act on it. How should she think about the conflict between direct evidence concerning a specific propensity and deference to a broadly calibrated external measure of chance? In particular, is there a coherent way to maintain that the physical chance is sixty percent while also holding that her own rational credence ought to be thirty-five percent, and if so, what exactly distinguishes treating the market figure as further evidence about the world from treating it as partly constitutive of the chance she is trying to estimate?
