I am a retired ecologist who spent three decades tracking coastal population dynamics and who, since leaving the laboratory, have begun auditing philosophy seminars with the patience I once reserved for long tidal records. Next month I am moderating a colloquium interview with a decision theorist I will call Dr. Elena Voss, who has recently argued that the classic money-pump objections to diachronically intransitive preferences depend on an implicit framing of the agent as static. She proposes replacing the standard transitivity constraint with what she terms trajectory coherence: a sequence of preference states is rational if each transition can be justified by an improvement in the agent's epistemic position, even when the overall path is cyclical and even when an adversarial bookie could in principle extract value across the loop. I want a sequence of interview questions, delivered entirely in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, that treats her position with the precision it demands and does not allow her to retreat to the familiar claim that diachronic rationality is merely synchronic rationality applied repeatedly.

Specifically, I need at least one question that forces her to distinguish trajectory coherence from simple vulnerability to exploitation, together with a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that the bookie's intervention supplies an external reason for preference change lacking the internal epistemic justification her view requires. I also want a question pressing whether her framework applies to institutional agents—say, a scientific consortium that revises its risk assessments across successive funding cycles—without collapsing into the claim that any institutionally sanctioned reversal is thereby rational. Finally, include a closing question asking what formal result or empirical observation, other than an intuitive aversion to Dutch books, could genuinely falsify trajectory coherence rather than merely show it counterintuitive, and anticipate her probable response that the view is normative and therefore immune to descriptive falsification. The tone should be formal and precise, suitable for an audience of philosophers and working scientists, and should avoid generic biographical openings or any request for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
