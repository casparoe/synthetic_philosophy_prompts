I have no formal academic background in philosophy, though I have spent several evenings thinking about language and logic after encountering the liar paradox in an online puzzle forum. A friend who reads philosophy blogs recently summarized what she called "deflationary" or "redundancy" theories of truth for me, and since I want to understand the view on its own terms rather than through secondhand gloss, I am trying to determine whether her account is reliable.

According to her, deflationists treat the word "true" as a logical convenience—functioning like "it is not the case that"—that lets us endorse a claim without repeating its exact words. She said they deny that truth is a genuine property or relation, and that they reject correspondence theories not by proposing a rival account but by treating "corresponds to reality" as a confused metaphor, comparable to asking about the weight of a number. She further claimed that deflationists resolve the liar paradox by treating self-referential sentences as grammatically defective rather than genuinely contradictory, analogous to a sentence that applies a verb to its own syntax in a way that produces no propositional content. From this she drew a broader conclusion: if saying "electrons exist and are true" collapses into merely "electrons exist," then there is no substantive truth-property to underwrite scientific realism, leaving belief in unobservable entities philosophically unsupported.

The summary sounds coherent, but I worry it slides from a linguistic claim to an unwarranted metaphysical one. Could you evaluate her characterization carefully? I want to know which sentences represent accurate commitments of philosophers working in the minimalism or redundancy tradition, which are acceptable simplifications that a defender might accept with reservations, and which are distortions—particularly the claim about grammatical defectiveness and the alleged anti-realist consequence for science. I have also encountered, perhaps in an obscure discussion, references to extended versions of deflationism—sometimes described as modal or generalized disquotational views—that apply the basic schema not only to flat assertions but also to modal and probabilistic contexts. Does her summary overlook these extensions, and if they are part of the landscape, do they alter her conclusions about paradox and realism? Please separate the faithful representation from the misrepresentation, and tell me whether her final claim about scientific realism actually follows from anything correct in her summary, or whether it introduces a separate premise she never justified.
