I am delivering the opening toast at the retirement dinner for Professor Miriam Okonkwo, who spent thirty-five years teaching ancient philosophy through Plutarch's Parallel Lives. The audience is split: one half are classicists and moral philosophers who know that Plutarch paired Greek and Roman figures not to celebrate heroes but to train comparative judgment; the other half are her family and university staff who expect an affectionate, human portrait. The philosophical idea must do structural work: Plutarch's method refuses hagiography because virtue is readable only across lives held in tension. I want the toast to honor her by practicing what she taught—not by praising her as a singular exemplary figure, but by presenting her career in what I will call moral stereoscopy, paired with one of her own subjects, the Pericles-Fabius comparison she spent years exploring, where deliberative patience saves Rome but nearly costs Athens its empire. Because her family is present, the parallel must be emotionally legible without requiring knowledge of Plutarch; because her colleagues are present, the structural homage must be exact. The toast should therefore avoid any sentence that admires her in isolation, and it should not collapse into either pure family anecdote or pure academic lecture.

Please structure your response in four named sections, in this order, with approximate lengths:

1. The Constraint (about 150 words): Explain why a conventional heroic biography of Okonkwo would be a philosophical betrayal of her own scholarship, referencing the anti-hagiographic logic of parallel lives without reducing it to jargon.

2. The Parallel Portrait (about 350 words): Construct the comparative portrait. Pair Okonkwo's career—specifically her long, patient mentoring of graduate students and her reluctance to rush publications—with the Pericles-Fabius pairing. Show the same quality, deliberative patience, producing flourishing in one context and a kind of cost or missed boldness in the other. Use concrete, invented but plausible biographical details (a student whose work was saved by slow attention; a book delayed so long it missed a scholarly moment) that feel real without inventing publication titles, dates, or page numbers.

3. The Toast Proper (about 200 words): The actual spoken words, addressed to the room. They should invite the audience to hold Okonkwo's life in comparison rather than admiration—ending with a toast that asks the room to drink not to Miriam alone, but to the comparison that makes her visible.

4. Delivery Note (about 100 words): Brief guidance on tone and pacing—how to make the comparative structure feel like affection rather than criticism to her family, and like rigorous homage rather than pretension to her colleagues.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote Plutarch verbatim; if you reference his text, paraphrase without quotation marks. Do not invent specific publication titles, dates, or page numbers for Okonkwo's work. The philosophical idea should shape what can and cannot be said: for example, you should not include a sentence that calls her the greatest scholar of her generation, because that collapses the parallel structure into hagiography.
