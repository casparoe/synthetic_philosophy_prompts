For an upper-level philosophy seminar of about eighteen students meeting for seventy-five minutes, design a structured in-class simulation that makes participants feel the logic of a commitment race: the way rational, incremental steps of escalation become individually hard to reverse even when everyone sees that the collective outcome is worse, and how early commitments function as credible signals that constrain later choice. The activity should involve real, low-stakes choices—tokens, points, or public pledges—rather than pure discussion, so that students experience in real time the pressure to match or exceed another party’s commitment. Specify roles or factions, rules for escalation and for exiting, a payoff structure that rewards consistency but punishes mutual over-commitment, and materials that make prior commitments visible and irreversible within the game. Include a minute-by-minute timeline for setup, play, and debrief; in the debrief, map specific moments from the activity onto the formal structure of commitment races—sunk-cost dynamics, credibility through irreversibility, and the difficulty of coordinated retreat—without naming the concept until after students have described their own experience. Be explicit about what is likely to go wrong the first time you run it, especially students misreading the interaction as a one-shot prisoner’s dilemma rather than a sequential escalation, and how you will correct that framing in the moment. Finally, include an honest note on which feature of real-world commitment races the classroom version cannot reproduce—such as long temporal horizons, genuine material risk, or institutional path dependence—and explain how you will supply that missing element verbally in the closing discussion so the analogy remains responsible rather than misleading. Keep the instructions formal and self-contained, with no request for external readings or citations.
