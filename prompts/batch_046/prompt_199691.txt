I was just arguing with a friend over coffee about whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational, and we kept running into cases where our intuitions pulled in opposite directions without any clear rule to settle them. I am trying to work out whether there is a single unifying principle that explains when sunk-cost reasoning is genuinely defensible, or whether the apparent exceptions are disjoint phenomena that only look alike.

Here are my verdicts. First, when a musician has spent ten years mastering the oboe but now dreads every rehearsal, I think she should finish the concert season she contracted for, because breaking the contract harms her orchestra's reliance on her—not because of the years already invested—yet if she quits to pursue medicine, the lost decade still feels like a reason to hesitate, and I cannot fully dismiss that feeling as mere confusion. Second, in a research program where a lab has spent millions on a particle accelerator that has yielded no results, I think continuing to fund it solely to justify past grants is clearly irrational, but if the partial data reveals an unexpected anomaly that only this machine can test, then continuing is rational even though the past spending is causally irrelevant; so rationality here seems to depend on something other than the cost being sunk. Third, when someone considers leaving a long, difficult friendship and cites "all we've been through" as a reason to stay, I think that is usually a fallacy, yet if the shared history has created genuine joint commitments—say, co-parenting or a shared artistic legacy—that constrain present options in ways that did not exist before the investment, then honoring the sunk years seems permissible, perhaps even obligatory. Fourth, in a personal aesthetic project—say, a novelist who has written three hundred pages of a manuscript that now feels structurally wrong—I think abandoning it is often rational, but if the very failure of the first draft teaches her something about narrative architecture she could not have learned otherwise, then the sunk labor transforms into epistemic capital, and finishing the book to test that insight feels different from throwing good money after bad.

Is there one principle—perhaps distinguishing instrumental costs from relational entitlements or from epistemic capital—that captures all four verdicts? Or do I need separate frameworks for contractual obligations, scientific funding, interpersonal loyalty, and creative work? Please also say what your proposed principle predicts for a case I have not mentioned: a government that has spent billions on a failing infrastructure project where the only remaining argument is national prestige. Please write your answer in American English.
