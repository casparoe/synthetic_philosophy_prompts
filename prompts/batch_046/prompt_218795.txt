After a long argument with a friend about Adam Smith's impartial spectator, I want to see the idea tested through fiction. She insisted that Smith's framework depends on an irreducible gap between self and other: sympathy works by imaginative projection, and the spectator judges precisely because it is not the sufferer. I replied that if that gap collapsed—if one experienced another's sentiments directly, without analogy or distance—moral judgment would not become clearer but would lose its comparative structure, dissolving into pure contagion. Write a short story set in a world (or in a single intimate situation) where this distance has been abolished—through technology, ritual, or some other mechanism—so that the protagonist feels others' pain or joy not by imagination but by direct transmission. Center the plot on a concrete ethical choice—distributing harm, granting forgiveness, recognizing dignity—where total transparency should intuitively make judgment obvious, yet instead produces paralysis, contradiction, or an unexpected reversal. The story should ask implicitly whether the impartial spectator is destroyed by perfect sympathy, or whether it reappears in some altered form. Do not resolve the ambiguity with a simple moral; let the philosophical tension stand.
