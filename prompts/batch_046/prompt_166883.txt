I am moderating a colloquium Q&A for a philosophy of science reading group I recently joined after a frustrating disagreement in my own research on rational belief: I could not see how to maintain strict deference to objective chance once an agent learns not merely the outcome but the specific causal pathway that produced it. Next week we are hosting a philosopher—unnamed here, but well-known for defending a position she calls “restricted deference” to the Principal Principle—who argues that Lewis-style alignment of credence and chance holds only when the agent lacks what she terms “mechanism-transparent information” about the very chance-generating process itself. I want the discussion to move well beyond exposition. Draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the actual architecture of restricted deference: include at least one on whether the view collapses into ordinary Bayesian conditioning once mechanism-transparency is admitted; one on how it handles self-locating beliefs in branching-world scenarios where structurally identical observers inhabit worlds with divergent chance outcomes; and one on the stability of first-order versus second-order chance distinctions when an agent’s structural information is itself probabilistically grounded. For each question, provide a follow-up keyed to her most probable reply—if she invokes a boundary between epistemic and metaphysical chance, for instance, ask whether that boundary survives her own transparency condition. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: state whether restricted deference ultimately preserves the normative authority of the Principal Principle or transforms it into a conditional heuristic, then present the questions with brief reasoning for why each targets a live vulnerability in the literature rather than a straw man. No preamble, no restatement of this request, and avoid generic biographical or introductory questions.
