As a PhD student in philosophy of mind working on social cognition and extended cognition, I am designing a test case for competing accounts of other minds. I need a thought experiment built to order, with no bibliography, citation, or reference to external literature—work strictly from first principles.

Construct a single-paragraph scenario set in a realistic neurorehabilitation unit. The subject is a human patient with complete locked-in syndrome except for vertical eye movement, communicating solely through a non-invasive brain-computer interface that decodes neural signals into grammatically perfect, context-appropriate sentences. The system produces no prosody, facial expression, gesture, or bodily movement; the patient is not a zombie, not a perfect behavioral duplicate of a healthy interlocutor, and there is no AI intermediary generating the text.

Engineer the case so that four approaches yield specifically different verdicts:
1. The argument from analogy delivers a negative verdict: the observer lacks sufficient first-person behavioral similarity to justify attributing mental states.
2. Direct social perception (the embodied, enactivist view) also delivers a negative verdict: there are no intercorporeal, affectively attuned dynamics through which mental states could be directly perceived.
3. A theory-theory / inference-to-best-explanation approach delivers a positive verdict: the structured, goal-directed, context-sensitive linguistic output is best explained by attributing beliefs, intentions, and affective states.
4. An extended-mind theorist in the Clark-Chalmers tradition delivers a distinct verdict: mental states are present, but they are partially constituted by the BCI-device coupling, so the boundary of the other mind extends beyond the skull.

After presenting the scenario, walk through the reasoning for each of the four verdicts in detail. Then identify the single isolating feature of the case—the precise structural element that makes the four approaches diverge—and explain what happens if that feature is removed (for instance, if prosodic feedback is added or if the BCI outputs random strings). Next, explain how a defender of the argument from analogy and a defender of direct perception would each attack the scenario, and note whether their criticisms converge on the same vulnerability. Finally, conclude with a brief assessment of whether the extended-mind verdict genuinely resolves the problem of other minds in this case or merely relocates the epistemic difficulty to the device-patient boundary. Keep the scenario realistic, avoid science-fictional elements beyond the existing BCI technology, and do not invoke demons, perfect predictors, or lottery structures.
