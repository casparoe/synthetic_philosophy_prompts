I am grading a take-home essay for an upper-level history of philosophy seminar. The essay prompt given to students was as follows.

In the Autobiography, Benjamin Franklin outlines a Project for Moral Perfection that pairs a list of thirteen virtues with a daily accounting system. He also draws analogies between moral improvement and his practices in printing and electricity — treating faults as debits to be discharged and virtues as balances to be maintained. Write an essay explaining how Franklin's moral method relates to his scientific empiricism and to his deist theology. In particular, discuss whether his acknowledged failure to reach perfection should be read as a rejection of Stoic apatheia, or rather as empirical confirmation that human nature possesses a kind of moral inertia resistant to absolute calibration.

Below is one student's answer. Please assess it as if you were the instructor or a TA: assign a specific grade (for example, B+, A-, or similar), then provide point-by-point comments that justify that grade. Identify precisely where the argument is sound, where it makes a subtle conflation, where a view is misattributed, or where an inference does not follow. Do not indicate whether you personally agree or disagree with the student's conclusion; the deliverable should be a point-by-point evaluation of reasoning and evidence rather than a general impression. Please write your assessment in British English.

Student answer:

Franklin's Project for Moral Perfection is essentially an empirical calibration of the self, modelled directly on his scientific and commercial practices. In the Autobiography he lists thirteen virtues and tracks them in a small ledger, marking black spots for each failure; this is not mere metaphor but a direct application of double-entry bookkeeping and of the electrical principle that positive and negative charges must be balanced. By treating vice as a kind of moral debt and virtue as a credit, Franklin transforms ethics into a measurable physical system.

This empirical approach is compatible with, and indeed supported by, Franklin's deist theology. His 1728 Articles of Belief and Acts of Religion state that God designed human beings with rational capacities for self-government, so the ledger becomes a form of religious observance: aligning one's internal accounts with the divine order that governs nature.

Franklin's engagement with Stoicism is also central. He derives his list of virtues and his ideal of emotional control directly from Seneca and Epictetus, adopting the Stoic doctrine of apatheia — the complete elimination of disruptive passions. His repeated failures to achieve perfection demonstrate that apatheia is unattainable for embodied, social beings. Rather than abandoning Stoicism entirely, Franklin modifies it: he replaces contemplative withdrawal with active civic virtue, practised through the Junto, but he retains the Stoic goal of internal equilibrium.

Consequently, Franklin's inability to reach perfection should be interpreted not merely as personal weakness but as empirical proof of moral inertia — a resistance built into human nature that prevents absolute calibration. Eventually he abandoned the project as a failure, turning his energies to diplomacy and politics where perfect virtue was neither possible nor required. The analogy between moral accounting and electrical charge thus confirms that human nature operates with an irreducible resistance to full rational ordering.
