I have recently begun consulting on clinical ethics cases involving patients with complete sensory deafferentation—individuals who appear to retain conscious awareness despite the absence of all peripheral sensory pathways. In reviewing arguments about self-awareness that do not depend on bodily sensation, I have turned to Avicenna's flying man thought experiment, but I want to move beyond the standard exegesis. Please describe the most important implications of that argument for the following three issues, treating each precisely rather than in generalities: first, whether phenomenal self-awareness can be genuinely non-sensory in a way that justifies assessing consciousness in deafferented patients without relying on behavioral or sensory-response markers; second, how the argument bears on contemporary reports of so-called pure consciousness events—such as those documented in prolonged sensory-deprivation and deep-meditation studies—particularly regarding whether these states constitute a direct intuition of the self structurally analogous to Avicenna's suspended figure; and third, whether accepting the argument commits us to attributing minimal consciousness to artificial substrates that implement self-referential processing loops in the absence of embodied sensory input, or whether the argument's implicit reliance on a specifically human soul-structure blocks that extension. In addressing these, please also resolve one tension: if the flying man's certainty is immediate and non-inferential, how should we answer the objection that what he grasps is not phenomenal presence but merely a form of cognitive self-reference, and what does that distinction imply for privileging first-person report over third-person neural markers in clinical assessment? I am not seeking bibliographies, reading lists, or citations—only a precise conceptual mapping and an honest statement of where the argument's implications are weakest.
