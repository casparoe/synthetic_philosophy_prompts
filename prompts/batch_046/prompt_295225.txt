I recently purchased a home adjacent to a 24-hour freight logistics hub, and my regular attendance at municipal noise-abatement hearings has made epistemic injustice personally salient in a way it never was before. I am trying to determine whether the framework—particularly the distinction between testimonial and hermeneutical deficits—can illuminate how residents' reports of harm are received, or whether applying it to environmental acoustics stretches it past coherence.

Here are my verdicts on six concrete patterns.

Case 1: A retired nurse living next to the hub describes chronic sleep fragmentation and cognitive fog. The planning board dismisses her account as anecdotal because she lacks credentials in acoustics or environmental medicine. Verdict: a clear credibility deficit, hence testimonial injustice.

Case 2: A recent immigrant who works nights reports that low-frequency vibration makes daytime sleep impossible; a commissioner advises her to adjust her expectations to urban living. Verdict: also a credibility deficit, but compounded by a hermeneutical gap—she lacks the local vocabulary of ordinance violations and is interpreted as complaining about ordinary city life rather than reporting specific harm.

Case 3: A licensed acoustic engineer testifies that decibel levels fall within statutory limits; residents who feel physically ill respond by describing sensations of pressure behind the eyes and an invisible wall of weight. The engineer dismisses these descriptions as subjective metaphors that do not map onto measurable parameters. Verdict: this is the case I keep going back and forth on. It feels different from Case 1—the engineer is not dismissing the speaker's social identity but enforcing a disciplinary boundary—yet the residents' phenomenology is rendered unintelligible within the only framework the board recognizes. I cannot decide whether this is hermeneutical injustice, a legitimate clash of epistemic standards, or both.

Case 4: A musician and long-time resident describes the sound as erasing the boundary between inside and outside, and is told her language is poetic rather than evidentiary. Verdict: hermeneutical injustice, if the recognized concepts for harm are restricted to decibel metrics that exclude phenomenological descriptions.

Case 5: A child reports that the noise makes him feel as though his brain is inside a jar being shaken, which teachers and parents interpret as anxiety or attention disorder rather than an environmental report. Verdict: hermeneutical failure—the child's experience lacks a recognized category—though I also wonder whether age-based credibility deficits make this simultaneously testimonial.

Case 6: A factory worker with pre-existing tinnitus is told his condition is not aggravated by the hub because he cannot distinguish tonal drone from broadband noise; he knows he feels worse but cannot articulate the difference in the terms the hearing requires. Verdict: a hybrid of both deficits.

My tentative principle is that epistemic injustice occurs in this domain when a speaker's experience of environmental harm is systematically rendered unintelligible or non-credible not because of evidential weakness, but because the recognized frameworks for harm exclude the speaker's social position or phenomenological vocabulary. Under this principle, Cases 1, 2, 4, 5, and 6 are captured, but Case 3 remains ambiguous: the engineer's exclusion seems to operate through disciplinary authority rather than social prejudice. So I ask: is there a more precise principle—perhaps one that distinguishes credibility deficits rooted in identity from those rooted in institutional role, and hermeneutical deficits rooted in missing concepts from those rooted in incommensurable vocabularies—that can accommodate Cases 1, 2, 4, 5, and 6, correctly classify Case 3, and survive without ad hoc adjustments? If no single principle can do this, does the failure indicate that the testimonial/hermeneutical distinction needs finer subdivision for applied contexts, or that my attempt to map acoustic phenomenology onto this philosophical framework is introducing distortions that should be abandoned?
