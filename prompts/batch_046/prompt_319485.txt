I work in software engineering with no formal training in philosophy of mind, but in conversations with colleagues about AI alignment and consciousness, a specific analogy keeps recurring. The meta-problem of consciousness — the puzzle of why fully physical descriptions of brain processes seem to leave phenomenal experience unexplained, and why that absence feels like a genuine explanatory failure rather than mere ignorance — is being mapped onto the interpretability problem in deep neural networks. The parallel is drawn as follows: just as we can specify every weight, activation function, and forward-pass computation in a model yet fail to read off the semantic content of an intermediate representation without external behavioral testing, so too we can map every neural correlate of awareness without deriving subjective experience from the physical account alone. Proponents use this to argue that the so-called explanatory gap is not an ontological deficit but an epistemic limitation — we simply lack the right decoder for first-person reports, much as we lack general tools for interpreting hidden layers. Please audit this analogy with precision. Give the correspondence table: what plays the role of the physical substrate, the hidden representation, the missing decoder, the behavioral probe, and the observer who registers the gap. Then identify the points of breakdown: does the analogy illegitimately treat phenomenal access as a form of external observation, does it conflate functional opacity with the absence of qualitative character, and does it smuggle in a third-person perspective that first-person consciousness claims are specifically meant to resist? Finally, assess whether the conclusion — that the meta-problem dissolves into a temporary engineering limitation — survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the comparison actually reveals that consciousness raises a problem structurally different from model interpretability.
