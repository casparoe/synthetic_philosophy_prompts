Design a 90-minute classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy seminar of about 14 students that makes them feel, before any lecture, the structural tension between Margalit's ethics of memory and Rieff's case for forgetting—not as a simple preserve-versus-erase choice, but as a problem built into the very mechanisms used to institutionalize remembrance.

Stage the session as a concrete deliberation: students are members of a "Resonance Council" for the fictional town of Vellum, which suffered a catastrophic industrial collapse thirty years ago that was officially covered up. The town's infrastructure still includes a public "sound archive"—anonymized survivor testimonies broadcast automatically in transit stations and school corridors. The council must adopt a permanent protocol governing this archive. However, arrange the rules and roles so that participants discover, through their own negotiation, that the criteria meant to safeguard remembrance (narrative coherence, non-traumatizing content, demographic representativeness, civic utility) systematically filter out the fragmentary, contradictory, or painfully specific voices that most resist institutional framing, while the emotional and social costs of maintenance fall unevenly across descendants, survivors, city planners, and representatives of future citizens.
