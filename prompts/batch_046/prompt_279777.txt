I'm taking a seminar on metaethics, and we've been circling a pattern in arguments about the is-ought gap that I can't find a precise label for. Here is what keeps happening. A speaker derives a normative conclusion from a descriptive premise about human biology or evolutionary history — say, that because humans are naturally cooperative, we ought to design cooperative institutions. A critic immediately objects that this violates Hume's law and commits the naturalistic fallacy. The speaker then retreats not by abandoning the normative claim, but by reframing the descriptive premise as a feasibility constraint: they say we ought to pursue justice, and it just so happens that just institutions must align with our cooperative nature, so the biological fact only enters as a practical limit, not as a source of value.

What strikes me is that the same interlocutors do not apply this scrutiny symmetrically. When they themselves argue from descriptive premises in history or sociology — for instance, that because democratic societies have produced higher welfare, we ought to prefer democracy — they treat the inference as ordinary practical reasoning rather than as a forbidden leap from is to ought. The gap seems to function like a one-way gate: it blocks inferences from biological or evolutionary descriptions to normative prescriptions, but allows inferences from institutional, psychological, or historical descriptions without the same suspicion.

This doesn't feel like plain hypocrisy or generic special pleading, because the asymmetry tracks a domain distinction rather than a convenient conclusion.
