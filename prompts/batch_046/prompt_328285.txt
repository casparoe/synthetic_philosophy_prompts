I will defend the following thesis at a moral philosophy colloquium: an act possesses full moral worth if and only if the agent is motivated by a direct, non-instrumental responsiveness to the right-making features of the situation, yet this responsiveness need not be conceptually articulated as "this act is right"; it may instead operate through a pre-reflective normative resonance with the situation. I further maintain that a concurrent affective state—what I term "moral alignment pleasure," a non-instrumental satisfaction taken in the coherence between one's motivating reason and the good—does not diminish moral worth provided it remains a byproduct rather than a component of the motivating reason-structure. Thus the Kantian corruption thesis is false for this class of affect, but the Aristotelian virtue-ethical integration of emotion and reason still misses the mark because it treats emotional attunement as constitutive of practical wisdom rather than as a separable byproduct of correct reasoning. The audience comprises four Kantians committed to the purity of the will, three Aristotelian virtue ethicists who reject any separation of reason and affect in phronesis, and two moral psychologists who study intrinsic motivation empirically and will demand operational criteria. Identify the weakest point in my overall argument that I should be ready to defend rather than hope goes unnoticed.
