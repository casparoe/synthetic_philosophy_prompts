For a philosophy-of-language reading group, I need to lay out my commitments on speech acts before others probe them. Here is where I stand:

(1) I accept Austin's three-level taxonomy—locutionary, illocutionary, perlocutionary—but I think the boundary between illocutionary and perlocutionary is porous rather than sharp, since many utterances achieve their characteristic effect only through the hearer's uptake in ways that blur act and consequence.

(2) I hold that performative utterances in fiction—say, an actor saying 'I do' during a staged wedding ceremony—lack the conventional institutional force they carry outside fiction, yet they still possess a kind of mock-illocutionary status that is not reducible to mere pretense, make-believe, or suspended commitment.

(3) I analyze lying as an abuse of the assertive procedure rather than as a misfire: the liar successfully performs the act of asserting, but violates the sincerity condition attached to it, leaving the illocutionary act intact.

(4) I distinguish institutional performatives (e.g., baptizing, sentencing) that depend on collective institutional acceptance and authorization from non-institutional ones (e.g., promising, warning) that depend only on the speaker's appropriate intention and the hearer's uptake, with no further social structure required.

(5) I take the direction-of-fit distinction—word-to-world for assertions and representations, world-to-word for directives and commitments—to apply cleanly and exhaustively across all speech-act types, with no residual category.
