Please evaluate the following undergraduate exam response. The assignment asked students to compare Christopher Browning's situational explanation in Ordinary Men with Daniel Jonah Goldhagen's cultural-characterological account in Hitler's Willing Executioners, with particular attention to the relative weight of situation versus culture and character in explaining perpetrator behavior. Students were then required to apply both frameworks to the participation of ordinary Hutu farmers and local officials in the 1994 Rwandan genocide, and to defend a judgment about which framework offers the more convincing explanation in that context.

Student response (approximately 500 words):

Christopher Browning and Daniel Jonah Goldhagen offer sharply divergent explanations for the participation of ordinary people in the Holocaust. In Ordinary Men, Browning examines Reserve Police Battalion 101, a unit composed largely of middle-aged, working-class policemen from Hamburg who carried out mass shootings of Jews in occupied Poland. Drawing on direct observational records he gathered from the unit's activities in 1942, Browning argues that these men were not particularly ideological antisemites. Instead, he emphasizes situational dynamics—peer conformity, deference to authority, career incentives, and the progressive dehumanization of victims—to explain why approximately 80 percent of the battalion eventually participated in killings. He explicitly links this pattern to Stanley Milgram's obedience studies, which demonstrated that ordinary individuals will inflict harm on strangers primarily when they are physically distant from their victims.

Daniel Goldhagen, in Hitler's Willing Executioners, rejects the situational model. He argues that ordinary Germans possessed a unique, centuries-old "eliminationist antisemitism" that was not merely cultural but biologically embedded in the German national character. For Goldhagen, the Holocaust was not produced by bureaucratic pressure or gradual habituation; it was the enthusiastic expression of a widespread popular will. He cites the voluntary cruelty of perpetrators and the absence of broad social resistance as proof that culture and innate character, rather than situation, drove the genocide.

Applying these frameworks to Rwanda in 1994 yields a clear asymmetry. The Interahamwe militias and local Hutu officials who executed Tutsis and moderate Hutus were, like Browning's policemen, largely ordinary farmers and civil servants without prior histories of violence. Their mobilization through local authority structures, radio propaganda, and peer networks strongly parallels the organizational dynamics Browning describes. By contrast, Goldhagen's account struggles because Rwandan anti-Tutsi sentiment was not biologically innate but was largely constructed by colonial racial taxonomies and post-colonial political elites within a single generation. Without a deep, enduring cultural hatred comparable to the one Goldhagen identifies in Germany, his characterological explanation cannot account for the speed with which ordinary people were turned to killing.

Consequently, the Rwandan case supports Browning's emphasis on situational and organizational factors. If people with no longstanding ethnic hatred can be transformed into perpetrators within months, then the explanatory priority should go to immediate social structures rather than to deep cultural or biological character.
