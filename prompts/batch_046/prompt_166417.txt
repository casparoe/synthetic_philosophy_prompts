I am preparing a seminar paper on whether so-called moral revolutions are genuinely driven by ethical reasoning or are better explained as structural adjustments in how societies manage status and violence. During a study-group session, another student presented what she called the "institutional-substitution thesis" combining Bernard Williams and Kwame Anthony Appiah. She put the view this way: Williams argued that major normative collapses—above all the abolition of chattel slavery—do not occur because people are persuaded by moral argument, but because the economic and political costs of sustaining the practice eventually exceed the prestige it grants the dominant class. Appiah, she continued, showed the same mechanism at work with dueling in nineteenth-century Britain: gentlemen did not abandon the pistol because they recognized its immorality, but because the rise of libel law and commercial reputation allowed the state and the marketplace to deliver the social control and status signaling that private honor combat had previously supplied. From these two cases she inferred a general claim—whenever a long-standing moral norm disappears, one should look for an institutional substitute that renders the old honor mechanism redundant, and one should treat the public moral arguments that accompany the shift as post-hoc rationalizations rather than causal forces. She added that this explains the geographic divergence we see, with dueling fading quickly in Britain where legal centralization was strong, but persisting longer in the American South where formal institutions remained weak and honor still had to be defended personally.

The framework strikes me as powerful, yet I worry it may be too tidy. I want to know whether her summary does justice to Williams's actual position on argument and interest, and whether she has captured Appiah's analysis of honor without reducing it to pure economics. In particular, does her "institutional-substitution" formulation conflate two distinct explanations, overstate the epiphenomenality of moral reasoning, or import a comparative claim about Britain and the American South that neither thinker actually endorses? If the summary is corrected in these respects, does her broader conclusion about the primacy of structure over argument still stand? Please respond in American English, and where parts of the summary are accurate, subtly misleading, or plainly wrong, separate them clearly.
