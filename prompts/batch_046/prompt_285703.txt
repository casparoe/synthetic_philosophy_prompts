A colleague in my philosophy-of-science reading group summarized Milton Friedman’s so-called F-twist as follows: “Friedman argued that in economics the descriptive realism of a model’s core assumptions is not merely secondary but positively irrelevant—indeed, the more counterfactual the premises, the stronger the model, because predictive accuracy is the sole criterion of merit. Since all models simplify, economists should deliberately select maximally unrealistic postulates—such as agents with unbounded rationality or markets with zero friction—and treat those postulates as permanently insulated from direct empirical scrutiny. The model is a pure instrument: if its forecasts match the data, then asking whether people actually maximize utility or face real transaction costs is not just unnecessary but a category mistake. Therefore, efforts to build more descriptively accurate microfoundations are not merely difficult; they are methodologically perverse, because increasing realism in the premises necessarily degrades predictive power.”

Is this a fair characterization? Does the colleague’s conclusion—that realistic microfoundations are methodologically misguided—actually follow from the defensible parts of the summary?
