Design a final-exam essay question for an upper-level undergraduate political-philosophy course (90 minutes, this question carrying 25 of 35 marks). The question must probe Mill’s harm principle at a point where standard textbook summaries fail, so that a student who has only memorized the self-regarding/other-regarding distinction cannot earn top marks.

Use the following boundary case: a democratic legislature proposes banning the publication and broadcast dissemination of climate-science denialism in mainstream media, not on the ground that it libels any particular scientist, but that it systematically degrades the collective epistemic conditions required for democratic self-governance, thereby harming future citizens’ capacity for autonomous decision-making. The exam question should ask students to determine whether this restriction is compatible with Mill’s harm principle, requiring them to address: (i) whether epistemic degradation counts as harm to others when no identifiable present victim suffers direct injury; (ii) whether Mill’s distinction between direct and indirect effects blocks the justification; (iii) whether treating speech restrictions as legitimate tools for protecting democratic rationality collapses the harm principle into a general paternalism about citizens’ beliefs; and (iv) whether Mill’s empirical argument about the progress of truth through unrestricted discussion is undermined once misinformation operates structurally rather than individually.

Write the exam question in clear, formal language suitable for students. Then provide a model answer of approximately 700 words that makes all four moves explicit and shows how they interrelate. Finally, provide a marking scheme that separates an A answer (all four moves present, with the collapse-to-paternalism objection handled precisely and the direct/indirect distinction applied correctly) from a B answer (three moves present, but either missing the paternalism-collapse worry or misclassifying epistemic degradation as a direct harm without argument). List three specific mistakes that should cost marks—for example, conflating harm to democratic institutions with harm to specific persons, treating Mill’s defense of free discussion as purely deontological rather than partly consequentialist, or asserting without analysis that climate denialism is self-regarding because belief-formation is internal.

The prompt should not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should avoid dependence on obscure biographical details or rarely discussed texts. Make the difficulty conceptual, not archival.
