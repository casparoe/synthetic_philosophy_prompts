Question: In his essay Famine, Affluence, and Morality, Peter Singer argues that physical proximity is morally irrelevant to our obligation to prevent serious harm, using the shallow pond case to illustrate a principle: if one can prevent something very bad from happening without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, one ought to do it. Consider a modified case: a remote sensing network detects a drowning child in an uninhabited region, and a distant human operator can authorize an automated drone to deploy a flotation device at negligible energy and risk cost, though the operator experiences the event only through a data feed and has no direct sensory presence. Applying Singer’s reasoning, does the operator have an obligation to authorize the rescue? In answering, (a) distinguish Singer’s principle of preventing serious harm from a general utilitarian mandate to maximize aggregate welfare, and (b) address whether technological mediation—rather than direct physical presence—affects the moral weight of the obligation.

Student Answer: Singer’s shallow pond argument shows that spatial distance does not diminish our duty to rescue, so the operator’s physical absence is clearly irrelevant. The principle states that whenever we can prevent a serious harm at little cost to ourselves, we must act; here the cost is only minimal energy and a moment of attention, which is obviously not of comparable moral importance to a child’s life. Singer’s reasoning is ultimately grounded in utilitarianism, specifically the requirement to maximize aggregate welfare, and in this case authorizing the rescue produces a large net gain in utility with almost no corresponding loss. One might object that the operator is not physically present and interacts only through a screen, but Singer’s rejection of proximity applies to any spatial separation, including mediated separation, so the technological interface does not weaken the obligation. In fact, because the drone allows precise, risk-free intervention, the mediated context actually strengthens the duty: the operator becomes part of a distributed rescue system in which moral responsibility is amplified rather than diluted. Therefore, failure to authorize the rescue would be morally equivalent to walking past the pond and letting the child drown.
