A recent essay by an unnamed philosophy blogger summarizes David Lewis’s modal realism for a general audience and then draws a critical conclusion from it. The blogger writes that, according to Lewis, possible worlds are concrete, spatiotemporally extended entities that are causally and spatiotemporally isolated from one another, and that “actual” functions indexically—like “here” or “now”—so that every world is actual relative to its own inhabitants. The essay further claims that Lewis introduced this ontology mainly to ground counterfactual dependence: because worlds share no parts, evaluating a counterfactual requires comparing worlds through a “similarity metric” that assigns quantitative weights to shared laws and particular facts. From this, the blogger concludes that modal realism collapses into a hierarchical “modal pluralism” in which each world contains its own complete ontology—including its own internal multiverse—so that there is no uniquely actual world and the theory ultimately dissolves into an unstructured regress of nested realities. In particular, address whether the “similarity metric” framing and the hierarchical “modal pluralism” conclusion are fair representations of his account of counterfactuals and of actuality, and whether the blogger’s critical conclusion actually follows from the parts of the summary that survive correction.
