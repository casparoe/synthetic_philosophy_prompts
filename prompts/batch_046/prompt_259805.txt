I am designing an assessment instrument for an advanced epistemology seminar, and I need a decision tree that sorts participants by their substantive commitments on epistemic justification rather than by the theoretical labels they happen to recognize. Please construct a tree of exactly eight binary branching nodes. The root should ask whether a belief can be fully justified in the absence of any supporting belief. Each subsequent node must pose a concrete, case-level commitment—such as whether circular justification can ever be non-vicious, whether an infinite regress of reasons is objectionable in principle or only in practice, whether basic beliefs are permissible only within restricted domains (for example, first-person phenomenology or logical axioms), and whether a system of beliefs can be globally coherent if every constituent part remains individually revisable. The tree must terminate in five leaves: strict foundationalism; moderate foundationalism that permits coherentist supplementation; pure coherentism; infinitism; and a hybrid view that I will call "domain-restricted meta-coherentism," defined for this purpose as the position that no belief is basic in absolute isolation, but that beliefs within a narrowly circumscribed domain may serve as provisional anchors for the broader system, subject to holistic revision. For each leaf, provide a one-sentence characterization of how that position justifies an ordinary empirical belief—specifically, that a particular chair is red. Identify any pair of leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate, and diagnose whether the overlap stems from logical equivalence, practical indistinguishability, or an unexplored combination in the logical space. Every node should be phrased as a direct yes-or-no commitment about belief or justification, not as a question about named theories, so that a respondent can traverse the tree without prior knowledge of the interpretive labels.
