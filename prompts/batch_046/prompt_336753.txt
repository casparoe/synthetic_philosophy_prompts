I have been studying the paradox of deontology in connection with a rule-based safety architecture I am designing, and I am trying to determine whether the tension is a genuine logical contradiction or merely a conflict of intuitions. As I understand it, deontology imposes an absolute agent-centered restriction—not to treat rational beings as mere means—even when doing so would prevent far greater violations of the same restriction. Yet deontology also grants agents an agent-centered prerogative to pursue their own legitimate projects rather than constantly maximize the prevention of harm. The paradox, on my reading, is that these two commitments are jointly unsatisfiable in formal deontic logic: if I am permitted to refrain from saving five strangers (the prerogative), then consistency seems to require that I am also permitted to kill one innocent person to save five (violating the restriction), because both involve allowing harm that I could prevent. This looks to me like an internal reductio that collapses deontology into a form of threshold consequentialism, with the restriction functioning only as a high-cost heuristic. Some responses I have seen introduce non-monotonic exception conditions or subjective weighting of moral reasons to block the derivation, but that strikes me as smuggling consequentialist calculus back into the framework. So my question is this: does the paradox actually demonstrate that deontological ethics contains a formal contradiction that can only be resolved by abandoning either the restriction or the prerogative, or is the appearance of contradiction an artifact of conflating act-based obligations with omission-based permissions? And if the latter, can a formal system encode the distinction without treating the prerogative as an ad hoc exception that undermines the absoluteness of the constraint?
