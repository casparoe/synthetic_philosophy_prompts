I've been trying to work out whether major scientific breakthroughs are just waiting to happen or whether the particular person actually changes the course of things. My friend Marcus works at a science museum and has been giving me his takes on historical priority disputes, and now I want to know what he would say about a case he hasn't weighed in on yet. His rulings so far: on calculus, he says Newton and Leibniz both deserve recognition, but he insists it was not inevitable—Newton tied the math to physics and mechanics, while Leibniz's notation and formal rules led to entirely different research programs in Europe, so who did it genuinely shaped mathematics for centuries; on natural selection, he says Darwin and Wallace publishing within months of each other shows the idea was bound to emerge from biogeography and Malthus, so Wallace should share full credit and he believes that if Darwin had never lived, Wallace's 1858 paper would have produced essentially the same evolutionary biology within a decade; on oxygen, he sides firmly with Lavoisier over Priestley, arguing that isolating a strange gas is not the discovery—understanding combustion, naming the element, and reworking chemistry around it is—so Priestley missed the actual breakthrough and Lavoisier's interpretation was never guaranteed; on the telephone, he gives Bell sole priority, saying Gray's caveat was only a sketch of an idea while Bell built and demonstrated a working transmitter and receiver, and he argues that practical implementation is never automatic, so the engineer who makes it function holds the credit; and on the jet engine, he says Whittle and von Ohain should share theoretical credit because turbine physics made the concept unavoidable, yet he still emphasizes that Whittle's 1930 patent actually changed the timeline of aviation development, so for practical historical priority Whittle stands alone.

Now Marcus is considering a new case he hasn't decided. In the late 1980s, a biophysicist in Heidelberg and a cell biologist in San Diego both proposed that cell membranes contain "lipid rafts"—small organized domains that control signaling—publishing within about six weeks of each other. The Heidelberg group derived precise sizes and physical rules using synthetic lipid bilayers in a dish; the San Diego group observed the structures in living cells through electron microscopy and predicted functional consequences for signaling that the pure physics missed. Both turned out to be correct, both influenced later research, but the synthetic-bilayer framework became the standard textbook model for two decades, while the living-cell framework was largely dismissed until recent work showed it explains phenomena like drug resistance that the artificial model cannot. I want to know: do you think Marcus would say they should share credit equally, that one should take priority, or that the discovery was inevitable? More importantly, what is the consistent rule running through his five earlier verdicts, what is the best alternative rule that fits those same verdicts just as well, and what kind of further case—say, a purely theoretical result in physics versus an engineered medical device—would actually force a choice between the two rules?
