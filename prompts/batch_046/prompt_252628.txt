As a doctoral student in comparative historiography working on narrative synthesis across non-commensurable temporalities, I am constructing a diagnostic taxonomy of positions on whether the past can be told from everywhere at once without implicit Eurocentric framing. I want a decision tree whose root asks whether the historian accepts that there is a single, globally valid chronological backbone against which all local pasts can be positioned. From there, each branch should split on a concrete historiographical commitment rather than on a named school: whether non-European archives can be interpreted without being translated into European categorical schemes; whether the historian's own situatedness makes universal narration epistemically impossible or merely ethically demanding; whether synthesis requires shared causal mechanisms or can proceed through what I will call contingent adjacency—the juxtaposition of events that share neither cause nor chronology but are made to resonate by the narrative form itself; and whether the absence of a global subject of history is a failure to be remedied or a condition to be embraced.

Each leaf should carry either the name of an actual position—such as provincializing Europe, connected histories, global microhistory, or the archival pluralist stance—or be marked unnamed if it represents a logically available but apparently unoccupied combination, for instance accepting singular chronology while denying that any archive is translatable, or embracing radical temporal pluralism while insisting on a unified causal narrative. For every unnamed leaf, include one sentence diagnosing why it remains unoccupied: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. The tree should contain six to eight branch nodes, should not ask respondents to identify themselves by label, and should flag any pair of named positions that the tree's questions cannot genuinely separate, particularly if two positions share all answers but differ only in rhetorical emphasis or disciplinary self-identification.

Please inject at least one genuinely niche or under-discussed distinction into the tree—something not standard in encyclopedia surveys—such as a branch on whether historical explanation requires indexical anchoring, the view that every explanatory claim implicitly presupposes a spatio-temporal index that cannot be generalized without distortion, or a split over whether narrative coherence is a cognitive virtue of the historian or a structural feature of the past itself. The taxonomy should be usable by someone without web access: do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, page references, or dependence on highly specialized monographs unknown outside a subfield. Phrase every node as a question that a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and build the tree from the logical space of answers first, locating named positions in that space only afterward.
