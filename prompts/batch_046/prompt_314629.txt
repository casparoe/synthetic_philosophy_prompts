You are a senior philosopher of language with expertise in speech-act theory, institutional ontology, and the philosophy of technology. Before drafting, please take a moment to brainstorm the logical space of agreements and disagreements, ensuring that every formulation could be signed by both parties without misrepresenting either view.

Your task is to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps that have spent years arguing past each other about performative utterances in algorithmically mediated environments.

Camp One—call them the Structural Felicity Theorists—holds that a performative succeeds if and only if the relevant felicity conditions (preparatory, essential, sincerity, and execution conditions) are satisfied within the operative institutional framework, regardless of whether the uttering agent is biological, artificial, or distributed across a network. They allow that an autonomous system or smart-contract protocol can execute a binding declaration (for example, "I hereby transfer ownership" or "We are now married in this virtual jurisdiction") provided the institutional rules recognize the syntactic-structural match and assign the appropriate deontic status.

Camp Two—call them the Embodied Accountability Theorists—holds that performative force is not reducible to structural matching; it requires an embodied agent who can be held accountable, who possesses what they call normative presence, and who is embedded in historically sedimented practices of intersubjective recognition. For them, algorithmic mediation introduces an irreducible accountability gap: even if all formal conditions are met, the utterance is at best a simulated performative, because there is no agent who can be blamed, praised, or held to the commitment in the mode that makes the institution live rather than merely function.

The collaboration requires a single constructive document both sides could put their names to. Structure it as follows:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims about speech acts, performative utterances, and our current capabilities—for instance, agreement that the distinction between locutionary and illocutionary acts remains useful; that felicity conditions are necessary but possibly not sufficient for full performative success; that institutional frameworks vary in whether they recognize algorithmically mediated declarations; and that there is a meaningful difference between a misfire (conditions unsatisfied) and an abuse (conditions satisfied but insincerely executed).

2. A table of precise disagreements, phrased in neutral wording so that each camp recognizes its own position in its row and accepts the wording of the opposing row as a fair statement. Separate genuine disagreements from terminological disputes (for example, whether "genuine performative" is being used stipulatively or descriptively).

3. The cruxes—the sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. I suspect two central cruxes: (a) whether normative presence is a constitutive condition or merely a pragmatic desideratum for performative success; and (b) whether institutional recognition alone can close the accountability gap, or whether recognition without embodied accountability is empty. For each crux, provide a concrete case or scenario that both camps agree is relevant and on which they have made opposing predictions in advance. For example, a jurisdiction that legally recognizes AI-executed marriage declarations: the Structuralists should predict successful performative binding with standard legal effects, while the Embodied Theorists should predict either nullity or a distinct, weaker normative status that lacks the full force of marital commitment.

Please think step by step: first map the logical combinations of answers to the cruxes and locate both camps within that space; then draft the agreed-claims section with formulations that are substantive but not slanted; then construct the disagreement table with exact, parallel wording; finally specify the predictive cases with each side's forecast recorded in advance. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific works. Avoid obscure terminology that requires specialist knowledge beyond standard philosophy of language and basic institutional theory. The result should be a polished, formal document suitable for publication in a philosophy journal's adversarial collaboration series.
