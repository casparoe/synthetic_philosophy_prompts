Here is an argument my study partner constructed to show that promises cannot be grounded in reliance, which I now find suspicious because the conclusion undermines a view I want to preserve. (1) A promise binds only through reliance harm: the loss a promisee suffers from arranging plans around an expectation that is then frustrated. (2) Wherever reliance harm exists, the obligation not to cause it applies, whether or not a promise was made; what generates the duty is the justified expectation and the dependent planning, not the speech act itself. (3) But such expectations and harms arise constantly outside promises—a commuter relies on the 8:03 bus, a colleague relies on you saving her a seat, a neighbor relies on you shoveling the shared walk after snow. (4) In each case, breaking the pattern causes real loss—missed connections, wasted effort, extra labor—yet none of these patterns generates a promissory obligation. (5) So if promises bind through reliance harm, then bus schedules, habitual seat-saving, and neighborly routines must generate obligations with identical moral structure and force, which is clearly wrong. (6) Therefore reliance harm is not the distinctive ground of promissory binding, and promises do not bind in that way. (7) I want to keep both that promises bind and that they do so through authorized reliance; if I must choose, I would keep (1) and (2) rather than accept (6). Where does the argument go wrong, which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me?
