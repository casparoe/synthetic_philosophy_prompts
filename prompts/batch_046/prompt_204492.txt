I have been trying to calibrate my actual position on whether autonomous lethal decision systems generate an irreducible responsibility gap—not the familiar diffusion of blame across designers, operators, and institutions, but the stronger claim that there exists a non-empty class of harmful outcomes for which no agent, individual or collective, satisfies the conditions for moral responsibility, even in principle. Give me your credence in that stronger claim, restricted to architectures plausibly deployable within the next two decades. Decompose the credence into: (a) the probability that moral responsibility requires a mental-state condition—knowledge, intent, or foresight—that is jointly absent from both the system and its human operators in these cases; (b) the probability that distributed or collective responsibility mechanisms can cover every harmful outcome without residue, given that each human contributor’s marginal causal role falls below a plausible culpability threshold; (c) the probability that closing the gap requires a genuinely novel normative category rather than an extension of command responsibility, negligence, or product liability, and whether introducing such a category should count as resolving the gap or merely relabeling it. Combine these with any other terms you think belong, state the overall number, identify which sub-question the result is most sensitive to, and say what kind of conceptual argument or empirical finding would move that term the most.
