In my philosophy of law seminar a colleague repeatedly invokes the line “if you didn’t author your own will, you can’t author punishment for others” to dismiss Dennett’s compatibilist defense of retributive institutions and to claim that Caruso’s skepticism about basic desert reduces all punishment to risk management with a moral mask. Before I respond, I want a disambiguation menu: what are the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing—metaphysical (about causal authorship of the self), normative (about the conditions for legitimate blame), institutional (about what practices can claim moral authority), and perhaps a hybrid “structural” reading that ties desert to one’s place in a social practice rather than to an inner origin? For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, false, or question-begging against Dennett’s functionalism; then indicate which reading actually does the work of collapsing punitive justification into bare social control, and whether achieving that collapse requires also rejecting Dennett’s distinction between basic and derived desert or only misreading it. Lay the readings out as a list I can bring to the meeting.
