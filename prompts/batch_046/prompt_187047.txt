You serve as the senior advisory philosopher and ethics secretary to the Joint Funding and Ethics Review Board of the National Translational Neuroscience Initiative. Before you begin drafting, please think carefully, outline the central tension in two or three sentences, and then proceed step by step.

The Board must reach a concrete decision by Friday on Proposal NT-447, Stochastic Resonance Neurofeedback for Treatment-Resistant Phantom Limb Pain. The principal investigator reports a pilot study of n=8 with marked subjective improvement, but no replication, no sham-controlled arm, and an internal statistical review commissioned by the Board finds that the primary endpoint was changed post hoc. The PI argues that delaying funding for a full Phase-II trial condemns hundreds of patients to years of suffering, and that the Board faces a living, forced, and momentous option in the Jamesian sense: either it acts on a promising but unverified belief in the intervention’s efficacy, or it withholds a potential remedy and accepts continued harm. Several board members invoke Clifford’s evidentialist position: an institutional funding body has no personal passional nature that could license belief beyond the available evidence, and to approve compassionate-access treatment on this record would violate the duty not to endorse scientific claims upon insufficient evidence.

Your task is to draft the formal decision memo—approximately 900 to 1200 words—in a polished institutional voice suitable for circulation to scientists, clinicians, and ethicists. The memo must recommend one clear course of action—approve, reject, or approve with strict conditions and an explicit timeline—and it must include reasoning the researchers could actually respond to, not merely philosophical abstraction.

In drafting, please do the following:

1. State the recommendation plainly at the outset, then justify it.
2. Examine whether James’s conditions for legitimate belief apply to an institutional body rather than to an individual believer, and whether Clifford’s prohibition applies with equal force to collective doxastic agents that do not hold beliefs in a first-person sense.
3. Address the PI’s distinction between “believing the therapy works” and “allocating risk under uncertainty,” evaluating whether that distinction saves the proposal from Clifford’s charge or collapses once the Board’s public approval is interpreted by patients and clinicians as an institutional endorsement.
4. Give the strongest version of the opposing philosophical view and explain precisely why it does not carry the day, without reducing it to a strawman.
5. If your recommendation is conditional, specify concrete safeguards—trial-design requirements, stopping rules, disclosure obligations to enrolled patients, and a hard deadline for interim data review—that would make the decision ethically defensible regardless of which philosophical position ultimately prevails.

Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to secondary literature. You may refer in passing to James and Clifford by the familiar titles of their essays, but do not quote either author verbatim; paraphrase in your own words without quotation marks. Write formally, think step by step, and ensure the memo ends with an actionable bottom line and a clear time frame for any conditions you impose.
