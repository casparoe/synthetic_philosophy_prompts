Write a dialogue between two equally capable moral philosophers—one a particularist, one a generalist—debating whether moral reasons can undergo what the particularist calls “polarity reversal” without being capturable by more complex principles. Ground the exchange in a concrete case: a museum conservator discovers that repairing a war-damaged fresco would erase the only physical record of a specific act of resistance that occurred on that wall; ordinarily, preserving cultural heritage gives a strong reason to restore, but here the damage itself carries historical and moral weight. The particularist should argue that in this precise context the reason to repair does not merely diminish but actually inverts—restoration becomes a form of erasure—because the moral relevance of the damage is not an independent consideration to be weighed against preservation, but transforms the very meaning of “preservation” in this case. The generalist should resist by showing how a context-sensitive principle—one that directs us to preserve the morally significant narrative embodied in an artifact—captures the intuition without conceding that reasons are genuinely holistic or that principles fail. Ensure real back-and-forth: the generalist must directly address the particularist’s claim that adding exception clauses leads to an infinite regress of qualifications, and the particularist must answer the generalist’s charge that without principles we cannot distinguish a genuine polarity reversal from a simple misjudgment of weights. Neither side should be a strawman; each should grant the opponent’s strongest point before pressing forward. End without a tidy resolution, but with both sides clarifying exactly where they believe the other has missed the structural point about how reasons function.
