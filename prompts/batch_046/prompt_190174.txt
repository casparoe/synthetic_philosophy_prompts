You serve as the ethics-compliance officer for a hospital research consortium. Every quarter you review roughly twenty proposals for high-risk clinical studies, and a recurring problem is determining whether a researcher’s stated justification for a study is genuinely capable of motivating them—connecting to their existing values, commitments, and professional identity—or whether it is only an external reason: valid, perhaps obligatory, but lacking any link to that particular agent’s motivational set. Drawing on the distinction between internal and external reasons, construct a reusable decision procedure you can run in about twenty minutes per proposal. The deliverable should be an ordered sequence of tests applied to the justification section of a proposal, specifying for each test what counts as a pass, what counts as a failure, and where the distinction collapses into judgment rather than procedure. The procedure should classify each proposal into one of four outcomes—internal, external-but-convertible, external-inert, or indeterminate—and must include explicit stop-points where you hand the decision to a human reviewer because the question is one of values or institutional policy rather than motivational fact. Apply the procedure to three cases: a proposal in which the researchers cite “advancing the field” but their publication and funding records suggest the real driver is career advancement; a proposal justified solely by patient welfare that aligns with documented commitments in the researchers’ prior work; and a borderline case in which the researchers invoke institutional duty, acknowledge it as binding, but explicitly disclaim it as personally motivating. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an approval recommendation that conflicts with your institutional intuition. Finish by stating precisely what the procedure cannot settle—for example, whether an external-inert reason should still be enforced, whether an agent’s motivational set ought to be revised, or whether the presence of an internal reason automatically warrants approval regardless of risk. Fix the role, the time and expertise constraints, and who must sign off on each classification.
