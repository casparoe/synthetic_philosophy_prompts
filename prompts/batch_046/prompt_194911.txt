I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of science working at the intersection of decision theory and conceptual AI alignment, and I've been trying to think through whether "low-impact" or "side-effect-free" behavior can be characterized for artificial agents without presupposing a complete specification of value. In conversations with colleagues, the discussion keeps collapsing into an unhelpful dichotomy: either one accepts that measuring impact requires comparing full world-states against a utility function, which makes low-impact design impossible for value-agnostic systems, or one retreats to informal heuristics like don't change too much, which seem to smuggle in normative assumptions about which changes count as damage. I want to avoid both extremes.

Recently I've been sketching a middle-ground framing that distinguishes preference-invariant structural changes from preference-contingent perturbations. On this view, a structural change is one that alters the causal topology or state-space accessibility of an environment in ways that remain constraining regardless of which specific preference ordering a future agent holds—destroying an irreplaceable resource, for instance, or locking in a physical configuration that eliminates option sets—whereas a preference-contingent perturbation only matters relative to particular tastes, like rearranging objects in a room. The idea is that we might define side effects solely in terms of the former category, using causal-model or graph-theoretic criteria rather than welfare comparisons, thereby allowing a system to avoid high-impact actions without knowing what anyone values. But I'm unsure whether this distinction is stable: classifying a change as invariant seems to require a prior normative judgment about which future agents and which option sets deserve protection, and defining a baseline world against which to measure deviation might itself depend on implicit value commitments about what counts as normal.

Given this framing, I want to ask not for a literature review but for a defended prioritization: what are the main open conceptual questions in defining impact measures without full value specification, and which two or three should actually absorb the limited resources of a small philosophy-and-theory research group over the next few years? In particular, should the priority be formalizing the conditions under which structural invariance can be defined non-arbitrarily; investigating whether any baseline for no change can be justified without normative premises; or examining whether intuitive judgments about harm and structural damage actually align, such that the distinction collapses into the very value-laden comparisons it was meant to avoid? For each candidate, explain why it is upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable it is for conceptual work rather than empirical machine learning, and what kind of argument or counterexample over the coming years would genuinely move it up or down the agenda. I am less interested in an exhaustive survey than in knowing where the crux actually lies.
