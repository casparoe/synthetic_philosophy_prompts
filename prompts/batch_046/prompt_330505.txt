You are acting as a senior lecturer in moral philosophy with specialist expertise in risk ethics, longtermist reasoning, and the conceptual distinction between existential risk and suffering-risk (s-risk). Before you begin your analysis, take a few moments to brainstorm: list the main families of value theory that could justify treating the prevention of catastrophic harm differently from the prevention of vast-scale suffering, and note any institutional design features that would betray a commitment to one family rather than another.

Now consider the following practice, which I ask you to treat as a concrete case study rather than an abstract puzzle. A major grant-making institution in the field of artificial-intelligence governance operates under a formal evaluation charter. The charter requires every funding proposal to be scored on two dimensions only: (i) the estimated reduction in existential risk to humanity, measured as a change in the probability of civilisational collapse within the next two centuries; and (ii) the technical feasibility of the proposed intervention within a ten-year horizon. The charter explicitly excludes any evaluation of whether the funded research might inadvertently create, scale, or fail to mitigate suffering among non-human or artificial moral patients — for instance, through the optimisation of environments that produce persistent negative welfare states, or through the proliferation of sentient sub-routines whose experiences are not monitored. The institution defends this exclusion by appealing to two claims: first, that the mandate is restricted to safeguarding human survival, and second, that there exist no validated instruments for measuring suffering-risk at the relevant scales.

Your task is to reconstruct the implicit philosophical architecture of this practice. What theory or theories of moral value, of the boundaries of moral consideration, and of the relationship between epistemic tractability and moral obligation does the charter presuppose? Is it internally consistent, or does it smuggle in contradictory commitments — such as treating human survival as possessing intrinsic value while treating the avoidance of astronomical suffering as possessing merely instrumental value, or invoking tractability selectively to excuse omission in one domain but not in another? Could the same institutional design be rationalised by rival normative frameworks — for example, by a person-affecting view, by a form of negative utilitarianism, or by a purely contractualist account of institutional purpose — or does it uniquely favour a particular position? Finally, specify what would have to change in the charter's normative architecture, not merely in its metrics, if it were genuinely redesigned to integrate s-risk assessment on equal footing with existential-risk assessment. Write your response in polished British English, maintain a formal academic register throughout, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations; if you draw on well-known arguments from the literature, paraphrase them without quotation marks.
