The debate over ontological commitment to permanently informationally inaccessible domains—sometimes framed as the quarantine problem—has stalled for years in metaphysics and philosophy of science. One side argues that if no causal, structural, or observational pathway could ever link a region to ours, then including it in our ontology erases the difference between existence and mere logical possibility; they hold that identification requires at least a potential informational bridge. The opposing side insists that ontology answers to reality rather than epistemic reach, treating inaccessibility as a contingent feature of our position rather than a metaphysical filter. Both camps share the same formal account of informational closure, agree on the physical descriptions involved, and neither denies the underlying mathematics; yet the positions remain immovable. Is this disagreement substantive—perhaps about whether existence conditions carry an epistemic clause—or is it verbal, with one side asking what we are rationally entitled to posit and the other asking what there is, both under the heading "ontology"? Or is the stall caused by bedrock intuitions about the purpose of metaphysics that neither side can defend? Diagnose the structure of the impasse, supported by what each camp actually says, and say what would count as progress: a formal result on identification conditions, a conceptual distinction that separates the questions, or an argument that no resolution should be expected and why.
