I am trying to articulate a structural problem I keep encountering in discussions of rational self-trust and automated reasoning, and I need to know whether philosophy or logic has already carved it out precisely. Consider an agent—human or artificial—whose rule for revising any belief B is: accept B only if I can demonstrate, from my current beliefs, that my method for forming B is reliable. At first this looks like responsible epistemic hygiene. But apply it to the belief that my method for forming beliefs is reliable. To justify that belief, the agent must deploy the very method whose reliability is in question. The deadlock is not mere logical circularity, because the agent is not assuming B in order to derive B; rather, the agent's standard of admissible evidence is calibrated so that no evidence can ever satisfy it from the inside. I have seen analogues discussed under the heading of reflection principles in modal logic and in debates about whether an AI can safely approve its own reasoning, but I am unsure whether these are one phenomenon or several related ones. Does this specific configuration—where a self-certification criterion makes self-certification structurally unavailable—have an established name? How is it different from ordinary vicious circularity, from Gödel's second incompleteness theorem applied to formal systems, and from simple skepticism about one's faculties? I am not looking for a bare label; I want the boundaries of the concept mapped: which formal results belong inside it, which practical cases are near-misses, and whether the logical version and the practical-epistemic version share an essential structure or only a family resemblance. Please also state what evidence or argument would change your assessment—whether a particular historical treatment, a formal distinction, or a counterexample—would persuade you that this is either a well-known problem misdescribed or a genuinely distinct one, and point out where your reasoning is weakest.
