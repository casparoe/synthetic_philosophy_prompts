I’m a PhD student in philosophy of economics, and I was chatting with my supervisor over lunch yesterday whilst trying to get my head round Keynes’s notion of the weight of argument in his Treatise on Probablity. From what I gather, weight measures the absolute amount of relevant evidence behind a belief rather than the balance of reasons for and against it, and a belief with high weight should be more stable — less easily overturned by a small bit of counter-evidence. Meanwhile, in a seminar on formal epistemology last term, a colleague floated this concept they called epistemic drag: the structural resistance a theoretical framework shows when you try to update it with anomalous data, particularly when there are lots of auxiliary assumptions that can soak up the shock. I’m wondering whether Keynesian weight and epistemic drag are essentially the same phenomenon in different clothing, or whether they split in ways that matter.

Specifically, Keynes seems to suggest that as weight goes up, our rational confidence ought to go up even if the probability itself doesn’t change, which sounds like a normative claim about belief stability. Epistemic drag, by contrast, feels more descriptive — it’s about how hard the system is to budge, regardless of whether anyone ought to feel more confident. So are they two sides of one coin, or does weight carry a normative load that drag simply lacks? And which concept better captures the case of a well-evidenced theory that collapses suddenly once a key auxiliary assumption is undermined — is that high weight with low drag, or does Keynes’s framework not allow for that kind of fragility?

I’d really value a comparison that teases them apart carefully, especially on whether weight is a property of the evidence base itself or of the agent’s psychological state. I also wonder if Keynes discusses situations where weight is high but the belief is actually brittle because all the evidence comes from one source or method — I feel like there might be a link to his later thoughts on uncertanty and animal spirits in the General Theory, but I’m wary of conflating the two works if that’s unfair. Any thoughts?
