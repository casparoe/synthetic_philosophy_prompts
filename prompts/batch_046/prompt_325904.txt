I'm drafting a seminar paper on Rousseau's letter to d'Alembert opposing a theater in Geneva, and I'm trying to push past the usual reading that he simply condemns theater for arousing passions without satisfying them or for substituting appearance for reality. My working thesis is that Rousseau's critique is more specifically an argument about the corruption of moral attention: he holds that civic virtue depends on a direct, unmediated practical recognition of one's fellow citizens, and that theatrical representation structurally replaces this recognition with a generalized, aestheticized form of sympathy. To sharpen this, I'm drawing a parallel—perhaps anachronistic, but conceptually illuminating—with the contemporary moral-psychological distinction between empathic concern and personal distress. On my reading, Rousseau anticipates this distinction but assigns it an inverted normative valence: the spectator's emotional distance, which modern theory often treats as a condition for mature empathic concern, is for Rousseau precisely the mechanism by which moral knowledge is disabled, since it permits emotional consumption without practical commitment or risk.

I'm particularly interested in whether this framing makes sense of Rousseau's specific objections to comedy and tragedy—namely, that comedy makes us laugh at vice and tragedy makes us weep for fictional suffering—without reducing his position to a simple moralism about content. Does the concept of mediated sympathy help explain why Rousseau thinks Geneva's particular civic structure makes it especially vulnerable to theatrical corruption? And where might this interpretation overreach: does it impose a cognitive-scientific vocabulary on a text that is really about political economy and gender? I'd welcome your critical comments on the coherence of this conceptual reconstruction, on whether the parallel with modern empathy theory genuinely illuminates Rousseau's argument or merely distorts it, and on any internal tensions you see in treating theatrical sympathy as a form of epistemic disablement rather than mere moral weakness. Please don't provide a bibliography or suggest specific secondary sources; I want to test the conceptual architecture on its own terms.
