I work as a software engineer and have no formal philosophical training, though I have been thinking about consciousness and physicalism in my spare time, particularly the conceivability argument involving philosophical zombies—creatures physically identical to conscious beings yet lacking any inner experience. I have developed intuitive verdicts about several engineered and hypothetical scenarios, but I cannot tell whether they hang together under a single principle, and I would welcome a precise, reflective analysis written in British English.

Here are my judgements. First, I find it genuinely conceivable that a perfect digital simulation of my brain, implemented on silicon and reproducing every behavioural output, might nevertheless lack subjective experience altogether; this suggests to me that consciousness is not metaphysically entailed by physical structure alone. Second, I think a being with inverted colour qualia—experiencing red where I experience green, yet behaving identically—is also conceivable, though I am unsure whether this undermines physicalism or merely reveals an epistemic gap. Third, when I imagine a hydraulic system arranged to mirror neural connections with perfect fidelity, I feel less convinced that a zombie version is conceivable; the substrate seems too remote from biological tissue for the absence of consciousness to be a live modal possibility. Fourth, I can imagine an entire universe physically identical to ours but entirely devoid of consciousness, which reinforces my sense that phenomenal properties are not necessary. Fifth, I have been considering a more specific, engineered case: a neural network that has been compressed so that ninety-nine per cent of its connections are pruned, yet still produces every behavioural response I would produce; I think it is conceivable that consciousness is present in the dense original but absent in the sparse replica, despite complete functional equivalence. Is there one principle—perhaps concerning substrate specificity, organisational invariance, or the modal relation between structure and experience—that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and what does the best principle predict for a case I have not listed: a brain gradually replaced, neuron by neuron, with functionally identical synthetic components until no biological tissue remains?

Please state the principle precisely, indicate which of my intuitions it accommodates and which it forces me to reject, apply it to the gradual-replacement scenario, and suggest whether my verdict on the compressed network ought to be revised.
