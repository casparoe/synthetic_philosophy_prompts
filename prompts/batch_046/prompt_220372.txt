I've been running a workshop series for an audience that reads rationalist and effective-altruism-adjacent blogs but has no formal training in political philosophy — about eighteen people, ninety minutes, mostly technical graduate students. We're examining subsidiarity not in its usual democratic or cultural register, but in a more austere formulation that rarely gets articulated in standard US-versus-EU comparisons: the idea that decentralization can be justified as an epistemic architecture rather than a normative preference. On this view, a central authority fails to allocate correctly not through corruption, incompetence, or raw computational limits, but because the relevant local conditions are structurally non-fungible — they exist in a form that cannot be transmitted upward without being destroyed or distorted, which means delegation is not merely efficient but necessary for any decision-relevant knowledge to survive.

For this session, I want a runnable in-class simulation that makes this structural knowledge problem felt before it is named. Please design a structured game for eighteen participants with about seventy-five minutes of play plus debrief. Split the group into one Federal Council of four people holding a global budget and a mandate for uniform rules, and five Cantonal Nodes of roughly three people each, with every node holding private, heterogeneous information about local conditions that the Council needs in order to make correct allocations. The rules must be arranged so that the Council genuinely tries to optimize for global equity and the Nodes genuinely want to cooperate, yet the information architecture makes correct central allocation impossible: the Nodes must either distort their reports upward (destroying fidelity) or withhold them (breaking coordination). Use a point-based payoff scheme — Council points for equity, Node points for local fit, plus a shared surplus bonus that requires both sides to coordinate — so that the tension is lived rather than narrated. Include the exact communication rules, the timeline, materials needed, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the simulation onto the epistemic argument. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first run — likely the Council inventing proxy metrics that systematically miss the private information, or the Nodes over-strategizing the reporting rules — and how to recover without undermining the point. Finally, include an honest note on which real feature of multilevel governance, whether Swiss fiscal federalism or EU subsidiarity enforcement, the activity necessarily flattens, and what the facilitator must supply to restore it.
