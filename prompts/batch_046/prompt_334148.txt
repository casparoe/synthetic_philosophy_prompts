I'm putting together a 3-year interdisciplanary working group — historians, philosophers, one archaeologist — and we're trying to decide what actually matters in the legend-to-history boundary problem. We're using the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus as our anchor cases, but I'm less interested in what happened and more in the conceptual machinery we use to decide that something has 'crossed over.'

So here's what I'm stuck on: there seem to be at least four open lines of work that people talk past each other on. First, the epistemic asymmetry problem — it's easier to show a story is mythologized than to prove there's a real kernel underneath, but our standards for 'kernel' keep shifting with new methods (like isotopic analysis or textual stratigraphy). Second, historiographic inertia: once a narrative gets institutionalized in museums, textbooks, or national identity, it develops a kind of functional resistance to debunking, and I'm not sure whether fighting that inertia is an epistemic duty or just politically naive. Third, negative archaeology and the burden of absence — when excavation finds nothing where a legend predicts a capital or a battle, does that absence disconfirm the legend, or just relocate it? And fourth, mythic scaffolding: the idea that a real event might survive only as a distorted narrative shell, so the 'history' is structurally unrecoverable except through the legend itself.

If you had to rank these four for actual effort — not just 'interesting' but which ones are upstream of the others, which ones change how we'd treat the three anchor cases differently, and which are probably dead ends for a small group — how would you order them? I'm especially unsure whether the inertia question is overrated; it feels like sociology of knowledge rather than philosophy of history, but maybe that's exactly where the action is. Defend the ranking with actual criteria — tractability for philosophers vs. empirical people, what a success would look like in three years, and which verdict you'd be most willing to overturn if the group made progress on another line first. Also, is there a fifth line I'm missing that makes the other four look like distractions?
