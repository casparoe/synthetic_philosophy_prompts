You sit on your university's Committee on Honors and Institutional Recognition. The committee must decide whether to award an honorary degree to a private benefactor who fully funded the rebuilding of a pediatric ward destroyed by fire. The project restored critical capacity and expanded access, but recently disclosed internal correspondence shows the benefactor designed the gift primarily to maximize tax shelter and to weaken a competing medical center; he expressed explicit indifference to patient outcomes and satisfaction that the rival institution lost funding. One faction argues that honorary degrees express institutional endorsement of a person's moral standing, and since moral worth requires acting from the right reason rather than merely producing good effects, conferring the honor would misrepresent the university's values. The opposing faction argues that honors are consequential instruments meant to shape future behavior, that private motives are epistemically opaque and institutionally irrelevant, and that withholding recognition punishes socially valuable outcomes for mental states the committee cannot reliably assess. Draft a formal decision memo for the committee—recommend awarding, withholding, or awarding with explicit conditions—that addresses the expressive significance of institutional recognition, treats the debate about whether right action without right reason merits moral esteem, and gives a fair account of the strongest consideration supporting the recommendation you do not adopt.
