I am a third-year undergraduate reading Philosophy and Economics, and as part of our Rational Choice Theory seminar I have been assigned the task of grading a peer's response to the following coursework question. I should like an objective assessment.

Question:
Consider the coherence arguments for rational preference and belief. (a) Explain the Dutch Book argument for probabilism, and show how a parallel coherence constraint can be applied to preference over acts so as to support expected utility maximisation. (b) Savage's representation theorem derives expected utility from axioms on preferences over acts. Identify the axiom that is most exposed to criticism in settings involving either ambiguity or state-dependent outcomes (such as medical treatment choices), and explain why the failure of this axiom in those settings does not, by itself, defeat the coherence argument you outlined in part (a).

Student's answer:
(a) The Dutch Book argument, as formalised by de Finetti for subjective probabilities and subsequently extended by him to cover preferences over acts, shows that if an agent's betting quotients violate the axioms of probability—say, if they assign probabilities to exhaustive and exclusive outcomes that do not sum to one—then a bookmaker can construct a combination of bets that guarantees the agent loses money regardless of the outcome. To avoid being exploited, the agent must hold probabilistically coherent beliefs. The same idea extends to preferences: if an agent's choices over acts violate expected utility maximisation, then there exists a set of trades (a money pump) that will leave her with less of every good than she started with. Hence coherence in belief and coherence in preference both point toward obeying the probability calculus and maximising expected utility.

(b) The vulnerable axiom is Savage's Sure-Thing Principle (P2). P2 requires that if two acts agree on a particular state of the world, then preference between them should not depend on what happens in that state. In Ellsberg-style ambiguity, people often prefer known risks over unknown ones, which violates P2 because the preference between two acts changes when the ambiguous state is replaced by a state with a known outcome, even though the acts still agree elsewhere. Similarly, in medical decision-making, the utility of an outcome such as survival depends on whether the patient was previously healthy or ill—so outcomes are state-dependent, and P2 fails because the evaluation of an act cannot be separated from the state in which it occurs.

Because P2 fails in these contexts, Savage's representation theorem cannot be applied directly. However, this does not undermine the coherence argument from part (a). The Dutch Book and money-pump arguments depend only on probabilistic coherence and transitive preference, not on the independence conditions embedded in P2. Therefore, even if Savage's axiomatic derivation collapses, the agent can still be coherence-bound to maximise expected utility with respect to her own subjective probabilities and state-dependent utility function.
