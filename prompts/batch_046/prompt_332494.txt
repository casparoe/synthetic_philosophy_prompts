I am writing a novel in which two characters, separated by distance and with no possibility of communication, must each choose whether to reveal a secret they share. They were raised with identical ethical training and share the same reasoning habits, yet one has recently undergone a partial alteration of memory that she suspects may have shifted her priorities, though she cannot determine whether the other has been similarly affected. Despite this uncertainty, each attempts to model the other's decision not as an independent causal agent but as a logical extension of herself. The result is a curious intermediate condition: they lack the perfect symmetry required for superrational cooperation, yet they refuse to revert to conventional strategic reasoning. I wish to know whether decision theory or philosophy possesses an established term for this suspended state—where agents strive for superrational coordination whilst acknowledging a probable divergence in their reasoning architectures. I am not seeking a bare label; rather, I want to understand precisely what the phenomenon comprises, where its boundaries lie, and how it differs both from pure superrationality and from standard game-theoretic expectation. If no established concept fits, I should like to know how one might define its conditions, its failure modes, and the edge cases that separate it from neighbouring ideas.
