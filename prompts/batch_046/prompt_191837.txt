Construct a thought experiment in the ethics of belief that isolates the disagreement between James and Clifford, but deliberately engineered so that the standard religious or existential framing is absent and a non-standard institutional feature carries the theoretical load.

Your scenario must satisfy the following specifications:

(i) The belief at issue must concern a practical, momentous institutional decision—for example, whether a municipal public-health board ought to treat an ambiguous, non-replicated epidemiological signal as warranting immediate precautionary measures—such that the option is genuinely live and forced, yet the direct evidential support is recognisably insufficient by Cliffordian lights.

(ii) The belief must be adopted not by a solitary individual in private deliberation, but collectively as a working presumption by a deliberative body; the influence that James permits the passional nature to exert should therefore appear as a shared institutional risk-culture or professional ethos rather than as personal hope or desire.

(iii) The case must be realistic, describable in a single paragraph, with no appeal to demons, perfect predictors, exotic physics, or science-fictional devices.

(iv) Design the case so that James permits—indeed, may require—the collective adoption of the belief, while Clifford strictly prohibits any such adoption on the grounds of evidential insufficiency.

(v) After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it meets each specification: state the precise belief, the method of its formation, the nearby worlds or counterfactual conditions relevant to assessing evidential sufficiency, and show why James's conditions of being live, forced, and momentous are satisfied while Clifford's evidential demand is not.

(vi) Identify explicitly which structural feature—particularly the collective, institutional framing—does the work in separating the two verdicts, and explain whether removing that feature causes the case to collapse back into a standard individual-belief scenario.

(vii) Provide an honest counter-move: how a Cliffordian defender might attack the case, specifically by arguing that the option is not genuinely forced once the institutional context is properly analysed, or by denying that a distributed institutional ethos can substitute for individual evidential responsibility.

Constraints: Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke specialised theories or obscure facts that depend on literature outside standard philosophical training. Write in formal British English.
