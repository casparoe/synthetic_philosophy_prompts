I'm trying to map my own intuitions about metaphysical grounding and fundamentality onto a single principle, and I need help seeing whether they cohere. Here are my verdicts. The fact that a statue weighs ten kilograms is grounded in the clay's mass distribution, but the fact that it depicts a ruler is not grounded in those same physical properties; it seems grounded jointly in the sculptor's intentions and in social conventions of representation. If mental states are grounded in neural states, and neural states in molecular ones, then mental states are ultimately grounded in molecular ones, so I take grounding to be transitive. Yet I also think negative facts—such as there being no life on Mars—lack any positive grounds and are therefore fundamental in that respect. I accept plural grounding: that A and B together can ground C even when neither does so alone, but I resist counting C as grounded twice over. I also keep returning to a monetary case: a particular shell is currency partly because of its physical durability and partly because of collective acceptance; if acceptance vanished overnight, I'd say it ceased being money, so the social component seems to do the actual grounding, while the physical properties merely enable it. Can these judgments be captured by one precise principle of grounding? If they can't, which verdict should be revised? Apply your principle to a case I haven't listed—whether the moral wrongness of an action is grounded in its natural properties, in social rules, or in both together—and tell me how confident you are in the overall account, identifying specifically the claim you are least sure about.
