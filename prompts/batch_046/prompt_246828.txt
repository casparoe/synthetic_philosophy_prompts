I'm working on a feature about decision-theory weirdness and I've been testing my gut against different flavors of the St. Petersburg paradox. Here are my verdicts — some feel rock-solid, others I'm wobbly on.

1. The classic setup: fair coin flipped til tails, payout doubles each round, expectation is technically infinite. I'd pay maybe $25 to play, definately not $500, even though the math says I should be willing to sell my house.

2. Same math, but there's a hard cap — if the payout hits $1 million the casino stops and pays that. The expectation is now around $20. I'd still pay about $25. So the cap barely moves my number, which feels strange.

3. Flip it: I have to pay the entry fee up front, and if I "lose" (which is near-certain in the first few rounds) I still owe the fee plus interest. I wouldn't play for $5.

4. A variant I read about in a niche forum: the coin is replaced by a deterministic pseudorandom generator with a hidden seed. You don't know the sequence in advance. I'd pay exactly the same as in case 1 — the hidden determinism doesn't change my gut at all.

5. The "subjective time" version: each successive flip takes exponentially longer to resolve — minutes, then hours, then years. A human player with a normal lifespan only ever experiences the first four or five rounds, so the infinite tail is literally unreachable in lived time. Here I think I'd actually pay more, maybe $40, because the game feels "fairer" to me even though the payout structure is identical.

6. The branching-worlds twist: every flip spawns a seperate copy of me, and every copy recieves its payout. So in the multiverse every reward is realized by some version. I'd pay less — maybe $15 — because my personal risk feels diluted across copies, even though some "me" hits the jackpot.

Is there one principle behind all six? I keep going back and forth on case 5 versus case 1 — same probabilities and payouts, just different temporal framing, yet my gut moves in opposite directions. If I have to drop one verdict to save a simple principle, which should go?

Also, what does your best principle predict for a case I haven't listed: the game is offered by an AI that can compute the exact sequence in advance and will only make the offer to me if I'm in the "losing" branch?
