I am organizing a ninety-minute workshop for ten adults who have some familiarity with philosophy of mind but are not specialists, and I want them to experience—before anyone names the concept—the fragility of the intuition that the self is confined to one brain, specifically along the lines Douglas Hofstadter explores when he argues that patterns of memory, care, and emotional investment can make another person into a kind of living extension of one's own identity, so that loss feels like literal self-amputation rather than mere bereavement. To avoid sentimentality and force the idea to emerge from rules rather than rhetoric, please design a runnable structured activity that uses a framing device I will call the intercranial ledger: pair participants, assign each pair a single fictional first-person biography they must jointly author and maintain in real time, but constrain each partner so that one holds exclusive access to all visual and spatial episodes while the other holds exclusive access to all emotional and linguistic ones, and require unanimous agreement on every ledger entry before the pair can take any joint action in a simple decision game with small symbolic stakes, arranging the payoffs so that cooperation yields far more reward than parallel solo play but only if the ledger remains coherent. The rules should be set up so that participants gradually begin using first-person plural language for the biography, defend the ledger against outside correction as if it were their own past, and feel a specific disorientation—not just sadness—when, at a predetermined midpoint, you silently remove one partner and instruct the remaining one to continue making decisions alone with only half the modalities, leaving gaps that cannot be filled. After describing the rules, the staged removal, and the predicted emotional arc, please provide a debrief in continuous prose—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if writing a letter—that begins with a bottom-line summary of the mechanism's purpose, then maps each phase of the game onto Hofstadter's claim about distributed persistence of the self, predicts the two or three specific rationalizations participants will use to deny that they felt genuine distributed identity, explains exactly how to surface those resistances without lecturing, and frankly states which metaphysical element of Hofstadter's view—perhaps the idea that consciousness patterns literally survive in another substrate—this exercise necessarily fails to reproduce and therefore must be supplied in discussion. Present the entire response in continuous prose only, beginning with that bottom-line summary.
