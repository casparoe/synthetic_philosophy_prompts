As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun reading moral philosophy with any seriousness, I am trying to calibrate my intuition about the conditions that defeat a person's standing to blame another. In my own field we distinguish between local instrument error and systematic bias in the measurement apparatus; analogously, I want to distinguish between reflexive hypocrisy, in which the blamer personally performs the very wrong they condemn, and structural hypocrisy, in which the blamer is embedded in an institutional or social arrangement that systematically produces that wrong, even though their individual conduct complies with the norms they invoke.

Please rank the following four conditions for standing to blame in order of how severely they are undermined when structural hypocrisy is present rather than reflexive hypocrisy. Assign a score from 1, least undermined, to 4, most undermined. By undermined I mean that the condition becomes either impossible to satisfy or normatively irrelevant for determining whether blame is appropriate.

The conditions are:
A. The non-hypocrisy condition: one lacks standing if one is guilty of the same moral fault.
B. The non-complicity condition: one lacks standing if one is causally implicated in bringing about the wrong.
C. The moral-authority condition: one lacks standing if one does not occupy a legitimate normative position from which to hold the agent accountable.
D. The epistemic-privilege condition: one lacks standing if one lacks adequate epistemic access to the agent's reasons or circumstances.

For each ranking, provide a concise justification that references the distinction between reflexive and structural hypocrisy. Then state explicitly what argument or piece of evidence would change your verdict—particularly whether a convincing demonstration that structural complicity always collapses into personal guilt, or that personal guilt is always reducible to structural position, would alter your ordering. Finally, indicate the point in your reasoning that is most vulnerable to failure, for example if the distinction between reflexive and structural hypocrisy itself proves unstable under philosophical analysis.
