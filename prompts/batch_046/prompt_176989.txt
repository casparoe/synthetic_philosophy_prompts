Here's an argument my study parter cooked up using Nāgārjuna's four-corner negation (catuskoti) but aimed at the hard problem of consciousness, and now it seems to swallow the whole world. (1) Either consciousness is identical to brain processes, or it's a distinct causal power, or it's an emergent property with downward causation, or it doesn't exist. (2) The first can't explain subjective character, the what-it-is-like aspect. (3) The second violates causal closure of the physical. (4) The third just renames the interaction problem. (5) The fourth is phenomologically absurd — I seem conscious right now. (6) So all four corners fail. (7) By Madhyamaka reasoning, if every position in the tetralemma collapses, the subject lacks svabhāva; consciousness is śūnya. (8) But run the same four-corner analysis on a table: atoms arranged table-wise, atoms plus table-essence, emergent table-property, or non-existence. Same failures. (9) So tables, numbers, even emptiness itself are śūnya. (10) But then (7) is empty too and can't support anything. The argument proves consciousness is unreal and that the proof is unreal. Where's the leak? Is it treating the hard problem as an exhaustive logical disjunction, extending śūnyatā to conventional objects, or assuming failure of all four corners licenses a metaphysical conclusion rather than just a therapeutic one? I'd like to keep the Madhyamaka move, but tell me what I have to pay to block the self-undermining part.
