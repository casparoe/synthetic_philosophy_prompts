I'm preparing notes for a seminar on Molyneux's problem and I'm trying to determine whether modern clinical cases actually adjudicate the classic debate or merely shift its terms. The problem, as Locke and Leibniz framed it, asks whether a person born blind who learns to distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch could immediately identify them by sight alone upon gaining vision, without prior learning to associate the two sensory streams. Locke answered no, denying any innate geometric idea that bridges modalities; Leibniz answered yes in principle, arguing that the blind man possesses an innate idea of extension that vision can activate once the sensory channel opens; and later commentators have treated rapid post-operative learning as evidence either for dormant innate structure or for remarkably fast associative construction.

Consider the real-world case of Project Prakash, the study of children in rural India born with dense congenital cataracts who received surgical removal of the lenses in late childhood or adolescence and were then tested on tactile-to-visual shape matching. The salient facts are these: immediately after sight was restored, the children could navigate and detect motion, but they could not reliably match a cube or sphere they had known only by touch to its visual presentation; within days to a few weeks of visual experience, however, they achieved accurate cross-modal identification. I want you to analyze this episode through the lens of the Molyneux debate, but I want the analysis to turn on a distinction that is usually collapsed: the difference between cross-modal identification (recognizing that this particular object is the same one encountered in the other modality) and cross-modal calibration (aligning the coordinate frames, size scaling, and feature-extraction routines of touch and vision so that shape representations can be compared at all). Map the Project Prakash results onto the Lockean and Leibnizian positions with this distinction in view: does the initial failure indicate a missing innate bridge, as Locke would predict, or does it indicate only an uncalibrated visual system that cannot yet deliver shape information in the right format for comparison, which even a rationalist might allow? Does the rapid learning support the empiricist story of fast association, or does it instead suggest an innate spatial architecture being activated once sensory input becomes available? And critically, does the episode expose a hidden premise in Molyneux's original formulation—namely, that recognition should be instantaneous if innate—such that any delay, however brief, is taken to defeat rationalism?

Give me an explicit correspondence table linking the clinical stages (pre-operative tactile knowledge, immediate post-operative vision, rapid cross-modal success) to the philosophical positions and to the identification-versus-calibration distinction. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended philosophical use from those that do not, say whether either Locke's or Leibniz's view survives the case intact, and tell me whether Project Prakash actually resolves Molyneux's problem or reveals that the problem was never about instantaneous recognition but about the underlying architecture of spatial representation. If the analogy between congenital blindness restored late and Molyneux's hypothetical never-seen subject breaks in a way that undermines the comparison, mark that precisely and say what a better test case would need to look like.
