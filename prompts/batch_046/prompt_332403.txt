As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I need help clarifying a disagreement that feels deeper than a simple misunderstanding. In my team, we use a structured architecture decision record format that explicitly separates "literal assertions"—statements that must be taken exactly as written—from what we call the "contextual envelope," comprising prior team conventions, section headings, and shared background assumptions that determine whether an assertion is complete or misleading. Recently, I authored a record concerning a distributed state machine. In the section titled "Standard Operational Guarantees"—which, by team convention, is reserved for properties that require no exceptional manual intervention—I included the literal assertion: "The system maintains consistency via a two-phase commit protocol." A colleague subsequently built a client that treated split-brain partitions as requiring explicit manual reconciliation, and he now claims that my statement was misleading because it did not literally specify automatic recovery from network partitions. I responded that placing a two-phase commit claim within the "Standard Operational Guarantees" section, given our established contextual envelope, necessarily communicated that split-brain scenarios were covered automatically, not merely permitted. He rejects this, arguing that headings and conventions cannot add content that is absent from the literal text, and that if I meant to include automatic partition recovery, I was obligated to state it explicitly in the literal-assertion field. Our conflict now turns on whether framing, placement, and shared conventions can generate legitimate communicative obligations that extend beyond explicit wording, and whether I bear responsibility for his interpretation. Specifically, I want to understand: under what conditions can context fill gaps in a statement without rendering it deceptive; does the manner of documentation—its section, its relation to prior agreements—carry independent communicative weight; and is there a principled distinction between a reasonable inference drawn by a knowledgeable reader and an unjustified projection onto the text? I am not looking for team-management advice, but for an analysis of the conceptual structure underlying this kind of communicative failure.
