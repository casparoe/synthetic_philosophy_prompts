Write the opening address for an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Legitimacy and Coercion," co-hosted by a law school and a philosophy department. The occasion is the first morning session; you are speaking to an audience split evenly between practicing lawyers and legal scholars on one side, and philosophers of law and political philosophers on the other. The remarks should run about six minutes—approximately 800 words—and should be polished, formal, and appropriate for an academic setting that also includes practitioners who do not work in conceptual analysis.

The philosophical idea must do structural work, not decorative work. Build the speech around Blaise Pascal’s distinction between justice and force: justice without force is powerless, and force without justice is tyrannical, yet the two must be joined and never collapsed into one another. This tension should determine what you can and cannot say. You must welcome both halves of the room without reducing legal legitimacy to pure moral correctness—which would ignore the daily reality of enforcement for the lawyers—or reducing it to effective coercion—which would betray the normative concerns of the philosophers. Hold the tension open and productive throughout; do not resolve it into a comfortable synthesis.

To keep the speech from being a generic rehearsal of a well-known fragment, inject and develop a specific conceptual twist derived from Pascal’s broader reflections on custom, habit, and imagination: the notion of a "tacit covenant of appearance," by which a legal order sustains legitimacy not chiefly through explicit consent or transparent justification, but through a pre-reflective, habitual recognition of institutional form. This idea should shape the architecture of the address. Use it in the opening to acknowledge why the two disciplines present need each other—because neither explicit normative argument (the philosophers’ strength) nor institutional mechanics (the lawyers’ strength) can alone account for this silent, habitual layer. In the middle, let it illuminate the Pascalian problem: the tacit covenant explains why people obey laws they have never explicitly endorsed, but it also explains why force can wear the mask of justice so convincingly. In the closing, invite the workshop’s work by suggesting that examining this tacit layer requires both conceptual precision and institutional literacy.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific editions or fragment numbers. Do not quote Pascal verbatim; paraphrase his position in your own words. The speech should be structured so that removing the Pascalian framework and the concept of the tacit covenant of appearance would cause the address to collapse—this is not ornamentation but the skeleton of the talk. Maintain a tone of respectful intellectual seriousness, address both professional cultures directly, and end with an invitation to the workshop rather than a conclusion.
