I'm preparing material for a graduate seminar in political philosophy, and a colleague recently posed a puzzle about the architecture of wealth transmission that strikes me as conceptually deeper than a simple disagreement over tax rates. In most developed economies, the state imposes progressive inheritance or estate taxes—sometimes at quite high marginal rates—yet the very same legal systems preserve instruments such as family limited partnerships, perpetual dynasty trusts permitted in particular jurisdictions, and stepped-up basis rules that erase unrealized capital gains for heirs. The result is an institutional design that simultaneously claims to limit dynastic accumulation and actively facilitates its preservation across generations. I want to understand what implicit theory of distributive justice, or of moral responsibility for one's social position, this pairing actually presupposes. Does it commit us to a view that equality of opportunity is satisfied whenever every individual begins life with roughly equivalent formal resources, regardless of whether those resources arrived through inheritance? Or does it instead presuppose that intergenerational transfers belong to a private moral domain lying outside the scope of public fairness altogether?

Reconstruct the philosophical commitments embedded in this practice, carefully distinguishing what the institutional pairing strictly requires from what merely happens to cohere with it comfortably. Consider, in particular, whether the framework is compatible with a position—familiar from debates about future generations—that altering economic arrangements changes who comes to exist, thereby undermining the claim that inheritance harms specific identifiable people. And indicate what a legal regime genuinely committed to life-chance equality, rather than to formal equality at the point of entry into economic life, would be forced to alter about these instruments of preservation.
