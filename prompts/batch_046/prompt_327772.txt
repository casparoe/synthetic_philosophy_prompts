I am a doctoral student in democratic theory reconstructing a boundary framework—one that determines membership in the demos through case verdicts rather than through a single axiomatic principle. The framework has delivered the following rulings.

Case 1. Permanent residents who are fully subject to local taxation and criminal jurisdiction, but who lack citizenship and do not intend naturalization: included in the demos.

Case 2. Citizens who have resided abroad for more than twenty years, maintain no domestic property or family ties, and bear no tax obligations: excluded.

Case 3. Minors aged sixteen to seventeen who score in the top decile on standardized assessments of policy reasoning and civic knowledge, but who lack full contractual capacity: included with advisory (non-binding) deliberative weight.

Case 4. Corporations incorporated within the jurisdiction, with localized stakeholder boards materially affected by zoning and environmental regulation: excluded.

Case 5. Future generations who will bear irreversible ecological costs from present carbon and land-use policy, but who do not yet exist and cannot participate: included, represented through proxy advocates.

Case 6. Non-human great apes with demonstrated territorial attachment, reciprocal social behavior, and responsiveness to local resource distribution: excluded.

These six verdicts are jointly compatible with more than one governing principle—whether jurisdictional subjection, mutual reciprocity, deliberative autonomy, affected interest, or some hybrid.

Now consider Case 7. An adult citizen—physically present, tax-paying, contractually competent, emotionally invested in policy outcomes—has permanently integrated an external, non-conscious AI deliberative module (a cognitive prosthesis). The module processes all political information, generates every voting preference, and articulates the citizen's positions in public forums. The human reports an inability to form independent political judgments without the module's mediation, though she retains formal legal identity, affective attachment to outcomes, and veto power over the module's outputs.

Predict the framework's verdict on Case 7—whether she belongs to the demos, and with what deliberative status. Identify the implicit principle or combination of principles that best explains the verdict, noting which of Cases 1–6 serves as the closest analog. State whether the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome; if they do, specify the principle the framework would be adopting with an included ruling versus an excluded ruling. Finally, propose one further boundary case—realistic, not science-fictional—that would distinguish between the two leading interpretations of the framework's logic.

Please indicate how confident you are in each component of your analysis—especially in your identification of the implicit rule—and explicitly single out the point about which you are least certain, explaining why.
