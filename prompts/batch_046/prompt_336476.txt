Write a workshop proposal (approximately 1,200 words, suitable for a philosophy or museum-studies conference pitch) on the moral grounds for restitution of artifacts removed during colonial rule, taking the Benin Bronzes and the Parthenon Marbles as central illustrative cases without restricting the analysis to them. The project should turn on a conceptual distinction that public debate rarely makes explicit: provenance-based claims, which ground restitution in the illegality or coercive violence of the original removal, and continuity-based claims, which ground it in the present-day cultural sovereignty of the source community, irrespective of how the object was acquired. Formulate a precise, answerable research question: can a single normative principle accommodate both grounds, or do they yield divergent verdicts in cases where acquisition was legally ambiguous but present cultural practice is robust (the Parthenon Marbles) versus where acquisition was clearly coercive but present continuity is disputed or mediated by diaspora communities (the Benin Bronzes)? Explain why existing philosophical answers—whether framed in terms of corrective justice for historical wrongs or in terms of present cultural rights—leave this particular tension unresolved, especially when the two dimensions vary independently. Set out the proposed approach: a conceptual analysis that specifies, for each ground, the conditions under which restitution is obligatory, permissible, or prohibited, tested against a structured matrix of hypothetical and real cases that separately vary the mode of acquisition and the strength and form of present cultural practice. State what a successful outcome would look like—either a unified principle with clearly weighted clauses, or a defensible pluralism that assigns explicit priority rules—and what would constitute failure, such as the two grounds collapsing into one another upon analysis or generating contradictions resolvable only by arbitrary stipulation about cultural authenticity. Give an honest assessment of scope and risk: the project is appropriate for a workshop pitch because it is primarily conceptual, but it could become unmanageable if it attempts to settle empirical controversies about cultural continuity or to adjudicate the detailed legal history of specific acquisitions; the proposal should therefore limit itself to deriving normative conditions and indicating precisely where further empirical or legal inquiry would be required, without attempting to conduct that inquiry within the pitch itself.
