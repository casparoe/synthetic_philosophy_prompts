I'm putting together a little philosophy zine on the separateness of persons objection — y'know, the Rawls-style idea that utilitarianism doesnt really respect the boundary between individuals. But I dont want another dry essay; I want a short story that digs into it without someone just giving a classroom lecture. Here's my twist to keep it fresh: set it maybe twenty years ahead, where there's a neural 'bridge' implant that lets people share physical sensations but not thoughts or identity. So you can literally feel your mum's arthritis, but you're still you and she's still her — no merging of selves. The government wants to wire everyone up so an AI can pool and redistribute suffering and pleasure for the greatest aggregate good, which only works now because pain is literally shared across bodies. The protagonist is a civil servant who has to argue against mandatory bridging, but she cant just say 'people are separate' in the old-fashioned way, since the tech connects bodies. She needs to show that even when suffering is pooled, treating people as interchangeable vessels for total welfare is still wrong.

Can you write a story with one scene in a grey, rain-soaked council room where she debates an economist who asks 'if pain is shared, why not minimise total pain?', and another quieter scene where she feels her mum's pain through the bridge and realises that shared sensation doesnt erase the fact that her mum is the one actually enduring it? Make it a bit messy — let her have doubts, maybe write a memo with a typo or two. I want the philosophical point to come from the situation, not from a speech.
