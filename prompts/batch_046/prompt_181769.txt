Over the past months I have been close to a family member undergoing treatment for chronic neuropathic pain, and her clinicians have raised the possibility of a targeted spinal neuromodulation protocol that suppresses the affective dimension of pain while leaving sensory-discriminative nociception intact—effectively producing a localized, iatrogenic form of pain asymbolia. This has made me think about whether the remaining pure sensory experience is still genuinely harmful, and I find myself wondering how an earlier philosopher might have framed the distinction.

Predict how David Hume—working from his empiricist psychology, his account of sympathy and the passions in A Treatise of Human Nature, and his sentimentalist ethics—would have analyzed this posthumous clinical phenomenon. Specifically: (1) Would he have considered the residual sensory pain to be bad for the patient in any meaningful sense, or merely a neutral signal? (2) How might he have responded to the modern philosophical-medical claim that pain's badness is entirely constituted by its affective component, so that asymbolic pain is not bad at all? (3) Given that Hume wrote long before the neuroanatomical separation of nociceptive and affective pathways was known, reconstruct the reasoning he might have drawn from his broader views on bodily sensation and emotional response—particularly his distinction between impressions and ideas of reflection—to either anticipate or resist that claim.

Please avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Keep the analysis grounded in Hume's known positions rather than in obscure contemporary neurophilosophy, and note where his sentimentalism and his naturalistic psychology might pull in different directions on whether asymbolic pain retains normative significance.
