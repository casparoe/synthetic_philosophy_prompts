I serve as the artistic programming director for a publicly funded opera company, and after a recent board crisis over whether to stage a canonical work whose final act depicts the graphic murder of a female protagonist by her lover, I have been asked to design a reusable ethical-review protocol for all future season proposals. I want this grounded in Bernard Williams's broader philosophical view that opera is not a vehicle for moral instruction but a distinct mode of moral exploration—one in which emotional and aesthetic structures can present ethical complexity without resolving it into propositional claims, and in which judging a work by ordinary moral standards can miss precisely the kind of moral seriousness it achieves. The protocol must be something my three-person programming committee can run in about forty minutes per proposal, without philosophical training, and it must distinguish between works that genuinely inhabit moral ambiguity, works that merely deploy moral content for sensational effect, and works that use ambiguity as an evasion. Please turn this distinction into a step-by-step decision procedure: the specific questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, the criteria for a mandatory "no" or for "proceed only with specified framing conditions," and the explicit points where analysis ends and an irreducible value judgment must be flagged to a human decision-maker rather than resolved by the procedure. The procedure should test for what I will call internal moral architecture—the way a work structures ethical perspectives among its characters and musical forms—separately from external moral posture—the way a production positions its audience relative to those perspectives—so that the two can come into conflict. Apply it to three proposals: (1) the canonical nineteenth-century work mentioned above, presented in a traditional staging with no directorial intervention; (2) a new opera that uses the rhetoric of colonial conquest but frames it through unreliable narrators, dissonant orchestration, and a final scene that undermines the protagonist's triumph; and (3) a commissioned piece marketed as "radically ambiguous" that, upon examination, distributes sympathy evenly across all positions without ever committing the music or the libretto to a coherent ethical weight, effectively using ambiguity as a shield. Include one case in which the procedure returns a recommendation I will personally dislike—either blocking a work I admire or authorizing one I find repellent—and explain precisely where the procedure reaches its limit, what kind of decision it cannot make, and why that residue must remain with the committee rather than being automated away.
