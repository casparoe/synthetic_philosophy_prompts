I spent about thirty years as a neurobiologist studying sensory integration in cephalopods, and since retiring I've been trying to make sense of philosophy of mind, mostly reading on my own and argueing with people in forums. I've come to think phenomenal consciousness is just access consciousness running through a specific self-monitoring loop in the global workspace—once information gets broadcast, there's a second-order module that tracks which representations are available for report, and the feeling of there being something it is like is just the structural signature of that tracking being applied to its own outputs. I don't think I'm being totally original; this sounds like a mashup of global workspace ideas and some higher-order theories, but I've added a twist that I think matters: the so-called phenomenal residue only seems mysterious because we treat first-person reports as having special transparent authority, when actually they're just noisy, contructed outputs of that same monitoring loop. So the hard problem dissolves into an architecture problem, or at least that's my claim.

But I'm not sure if I'm just insulating myself from counterevidence. What would count against this view? I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward—specific neuropsychological dissociations where access and phenomenal consciousness come apart, formal problems with recursive self-reference, or empirical results that undermine the idea that introspection is contructed rather than transparent. I also want to know which of these defenders of workspace-reductionism would actually concede, versus which they'd dismiss with extra qualifications, and whether those qualifications risk making the view unfalsifiable in a bad way. Is there anything this position genuinely risks, or has it been qualified into safety?
