Consider the following argument about retrospective confirmation in Bayesian epistemology. (1) Rational belief revision requires conditioning on information whose prior credence is strictly between 0 and 1. (2) Old evidence—facts an agent already knows before encountering a theory—therefore carries prior credence 1 and cannot confirm that theory, since Bayes's theorem yields P(H|E) = P(H) when P(E) = 1. (3) The standard response distinguishes knowing E from knowing that the theory predicts E, allowing the agent to update on the logical relation D ("H entails E") rather than on E itself. (4) But D is a derivational or logical fact; a rational agent should assign prior credence 1 to every logical consequence within her conceptual competence, or at least to any derivation she could in principle execute. (5) Hence D also has prior 1 and is unavailable for conditioning. (6) By extension, any retrospective explanation involves either the old fact (prior 1) or its logical connection to the theory (prior 1 if logical omniscience is even approximately required). (7) Therefore Bayesianism entails that no theory can ever gain support from evidence discovered before the theory was formulated. (8) Yet scientific rationality clearly depends on such retrospective confirmation—think of evolutionary biology explaining the fossil record, or general relativity accounting for Mercury's perihelion precession. (9) So Bayesian epistemology either collapses into skepticism about retrospective science, or it must abandon the conditioning rule altogether.

The argument feels valid but its conclusion is intolerable. I want to preserve both the conditioning framework and the rationality of retrospective explanation, and I am especially reluctant to deny that logical derivations can be epistemically novel. Which premise should be rejected, and why did it seem plausible? In particular, does the flaw lie in conflating subjective prior probabilities with objective logical status, in overestimating the rational requirements of logical omniscience, or in misapplying Bayes's theorem across diachronic stages? And what do the standard positions—logical-learning accounts, counterfactual-prior reconstructions, and subjective-probability deflation—each have to pay if they reject that step?
