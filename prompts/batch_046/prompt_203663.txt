I defend the view that an AI system's "side-effect footprint" can be defined in a rigorously value-neutral manner: it consists in the divergence between the actual trajectory of world-states and the counterfactual trajectory that would have obtained in the system's absence, restricted to changes occurring within an independently specified spatiotemporal boundary and quantified without appeal to any preference ordering, utility function, or normative conception of harm. On this account, "avoiding side effects" is not a value-laden judgment but a purely structural property of an intervention profile—comparable to limiting the physical reach of a disturbance—because the boundary conditions and the divergence metric are fixed prior to and independently of any assessment of which outcomes are good or bad. What would count against this position? Present the observations, arguments, formal results, or conceptual distinctions that should move its defender, ordered by how decisive they are. In particular, address whether any spatiotemporal boundary can be specified without implicitly encoding normative judgments about which effects are salient; whether counterfactual comparison over world-state trajectories requires a representational framework that already presupposes value-laden categorization; whether the view has been or could be qualified into unfalsifiability by allowing defenders to adjust the boundary or the divergence metric in response to counterexamples; and what distinctive predictions the position makes that rival, value-dependent impact measures cannot replicate. Distinguish between evidence that would be merely awkward and evidence that would be structurally fatal, include an honest assessment of whether insulation from refutation is a virtue or a vice for a proposal of this kind, and say what defenders of the view would themselves concede as genuinely damaging.
