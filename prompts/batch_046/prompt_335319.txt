I coordinate a small community mutual-aid pool that receives far more requests than it can fund—emergency heating, medical travel, eviction prevention, and similar needs—so we face repeated allocation decisions with no formal framework. I have been thinking on my own about whether we should direct aid according to how badly off each recipient is, with greater weight given to the worse off, or whether there is something genuinely wrong with reducing support for the relatively secure merely to make outcomes flatter, even when that reduction frees no resources to help anyone in greater need. I take this latter worry to be the leveling-down objection to the view called prioritarianism. At the same time, I keep encountering a distinction I do not fully understand between judging who is worst off before we intervene and judging them afterward, and I want any procedure I adopt to be explicit about which of these two perspectives it employs rather than hiding the choice behind technical language.

Please construct a reusable decision procedure that my team can run on any funding proposal. The procedure should present its questions in strict order, specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, identify the precise points where the answer is “do not fund,” where it is “fund with conditions,” and where the decision must be handed to a human judgment because it involves values rather than checkable facts. It should build in both the prioritarian weighting of benefits and an explicit, non-evasive response to the leveling-down worry, and it should clearly flag whether it is using an ex-ante or an ex-post measure of deprivation.

Then apply the procedure to two concrete proposals. First, a plan to provide winter heating subsidies to ten households that are cold but not in immediate physical danger, funded entirely by cutting an already-secure education grant serving fifteen other households—so that the total aid budget is unchanged, no one receives more, but the distribution becomes more equal. Second, a plan to provide the same heating subsidies by reducing the education grant only to the extent that every dollar freed is redirected to the colder households, leaving the grant smaller but intact, with the worst-off households receiving substantially more per capita and no household being made worse off solely for the sake of flatness.

Finally, state honestly what the procedure cannot settle on its own: in particular, whether there should be an absolute floor below which we never reduce anyone regardless of equality gains; whether people who have adapted their expectations to long-term hardship should count as less badly off; and what further kind of case would force us to choose between these two unresolved rules. I am less interested in a single yes-or-no answer than in seeing the procedure work, misfire, and expose its own limits.
