I am a high school junior working through F. H. Bradley's Appearance and Reality (first published in 1893) for an independent philosophy project. In the discussion of relations and qualities, Bradley considers a simple judgment such as that sugar is sweet, and asks what binds the quality sweetness to the subject sugar. He introduces a relation—being sweet of—to link them, but then observes that this relation itself must be linked to sugar and to sweetness, which seems to require further relations, and then further ones still, producing an infinite regress. From this he concludes that relations cannot be independent realities on a par with qualities, and that the proposition must possess an indivisible unity rather than being a mere assembly of terms.

I am struggling to see exactly where the regress is vicious rather than merely descriptive. Bradley seems to treat every relation as if it were itself a kind of term that demands further linking, yet in modern formal treatments a relation can be specified extensionally—for example, as a set of ordered pairs—without invoking an additional linking entity. Is there an established name for Bradley's implicit assumption that relations must always be substantial or internal in the same manner as qualities, and do philosophers distinguish his metaphysical regress from cases where a relation is well-founded precisely because it does not require further relating relations? Could you help me understand this specific passage and clarify whether Bradley's argument depends on rejecting any extensional or structural conception of relation? Please respond in American English.
