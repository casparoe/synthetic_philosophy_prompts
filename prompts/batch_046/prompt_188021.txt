Question: Du Bois argued that all art is propaganda and that the Black artist bears a special duty to represent racial truth rather than pursue beauty for its own sake. Assess whether this artistic program is compatible with the psychological framework of double-consciousness that Du Bois developed, which describes a divided individual self shaped by internalized social judgment. In particular, does his demand for collective representational unity require suppressing the very divided self that double-consciousness describes?

Student answer: Du Bois clearly holds that art must serve racial uplift, so beauty is always subordinate to truth. He describes double-consciousness as the feeling of always seeing oneself through the eyes of others, which splits the Black self into two conflicting identities. Because this division gives the Black artist direct insight into both the internal racial experience and the external white gaze, it functions as a strategic resource for propaganda: the artist can craft work that affirms Black dignity while countering stereotypes. Therefore there is no conflict. The artist draws on this divided perspective to produce a unified collective image that heals the racial split. Du Bois allows limited individual expression, but only when it supports this larger unifying purpose. His essay on Negro artistic criteria confirms that pure aestheticism is a luxury the race cannot afford. Thus the two doctrines are fully compatible, since double-consciousness supplies exactly the binocular vision that truthful propaganda requires.
