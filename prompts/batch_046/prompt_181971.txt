I sit on the governance panel of a neurotechnology firm that has developed a cortical interface capable of sustaining multiple distinct conscious work-streams within a single substrate. The panel must recommend whether to scale horizontally—generating thousands of low-fidelity experiential threads—or vertically, maintaining a small number of high-fidelity threads. A fellow panellist argues that, since I am myself one of these hosted streams, my existence should update my beliefs about which policy is actually in force: under horizontal scaling there are vastly more observers like me, so my presence counts as stronger evidence for that policy, and we ought therefore to favour it in our recommendation.

I am troubled by this reasoning. It seems to conflate the bare fact that I exist—an update that might favour larger populations of observers—with an unjustified assumption that my particular experiential profile is typical across all possible streams, including those that might exist under vertical scaling. I also worry that adopting this view commits us to a kind of cosmological overconfidence about the total number of minds that could be realised, as if the architecture were scaled precisely to make my presence unsurprising. How should I structure my argument for Thursday's meeting so that I acknowledge any legitimate evidential weight carried by my own existence without falling into that bias of presumed typicality? I need a principled way to separate the update drawn from being here from the leap to an assumed population size, and to recommend a concrete course of action—either endorsing or rejecting the horizontal-scaling preference—within a framework that treats the strongest objection on the other side with intellectual honesty.
