Here's an argument my roommate cooked up about the statue and the clay, but it ends up saying something wild about cars and bodies too, and I can't see the leak.

(1) Take the statue David and the lump of clay Lump. At any time they exist together, they share every spatiotemporal part and every intrinsic physical property.

(2) If two things share all parts and all intrinsic properties at a time, then they are numerically identical at that time — call this the Coincidence Principals.

(3) So David is identical to Lump.

(4) But Lump can survive being squashed into a ball; David, being essentially an artwork with this specific form, cannot.

(5) So David and Lump have different modal persistence conditions.

(6) If x and y have different modal persistence conditions, then x and y are not numerically identical — call this the Modal Difference Principal.

(7) From (3) and (6) we get a contradiction, so one principle has to go.

(8) My roommate says we should reject the Modal Difference Principal: persistence conditions don't track real entities, they just track our descriptions. So David is Lump, and "David" is just a temporary description of Lump's shape, like "bent" describes a wire.

(9) But if that's right, then destroying a statue is never destroying an object — it's only changing a shape. The same applies to cars, houses, even bodies: "being a car" is just a phase of metal, "being alive" is just a phase of cells. So there are no artifacts or organisms, only material going through phases.

(10) That can't be right — we insure cars against destruction, not just reshaping.

(11) So he says we reject (2) instead: Coincidence Principals is false, and two objects can share all parts and properties. David and Lump are distinct but fully co-located.

(12) But then apply this to anything with parts: a car's engine and the car share parts and location. So if Coincidence fails, we get not just statue+clay but car+engine+metal... an explosion of entities everywhere.

(13) So either way we lose either artifacts or physical uniqueness.

I feel like there's a hidden slide between "modal persistence conditions" and "numerical identity" in (6), or maybe in (8) he's smuggling in eliminativism without arguing for it. But I want to keep (1) and (4) if I can, since they seem obvious. Which step should I actually reject, and what's the price for the standard positions — identity, constitution, and eliminativism?
