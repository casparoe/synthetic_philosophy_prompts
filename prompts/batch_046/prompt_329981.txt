I'm trying to identify whether there is an established label for a particular asymmetry I keep noticing in arguments over whether awareness is present at the most basic physical level. The pattern runs like this: a critic points out that if every elementary physical unit carries some minimal form of experience, then any subset of my neurons—say, the left hemisphere alone, or a random cluster of cortical cells—should also constitute its own unified subject, since those parts stand in structured internal relations just as the whole brain does, yet I only ever report a single consciousness rather than thousands of overlapping ones. The defender replies that experience belongs not to individual units but to a specific kind of highly integrated relational organization, which is why disordered aggregates like chairs possess none and why the brain as a whole possesses one. But when the critic then observes that this same organizational account entails that my consciousness should persist through the gradual replacement of every underlying particle—because the relational pattern, not the material substrate, is what matters—the defender treats that substrate-independence as an attractive feature rather than a reductio. Meanwhile, the defender never applies the organizational criterion downward to explain why those internal neural subsets, which also exhibit integration, do not generate separate coexisting subjects. So the criterion is invoked upward to preserve macro-subjects and outward to deny consciousness to non-neural objects, but not downward to block unwanted subset-subjects. Is there a recognized name in the literature for this selective, directional use of an organizational or relational condition—distinct from the general combination problem and from mere boundary vagueness—or should it be defined separately?
