In discussions of P.F. Strawson's framework of reactive attitudes and moral responsibility, there is a recurring stall that does not align with the familiar compatibilist versus incompatibilist divide. I want a diagnosis of its structure, not a survey of positions.

Consider the following pattern, which I have observed in both academic and informal philosophical debate. One side insists that reactive attitudes—resentment, indignation, gratitude—are essentially second-personal and addressive: they presuppose that the target is an interlocutor who could respond to a demand, and they lose their point if no such interaction is possible. From this, they argue that determinism undermines moral responsibility because it removes the possibility that the offender could have satisfied the demand embedded in the attitude.

The opposing side points to clear cases of third-party moral indignation: an observer who feels outrage on behalf of a victim, directed at a perpetrator she will never meet and who has no opportunity to respond. They claim this is still a reactive attitude in Strawson's sense—it responds to quality of will, not to causal history—yet it involves no interpersonal demand and presupposes no alternative possibilities. Therefore, they conclude, determinism does not threaten this core form of moral response.

The impasse arises when the first side replies that such third-party outrage is not a genuine reactive attitude at all, but a derivative evaluative judgment that borrows its emotional color from second-personal resentment. The second side then replies that stripping away the second-personal element changes the subject away from Strawson's actual concept. Each side accuses the other of either smuggling metaphysical commitments into the psychology of blame or of watering down reactive attitudes into mere moral evaluation.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not mainly about determinism or about the psychology of blame, but about which phenomena should serve as the core data for a theory of moral responsibility. I want an honest assessment.
