Next Thursday I defend, in our interdisciplinary philosophy-of-science and computational-biology colloquium, a revised demarcation criterion I call structural-risk falsificationism. The claim is that Popper's binary falsifiability collapses for high-dimensional models that can always absorb anomalies through parameter adjustment; instead, a program is scientific only when it regularly stakes its core theoretical architecture on risky structural predictions—predictions about causal mechanisms that, if falsified, cannot be saved by local tweaking but require abandoning the framework itself. I apply this to argue that several prominent AI-interpretability research programs currently fail the criterion, while evolutionary developmental biology passes it comfortably. The audience is split: strict Popperians who see any revision as betrayal of the criterion; Kuhn- and Feyerabend-influenced philosophers who treat demarcation as a social-power construct and normative criteria as ideologically loaded; and working computational biologists with no patience for metaphilosophy who will defend their field's status aggressively. Prepare my Q&A defense sheet: list the objections in the order they will likely come, phrased in the vocabulary each faction actually uses; for each, indicate whether I should concede, hold, or partially concede, provide a reply suitable for about two minutes, and flag the weakest point in that reply. Order them by likely damage to the proposal, tell me which single reply has to land for the talk to survive, and include the objection I am hoping nobody raises—namely, the challenge that in complex models the distinction between "structural" and "parametric" predictions itself depends on prior theoretical commitments, so my criterion smuggles in the very framework-dependence it claims to avoid.
