Assume the role of a philosopher of science writing deadpan academic satire. First, in two or three sentences, map the distinction between self-sampling and self-indication assumptions, and pinpoint the specific overreach of the “presumptuous philosopher” who treats his own existence as decisive statistical evidence about the total population of observers. Then write a parody peer-reviewed journal abstract and brief “Results” section (about 450–550 words total, formatted with a title, authors, abstract, and one subsection) reporting an invented study: subjects apply anthropic reasoning to choose a restaurant in an unknown city, treating the fact that they are dining at all as evidence that restaurants must be extraordinarily common across possible worlds. Exaggerate the target logic—reference-class inflation, conditioning failures, and the slide from personal indexical evidence to cosmic conclusions—while keeping the jargon plausible; invent one technical term such as “indexical inflation coefficient.” The satire must be fair: a specialist should recognize real features of anthropic reasoning rather than invented ones, and a lay reader should sense the absurdity without being told. Maintain a dry, formal scientific voice throughout; do not cite real authors or texts by name; and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations.
