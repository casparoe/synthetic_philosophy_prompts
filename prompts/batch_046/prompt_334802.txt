I am a high school student organizing an advanced peer interview series on normative ethics, and our next guest is a philosopher who defends a modified version of indirect normativity. Her specific view is that an action or institution is justified not by direct assessment of its outcomes, but by whether it survives a particular form of idealized deliberation—except that she revises the standard model in two ways. First, she insists that the idealized deliberator retains specific, non-filtered affective attachments rather than bracketing them, arguing that this preserves practical identity rather than smuggling in direct evaluation. Second, she requires that the deliberator operate under deliberately partial epistemic conditions, not full omniscience, claiming that this prevents an unrealistic bias rather than modeling mere human ignorance. She further holds that this “situated idealization” strengthens, rather than collapses, the boundary between indirect justification and direct consequentialist judgment. I would like you to draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond asking her to restate the position. Specifically, please include focused questions on: (1) how retaining affective attachments avoids the adaptive-preference objection without covertly treating those attachments as directly evaluable goods; (2) whether the partial-information condition functions as a realistic constraint on human agents or as a normative filter on what counts as legitimate justification; (3) how one distinguishes a case where indirect deliberation rejects a practice from a case where direct consequentialist reasoning merely notes that the practice produces undesirable outcomes; and (4) whether norms governing how we ought to revise our own normative commitments are themselves subject to the same indirect test, and if so, whether this introduces a regress. For each area, provide a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to offer—especially the claim that affective retention is structurally analogous to preserving one’s practical identity, and the claim that partial epistemic conditions correct for omniscient bias. The questions should press where the literature presses, anticipate her standard defenses, and avoid generic biographical openings. Please do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and keep the tone formal and suitable for a rigorous student-led discussion.
