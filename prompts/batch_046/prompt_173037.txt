Consider Nietzsche's judgments on bodily regimen and diet: he condemns heavy German fare—potatoes, boiled meat, and beer—as producing sluggishness and dull thought; he praises lighter Mediterranean eating, olive oil, and fish for sharpening mental clarity; he rejects vegetarianism adopted out of Christian pity or ascetic morality, calling it decadent; he approves periodic fasting, cold exposure, and walking in mountains as exercises in hardness and self-overcoming; he mocks modern hygiene movements that treat the body with fear and avoid all risk of contamination; and he insists that a thinker's diet must match his climate and individual constitution rather than follow universal prescriptions.

Now take a present-day philosopher living in a cold northern climate who tracks every macronutrient with software, takes synthetic nootropics and adaptogens, practices precisely calibrated intermittent fasting, consumes no animal products for purely ecological rather than moral or ascetic reasons, avoids alcohol completely, and reports sustained mental energy—though she never subjects herself to physical hardship or climatic extremes. Predict Nietzsche's verdict—approval, qualified approval, or rejection—identify the unifying principle behind his earlier judgments (vitalist enhancement, climate-constitution fit, or anti-decadence), and say which earlier case best supports each reading. Then specify a further hypothetical case that would distinguish these principles, and state honestly which of Nietzsche's earlier verdicts each principle struggles to accommodate.
