I'm trying to settle my views for a discussion on moral psychology and want a consistency check before I present them. Here is where I stand: first, I accept Bernard Williams's integrity objection in a strong form, holding that some personal projects are constitutive of my practical identity and that abandoning them for aggregate welfare is not merely wrong but a kind of self-betrayal. Second, I think the problem Williams raises about having one mediating thought too many — inserting an abstract principle between direct concern and action — corrupts the value of the act not only in intimate relationships but in any case where calculated justification replaces direct responsiveness. Third, I maintain that moral reasons are genuinely universal in form: the claim made by a suffering stranger is not different in kind from that made by my child, though urgency and proximity may vary. Fourth, I hold that integrity-preserving sacrifice must flow from the internal logic of the project itself, as when a parent risks everything for her child, whereas abandoning that child for the greater good violates integrity regardless of the numbers involved. Fifth, I believe reflective equilibrium can still be the right method in ethics, but only if it operates at the level of character formation rather than in moment-to-moment deliberation, thereby avoiding the corruption the mediating-thought problem identifies. Sixth, I am inclined to think the distinction between agent-relative and agent-neutral reasons ultimately collapses, since every reason is anchored in some particular practical identity and apparently agent-neutral reasons are just those anchored in widely shared projects. Do these hang together? I want an audit, not a lecture: rank the tensions by severity, identify which pairs are only apparently in conflict, say for each real tension what commitment has to give and at what cost, and tell me whether there is a single revision that would clean up the most problems at once. Please write your answer in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — as in a letter or email.
