Since I began volunteering in palliative care last month, I have been wondering whether the music and images we use there qualify as genuine art in any serious philosophical sense. A colleague lent me a popular book that summarizes Tolstoy's late essay on art. According to the summary, Tolstoy defines art as a conscious infection: the artist transmits a feeling to an audience so that they genuinely share it. The author claims that for Tolstoy an artwork succeeds only when the feeling transmitted is one of universal human brotherhood; everything else, however technically brilliant, is counterfeit art designed for pleasure or prestige. The summary also says Tolstoy believed an artist's sincerity is sufficient for artistic success—if she genuinely feels brotherly love, the infection necessarily reaches the audience regardless of the listener's culture, education, or emotional state. From this, the author concludes that Tolstoy's standard condemns nearly all modern abstract painting and instrumental music as counterfeit, since these forms rarely aim at moral unity. I am unsure how faithful this is. Could you audit the summary claim by claim: which parts accurately reflect Tolstoy's argument, which are subtle overstatements or conflations, and does the dismissal of abstraction actually follow from his premises? In particular, does Tolstoy really hold that sincerity alone guarantees transmission, and does he reject every feeling except brotherhood as artistically illegitimate?
