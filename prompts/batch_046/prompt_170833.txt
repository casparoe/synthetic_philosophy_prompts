I am delivering a paper at a workshop on intersubjectivity and institutional recognition next month, and I find myself rather anxious about the question period. The room will contain mostly analytic philosophers of social ontology who treat recognition as a dyadic status-conferring relation between fully rational agents, two Hegel scholars who favour Kojève-inspired existential readings of the Phenomenology, and a critical theorist who thinks the master-slave dialectic is primarily about labour and domination rather than consciousness. My thesis—developed from some discussions with colleagues about algorithmic management—is that the dialectic ought to be read not as a normative argument for mutual recognition, but as a structural analysis of what I term "asymmetrical recognition": the master's acknowledgement is inherently unstable because it is granted by a being the master regards as lacking full autonomy, and this instability is not resolved by the slave's formative labour but only by the dissolution of the hierarchical relation itself. I extend this to modern "performance-recognition" systems, where workers receive evaluative feedback from algorithmic metrics that possess no consciousness whatsoever, thereby replicating the structural defect of the master's recognition without even the possibility of reciprocal struggle.
