In recent discussions of the First World War's origins, particularly around the Fischer controversy over German war aims and the July Crisis, a familiar analogy treats the attribution of national responsibility like a criminal prosecution: the state is the defendant, the archival record is the evidentiary file, and the historian functions as prosecutor or defense counsel arguing over mens rea at the collective level. Proponents use the analogy to insist that without a documented, unified decision for preventive war, Germany should be acquitted of primary culpability, leaving only diffuse structural failure; critics reply that a nation-state is not a natural person and that war causation is not analogous to a single intentional homicide. Audit this analogy precisely: map the correspondences—defendant, burden of proof, principal versus accomplice liability, mitigating circumstances, and the act-omission distinction—onto the historiographical debate; identify which disanalogies matter specifically for collective moral agency and which are merely decorative; ask whether the analogy quietly imports an individualist psychology of action that makes collective blame conceptually impossible from the outset, or whether it can be reconstructed with a non-reductive account of group intentionality; and finally, say whether the Fischer conclusion—that Germany bears a distinctive, heavier burden of responsibility than the other powers—survives once the analogy is either repaired or abandoned, or whether that conclusion depended on the very individualist framework the analogy supplies.
