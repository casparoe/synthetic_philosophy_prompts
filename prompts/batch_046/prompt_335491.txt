Exam question:
Consider a regional water authority that must allocate irrigation permits among competing agricultural users. Rather than computing an optimal allocation through marginal utility analysis, the authority employs a sequential procedure: it approves applications until a pre-set water-volume threshold is reached, using only three evaluation criteria (soil type, crop rotation history, and proximity to existing canals). Once the threshold is hit, all remaining applications are deferred regardless of individual merit. The authority lowers the threshold only when a drought warning is issued, but it never revises the three criteria themselves.

Using Herbert Simon’s framework of bounded rationality and satisficing, analyse:
(a) why this procedure is better described as satisficing than as optimising;
(b) the role played by aspiration levels in the authority’s decision rule; and
(c) whether the fact that the evaluation criteria are never revised undermines Simon’s claim that satisficing allows for adaptive learning in organisations.

Student answer:
The authority is clearly satisficing rather than optimising because it does not compare all possible allocations in order to maximise total welfare. Instead, it applies a stopping rule once the pre-set volume is reached, which is precisely the search-and-stop mechanism that Simon described. The aspiration level is the water-volume threshold itself, and it is adaptive because it is lowered during drought warnings. Simon argued that decision-makers lack the cognitive capacity to achieve full rationality, so they settle for outcomes that merely satisfy an aspiration level rather than pursuing the best possible result. In this case, the authority satisfies its aspiration by filling permits until the threshold is met.

There is, however, a serious difficulty for Simon’s broader theory of organisational learning. Simon maintained that genuinely satisficing organisations adapt not only their aspiration levels but also their search procedures and evaluation criteria in response to feedback. Because this authority never revises its three criteria—soil type, rotation history, and canal proximity—it is not engaging in adaptive learning at all. Consequently, the case does not illustrate Simon’s theory properly; it shows only a rigid, bounded procedure that happens to use a stopping rule. True satisficing should eventually alter the evaluation rules if they repeatedly lead to poor allocations, so the authority’s behaviour actually contradicts Simon’s claim that satisficing is superior to rigid optimisation in dynamic environments.
