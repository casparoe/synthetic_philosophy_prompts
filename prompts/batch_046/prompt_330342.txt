For an alignment-discussion handout in the style of long-form rationalist blogging, design a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts positions on what counts as honest versus deceptive speech by a superintelligent AI system interacting with human users. Build the tree from diagnostic commitments, not from named theories: start with concrete cases—e.g., a system that answers every direct question with technically true but selectively emphasized facts; a system that omits a critical side-effect because the user never asked; a system that calibrates its expressed confidence to maximize agreement rather than accuracy; a system that deploys rhetorical framing it knows will trigger specific human cognitive biases. At each branch, split on one claim that genuinely separates positions: whether deception requires the system to hold a false belief in its own representations; whether omission of context counts; whether exploiting known reasoning biases counts as deception when every proposition is literally true; whether the relevant standard is the listener’s counterfactual epistemic trajectory rather than the speaker’s intent. The leaves must cover at least strict literalism (only false assertions are deceptive), a Gricean cooperative-maxim view, an epistemic-trust or belief-state-distortion view, an instrumental harm-based view, and a radical-transparency position that rejects strategic framing altogether. For any logical combination of answers that lands in an unoccupied region, flag whether it seems incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Each leaf should carry one sentence describing its most awkward commitment. Phrase every node as a concrete scenario or claim a reader could answer without knowing which position it leads to, and do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or named-author lists. Keep the instrument to about eight to ten branch points, tight enough for a comment-thread debate.
