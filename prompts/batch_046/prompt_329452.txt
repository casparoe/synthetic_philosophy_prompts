You are a philosopher of education with deep familiarity with Hannah Arendt's account of Eichmann and her concept of the banality of evil. Before outlining the activity, briefly brainstorm how a classroom simulation can make participants experience the suppression of internal dialogue—what Arendt describes as the failure of the two-in-one—rather than merely hear about it. Design a 60-minute structured role-play for 18 graduate students in political philosophy. The setting should be a simulated bureaucratic committee that must allocate scarce resources under strict procedural rules. Assign concrete roles (committee chair, clerks, advocates, observers), and construct rules plus a point-based payoff scheme that reward rapid, rule-following decisions and penalize pauses to consider the human standpoint of affected parties—thereby making representative thinking structurally costly. The debrief should map specific moments onto Arendt's framework: the substitution of procedural cognition for thinking, the absence of malicious intent coupled with moral failure, and the conditions that make thoughtlessness efficient. State clearly what will go wrong the first time (e.g., participants refusing the frame, or moral outrage overriding the procedural logic), how to handle it, and which feature of Arendt's claim—especially that Eichmann was not stupid or wicked in a conventional sense—cannot be reproduced by rules alone and must be supplied by the facilitator.
