I am preparing a feature for a science-and-ideas publication on the metaphysics of authenticity, and I wish to move beyond market-based arguments about forgeries to examine a stricter philosophical claim: that an original possesses a non-transferable historical indexicality grounded in its causal trajectory through time, such that even a perceptually perfect replica is ontologically deficient because it lacks that diachronic biography. I want to apply this framing to Oscar Wilde's The Picture of Dorian Gray—not as a mere illustration, but as a serious interlocutor. In the novel, the portrait absorbs the physical and moral decay that should have marked Dorian, who remains an unchanging, youthful facsimile of himself; ultimately he destroys the canvas and dies, his body suddenly displaying the corruption the painting had stored.

Read the novel through the lens of original versus forged temporal identity. Is the portrait the true original of Dorian's lived moral history, while Dorian himself becomes a high-fidelity forgery detached from the causal process that ought to have aged and marked him? Which scenes—the concealment of the picture in the locked attic, Basil Hallward's horror at its transformation, the final knife-wound to the canvas—most sharply dramatise this inversion? At the same time, where does the text resist the reading: does the fact that the portrait is itself a manufactured artistic object undermine its status as an unproblematic original, and does Wilde's aestheticism suggest that surface appearance is all that ever mattered?

I am looking for a balanced critical reading that neither collapses the novel into philosophy nor distorts the concept to fit the plot, and that concludes by asking what Wilde's temporal split between body and image contributes to the debate about originals and copies that pure conceptual argument cannot reach.
