I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy or biology, though I have been reading about synthetic biology and artificial life in my spare time. Recently I was discussing with friends whether a hypothetical future organism—one that metabolizes, divides, and evolves but has no internal mechanism for correcting its own organizational errors, relying entirely on external laboratory conditions to stay within viable parameters—should count as alive. That conversation led me to a view I now hold, but I want to know what should genuinely change my mind rather than what I can dismiss as a definitional quibble.

My view is that life is not primarily a matter of metabolism, reproduction, or evolutionary lineage, but of what I would call autonomous normative closure: a system is alive only if it contains an internal subsystem that represents the system's own organizational rules and actively corrects deviations from them, where that corrective mechanism is not reducible to the system's overall metabolism or to the environment in which it sits. On this framing, viruses are not alive because they borrow host machinery for error correction; a minimal synthetic cell with full metabolic autonomy but no internal self-monitoring loop is not alive either; but a computer virus that runs a self-checking integrity loop over its own code might actually be closer to life than either of the biological cases, despite having no chemistry at all. I realize this inverts the usual biological bias, but it seems to capture why we hesitate to call a crystal or a flame alive even though both exhibit self-organizing dynamics.

Given that I am not a philosopher, I want an honest map of this position's exposure. Which observations, arguments, or conceptual distinctions should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be? Are there borderline cases—perhaps in prions, in certain RNA-world scenarios, or in distributed ecological networks—that genuinely threaten autonomous normative closure as the defining feature? Has my view been qualified into unfalsifiability, for instance by allowing the corrective subsystem to be distributed, emergent, or defined so loosely that no system could fail to meet it? And if defenders of the more standard metabolic or evolutionary definitions have themselves conceded weaknesses—such as the difficulty of drawing a clean line for viruses or for synthetic minimal cells—does that strengthen my position, or does it only show that the entire debate is stuck? I am especially interested in whether there is an established concept that already captures the difference between being maintained by an environment and maintaining oneself through an internal representation of one's own rules, and if so, whether my framing misapplies it.
