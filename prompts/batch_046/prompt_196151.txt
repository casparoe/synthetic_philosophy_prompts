As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy in earnest, I have encountered repeatedly in seminar discussions the maxim that tolerance of intolerance is itself intolerance. Participants invoke it to justify excluding particular viewpoints from institutional forums, yet—having spent decades calibrating instruments to distinguish genuine signals from artifacts of the detector—I am struck by how casually the variables shift between epistemic, moral, and structural registers. Could you disambiguate the slogan for me? Rather than selecting one interpretation, please lay out the distinct precise theses it might express, organized as an explicit menu. For each reading, specify: first, whether tolerance denotes an epistemic posture (withholding belief pending sufficient evidence), a moral virtue (respect for the autonomy of others), or an institutional design principle (procedural neutrality of rules); second, whether the intolerance being tolerated is a private doxastic state, a public speech act, or a structural feature embedded in organizational rules; and third, whether the claim is conceptual (analytic), normative (prescriptive), or empirical (predictive of social outcomes). For each combination, indicate whether it genuinely supports excluding a viewpoint from a forum, or whether it commits a category error—such as treating a second-order refusal to accommodate exclusionary norms as if it were a first-order instance of the intolerance being rejected. I am especially interested in whether any coherent reading permits intolerance of intolerance without collapsing into a mere circular preference for one's own first-order commitments, and whether such coherence requires treating tolerance not as one competing value among others, but as a non-derogable boundary condition analogous to the calibration baseline of a measuring apparatus.
