In a seminar discussion on transitional justice, a colleague pressed me on whether formal state apologies for historical wrongs—issued by present governments for violence committed generations earlier—can be morally coherent without committing us to an implausible metaphysics of collective agency. I have come to defend the view that such apologies are coherent, but only if we accept a form of diachronic corporate personality for states: a moral agent that can persist through radical changes in membership, leadership, and even constitutional form, anchored not in psychological continuity or strict legal succession but in something like a continuous territorial-political project or shared institutional narrative. On this account, a republic born from revolution can genuinely apologize for acts committed by the monarchy it replaced, because the moral subject survives the institutional rupture. Before I commit further, I want a rigorous assessment of what would actually count against this position. Which observations, arguments, or institutional practices would be decisive—say, the non-consent of present citizens, the dissolution or partition of the state, or cases where the victim group no longer exists to receive the apology—versus which would merely be awkward? I want you to distinguish objections that defenders of corporate-personality theories would themselves concede as damaging from those they would dismiss as category errors, and I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely inconvenient.

I also want an honest evaluation of whether my position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—if any counterexample can be absorbed by redrawing the boundaries of what counts as the continuous project—so that no institutional rupture could ever break the chain of responsibility. Is that insulation a virtue that saves the theory, or a vice that empties it of predictive content? Finally, tell me what this view predicts that its rivals do not: for instance, does it commit me to treating secessionist movements or successor states as inheriting specific moral burdens rather than others, and are there concrete policy differences—about reparations versus symbolic apology—that follow from accepting this metaphysics rather than rejecting it? I am not asking for historical case studies to cite, but for the conceptual structure of the exposure: what the position risks, where its defenders would admit weakness, and whether it has been protected by qualifications that should worry me.
