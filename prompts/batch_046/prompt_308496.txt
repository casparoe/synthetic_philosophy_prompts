You are grading a philosophy exam on personal identity and essential origins. The exam question was:

“Standard origin essentialism holds that a person is essentially tied to the particular gametes from which they developed: had a different sperm fertilized that egg, the result would not be you. Now consider synthetic blastoids—stem-cell-derived embryo models that develop without sperm or egg—implanted and carried to term, producing a person A. Some propose revising the view into ‘developmental origin essentialism,’ holding that what is essential is not gametes but the specific initial cellular configuration and culture environment. Others reply that this revision is either too fragile (since a different incubator temperature might seem to change A’s identity) or that the absence of gametes shows origin essentialism was never a universal metaphysical thesis, only a contingent feature of sexual reproduction. Assess whether the blastoid case forces a revision, a restriction of scope, or an abandonment of origin essentialism.”

Here is a student’s answer. Grade it (suggest a point range or letter) and provide specific, point-by-point comments justifying the grade. The answer is genuinely mixed: acknowledge what it gets right about the core view and the synthetic-case challenge, then flag the subtle errors—especially any conflation of genetic identity with cellular origin, any misjudgment about what conditions a revised view must treat as essential, and any mistake about whether restricting the thesis to sexually reproduced organisms undermines its metaphysical status. Do not signal your own agreement or disagreement; deliver only an assessment of reasoning and accuracy.

Student answer:

“Origin essentialism says a person essentially comes from the specific sperm and egg that produced them; change either gamete and you get a different person. In the blastoid case there are no gametes, so the view faces a choice. One path is developmental origin essentialism: A is essentially tied to the particular stem-cell line and initial culture setup that began her development. This saves the core intuition that origin matters, but it seems too fragile—if the incubator had been two degrees warmer, would that really be a different person? That fragility suggests the revision fails. The other path is to say origin essentialism applies only to sexually reproduced organisms, not to synthetic ones. But then the thesis is not a deep metaphysical claim about personal identity; it is just a biological contingency. So for synthetic embryos we should abandon origin essentialism entirely, because either revision makes the view implausible or reduces it to a local biological fact.”
