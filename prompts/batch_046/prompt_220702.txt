I was discussing narrative structures with a friend over coffee—I am not trained in philosophy, just someone who notices patterns in stories and arguments—and we found ourselves returning to that old parable about the servant in Damascus who is told he will meet Death there, flees to Samarra, and finds Death waiting with the explanation that they had an appointment in Samarra all along. We began wondering whether there is a precise term for the specific mechanism at work, not fatalism in general but this particular shape: a prediction fixes on a specific location, the subject’s evasion changes that location, and the outcome is then retrospectively reinterpreted so that the location was never essential, only the inevitability of the encounter itself.

I have since noticed what feels like the same structure in policy debates. A forecast warns that adopting Policy A will cause Harm H in Region R; to avoid it, authorities adopt Policy B and relocate resources to Region S; then Harm H strikes Region S, and defenders of the original forecast claim this proves the harm was unavoidable, treating the geographic specificity as if it had always been incidental. Is there an established concept that captures this exact move—the retrospective dissolution of a spatial or conditional specificity after an agent’s evasion? How exactly does it differ from a self-fulfilling prophecy, since here the flight does not causally produce the harm but merely shifts the conditions under which it occurs, and from the Oedipus effect, since there is no direct causal loop between the act of avoidance and the occurrence of the harm? If no standard term exists, I would welcome a clear coinage together with its boundary conditions, particularly whether it requires an intentional predictor or can operate with impersonal forecasts, and whether the relocated outcome must be identical in kind or merely analogous.
