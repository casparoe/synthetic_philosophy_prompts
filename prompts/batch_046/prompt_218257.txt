I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar this semester and we just got to Dennet's idea that the self is basically a center of narrative gravity — like the center of mass of a chair, not some little ghost sitting behind the eyes. Last night I was arguing about it with my friends Sam and Riley at a coffee shop and things got kinda heated. I want someone to break down exactly where each of them is right or wrong, because I honestly can't tell anymore.

So Sam started by saying that if the self is literally just a center of narrative gravity, then personal identity is nothing more than whether your internal autobiography hangs together. That means there's no deep metaphysical mystery when someone dies or gets Alzheimer's — it's just the story breaking up or ending, not the destruction of some hidden essence. Riley pushed back and said Sam is treating "fiction" like it means "fake," but Dennet doesn't think the self is an illusion in that sense; it's a theoretical construct that explains real stuff, like center of gravity explains why a chair doesn't fall over. The problem, Riley said, is that people still feel like there's a "me" that actually owns the story. If there's no owner behind it, then who is doing the experiencing? You can't just delete the subject and keep the narrative running.

Then Sam came back and said that's exactly the Cartesian theater Dennet wants to kill — there's no homunculus watching the movie. The brain pulls together memories, intentions, and sensations into one point, and that's the self. Even the feeling of ownership is just another line in the story. Sam brought up this hypothetical split-autobiography case — imagine a brain where the left and right hemispheres each maintain a fully continuous first-person narrative with zero awareness of each other, taking turns controlling the body. Then you've got two centers of narrative gravity living in one skull, and there's definately no hidden soul deciding which one is the "real" person. Riley wasn't convinced. She said if you can have two centers of gravity in one object, then the metaphor is broken — a chair can't have two centers of mass. So either the metaphor fails, or there's still an underlying biological system, the living organism, that hosts both narratives. That organism, Riley argued, is the real subject, and the narrative just organizes its point of view. Otherwise you'd have to say that a really advanced AI with a coherent autobiography has a self exactly like ours, which doesn't seem right. So maybe Dennet is correct that there's no ghost inside, but wrong that the narrative is all there is; there's also the embodied point of view that generates it.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate Sam's and Riley's four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then tell me who, if anyone, wins the core disagreement, and point out what both of them are missing — especially whether there's a middle position that keeps Dennet's anti-Cartesian point without either dissolving the self into pure fiction or sneaking a hidden soul back in.
