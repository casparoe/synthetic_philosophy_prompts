Write an essay defending a verdict on the following controversy in reproductive ethics. The debate concerns whether the principle of procreative beneficence—roughly, the moral idea that if one can choose among possible future children, one ought to choose the child expected to have the best life—can be reconciled with liberal eugenics, which holds that genetic selection should remain a private, value-pluralistic choice rather than a state-imposed optimization program.

To focus the dispute, consider the "plural-index selection problem": suppose preimplantation screening yields not a single welfare ranking but three incommensurable sub-indices for each embryo—bodily health, cognitive adaptability, and emotional range—such that no embryo dominates all others. One embryo scores highest on health and emotional range but lowest on adaptability; another dominates on adaptability but is weaker on emotional range.

Some philosophers maintain that procreative beneficence imposes only a negative duty: parents must avoid embryos carrying serious genetic diseases, but among healthy embryos they may legitimately select according to their own conception of a good life, even if that means accepting lower scores on one or more welfare dimensions. Others contend that beneficence requires parents to maximize the child's expected open-ended flourishing, which should push them toward the embryo with the best aggregate or balanced profile, regardless of whether that profile matches the parents' values or life plans.

Weigh the main arguments on both sides, addressing specifically: (1) whether liberal eugenics can coherently distinguish "medical" traits from "life-plan" traits when both are genetically influenced and both shape welfare; (2) whether the plural-index problem undermines the idea of a uniquely "best" embryo and therefore collapses beneficence into mere parental autonomy; and (3) whether a regulatory regime that permits but does not subsidize genetic selection avoids the moral hazards of state eugenics while still leaving room for beneficence to operate.

Do not end on a non-committal note: defend a clear conclusion about whether parents have a moral obligation to select the embryo with the highest aggregate welfare potential, or whether they are morally permitted—perhaps even encouraged—to prioritize congruence with their own values even when that choice reduces the child's range of open-ended options.
