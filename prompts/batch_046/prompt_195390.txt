Question: Contrast Appiah’s honor-based explanation of moral revolutions with the Williams thesis regarding the decline of dueling in Britain and foot-binding in China. For each thinker, state whether moral argument can play any independent causal role, specify the primary mechanism each identifies, and apply both frameworks to the two cases.

Student answer:
Appiah argues that practices like dueling and foot-binding collapse when they become incompatible with the honor code of dominant groups. In Britain, dueling faded because private combat came to signal a failure of civic honor—aristocrats risked being seen as putting personal vanity above state loyalty. Foot-binding in China ended through a parallel mechanism: as China encountered Western powers, the practice became a symbol of backwardness that damaged national honor, and elite families abandoned it to preserve status in a modernizing world. In both cases moral arguments were causally relevant for Appiah, but only as levers that reshaped honor; they never persuaded individuals through reason alone.

The Williams thesis rejects honor as an independent force. Williams holds that normative change is driven by shifts in material interest and institutional power, with honor-based discourse serving mainly to legitimate outcomes that economic conditions have already fixed. Thus dueling disappeared because the British state monopolized legitimate violence and land-based aristocracy lost economic ground, while foot-binding ended because modernizing elites needed mobile labor and international respectability defined by economic rather than moral criteria. Honor is therefore not a mediator but an ideological afterimage. Consequently, both Appiah and Williams deny that pure moral reasoning can drive large-scale change, but they differ in that Appiah treats honor as a genuine explanatory mechanism whereas Williams treats it as an epiphenomenon of underlying interests.
