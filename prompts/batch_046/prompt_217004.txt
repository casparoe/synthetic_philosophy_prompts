In our philosophy of mind working group last week, two colleagues—let us call them M and K—fell into a sustained disagreement about whether framing effects in moral-intuition research undermine introspective reliability specifically at the level of phenomenal quality, not merely at the level of causal attribution. M opened by observing that subjects in presentation-order studies consistently report the same felt “directness” and “force” behind their judgments regardless of framing, yet their verdicts systematically flip; she took this to show that introspection fails even for first-order phenomenal access, because if the phenomenal character were genuinely stable, framing should not be able to alter the verdict without altering the reported experience. K pushed back by distinguishing access to phenomenal content from access to causal etiology: he granted that we routinely misattribute the sources of our intuitions, but insisted we can still reliably distinguish an experience of “moral wrongness” from one of “aesthetic displeasure,” just as we can distinguish seeing red from seeing green despite conceptual mediation. M then argued that K’s distinction collapses in practice because phenomenal concepts for intuition-quality lack the intersubjective calibration that anchors color concepts; without public correction, there is no stable standard against which to check first-order reports, so claiming “pure” phenomenal access is epistemically empty. K conceded the calibration asymmetry but countered that it only shows introspective reports about intuition-quality require normative calibration, not that they are globally unreliable; he accused M of illicitly generalizing from causal misattribution to phenomenal unreliability, and noted that if M were right, philosophers using first-person methods in aesthetics or epistemology would be in the same skeptical boat, which seems excessive. They went back and forth on whether intersubjective calibration is a necessary condition for first-order reliability or merely a useful heuristic, and on whether the color-perception analogy survives once calibration is removed, and neither would concede.

As a bystander with no prior stake in the view, I am trying to determine exactly where the argument stands. Could you adjudicate point by point: on whether the phenomenal-access / causal-attribution distinction holds up under framing-pressure; on whether the absence of intersubjective calibration for intuition-quality genuinely undermines first-order reliability or only second-order correction; on whether K’s color-analogy succeeds despite the calibration gap; and on whether M’s skeptical conclusion overreaches by treating all first-person philosophical method as equally threatened. For each point, say who has the stronger position and why, and identify what both parties are missing—whether a hidden empirical premise about trainability, a conceptual ambiguity in “reliability,” or a further distinction (for example, between token-level and type-level access) they have failed to draw. Please also state explicitly what argument or consideration would change your verdict: for instance, would empirical evidence that trained introspectors can correct framing-induced shifts in reported phenomenal quality resolve the dispute, or is the disagreement ultimately conceptual? Finally, flag the specific point in your reasoning where you are least confident and most likely to have gone wrong.
