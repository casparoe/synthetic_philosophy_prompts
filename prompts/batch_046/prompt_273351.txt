I'm drafting a short essay for a philosophy of science reading group on conceptual slippage across microdomains, taking the migration of 'filtering' as my central case. I argue that when a technical concept travels from signal processing into cognitive science and then into social epistemology, it does not merely change meaning; rather, it undergoes what I should like to call 'semantic aliasing' — borrowing the form of a concept without its original error model, so that the new usage inherits spurious precision. In signal processing, a low-pass filter is defined by a cutoff frequency and a quantifiable roll-off that specifies exactly how much noise remains; in Broadbent's attentional filter model, the language of selective permeability is retained but the quantifiable error bounds vanish, turning a mathematical operation into a metaphor for cognitive limitation. By the time the concept reaches discussions of 'filter bubbles' in social epistemology, the vocabulary of noise and signal is applied to politically weighted information flows, yet there is no sampling rate or stochastic error model underlying the metaphor — only an opaque algorithmic process. My tentative claim is that this is not simply vagueness but a specific kind of calibration drift: the metric that made 'filter' rigorous in its home domain is absent, yet the concept still carries the authority of technical precision, which then distorts the normative evaluation of information environments.

I should welcome your comments on several points. First, is the 'semantic aliasing' framing genuinely illuminating, or does it collapse into a familiar point about metaphorical extension? Second, have I misrepresented the transition from cognitive science to social epistemology — particularly in treating filter bubbles as lacking any underlying statistical structure rather than possessing one that is merely hidden from view? Third, does the essay require a sharper distinction between benign conceptual borrowing and the pathological kind I am attempting to isolate? Any suggestions for sharpening the argument, or for comparable microdomain examples that might strengthen the comparison, would be especially useful.
