In recent applied-ethics discussions, a refined analogy has replaced the older blueprint comparison: generalist moral reasoning is said to work like tuning every instrument to equal temperament with an electronic reference—portable, intersubjectively correctable, and uniform across contexts—while particularist moral reasoning is said to work like a master luthier who adjusts each string by listening to the unique harmonic resonance of that specific wood and the acoustic properties of the room in which it will be played, with no universal pitch table. Critics invoke this image to argue that particularism forfeits rational accountability: without the tuner, there is no shared criterion by which one listener can correct another's judgment, so moral disagreement collapses into mere difference in taste.
