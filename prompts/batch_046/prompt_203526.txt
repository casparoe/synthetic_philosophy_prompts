Write an essay examining whether Zahavi's handicap principle remains a coherent explanatory framework for human moral and political signalling, or whether its philosophical application collapses once we distinguish index signals from handicaps and account for culturally cheapened costs. I come to this as a retired evolutionary biologist who has recently turned to philosophy of science and ethics, and I am struck by how readily philosophers borrow the peacock's tail as an analogy for human altruism, religious ritual, or political activism—treating wasteful expenditure as an honest indicator of cooperative intent—without asking whether human culture has broken the cost structure that makes the analogy work.

Weigh the arguments with care. On one side, defenders maintain that even in modern societies genuinely costly commitments—sustained risk, irrecoverable time, or substantial resource expenditure—function as credible signals of underlying disposition, so that the logic of the handicap survives, albeit in a modified form. On the other side, critics argue that cultural evolution and digital mediation have decoupled cost from credibility: a signal that was once expensive can now be performed at negligible personal cost, turning what looks like a handicap into a low-cost index signal or a purely conventional badge. If that is right, then invoking Zahavi to explain human moral behaviour may commit a category error, conflating a physically wasteful display with a causally tied or conventionally assigned marker.

In your analysis, address specifically whether the distinction between an index (a signal honest because it is physically or causally tied to the trait signalled) and a handicap (a signal honest because it is damaging or wasteful to produce) undermines the standard philosophical analogy between animal ornamentation and human virtue. Consider whether human signalling is better modelled as an index, a culturally cheapened handicap, or something else entirely—perhaps a hybrid in which institutional scaffolding restores cost where individual physiology no longer can. Reach and justify a definite conclusion: either that the handicap principle can be rehabilitated for human moral behaviour with suitable modifications, or that it ought to be abandoned in favour of an index-based, culturally evolutionary, or institutionally grounded account.
