I have just concluded a lengthy and rather circular argument with a close friend about moral worth and the conditions under which an agent can genuinely be said to act for the right reasons. We found ourselves repeatedly invoking the standard Kantian and virtue-ethical frameworks, but both felt like rehearsed summaries rather than live philosophical problems. I want an exam instrument that compels a student to navigate a genuinely underexplored crux rather than reproduce familiar labels.

Please design one final-exam essay question for an upper-level moral philosophy seminar, together with a model answer and a detailed marking scheme. The question should concern moral worth and acting for the right reasons, but it must turn on a specific conceptual problem that does not reduce to the familiar duty-versus-inclination distinction or to standard accounts of moral motivation.

Specifically, construct the problem around cases of motivational overdetermination: an agent performs a morally correct action, and a correct moral reason is genuinely present and causally efficacious, yet an independent non-moral reason — for instance, a desire for social approval, a habit of deference, or an emotional reflex — is also present and, on its own, would have produced the identical behavior. Ask the student to determine whether moral worth requires that the correct reason be counterfactually necessary (the agent would not have acted in its absence) or whether mere causal presence suffices. Further ask whether demanding counterfactual necessity renders moral worth psychologically unrealistic, given the prevalence of habituated virtue, emotional contagion, and self-deception, and whether this result undermines any sharp distinction between acting from duty and acting from inclination.

The exam should be answerable in approximately 90 minutes, with the model answer running to about 700 words. Provide a marking scheme totaling 25 marks that distinguishes an A-level response from a B-level response by identifying specific required conceptual moves — for example, a clear distinction between causal presence and counterfactual robustness; a direct engagement with the redundancy objection; an evaluation of whether the counterfactual requirement is self-undermining; and an assessment of the implications for virtue ethics — together with a brief note on the characteristic failure mode for each component. Do not include any request for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and frame the task so that it can be completed without reference to obscure or highly specialized literature that a model without web access would not reliably recall. Write in a formal, polished register.
