I have been thinking, without formal academic training, about what people call the “collapse” of societies—the Bronze Age, the Maya, Easter Island—and I keep returning to a confusion I cannot settle alone. The populations did not disappear; they continued to live, cultivate, speak languages that evolved. Yet we call these moments collapses because monumental construction stopped, long-distance coordination dissolved, and the ecological footprint shifted. I want to understand whether this naming reflects a genuine failure of a collective project, or merely a transformation into something that no longer resembles what came before.

I run an informal workshop for about twelve adults with mixed backgrounds. We meet for ninety minutes seated in a circle, with only paper, cards, and handwritten score sheets—no technology, no required reading. Could you design a participatory exercise, with explicit rules, assigned roles, and a structured choice mechanism, that makes the participants feel the force of this ambiguity before anyone in the room names it? The exercise should distribute partial “evidence packets” drawn from the three cases—material remains, ecological indicators, fragments of narrative—among small subgroups who must jointly classify each case as either collapse or transformation according to a single clear criterion they agree upon in advance. Arrange the rules, the information asymmetry between subgroups, and the scoring so that applying the criterion consistently forces the group into judgments they find intuitively wrong—labeling a case they wish to call mere change as collapse, or vice versa—and so that they discover the breakdown through their own disagreement rather than through explanation.

Please include a debrief that maps specific moments in the exercise onto the conceptual distinctions: institutional continuity versus demographic survival, the role of collective self-narrative in defining a society’s boundaries, and whether an ecological regime shift should count as failure. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time—perhaps one interpretation dominating the discussion, or participants importing moral judgments about suffering into what should be descriptive classification—and how to manage it without lecturing. Be explicit about which feature of the real historical debate—most likely the centuries-long timescales or the absence of first-person testimony—the exercise cannot reproduce, and which I must supply through guided discussion afterward.

Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific scholarly works; I want the activity to run entirely on reasoning and the materials you describe.
