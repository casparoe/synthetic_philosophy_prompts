I am writing a novel about a remote island commune that funds an equal monthly living share for every adult resident through revenue from a collective wind farm, and in drafting the central conflict I have accidentally written myself into an ethical knot that demands a committed verdict rather than a survey of positions. The community's charter grants the share to all adults without conditions—no labor requirement, no income check—yet one character, a former shipwright who now spends his days painting, treats the payment as a simple entitlement of belonging, while another, a nurse working exhausting double shifts, feels cheated because the pool is not limitless and some residents could contribute labor but choose leisure instead. The tension sharpens when a woman moves to the island only after hearing of the dividend, prompting the nurse to argue that membership should require either a period of prior contribution or a long waiting period, while the painter insists that tying support to work reduces people to mere economic inputs and violates the charter's purpose.

I recognize that part of my unease may come from conflating fair treatment with identical treatment regardless of need or effort, but I cannot tell whether that intuition points to a real flaw in the arrangement or to my own prejudice. Please take a definite position on whether an unconditional, equal share can be morally sustained in such a closed system where leaving is difficult but not impossible, and say precisely what—if anything—should determine who receives it.
