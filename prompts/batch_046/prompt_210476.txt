You are a senior philosopher of practice specializing in existentialist ethics and institutional-transition counseling. Before constructing the deliverable, briefly brainstorm how Sartre’s distinction between facticity and transcendence applies specifically to people leaving long-term institutional care, and identify the particular mode of self-deception you will target—namely, treating accumulated “facticity debt” (institutional records, disrupted family ties, legal restrictions, and disrupted employment histories) as an unchangeable essence rather than as contingent conditions that radical freedom can transcend. Then write a reusable, step-by-step decision procedure for a caseworker in a nonprofit “life-design program” who must determine, within a single 45-minute documented session, whether a client’s refusal of a concrete opportunity—such as a job placement, a housing lease, or a family reunification plan—is an authentic exercise of radical freedom or an instance of bad faith. The caseworker has no clinical psychology training, must justify the classification to a supervisor, and operates under high stakes: labeling an authentic choice “bad faith” risks coercion, while labeling avoidance “authentic” can undermine release conditions.

Your procedure should list the ordered questions or tests in sequence, specifying for each what counts as a sufficient answer versus an inadequate one; it should yield one of three outputs—“authentic refusal,” “bad faith refusal,” or “indeterminate / escalate to senior reviewer”—with explicit triggering conditions for each. Include a “facticity audit” step that checks whether the client is treating contingent institutional constraints as absolute destiny, and mark precisely where the procedure terminates and irreducible human judgment must take over, especially where questions of value rather than fact remain. Apply the procedure to three worked cases: (a) a client refusing a warehouse job because the shift schedule conflicts with a self-initiated woodworking apprenticeship; (b) a client refusing family housing based on the belief that “people like me never get a second chance,” despite available evidence of successful reunifications; and (c) a client refusing vaccination-required employment out of a long-held, principled political commitment, where the procedure produces an answer the caseworker may personally find unwelcome. For each case, show the step-by-step reasoning and final classification. Conclude by stating exactly what the procedure cannot determine—for example, whether the refusal is wise, just, or conducive to flourishing—and explain why those limits are philosophically necessary given the nature of radical freedom. Write in formal, polished prose suitable for a staff training manual. Do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not include verbatim quotations from Sartre’s texts; paraphrase his concepts without quotation marks.
