Two colleagues in my department have been arguing about the philosophical structure of federal obligation in refugee and asylum policy, and they have asked me to referee their exchange. The first colleague opened by claiming that subsidiarity, properly understood, is not an administrative efficiency rule but a moral principle of democratic proximity: decisions about membership in a political community should be made at the lowest level capable of representing those affected. From this she inferred that states, provinces, or cantons ought to possess a substantive veto over resettlement allocations within their territories, since compelling a locality to accept refugees against the will of its residents violates the self-governing pluralism that federal arrangements exist to protect. She then pressed a symmetry argument: if EU member states invoke subsidiarity to resist centralized refugee quotas imposed from Brussels, they must grant their own subunits the same right of resistance downward; otherwise they are applying a double standard that reveals their appeal to federal principle is merely strategic. The second colleague replied that this misinterprets the logic of competence allocation. Subsidiarity, he said, assigns tasks to the level that can actually perform them, not automatically to the geographically closest authority. Because cross-border movement and international protection are inherently beyond local capacity, central or federal allocation is actually the arrangement that respects subsidiarity; a municipal veto over resettlement would be analogous to a city council opting out of a treaty obligation. He then invoked the German constitutional concept of Bundestreue, or federal loyalty, to argue that constituent units do not enter the federal compact as isolated sovereignties but as participants in a shared constitutional order that already includes collective obligations, so there is no democratic deficit in a federal resettlement scheme. The first speaker countered that this drains federalism of all moral content and reduces it to a pragmatic division of labor: if federal loyalty is only a functional bond, then there is no principled reason to preserve federal structures whenever centralization proves more efficient, and the entire justification for plural self-government collapses. She proposed instead that genuine Bundestreue runs horizontally among communities rather than vertically toward the center, which means solidarity must be voluntary to be authentic, and that coercing local acceptance in the name of solidarity destroys precisely the pluralism that federalism is meant to safeguard. The second speaker responded that this romanticizes federalism into incoherence. He noted that the United States routinely employs spending conditions and federal preemption to compel state compliance, and that no serious constitutional theory requires unanimous local consent for federal obligations to be legitimate. He also accused her of conflating two distinct questions: the procedural issue of who assigns refugees to particular jurisdictions, and the substantive moral issue of non-refoulement and protection. A locality might have legitimate democratic authority over integration policy, housing, education, and cultural adaptation, he granted, but not over whether vulnerable people are turned away at a national border or denied entry altogether. They have asked me to adjudicate each move in turn. Please go through their exchange point by point—the interpretation of subsidiarity as democratic proximity, the symmetry argument about EU and subnational levels, the foreign-trade analogy, the vertical reading of Bundestreue, the horizontal-voluntary reading of federal loyalty, the appeal to US federal coercion, and the distinction between assignment authority and integration authority versus exclusion—and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justifying each rating. Then indicate who is more right overall, and say what both parties are missing, particularly whether there is a principled line between legitimate local control over integration and illegitimate local exclusion, and whether the Swiss cantonal model offers a genuinely different constitutional logic from the German one rather than merely a different scale of the same problem. Please write entirely in continuous prose: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a formal letter.
