I'm writing this for my formal epistemology seminar, where we've been working through belief revision for conditionals. The case that keeps surfacing is the Judy Benjamin scenario: a soldier learns she is equally likely to be in Red Platoon or Blue Platoon, and then learns that if she is in Red Platoon she is in Radio Company and otherwise in Rifle Company. For decades, authors have disagreed about whether her credence in Radio Company should stay at one-half after receiving the conditional, with some insisting that Jeffrey conditioning forces the preservation of prior independence relations and others insisting that accepting the conditional as evidence requires an update that disrupts those relations.

My working hypothesis is that the stall isn't about which formal rule is correct, but about an unstated choice between two roles a conditional can play. On one side, the conditional functions as a structural constraint on the belief space—something that fixes the geometry of probabilities among outcomes and should be built into the framework before updating. On the other side, it functions as ordinary evidential input—something that reports a discovered correlation and must be incorporated like any other observation, even if it breaks prior independence relations. The two camps seem to be answering different questions under one name: one asks how to preserve coherence given a new logical relation, the other asks how a rational agent should respond to testimony asserting a correlation.
