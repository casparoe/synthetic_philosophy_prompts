I'm preparing a feature on the current state of the epiphenomenalism debate and need to identify which conceptual fronts deserve serious philosophical labor rather than mere repetition. The classic paradox of phenomenal judgment—if phenomenal experience lacks causal efficacy, then judgments about it seem impossible—is well-trodden ground. I want to compare three less-discussed lines: first, whether the paradox survives if we replace causal efficacy with structural correlation between phenomenal states and their neural representations; second, whether extending epiphenomenalism to artificial self-models reveals the paradox to be about self-representation rather than consciousness itself; and third, whether the debate should be reframed around "diagnostic exclusion"—the claim that phenomenal properties cannot enter the evidential basis of scientific theory-building even if they need not cause particular judgments. For a small research program of philosophers with roughly three years and no lab access, which of these should take priority? Rank them, defend the ordering by tractability, downstream dependency, and what evidence over the next few years would change the ranking, and indicate which you would drop first if the budget were cut.
