I'm working through Pascal's wager with a small reading group, and we've reached a stalemate that feels structurally familiar. On one side, participants treat the argument as a formal decision problem with an infinite payoff matrix: they debate whether expected-utility reasoning collapses when one outcome carries infinite value, whether dominance principles can substitute for maximization, and whether the "many-gods" objection is best handled by restricting priors over a partition of live theological options or by abandoning cardinal utility altogether. On the other side, participants reject the matrix framing as a category error: they insist that belief is not a voluntary action susceptible to betting-behavior, that the wager concerns practical commitment or hope rather than doxastic assent, and that formalizing the choice obscures the difference between judging a proposition likely and adopting a stance toward it for prudential reasons. For months the exchange has cycled through the same moves—counterexamples about religious conversion are dismissed as psychological anecdotes, while formal proofs about infinite-utility maximization are dismissed as irrelevant to the actual concept of belief—without either camp conceding ground. Is this stalemate a substantive disagreement about rational choice under total epistemic opacity, a verbal dispute about whether "belief" in the wager means assent, commitment, or betting-behavior, or are the two camps answering different questions—one about what a rational agent should do given ignorance, the other about whether prudential reasons can ever constitute epistemic justification—under a single heading? I would also like to know whether a hidden empirical claim, perhaps about the psychology of voluntary belief-formation or about the cognitive structure of religious commitment, is doing silent work in the disagreement. Finally, given whatever diagnosis you offer, what would actually count as progress: a conceptual distinction that separates the decision-theoretic and epistemological questions, a formal result about rational choice with unbounded payoffs, or an argument that the impasse is unavoidable because the underlying question is ill-posed?
