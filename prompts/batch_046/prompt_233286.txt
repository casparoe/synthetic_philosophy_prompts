Question: In Berkeley’s Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous, Philonous advances the argument now commonly called the master argument against Hylas’s belief in mind-independent material substance. Reconstruct the argument with precision: trace the move from the attempt to conceive an object existing without being perceived to the conclusion that such an object is strictly inconceivable, and clarify whether Berkeley presents this as a logical contradiction, as a fact about the content of conception, or as a metaphysical dependence claim. Then examine one standard reply—the reply that one can conceive of an object existing unperceived without including oneself as perceiver within the conceived scene—and explain whether Berkeley’s response to this reply succeeds.

Student answer: The master argument starts when Philonous asks Hylas to imagine a tree in a garden with no one around to perceive it. Every time Hylas tries, he notices that he himself is still conceiving the tree, so the tree is never really unperceived. From this Berkeley derives esse is percipi, which applies not just to sensible qualities but also to abstract ideas like triangularity or moral relations, since all ideas require a mind. The argument is best understood as revealing a logical contradiction: the notion of an unperceived object is self-contradictory, comparable to a round square. Hylas tries to block this by appealing to a negative idea—an idea that represents the absence of all perception—but Berkeley rejects it because absence is not a positive mental content. I think Berkeley’s counter succeeds for colors and sounds, which are plainly mind-dependent, but it fails for primary qualities like extension or shape and for abstract mathematical objects, since those can be grasped in purely geometric terms that do not invoke any particular perceiver. Therefore the master argument is convincing only for secondary qualities.
