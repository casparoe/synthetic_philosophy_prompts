A philosophy blogger summarized Aristotle's account of exchange and money for me in the following way: Aristotle held that money (nomisma) was invented purely as a conventional device to overcome the impracticality of direct barter, and because its value is entirely a matter of human agreement (nomos) it lacks any natural basis—this, the blogger claims, is exactly why Aristotle condemns usury (tokos), since charging interest treats an artificial token as though it were a naturally reproductive substance like seed or livestock. The blogger further claims that, for Aristotle, every just exchange must maintain strict equality of value, which is determined not by labor or scarcity but by the reciprocal need (chreia) between the parties; therefore, prices ought ideally to be fixed by communal agreement rather than left to fluctuate with supply and demand. On this reading, Aristotle's distinction between natural wealth-getting (oikonomia) and the unnatural art of chrematistike amounts to an early form of anti-commercial ideology, defending static, self-sufficient households against market expansion. The blogger concludes that Aristotle's biological teleology—according to which every thing has a natural limit and purpose—directly entails the rejection of modern banking and unlimited capital accumulation. How fair is this summary? Examine it claim by claim: which parts accurately reflect Aristotle's arguments in the Politics and the Nicomachean Ethics, where does the blogger conflate the conventional origin of money with the idea that its value is therefore arbitrary, to what extent does Aristotle actually oppose market-determined pricing, and does the final inference—that Aristotelian teleology necessarily condemns modern finance—follow from anything in his economic writings?
