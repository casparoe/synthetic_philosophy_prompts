I've been arguing with my friend about whether there's a stable moral line between society having to remember big historical evils and individual survivors being allowed to forget. The view I'm leaning into — call it mnemonic subsidiarity, maybe? — is that remembrance is primarily an institutional duty: museums, education, public commemoration carry the load so that individuals, especially those directly affected, don't have to bear an existantial tax of constant recall. The collective owes recognition to victims, but that debt is paid upward into institutions, not downward onto people. I think I got some of this vibe from Margalit on the ethics of memory and from Rieff's take on how modern culture handles trauma collectively, though I'm definatly not quoting either precisely.

What would actually defeat this? I don't want a literature review — I can't search well right now — but I want the arguments and observations that should move me, ranked by how fatal they'd be. For instance, if it turned out that institutional remembrance reliably amplifies intergenerational trauma rather than absorbing it, does that break the view or just show bad design? Or if collective moral agency is a fiction and groups are just aggregates, does the institutional duty collapse into a duty of every single person? I'm also worried the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: any time remembrance harms someone we blame implementation, not the principle. Is that insulation a virtue or a vice for a view like this? Give me what defenders of the split would themselves admit counts against them, plus any weird edge cases — like digital archives that make forgetting technically impossible — that might show the individual/institutional boundary doesn't hold up anymore.
