I am a doctoral student in philosophy of action and moral psychology. I want to work through a tension I think is under-examined in the free-will literature: Daniel Dennett’s well-known argument that the only free will worth wanting is an evolved, socially embedded capacity for reason-responsive self-control, not a metaphysically extravagant power to do otherwise. Recently, some theorists have pressed that this framing may illegitimately unify two quite different normative achievements—deliberative autonomy, understood as the capacity to revise one’s motivations in light of reasons, and narrative authorship, understood as the capacity to integrate one’s actions into a temporally extended, self-told life. The controversy I want you to address is whether these two capacities can come apart in ways that undermine Dennett’s unified account, or whether they are so functionally interdependent that the loss of one necessarily erodes the other, leaving us with only a diminished but still sufficient variety of agency.

Write an essay that weighs the case for treating deliberative autonomy and narrative authorship as separable conditions for morally significant free will against the case for treating them as mutually supporting aspects of a single, evolutionarily grounded capacity. Reach and justify a specific conclusion about whether Dennett’s framework should be interpreted as accommodating plural, dissociable varieties of free will, or whether it should be read as defending one integrated package. In your argument, address the practical stakes for assigning moral responsibility in cases where an agent might retain intact reasoning capacities but suffer severe disruption to narrative integration—say, through trauma or neurodegenerative change—without appealing to empirical clinical literature you cannot verify.

Please push back plainly if this framing rests on a confused premise: in particular, if the distinction between deliberative autonomy and narrative authorship is ill-drawn, if the task requires empirical claims about cognition or identity that cannot be adjudicated conceptually, or if the question as posed cannot be answered well without resources beyond conceptual reasoning. I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want the argument developed from philosophical reasoning alone. Be explicit about where your conclusion is firm and where it remains tentative, and flag any steps that depend on contested assumptions.
