I cover tech culture and science-adjacent stories for a general audience, and lately every profile of some reckless founder gets tagged by commenters as "classic costly signaling—handicap principle, Zahavi-style." I want a reusable decision procedure I can run in about ten minutes before filing, to check wether a behavior is actually an honest handicap signal or just expensive noise that looks like one. Ground it in the actual logic of the handicap principle—cost has to be differentially affordable, the signal has to be hard to fake, and the audience has to be able to verify the cost—but build in the distinction I keep running into: proxy costs, where a company, patron, or institution burns resources so the individual signaler doesn't personally bear the handicap. The checklist should run in order, say what counts as pass/fail/maybe, flag the exact step where I'm just doing armchair evolutionary psychology and have to stop, and be honest about what the procedure can't settle.

Then apply it to three quick cases: a CEO who publicly kills a profitable product line to prove a privacy commitment; a luxury watchmaker that deliberately engineers fragile movements; and a politician who takes a wildly unpopular stance that costs them donor support but also generates massive media coverage. One of these should come out as "probably not a true handicap" even though it smells like one. Make it practical, no citations needed, max 350 words.
