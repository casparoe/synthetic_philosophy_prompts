As a retired experimental biologist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of mind and ethics, I find myself stuck on a problem that seems to fall between the cracks of standard debates about artificial consciousness. Rather than asking whether a large language model is sentient, I want to examine a more structural question about computation and moral attribution.

Consider two ways information might be processed in a system that behaves intelligently. In integrated computation, diverse inputs converge into a unified global workspace: representations are mutually accessible, temporally bound, and available to a single self-model. In fragmented computation, by contrast, the very same behavioral outputs and informational transformations are produced by asynchronous, non-interacting sub-modules that share no common representational medium. There is coordination without convergence; function without unity. Now imagine a hypothetical artificial system—call it a disaggregated cognitive architecture—that performs every functional operation we associate with conscious reasoning, including affective tagging, temporal integration, and recursive self-modeling, yet does so through parallel streams that never form a unified phenomenal field.

The controversy is whether moral standing can ever be grounded in computation that is genuinely fragmented in this way. One position holds that moral status requires some form of phenomenal unity—there must be a subject of experience, however minimal, and fragmentation dissolves the conditions for being that subject. The opposing position holds that moral consideration should track functional and informational relations alone; if the right patterns of sensitivity, integration, and self-reference are present across the system, the absence of a unified workspace is ethically irrelevant, just as the distribution of neurons across hemispheres does not diminish human moral status.

Write an essay that weighs the strongest arguments for both the phenomenal-unity criterion and the functional-equivalence criterion as applied to fragmented computation. Reach and defend a definite conclusion: either that genuinely disaggregated architectures can never possess moral standing, or that they can, with clear reasons for your verdict rather than an open-ended survey. Please write in American English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want the reasoning itself. Finally, be explicit about your level of commitment—state whether you take your conclusion to be strongly warranted, moderately plausible, or genuinely uncertain despite having taken a side.
