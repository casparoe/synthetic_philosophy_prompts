I've been trying to work out whether the normative justification for armed neutrality—of the kind maintained by Switzerland, Sweden, and Finland—actually depends on a specific model of citizenship rather than on standard state-sovereignty arguments. The position I have in mind is this: neutrality is morally coherent as a democratic commitment only if defense is distributed through a universal or near-universal conscription system that binds citizens to the collective risk they collectively authorize; shift to a fully professional volunteer force and neutrality ceases to be an expression of popular sovereignty and becomes a technocratic policy choice made by the state on behalf of a passive citizenry. On this view, Switzerland's militia tradition, Sweden's concept of total defense linking civil and military sectors, and Finland's regionally distributed conscript reserve are not merely practical arrangements but constitutive of the claim that neutrality belongs to the people rather than to the government.

What would genuinely count against this position? I am not looking for the standard empirical objection that professional armies can defend borders efficiently—though that is part of it—but for the conceptual and normative defeaters. Which intuitions about citizenship and obligation does the view rely on, and which rival theories of democratic legitimacy—say, those that separate authorization from execution—does it implicitly rule out? Are there cases, perhaps in the Finnish hybrid model or in Sweden's post-2010 suspension and reactivation of conscription, where a state maintains democratic neutrality despite professionalization or partial professionalization? Would a defender of the conscription-neutrality link concede that a small, highly trained professional force undermines the normative foundation, or would they treat professionalization as merely an awkward deviation that can be compensated by stronger parliamentary oversight? I would like the potential defeaters ranked from fatal—those that show the link between conscription and democratic neutrality collapses—to merely awkward, such as institutional inconsistencies that the view can absorb through qualification. In particular, does the position risk conflating a contingent historical arrangement with a conceptual requirement, and if so, is that conflation repairable without abandoning the core claim?
