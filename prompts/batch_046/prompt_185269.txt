I am a clinician-researcher overseeing a single-patient expanded-access protocol for an experimental therapy, and I must nominate one recipient within the next fortnight. The inclusion criteria are broad enough that two people under my care technically qualify: my partner of eighteen years, whose condition is serious but not immediately life-threatening, and a younger patient with no personal connection to me whose clinical profile suggests a substantially higher probability of durable remission. The institutional framework directs selection by expected clinical benefit, calculated without regard to personal relationship. I understand the rationale—impartiality prevents corruption and maximizes aggregate outcome—but applying it to my partner requires me to treat our shared history, my commitments to her, and the particular obligations I have undertaken as though they carried no more weight than statistical noise. If I nominate the other patient, I will have acted on a principle that seems correct in the abstract, yet I feel it demands of me an extra layer of calculation: weighing her well-being against a stranger's on the same scale, as though the fact that she is mine should enter the ledger only as one factor among others. At the same time, if I nominate her, I am directing scarce institutional resources toward private ends, and I worry that what I experience as fidelity is in fact merely favoritism.

What I seek is not a legal ruling or a risk assessment, but a philosophical analysis of whether there is a defensible distinction between appropriate partiality and unjustified bias in a case like this. Is the discomfort I feel with the impartial calculus a sign that something is genuinely wrong with requiring it here, or is it a failure of moral discipline on my part? And does the answer shift once we recognize that my professional role imposes duties that my personal life does not?

Please advise.
