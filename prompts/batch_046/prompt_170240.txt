I serve on the National Agricultural Futures Council, a statutory body that advises regional legislatures on long-range resource planning. In 1998 a consortium of systems ecologists published the Brennan-Carver Carrying-Capacity Framework, which used soil-nutrient depletion curves and population-projection matrices to predict that by 2035 the Upper Mississippi Watershed would breach its sustainable caloric-production ceiling, triggering a cascading food-system collapse. The prediction failed: 2035 arrived without crisis, largely because of unanticipated adoption of precision-fermentation protein and perennial-grain substitution, which decoupled caloric yield from topsoil erosion faster than the model allowed. Now, in 2042, a coalition of watershed planners has petitioned the Council to formally rehabilitate the Brennan-Carver framework—not by denying the 2035 miss, but by adopting what they call a "delayed-falsification protocol." Under this protocol, a failed near-term prediction is treated as a temporary rescue event (an auxiliary adjustment) rather than a refutation, and the original ceiling dynamics are retained for forecasting on fifty-year horizons. The coalition wants the rehabilitated model adopted immediately to justify moratoria on new land-use expansion and to mandate nutrient-loop closure in existing agricultural zones.

Half the Council holds that honoring sunk predictive costs is irrational: a model that missed its central test should be abandoned, and adding rescue rules after the fact makes the framework unfalsifiable in practice. The other half argues that Malthusian dynamics are structurally correct but chronically "right but early," that short-run technological rescue is precisely what long-run theory expects, and that discarding the Brennan-Carver framework now—just as demographic transition effects may be reversing—would be the true error. Draft the Council's formal decision memo—approve adoption, reject adoption, or approve with binding conditions—with reasoning that the petitioners and the dissenting members could actually respond to. The memo should take seriously the philosophical problem of distinguishing a legitimately rescued long-run prediction from an ad hoc immunization strategy, address whether there is a principled boundary between "falsified and rescued" and "right but early," and include an honest, substantive treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, without asking for citations, bibliographies, or appeals to literature the Council could not verify from its own archives.
