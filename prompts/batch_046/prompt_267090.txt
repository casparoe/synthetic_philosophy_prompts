I have spent my career in physical chemistry and only recently, since retirement, have I begun to read philosophy systematically. I now sit on a university research-ethics committee that meets every two weeks to review protocols involving emerging technologies, and I find myself trying to apply Quine's picture of knowledge—his idea that moral judgments sit at the periphery of our web of belief and are revised holistically with empirical findings rather than in isolation—to our actual deliberations. I need a reusable decision procedure, in the form of an ordered checklist with explicit decision nodes, that a committee member without philosophical training can run in about ninety minutes to determine whether a new empirical study should prompt revision of an existing moral guideline (for example, our rule against deceptive recruitment in behavioral studies). The procedure should preserve Quine's core distinctions—between central and peripheral beliefs, between revising the target belief versus revising auxiliary assumptions, and between ad hoc adjustments and genuine theoretical improvement—while dropping anything that requires technical philosophy of science. It must flag every point where judgment is unavoidable and cannot be replaced by a rule. Include three worked applications: (1) a clear case where the empirical result forces revision of the moral guideline; (2) a case where the procedure recommends retaining the guideline despite strong countervailing data because the auxiliary revisions required would be too disruptive; and (3) a borderline case involving ambiguous statistical evidence and conflicting institutional commitments. In each case, show the step-by-step path through the checklist. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do—particularly regarding the weighting of scientific versus moral values—and note who must sign off on the judgment call (our chair, a philosopher of science, reviews all non-routine outcomes).
