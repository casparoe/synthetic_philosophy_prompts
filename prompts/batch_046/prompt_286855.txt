I'm taking a seminar on philosophy of history and the demarcation problem, and I've been developing a structural argument for distinguishing pseudohistory from legitimate revisionism. The position is this: theories such as Atlantis narratives, ancient-astronaut interventionism, and Fomenko-style chronological revisionism share a feature we might call chronological elasticity — they preserve a core timeline or causal claim (a lost advanced civilization, extraterrestrial engineering of monuments, a radically compressed medieval chronology) and, when confronted with radiometric, stratigraphic, or textual counterevidence, do not revise that core but instead expand an auxiliary narrative of suppression, misinterpretation, or phantom documentation. By contrast, legitimate revisionist history is supposed to adjust its central chronology in direct response to new primary sources. If this structural distinction is sound, it offers a demarcation criterion that does not depend on mere appeal to disciplinary authority.

What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? Could mainstream historiography itself be shown to exhibit the same elasticity — for instance, in accommodating anomalous artifacts or conflicting dynastic chronologies through auxiliary hypotheses about scribal error or sampling bias? Could a defender of Fomenko's framework or of ancient-astronaut theories demonstrate that their narrative expansions are methodologically parallel to accepted revisions, such as adjustments to Egyptian chronology following radiocarbon reassessments? Is there a decisive case — perhaps a pseudohistorical theory that genuinely abandoned its core claim in response to evidence — that would show chronological elasticity is not a reliable marker? I want an honest map of the argument's exposure: which failures of assumption would be decisive rather than merely awkward, what its defenders should concede counts against it, whether it risks collapsing legitimate revisionism into pseudohistory, and which escape routes are live versus merely logical possibilities.
