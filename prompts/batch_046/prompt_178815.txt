In several European education systems, students are streamed into vocational or academic tracks around age thirteen or fourteen, and policy documents sometimes cite behavioral-genetics heritability coefficients—specifically twin-study estimates for general cognitive ability—as part of the justification: if cognitive differences are substantially heritable, the argument runs, then early sorting sorts by stable, biologically grounded potential rather than by transient social advantage. What philosophical commitments does this institutional practice presuppose about what heritability means? Does it implicitly treat a population-level variance decomposition as an individual-level predictive property? Does it commit to a form of genetic essentialism about educational destiny, and if so, is that essentialism compatible with the fact that heritability is environment-relative—so that reducing environmental inequality can actually increase the heritability coefficient without changing any genetic influence? Reconstruct the implicit theory of merit, potential, and distributive justice that makes the practice coherent; distinguish what it strictly requires from what merely accompanies it; and say what structural changes the tracking system would need if we replaced that implicit theory with a view that treats heritability statistics as purely descriptive, with no normative force for individual placement, and that regards potential as irreducibly interactionist.
