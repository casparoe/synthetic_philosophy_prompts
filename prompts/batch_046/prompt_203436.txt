I have noticed a recurring evaluative pattern in discussions of extreme moral commitment, and I want to know whether philosophy or social psychology already has a precise name for it.

The pattern works like this. People will sincerely endorse the idea that society needs more individuals who devote themselves almost entirely to helping strangers — for example, those who live in extreme poverty to maximize charitable giving, or who take on dangerous and thankless work to serve distant populations. They rate such people as highly admirable, even as moral ideals. Yet when asked whether they would want such a person as a close friend, a neighbor, or a spouse for their child, the same respondents consistently say no. Crucially, they do not cite fear of harm or personal risk; they cite a kind of relational fatigue. They describe being near such a person as implicitly exhausting, as carrying a constant, unspoken moral demand that makes ordinary reciprocity feel inadequate.

What makes the pattern distinctive is that respondents do not treat these two judgments as contradictory. They do not say, "They are admirable, but I am too weak to be around them." Instead, they seem to treat extreme moral goodness as a kind of public good that should exist in the world but should be distributed at a distance — produced "out there," not localized in one's own social circle. They want the trait scaled up across society, but they want it scaled down in their immediate environment.

I am aware of discussions about whether maximally good people make poor companions because they lack ordinary personal projects or humor. But this seems different: it is not a judgment about the saint's personality, but about the respondent's own desire for proximity, and it operates even when the saint is otherwise warm and personable. It also differs from ordinary hypocrisy or from NIMBY-style resistance, because the respondents genuinely want the outcome (more moral extremism) — they just want it spatially separated from themselves.

Does this configuration have an established name? If an existing concept comes close, I want a committed argument for why it does or does not fit — not a survey of candidates, but a definite identification with clear boundary conditions separating it from the "moral saint as undesirable friend" problem, from simple admiration-at-a-distance, and from localized resistance to social change. If nothing established captures it, then coin a term, define it precisely, and defend its edges: what counts as an instance, what is a near-miss, and why the spatial or distributive framing is essential rather than incidental.
