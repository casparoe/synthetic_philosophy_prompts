I'm trying to defend a revised naturalism about health—basically that health isn't statistical normality across a species, but "systemic coherance" between an organism's funcitonal parts and its particular ecologial niche, including microbiome symbiosis. So being born with one kidney isn't unhealthy if that system coheres well with the environment, and vaccination restores coherance with pathogen pressure rather than returning to some baseline. I think this dodges Wakefield's harmful dysfunction objection and avoids the normativist trap of reducing health to whatever we happen to value.

But I want to know what would actually count against this, not just the usual "too relativistic" handwave. Which biological cases—maybe adaptive plasticity, or organisms thriving in ways that look dysfunctional—would break it? And what philosophical commitments about funciton am I smuggling in? If coherance is the criterion, is a human engineered for zero-G with bone loss "healthy" in orbit but sick on Earth—feature or bug? Rank the defeaters: fatal (collapses into normativism or pure relativism), awkward (has to bite bullets about disability or aging), and ones defenders concede vs dismiss. Also, does this risk being unfalsifiable—if every state can be framed as coherent with some niche? Is that insulation a problem for a biological concept, or just par for the course?
