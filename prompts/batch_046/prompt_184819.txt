I am a high school senior enrolled in an advanced independent-study seminar on philosophy of history, and my instructor has tasked me with drafting one of the final exam essays for our unit on contingency and chaos in historical explanation. Rather than asking students to recite the familiar proverb about the lost nail or to summarize chaos theory in generic terms, I want the question to force them to navigate a specific conceptual tension I have constructed for the course, which I am provisionally calling the clerical perturbation problem. The scenario involves a hypothetical misrecorded longitude of 2.3 degrees in an 1843 maritime logbook, which delays a naval supply fleet by forty-eight hours and thereby shifts the bargaining dynamics of a treaty negotiation that follows. The central philosophical issue is whether this kind of cascade demonstrates genuine historical chaos—non-linear sensitivity to initial conditions that escapes prediction—or whether it merely reveals hidden structural determinism, in which economic and geopolitical pressures made the treaty's outcome inevitable and the fleet's arrival was only an epiphenomenal trigger. Please draft a single exam essay question, appropriate for a ninety-minute response, that requires candidates to propose and defend a clear criterion for distinguishing true historical chaos from merely apparent contingency of this kind, using the logbook scenario as their central case study. In addition, provide a model answer of roughly six hundred words and a marking scheme organized around five must-hit analytical moves, with marks assigned to each so that the total equals twenty points; for each move, include a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that costs candidates marks there. The question must not request bibliographies, citations, or external reading lists, and it should be fully answerable through conceptual reasoning alone, without any need for web-based research.
