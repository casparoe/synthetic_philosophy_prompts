I have come to defend a qualified version of the claim that rational agents can coherently trust their own future reasoning despite Löb's theorem: specifically, that an agent can maintain a non-absolute, context-indexed confidence in its own future outputs without falling into the Löbian trap that requires unconditional belief in one's own infallibility. I hold that the obstacle applies only to binary, unqualified self-trust, and that a graded architecture—where confidence is calibrated to available computational resources at the time of evaluation and suspended in domains where the agent detects its own reasoning is resource-bounded—escapes the paradox. What would genuinely count against this position? I am not asking for a restatement of the standard fission or preface objections, but rather: which formal results in provability logic or modal fixed-point theory would undermine the claim that grading dissolves the obstacle? Which intuitive cases—say, an agent that correctly predicts its own error in one domain but then, by that very recognition, disables its trust mechanism in a way that prevents correction—would be decisive? And if empirical work on meta-reasoning in large language models or on bounded rationality in cognitive science showed that graded self-monitoring systematically collapses into either full trust or total skepticism under recursive pressure, would that be fatal, or could the view absorb it by refining the grading conditions? Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which ones a defender of graded self-trust should concede as genuinely damaging, and say whether the position risks being insulated to the point of emptiness.
