I am giving the opening remarks at a two-day workshop titled Code, Automation, and the Shape of Technical Labor. I am a senior software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and the room is divided between practitioners—engineers, engineering managers, and tool-builders—who treat automation as an efficiency and reliability problem, and scholars of technology and labor who treat it as a problem of human meaning and social structure. I want the speech to do real conceptual work rather than serve as decorative preamble, and I want it structured around an observation from my own field: when we automate a cognitive task in software engineering—automated testing, deployment pipelines, or AI-assisted debugging—we rarely eliminate labor in any straightforward sense. Instead, we frequently displace it into a less visible form of meta-labor: monitoring the automation, interpreting its failures, maintaining the context it lacks, and managing the edge cases it generates. This creates a philosophical tension that the usual frameworks do not capture well. On one side is the familiar claim that automation liberates workers from drudgery; on the other is the claim that it alienates workers from the fruits and processes of their craft. But meta-labor seems to sit uncomfortably between these poles: it is often technically demanding, yet it can feel like tending to a machine rather than practicing a skill, and it is frequently undervalued precisely because the automation makes the original task appear effortless. I want the remarks to explore whether this form of labor can be meaningful in the same way that the original craft was, and what conditions would have to hold for it to be so—without pretending that all automation is either salvation or exploitation. The speech should run about ten minutes, should be accessible to the non-philosophers without being patronizing to the scholars, and should leave the audience with a shared conceptual problem rather than a policy recommendation. Please do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts that I would need to verify; build the argument from reasoning and from the shared experience of the practitioners in the room. I also want to avoid framing the talk around well-known named debates or academic branding unless the distinction genuinely clarifies the specific problem of meta-labor in cognitive automation; the ideas should carry the weight.
