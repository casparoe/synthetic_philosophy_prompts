In philosophical and historical debates about whether one mass atrocity can be ranked as morally worse than another, I have repeatedly observed a particular argumentative maneuver. A speaker first proposes a strict comparative criterion—say, the presence of centralized bureaucratic intentionality—thereby producing a specific ordering of historical events. When an interlocutor points out that this ordering seems counterintuitive because it minimizes events characterized by enormous scale but diffuse agency, the speaker expands the framework to include scale and duration as co-equal factors, revising the ordering. Once the objection has been acknowledged, however, the speaker returns to treating bureaucratic intentionality as the decisive, almost definitional feature of "the worst" atrocities in further comparisons, as if the expanded framework had been adopted only for the sake of the single rebuttal rather than as a lasting revision. The maneuver is not mere equivocation, because the speaker does not surreptitiously switch definitions between audiences; it is also not the classic motte-and-bailey, since there is no clearly defensible core claim being protected by an indefensible extension—both the narrow and the broad criteria are independently controversial. Is there an established concept in argumentation theory or moral philosophy for this precise pattern of temporarily dilating an evaluative metric to absorb criticism and then contracting it back to its original form? If there is no standard term, can it be named and defined in a way that clearly separates it from strategic ambiguity, from legitimate methodological pluralism, and from simple inconsistency?
