Hey, quick question for anyone who thinks about work and automatation. I keep noticing a pattern when a job gets partly automated — like radiologists using AI for scans, or translators leaning on MT. The usual defense is that the machine handles the rote bits so the human can do 'higher-level' stuff: judgment, oversight, strategy. But then if you ask what makes that higher-level part meaningful, the criterias slide around. Before automatation people said the job was good because it needed real skill and direct impact; after automatation they say it's good because it involves authority, even though that authority is mostly rubber-stamping outputs from an opaque box mediatied by algorithms. And if you call that out, they shift again and say the meaning is in 'being part of the process,' which they never said was the point before. It's not just rationalization, becasue the same folks will still defend the fully manual version as meaningful too, just with totally different reasons. Is there an established name for this sliding-scale of meaningfulness that tracks automatation rather than the actual job? How does it differ from plain deskilling or Marxist alienation? And if there's no real term, how would you name and bound it? Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
