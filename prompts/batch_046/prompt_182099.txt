I was discussing Quine's indeterminacy thesis with a friend who studies philosophy of language, and we realized that most classroom quizzes just ask whether you "agree with Quine" without diagnosing what kind of agreement that is. I'm putting together a handout for an advanced philosophy-of-language seminar, and I need a decision-tree taxonomy—roughly six to eight leaves—that sorts people by their actual commitments on translation indeterminacy, not by which philosopher they claim to follow. The tree should begin with the shared premise that behavioral evidence alone cannot uniquely determine whether "gavagai" refers to rabbits, undetached rabbit parts, or temporal rabbit-stages, but then it needs to split on distinctions that genuinely come apart in the literature and in some underexplored combinations: does the indeterminacy stay confined to ontology, or does it bleed into logical constants, arithmetic, and modal language? Is the underdetermination metaphysical—there literally is no fact of the matter—or epistemic, or perhaps a pragmatic recommendation to stop asking? And if one accepts it globally, does that push you toward Quinean ontological relativity, toward a Davidson-style rescue via charity and radical interpretation, toward a mentalist rejection along Chomskyan lines, or toward a skeptical-paradox reading in the style of Kripke's Wittgenstein? Each branch should be a concrete question a student could answer without already knowing the label it leads to—no asking "are you a Davidsonian?"—and each leaf should carry the name of the position (or "unnamed / unexplored") plus a single sentence on its most awkward commitment. I also want you to map out the logical space first: note any unoccupied combinations—say, a view that accepts global indeterminacy for empirical language but insists on determinacy for pure mathematics—and briefly diagnose why nobody occupies them (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?). Please phrase every node as a claim or case the respondent can evaluate on its own, flag any pair of leaves the tree genuinely cannot separate, and include an epistemic note stating how confident you are in the branching logic and identifying the specific split you are least certain about.
