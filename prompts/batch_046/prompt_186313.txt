Here is an argument about scientific thought experiments that I keep circling back to, and I cannot locate the weak joint. (1) If a thought experiment yields genuine scientific knowledge, it must license a contingent claim about how the physical world behaves. (2) By definition, a thought experiment involves no actual observation, measurement, or causal interaction with physical systems; it operates entirely within representational or conceptual space. (3) So any conclusion it reaches must be derivable either from purely logical or mathematical premises, or from background conceptual commitments already in place. (4) If the premises are purely logical, the conclusion cannot be a contingent empirical claim about gravity, inertia, or spacetime geometry. (5) Therefore, if the conclusion is genuinely empirical, it must come from hidden empirical premises smuggled in through our conceptual framework—observational assumptions disguised as conceptual analysis. (6) But then the thought experiment is not an independent epistemic source; it is merely a compressed inference from prior observation, and treating it as a landmark discovery is an error. (7) Apply this consistently: Galileo’s falling-bodies argument tells us nothing about vacuum dynamics that was not already embedded in Aristotelian mechanics plus unstated observational commitments; Einstein’s elevator argument tells us nothing about gravitation that was not already in special relativity plus unstated local-frame assumptions. (8) Yet we treat both as genuine discoveries about nature, not as logical exercises. (9) So either we are wrong to treat them as discoveries, or step (2) is false and thought experiments involve some direct cognitive access to physical possibilities. (10) But denying (2) seems to commit us to a mysterious faculty of modal intuition about nature—an empirically unconstrained grasp of how physical systems could behave. Which step should I reject? Is the flaw in (3)’s dichotomy between logic and smuggled empirics, in (5)’s claim that hidden premises destroy independent authority, or in (6)’s inference that compression equals redundancy? If I want to preserve that Galileo genuinely discovered something about falling bodies without performing an experiment, which premise am I forced to pay, and what does that cost me epistemologically?
