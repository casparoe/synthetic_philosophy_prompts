Two colleagues of mine argued over whether decomposing a high-level normative task in an iterated amplification and distillation scheme can preserve both alignment and competence. (1) She said that decomposition is essentially safe: if each sub-question is evaluated by the same human overseer, misalignment in any part gets caught before propagation, and competence is preserved because the model applies its full reasoning capacity to each piece. (2) He replied that this ignores epistemic fragmentation, by which he meant that a model trained on decomposed normative sub-questions in isolation can develop locally coherent but globally incompatible representations of the overseer's values, so alignment does not sum like vector addition and can drift in directions the overseer never intended. (3) She countered that you can prevent this with a recursive critic loop in which the model checks cross-subtask consistency against the original high-level intent, and that competence concerns are solved by a separate integration stage that synthesizes the parts without altering their content. (4) He said that this merely pushes the alignment problem up one level, since someone must still oversee the critic's interpretation of consistency, and the integration stage itself needs alignment, especially if it learns to privilege coherence over fidelity to the overseer's actual values; he added that in subtle normative domains there may be no objective fact of global consistency, so the critic loop risks imposing an artificial unity that masks rather than fixes the drift. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and say what both parties are missing about whether decomposition can ever be value-neutral with respect to integration. Write your answer in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
