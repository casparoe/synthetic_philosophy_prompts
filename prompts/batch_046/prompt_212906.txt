I'm working through the philosophy of pain literature and keep encountering a configuration that seems to sit between well-known categories. Consider a subject with fully intact nociception: tissue damage produces the appropriate neural signals, reflexive withdrawal occurs automatically, and the subject can correctly identify the sensation as a form of bodily damage requiring avoidance. Yet the experience carries no negative affect whatsoever—there is no "hurting" quality, no suffering, no emotional aversion. More specifically, the subject also lacks any motivational urgency: they do not seek relief, do not guard the affected area beyond the reflex, and can remain in the damaging state indefinitely without distress or desire to change it. This is not congenital analgesia, since they detect and identify the damage; it is not standard pain asymbolia, since they do not deny that the sensation is painful or unpleasant, but rather report that it lacks both the affective sting and the conative drive usually taken to be inseparable from it. I am interested in whether there is an established clinical or philosophical term for this precise dissociation—intact sensory-discriminative and cognitive-evaluative dimensions paired with absent affective and motivational dimensions—or whether the literature treats this as merely a variant of asymbolia. If no precise term exists, I would like a proposed name together with clear boundary conditions distinguishing it from asymbolia, from congenital analgesia, and from ordinary pain with suppressed expression.
