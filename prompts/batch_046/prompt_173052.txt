In comparative free-speech jurisprudence, American defenders of broad protection often invoke a marketplace of ideas, treating harmful speech as a defective product that is outcompeted rather than banned, while European-style advocates analogize hate speech to toxic contamination in a shared water supply—cumulative, unavoidable, and best filtered at the source. Audit both analogies systematically: for each, provide the explicit correspondence table (consumer, producer, defect, contamination threshold, filtration mechanism, regulator); identify the precise points where the mapping breaks (for instance, speech is non-rivalrous, harm is rarely dose-dependent in the same way, the marketplace lacks price signals, and contamination models tend to assume homogeneous vulnerability); state which normative premises about individual autonomy, collective goods, state competence, and the excludability of harm each analogy quietly imports; and determine whether either can actually license its intended legal conclusion—strict content neutrality versus proactive state or platform intervention—or whether both collapse into rhetorical framing once the disanalogies are made explicit. In particular, test both analogies against the distinction between interactional harm, which is direct and targeted like an assault, and aggregative harm, which is diffuse and statistical like air pollution: does either analogy genuinely accommodate both modes, or does each silently privilege one? If both analogies fail to carry the normative weight they are asked to bear, propose a more precise structural comparison—perhaps drawing on the economics of public goods with non-excludable costs—that captures the real disagreement about institutional competence and harm without importing contested empirical premises about how audiences actually process speech.
