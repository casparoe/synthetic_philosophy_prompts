I'm delivering the opening remarks at the first session of a workshop on artificial cognition. The audience is split evenly between philosophers of mind and machine-learning researchers, and the framing must do real structural work rather than serve as decoration. The workshop's precise focus is whether Daniel Dennett's intentional stance—specifically, the attribution of counterfactual beliefs about what a large language model would output had its training distribution included different evidence—can be applied coherently to these systems. The philosophers present tend to hold that without causal integration within token-generation there is only predictive pattern-matching and no genuine belief; the researchers tend to counter that mechanistic interpretability reveals context-dependent internal representations supporting the relevant counterfactuals, even when behaviorally invisible. I want a five-minute address that navigates this tension without alienating either half: it should explain why adopting the stance might be valuable for system evaluation without committing to the metaphysical claim that the model literally believes anything, and it should identify one precise point where the two sides talk past each other—likely whether a cognitive explanation must cite subpersonal mechanisms or can remain at the personal level—without resolving the dispute by fiat. The remarks should conclude by posing the workshop's central question in terms that make both sides see their own core concern reflected in it, avoiding both technical obscurity for the philosophers and philosophical naivety for the engineers.
