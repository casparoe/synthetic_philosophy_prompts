I'm currently enrolled in an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, and we're examining competing accounts of emotional ontology—specifically, whether emotions are best characterized as evaluative judgments, raw bodily feelings, or direct perceptions of value. To move beyond the usual taxonomy, our instructor proposed a more granular hybrid: the "calibrated perceptual judgment" model, according to which an emotion is an affective state that combines the normative structure of a judgment with the immediacy and world-directedness of perception, but which malfunctions when either its cognitive appraisal or its somatic calibration is disrupted. Write an essay that critically examines this hybrid against the three standard positions. You should weigh whether it genuinely resolves the classic objections to pure judgment theories—such as the problem of recalcitrant emotions that persist despite contrary beliefs—and to pure feeling theories—such as the difficulty of accounting for emotional intentionality without smuggling in cognitive content—or whether it merely inherits both sets of difficulties. Reach and defend a specific conclusion about whether emotions are fundamentally judgments, perceptions, feelings, or this kind of hybrid. Don't conclude with indecision; argue for a verdict and justify it with reasons that address both the conceptual coherence of the hybrid and its explanatory advantages or failures relative to the purer alternatives.
