Consider the certification practices of a municipal occupational-health board that administers a statute granting workplace accommodations only for conditions involving "substantial functional impairment" relative to a defined reference population. In its published guidelines, the board states that a condition qualifies only when it reflects a "significant deviation from species-typical functioning." Yet in adjudication, examiners consistently approve accommodations for applicants with rare congenital sensory deficits—conditions that are statistically abnormal across the general human population—even when the measurable loss of capacity is mild. Conversely, they routinely deny applications from warehouse workers with severe repetitive-load spinal degradation or from orchestral musicians with chronic acoustic trauma, classifying these as "normalized occupational wear" rather than "medical dysfunction," despite the fact that the physiological damage and the objective functional limitation are often more severe than in the approved congenital cases.

What implicit theory of health and disease renders this pattern philosophically coherent? Does the board's reasoning presuppose a strictly naturalist framework, according to which disease status depends solely on statistical deviation from universal human biological design, with social context being irrelevant? Or does it instead smuggle in a normativist commitment, treating impairment as a medical disorder only when it violates norms that apply across all social roles, while accepting identical damage as non-pathological when it aligns with the expected risks of a particular vocation? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say whether several rival accounts—pure biostatistical naturalism, a harm-based hybrid, or a fully contextual normativism—could license the same outcomes. Finally, indicate what the certification criteria would have to change if the board adopted a consistently normativist stance that evaluated every substantial functional limitation as health-related regardless of its statistical distribution within an occupational reference class.
