Design a 90-minute seminar activity for about 14 upper-level philosophy students (no required prior reading) that makes participants feel the structural tension shared by the lottery paradox and the preface paradox—specifically, the pull of rationally accepting each member of a large set of high-confidence judgments while also being compelled, by statistical aggregation or authorial fallibility, to reject their conjunction. Students should not hear the names “lottery paradox” or “preface paradox” until the final debrief.

Frame the session as an “Epistemic Audit Committee” simulation. Divide students into four groups of three or four. Give each group a packet of 25 “Archive Verification Cards,” each describing a specific empirical claim about a fictional 19th-century meteorological dataset (e.g., “Station 14 recorded 42.3 inches of rainfall in June 1887”) with a stipulated reliability of 99 percent. The rules must include an explicit “Closure Constraint”: if a group marks every individual card as “Rationally Accepted,” they are required by the rules of rational audit to also sign a “Master Verification Statement” asserting that all 25 claims are true.

Structure the session in four phases with a visible timeline (suggest 10 / 25 / 20 / 20 / 15 minutes):

1. Individual audit: groups review cards and vote Accept / Reject / Withhold using a consistent confidence threshold (e.g., accept only if confidence exceeds 98 percent). They should accept nearly all.
2. Aggregation: groups apply the Closure Constraint and vote on whether to sign the Master Verification Statement.
3. Fallibility disclosure: each student writes a one-sentence “Preface Acknowledgment” admitting that in any large body of expert judgments, at least one error likely remains; groups then vote on whether to include this acknowledgment in their final report.
4. Collision and negotiation: groups assemble a final report containing the 25 accepted cards, the Master Statement (if signed), and the Acknowledgment (if included). They discover that signing both the Master Statement and including the Acknowledgment produces a direct contradiction. They have 15 minutes to negotiate which element to drop, with “publication points” at stake (a simple scoring rubric: 10 points for consistency, 5 for honesty, 5 for completeness).

The rules, not the facilitator, should generate the contradiction. The facilitator should not lecture on closure or fallibilism until the debrief.

In the debrief (15 minutes), map each phase onto the corresponding philosophical structure: Phase 1 onto the lottery-style accumulation of individually rational beliefs; Phase 2 onto the closure principle that generates the paradox; Phase 3 onto the preface-style admission of fallibility; Phase 4 onto the contradiction itself. Then distinguish the probabilistic aggregation version (lottery) from the authorial fallibility version (preface), showing where the fictional audit scenario approximates each and where it diverges.

Also include:
- A specific prediction of what will go wrong the first time this is run (e.g., groups resolving the contradiction by redefining “accept” ad hoc, or social pressure overriding philosophical reasoning).
- An honest note on which feature of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply (e.g., the precise mathematical expectation of error in the lottery case, or the distinction between belief and acceptance if students conflate them).
- Materials list (cards, voting slips, point sheets) and a brief explanation of the payoff scheme.

Keep the prompt formal, polished, and self-contained. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke obscure theories or named authors that a model might not reliably recall; refer to the paradoxes by their common names only in the debrief instruction.
