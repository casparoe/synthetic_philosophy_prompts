Draft the joint statement that a modernist constructivist and a perennialist ethno-symbolist should have produced after an adversarial collaboration on the age and nature of nations. The modernist camp holds that nations are essentially modern artifacts—shaped by print-capitalism, bureaucratic standardization, mass education, and elite invention of shared symbols—while the perennialist camp holds that modern nations draw upon deep, pre-modern ethnic substrates of shared myth, memory, and cultural practice that provide continuity across institutional breaks.

Structure the document in four parts. First, present a numbered list of claims both sides can sign: for example, that state institutions play a causal role in national consciousness; that some modern states clearly lack older cultural substrates; that national identity varies in intensity across populations and time; and that modern communications technologies have transformed how collective identities are maintained. Second, present a table or marked list of the precise residual disagreements, phrased in wording that each camp accepts for its own position—distinguishing, for instance, whether the modernist denies any meaningful pre-modern continuity or merely denies that such continuity is sufficient for nationhood, and whether the perennialist claims deep continuity for all nations or only for a subset. Third, identify the cruxes: sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. One crux should concern whether collective identity requires continuous institutional embodiment—analogous to a legal corporation sole—or can persist through dispersed cultural memory alone without institutional continuity; another should concern whether mass literacy is a necessary condition for national consciousness or merely one vehicle among others. For each crux, specify a concrete comparative case both sides agree is relevant—such as the institutional continuity claimed by the medieval University of Bologna versus the post-colonial discontinuity of state boundaries in parts of sub-Saharan Africa—and record in advance what each camp predicts about that case. Fourth, state explicitly whether any portion of the disagreement is verbal—say, over whether "nation" should be defined by subjective identification, objective cultural traits, or state membership—and explain exactly which part and why. Begin the response with the document itself, with no preface or commentary.
