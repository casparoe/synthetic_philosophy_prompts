I'm currently taking an advanced undergraduate seminar on science and religion in early modern Europe, and for my final assignment I'm supposed to draft a one-page research proposal for a project I could realistically pursue over a single semester, yielding roughly 5,000 to 6,000 words. I want to avoid both the well-worn question of whether Galileo was sincerely religious or covertly secularist and the standard summary of his metaphor comparing the book of nature with the book of scripture. Instead, I'm interested in a more precise hermeneutic problem that I think the existing literature leaves genuinely unsettled.

Here is the framing I have in mind, though I need help sharpening it into a tractable proposal with a clear deliverable. Galileo insists that when a conclusion about the natural world is rigorously demonstrated, scriptural passages that appear to conflict with it must be reinterpreted rather than taken literally. Most discussions treat this as a one-way corrective rule: demonstrated science revises probable exegesis. But Galileo also distinguishes, especially in his astronomical writings, between knowledge that is genuinely demonstrated and knowledge that remains merely probable or hypothetical. My proposed question is whether he applies this same epistemic gradation symmetrically to biblical interpretation. Specifically, does Galileo ever allow that a rigorously demonstrated theological or exegetical conclusion could oblige scientists to revise a merely probable astronomical hypothesis? Or does he implicitly classify all scriptural interpretation as permanently residing on the probable side of the divide, thereby presenting his hermeneutic principle as balanced while actually stacking it in favor of science?

The proposal should state this question with enough precision that it has a determinate answer, explain why both apologetic and secularist readings fail to resolve it, outline an approach that works primarily with Galileo's own texts—particularly his well-known letter to the Grand Duchess Christina and the relevant methodological sections of the Dialogue—rather than with secondary commentary alone, specify what a positive result, a negative result, and a null result would each look like, and include an honest assessment of realistic scope for one semester: what can actually be settled, and where the project risks ballooning into an unmanageable study of Counter-Reformation hermeneutics more broadly. I will supply specific citations and textual references myself; I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or literature review. Could you write this proposal?
