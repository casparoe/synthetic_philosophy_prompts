I have been thinking, without any formal training in logic or political philosophy, about a tension I notice whenever groups try to reach a collective decision by breaking it into smaller questions. I have sketched some notes for a short essay and would welcome careful comments on whether my reasoning is sound, whether my framing introduces hidden errors, and whether my concrete example genuinely illustrates the conceptual problem rather than muddying it.

The core of my argument is this. A group facing a complex judgment can aggregate in at least two ways: they can vote directly on the final conclusion, or they can vote separately on the premises that are meant to determine that conclusion and then derive the result mechanically. I have observed that these two paths can diverge sharply, even when every individual voter is internally consistent. In my notes I call this divergence a "structural inversion": the group may end up endorsing a conclusion that no individual actually holds, or rejecting one that a clear majority favors, solely because the aggregation was performed premise by premise.

To ground this, I imagine a three-person neighborhood committee deciding whether to build a communal greenhouse. Each member judges three premises—whether the soil is adequate, whether the local climate is suitable, and whether enough volunteer labor exists—and they have agreed in advance that the greenhouse should be approved only if all three premises are satisfied. If the committee votes on each premise separately and applies the rule automatically, it is possible for two premises to win a majority and one to lose, yielding a negative collective verdict. Yet if those same three people had been asked directly, "Should we build the greenhouse?" a majority might well have answered yes, because they weigh the premises differently or are willing to compromise on one condition. I argue that premise-based aggregation introduces what I call a "hidden veto": a minority position on any single premise can block a majority preference for the overall outcome, in a way that direct voting on the conclusion would not permit.

My specific questions are these. First, is the logical tension I describe actually present in collective reasoning, or am I illegitimately conflating logical consistency with democratic legitimacy? Second, does labeling the phenomenon a "hidden veto" unfairly prejudice the case—might premise-based voting be a deliberate feature rather than a bug, designed precisely to protect minority concerns against majoritarian overreach? Third, have I corrupted my own example by smuggling in practical considerations—such as individual willingness to compromise or to reinterpret premises—that do not belong to the pure logical structure I am trying to isolate?

I am not asking for bibliographies, citations, or a list of texts to read; I only want to know whether the idea, as I have constructed it, holds up under scrutiny, and where exactly it is most likely to collapse.
