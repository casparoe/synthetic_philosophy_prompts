I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I've been trying to work out my own position on rational decision-making, particularly around project abandonment and long-term technical commitments. Before I present these thoughts to a discussion group, I'd like a consistency check. Here is roughly where I've landed:

(1) In engineering management, continuing with a failing architecture solely because we've already invested months of development is irrational; only future maintainability, performance, and team fit should matter.
(2) I take rationality to be synchronically evaluated: whether a decision is rational depends only on the present state of affairs and future prospects, not on the path taken to arrive here.
(3) At the same time, I think that in collaborative technical environments, visible sunk investment in a shared codebase can serve as a credible commitment signal to other teams; from a system-level perspective, honouring that investment can therefore be instrumentally rational, even when the local project evaluation is negative.
(4) I also believe there's a sharp distinction between "costs that are genuinely sunk" (unrecoverable past expenditures) and "present capabilities that are path-dependent" (e.g., a team now specialised in a deprecated stack); the latter are real present constraints, not fallacies.
(5) Finally, I think that if honouring sunk costs is always and everywhere irrational, then practices like maintaining backward compatibility, supporting deprecated APIs, or honouring long-term service contracts become inexplicable, yet these seem clearly rational institutional behaviours.

Do these commitments form a coherent package, or am I helping myself to incompatible motivations? Please audit them: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each tension, say what the least costly revision would be and what it sacrifices. I am not signalling which commitment I would prefer to drop—please treat all five as held with equal conviction unless logic forces otherwise. I'd like the answer in British English, and please avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citation lists; I'm interested in the conceptual audit, not a literature survey.
