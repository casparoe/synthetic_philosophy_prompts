I have been working through the Pasadena paradox, in which the expected payoff is undefined because the outcome series oscillates without converging to infinity, zero, or any finite limit, rather than diverging uniformly like the St. Petersburg case. Because the expectation is literally absent—not merely infinite—it seems to me that the standard framework for comparing a gamble to a certain entry fee collapses entirely: there is no long-run average value to serve as the decision-relevant summary of the game. To address this, I have sketched a parity-conditional decision rule that partitions the outcome space according to whether the decisive coin flip occurs on an even or odd round, computes separate expectation-like aggregates within each branch, and then applies a lexicographic risk ordering between the two partitions. My question is whether this repair genuinely resolves the paradox, or whether it rests on a false presupposition that conditioning on flip-parity restores convergence within each branch, which may fail just as severely as in the unconditioned case. More broadly, if an expectation is undefined in this structured, oscillating way, should it be treated as a categorical reason to reject the gamble regardless of outcome partitioning, or is there a defensible framework for rational choice under undefined expectation that does not collapse into arbitrary truncation?
