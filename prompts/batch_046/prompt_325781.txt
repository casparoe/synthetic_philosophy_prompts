I want to push past that framing and examine whether the counterexample tacitly depends on two background commitments: an absolutist ontology of space, in which spatial points are individuated independently of their occupants, and a classical ontology of material objects, in which spheres are discrete individuals with well-defined trajectories.

Specifically, suppose we adopt a relationalist view of space—so that "being at point L" is not an intrinsic or even a genuinely relational property of a sphere unless L is itself individuated by other material structures, which are absent in the homogeneous universe. Under relationalism, the spheres cannot be differentiated by location. Now add a further, somewhat more speculative move: instead of treating the spheres as classical point-particles or extended bodies with primitive thisness (haecceities), we treat them as localized excitations—analogous to solitons—in a continuous fundamental field, where individuation might depend on topological charge or asymptotic boundary conditions. In Black's perfectly symmetric, infinite, homogeneous space, however, those field-theoretic markers also vanish: there is no boundary at infinity that distinguishes one excitation from the other, and no external reference frame to break the symmetry.

So the conceptual issue I want you to explain is this: does Black's counterexample survive only if we presuppose both spatial absolutism and a non-field ontology of individuals? If we reject both—adopting relationalism about space and a field-theoretic ontology of matter—does the scenario collapse into a description of a single excitation (or of no determinately individuated entities at all), thereby dissolving the counterexample? Or does the problem merely relocate: must the field itself possess primitive structural features or gauge-invariant labels that do the individuating work, and if so, have we simply traded sphere-haecceities for field-haecceities?

In your answer, please separate what is settled—such as the standard weak versus strong PII distinction and the basic structure of Black's thought experiment—from what is genuinely contested among metaphysicians who take these issues seriously, particularly regarding whether relationalism can consistently accommodate numerically distinct but qualitatively identical material objects without appealing to primitive thisness. Do not present any contested position—for example, the claim that relationalism straightforwardly dissolves Black's problem, or that field ontology eliminates the need for individuation altogether—as if it were the consensus view.
