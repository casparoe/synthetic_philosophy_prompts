I'm defending a dissertation chapter—working title "Phase Transitions Without Collapse: Distributed Metamorphosis in the Late Bronze Age, Classic Maya Lowlands, and Rapa Nui"—in an interdisciplinary oral exam. My core thesis rejects the standard "collapse" framework, which treats rapid depopulation plus political fragmentation as evidence of societal failure measured from a state-centric archive. Instead I argue that all three cases represent a "managed contraction" or "distributed metamorphosis": in the Late Bronze Age (c. 1177 BCE), the dissolution of palace economies relocated scribal and metallurgical knowledge to decentralized maritime and highland nodes rather than destroying it; in the Classic Maya lowlands (c. 800–900 CE), the cessation of monument-building coincided with sustained population and ritual continuity in the northern plains and highlands, indicating demographic rebalancing rather than civilizational death; and on Rapa Nui, the deforestation and end of moai production reflect a deliberate shift from competitive monumentality to a kin-based ritual economy centered on the Birdman cult, not an ecological suicide driven by overpopulation.

The examiners are precisely these five: Dr. Aris Thorne (processual archaeologist, systems-theory collapse models, deeply skeptical of post-processual narratives that dissolve causal mechanisms); Dr. Beatrice Voss (environmental historian, emphasizes abrupt climate forcing, particularly the 4.2 kiloyear event and Late Bronze Age drought sequences); Dr. Cyril Mendez (post-processualist, rejects universal stage models but is suspicious that my "managed" language smuggles teleology and agency back into structural change); Dr. Diana Okonkwo (economic historian of Bronze Age Mediterranean networks, intimately familiar with the Uluburun and Amarna corpora and with palace-to-port economic transitions); and Dr. Elias Reed (anthropological demographer, studies pre-industrial population dynamics, methodologically cautious but theoretically neutral on collapse).

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. Order the objections by the combined likelihood that each examiner will actually raise them in this room and the damage a poor answer would do to my argument. Finally, identify the single objection I am hoping nobody asks—the one that strikes at the comparative architecture of my thesis rather than at any individual case—and draft both that objection (phrased as Dr. Thorne or Dr. Okonkwo would put it) and a damage-control reply that admits the methodological limitation without letting it collapse the entire argument.
