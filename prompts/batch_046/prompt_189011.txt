I was talking with my friends over dinner — they work in city planning and deal with zoning borders constantly, so thier default is that philosophy is just word games about nothing real. I want to run one session for them on vagueness and the sorites paradox, but I don't want the tired heap-of-sand version. Instead I want to build the arc around zoning transitions: imagine one block that starts legally 100% residential, then you change one building at a time to mixed-use, then eventually it's 100% commercial. At some point it's "mostly residential" but there was never an official line drawn. I need the teaching plan, not the lecture itself: what concept has to land first before the paradox can even feel like a problem rather than a legal mistake, the exact carrying example and what properties it must have and lack, and the dependency order so the pieces don't collapse into each other.

The audience will bring misconceptions in a pretty predictable sequence, I think: first they'll insist there's just a hidden municipal cutoff we haven't looked up, then they'll say it's only language being sloppy, then maybe they'll float that a sharp boundary exists but human cognition is too weak to detect it. I want the plan to map each misconception to the precise point in the arc where it should be headed off, and how the sequence absorbs it without losing momentum. What's the right check at the end that shows they've grasped the conceptual knot rather than just nodding along? And tell me explicitly what to leave out — I'm guessing formal supervaluationism, epistemicism about hidden sharp lines, and any fuzzy-logic formalisms should stay off the table since this is one casual session, but confirm that. Don't suggest readings, they won't do them, and keep everything meaningful for someone who can't search things up.
