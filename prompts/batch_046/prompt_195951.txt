ok so i'm putting together my thoughts on sunk costs and rationality for a seminar and want to see if the package hangs together. here's roughly where i landed:

1. the classic sunk-cost fallacy is real — if A and B have identicle future prospects except i already paid for A, that's not a reason to stick with A.
2. but i think there's a real distinction between instrumental costs and what i'd call diachronic-identity costs: the years i put into learning a language or a friendship aren't just wasted resources if i quit, they're part of the practical identity that makes me this agent, and abandoning them fratures that continuity.
3. so honoring sunk costs isn't always irrational; it's wrong for pure instruments, but can be right when the "cost" is actually a constitutive part of who i am.
4. i also think buridan's ass is basically right: if two options are genuinely tied in value, rationality permits flipping a coin — there's no hidden preference we must discover.
5. but i think you shouldn't coin-flip away a diachronic-identity commitment; if quitting fractures your practical identity, that's a reason that should break the tie before you ever reach for the coin.
6. and i think evolutionary debunking of sunk-cost reasoning misses the mark, because the heuristc probably tracks commitment-stability rather than pure error.

my worries: (2)-(3) might just be relabeling the fallacy with fancy words. (4) and (5) seem to pull in different directions — either commitments always enter the value calculus (so buridan never hits personal choices) or they don't (and then coin-flipping risks violating them). also is (6) compatible with (1) if the heuristic is tracking something real, or does it undercut the idea that it's a fallacy?

go through these, tell me which pairs are genuinely tense vs only look like it, rank by severity, and say what'd have to give. also tell me which single revision cleans up the most, and whether (2) is a legit distinction or just rationalising. be specific.
