I'm developing an evaluative framework for a seminar on narrative moral education, and I want to stress-test it against biographical form. The danger I call "exemplar lock-in" is the risk that a life-narrative encourages learners to copy contingent surface traits—social station, temperament, or unrepeatable historical circumstance—rather than to appropriate transferable practical reasoning. To see how different genres resist or succumb to this, consider five strategies: (A) Plutarch's parallel pairing of Greek and Roman lives, which isolates virtue from cultural packaging by forcing cross-context comparison; (B) classical hagiography, which presents a singular, miracle-attested figure as an indivisible and non-negotiable model of holiness; (C) Enlightenment philosophical biography, which deliberately suppresses private vice and domestic disorder to preserve an unblemished public moral architecture; (D) modern psychobiography, which foregrounds moral failure, ambivalence, and psychological complexity specifically to disable hero-worship; and (E) "branching biography," a hypothetical mode I want included that traces the subject's major decision nodes into counterfactual paths so that contingency, not destiny, becomes the visible narrative structure.

Order these five strategies from most vulnerable to least vulnerable to exemplar lock-in, labeling each with a tier—S, A, B, C, or D—rather than numbering them 1 to 5. For each strategy, provide exactly one sentence explaining whether a specific formal feature insulates it from lock-in or exposes it to the risk. Then conclude with a single sentence stating which strategy you judge best balances resistance to lock-in with genuine moral guidance, and briefly why. Keep your entire response within 220 words, arranged in one paragraph or as a concise list if needed, but do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to specific editions.
