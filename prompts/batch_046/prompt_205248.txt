I run the moderation team for a mid-sized city's public deliberation platform -- not social media, more like structured town halls and policy forums where citizens post arguments about zoning, policing, school budgets, that kind of thing. Every week we get a post that makes us ask: is this harmful speech we should restrict, or just ugly opinion we should leave up? The usual checklist (is it a direct threat? is it harassment?) misses a lot of the hard cases.

I want a reusable decision procedue my team can run in about fifteen minutes per post, grounded in Mill's harm princple, but I want it to handle a distinction people usually ignore: the difference between 'transactional harm' (the speech directly causes a harmful act, like telling someone to burn a building) and 'ecological harm' (the speech doesn't hurt anyone on its own, but it gradually degrades the shared conditions for rational deliberation -- like flooding the forum with bad-faith analogies so people stop trusting evidence, or making certain groups so routinely dehumanized that they stop showing up). This second kind is tricky because Mill usually talks about direct harm to others, not about slowly poisoning the well.

Write me the step-by-step procedure: the gates in order, what counts as yes/no at each, and explicit 'hand off to human judgment' points where values can't be automated. Then run it on three posts: (1) someone writes "the zoning board is corrupt and should all be fired" with zero evidence; (2) a user repeatedly compares housing advocates to a specific historical atrocity in ways that don't directly threaten anyone but seem designed to make good-faith discussion impossible; (3) one case where the procedue tells us to restrict the post, but I think that's the wrong call -- I want to see the answer I won't like.

Also flag where it's likely to misfire because of my framing, especially around 'ecological harm'.

Please organize like this:
1. 'The procedue' -- about 400 words, the checklist with gates.
2. 'Three runs' -- about 500 words, the worked cases.
3. 'Where it breaks' -- about 250 words, honest limits and misfires.
4. 'A note on framing' -- about 150 words.

One more thing: if 'ecological harm' is just a confused way of dressing up content moderation I happen to dislike, or if Mill's principle can't actually support this distinction without collapsing, say so plainly instead of playing along. Don't be polite about it.
