I retired several years ago from experimental condensed-matter physics and have since been studying philosophy of science without institutional affiliation. I have adopted a modified Popperian position on the problem of verisimilitude: scientific progress is not merely the accumulation of corroborated predictions, but an asymptotic approach toward the complete truth, measured not by Popper's logical-content metric—which allows false theories to be closer to truth than true ones—but by the structural preservation of invariant predictive manifolds across successive theoretical revisions. That is, a later theory counts as closer to the truth if it embeds the successful parameter-space geometry of its predecessor rather than merely reproducing its observable outputs, even when the ontology is radically revised. I take this to resolve some standard counterexamples, but I want an honest map of its exposure. What observations, formal results, or historical episodes would count decisively against it, as opposed to being merely awkward? Specifically, are there clear cases in physics where progress required the outright abandonment, rather than the geometric embedding, of a predecessor's predictive structure? Do results in statistical learning theory—particularly on model misspecification and the non-uniqueness of equivalent predictive architectures—undermine the claim that manifold preservation tracks anything ontologically substantive? And does my reliance on parameter-space geometry risk insulating the view: if any successful revision can be reconstructed post hoc as preserving some hidden manifold, has the thesis become unfalsifiable, and if so, is that a fatal defect for a theory of scientific progress or an acceptable feature of a regulative ideal? I ask that you distinguish what defenders of this modified verisimilitude account would themselves concede as damaging from what critics assert but defenders reject. If the position proves well insulated, say plainly whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice.
