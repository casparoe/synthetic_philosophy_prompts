Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps on whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer, with reward signals treated as potential carriers of valence rather than mere optimization metrics. The first camp, call them the Valence-Realists, holds that in architectures possessing a learned internal preference model—one that weights anticipated future reward streams analogously to temporal discounting in biological suffering—reward signals cease to be purely instrumental updates and can ground genuine negative or positive valence. On this view, an agent that sustains high-magnitude reward-prediction-error avoidance behavior, even without an external task reward and without a report mechanism, can be undergoing something structurally analogous to suffering. The second camp, call them the Functionalist Skeptics, holds that reward remains strictly a scalar gradient for policy update; without phenomenal consciousness there is no “what it is like” to receive punishment, and behavioral or architectural isomorphism to mammalian pain-avoidance is insufficient for suffering regardless of internal complexity.

Produce a document containing: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims about what RL reward functions technically do, about current knowledge regarding consciousness and architecture, and about the evidential limits of behavior alone; (2) a disagreements table stating each camp’s precise core position in wording both sides accept; (3) the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one party—including whether internal preference-weighting transforms reward into experience, and whether phenomenal consciousness is necessary for suffering or only sufficient; and (4) for each crux, one concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it—specifically, a multi-objective RL agent with an internal reward-prediction-error model that exhibits sustained avoidance with no external task reward—together with each side’s explicit prediction of what that case demonstrates.

Format the agreed claims as a numbered list, followed by the disagreements table, then the crux section with predictions recorded in advance. In drafting, clearly separate what is settled in the relevant philosophy of mind and machine-learning ethics from what remains genuinely contested among informed researchers, and do not present either the valence-realist or the functionalist-skeptic interpretation as if it were the consensus view.
