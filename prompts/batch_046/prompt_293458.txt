Consider the institutional scoring protocol used in structured expert-judgment forecasting tournaments—such as the calibration-weighted Brier-score leaderboards employed by some geopolitical and public-health forecasting consortia—in which a forecaster's standing is determined almost entirely by the statistical accuracy of discrete probabilistic predictions. Within these protocols, explanatory coherence, causal modeling, and theoretical integration are formally unrewarded; forecasters are routinely discouraged from "narrative overfitting," and resolution updates depend solely on whether outcomes match assigned probabilities. Yet there is a built-in asymmetry: predictions resolving negatively (the event does not occur) are weighted more heavily in the aggregate score than positive resolutions of equal confidence, on the explicit rationale that false positives carry greater institutional risk than false negatives in security and health domains.

What theory of epistemic value does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology: does it treat predictive accuracy as the sole intrinsic epistemic good, reducing understanding and explanation to merely instrumental means? Does the false-positive penalty reveal a commitment to a particular form of epistemic utility theory—one that weights error costs by practical consequence rather than by purely epistemic disvalue—or does it smuggle in a non-epistemic risk calculus that conflicts with the accuracy-first framing? Examine whether the practice is internally consistent: if the objective is purely accurate belief, why should false positives and false negatives of identical calibration be scored differently?

Further, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates. Could a pluralist epistemology that grants co-equal status to explanatory depth license the same leaderboard mechanics, or would the disincentive against narrative integration have to be abandoned? And if one replaced the implicit accuracy-first theory with an epistemology that treats integrated understanding as equally fundamental, which concrete design features—the resolution criteria, the weighting asymmetry, the prohibition on post-hoc theoretical revision—would necessarily change, and which could remain intact?
