Last night a friend and I argued for hours about whether Scanlon's contractualism genuinely collapses under the aggregation problem, or whether the standard objections impose a structure that is not actually part of the view. He pressed the familiar charge: contractualism tests principles by asking whether any affected person could reasonably reject them. In the case where one must be killed to save five, the one can reject the principle permitting the killing, and the view supplies no further mechanism to weigh that rejection against the five separate rejections of being left to die. So aggregation is not merely counterintuitive within contractualism; it is structurally excluded by its pairwise architecture of justification. I replied that this conclusion holds only if we treat reasonable rejection as standpoint-modular—each person's rejection is evaluated in isolation from every other affected party. If we instead read the framework as principle-global, assessing whether a principle can be justified to all affected parties simultaneously, then the numbers enter not by summing harms but by altering what it is reasonable to demand of any one individual given the total stakes for the rest. On this reading, the single person's rejection might be overridden not because five outweigh one, but because the burden of refusal becomes unreasonable when seen against the principle's full distributive impact. He said this smuggles aggregation through the back door of "reasonableness," converting contractualism into disguised consequentialism. I said he was treating "reasonable rejection" as a binary veto that the framework never endorsed. We stalled precisely here: he insists I am changing the subject from justification to each person into collective optimization, and I insist he is conflating the absence of a weighing mechanism with the absence of multi-personal reasons altogether. So I am not asking for a verdict on who saves whom. I want a diagnosis. Is this disagreement about an empirical claim regarding how moral reasoning is structured—whether people actually reason in modular standpoints—or about a normative standard embedded in the very concept of reasonableness? Is it verbal, turning on whether "justification to each" permits any reference to aggregate effects without betrayal? Or are we answering different questions—he asking whether contractualism can reproduce utilitarian verdicts, me asking whether it needs to reproduce them in order to be adequate? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal model of multi-party rejection relations, a precise distinction between veto-based and burden-sensitive reasonableness, an argument that the dispute is ill-posed because "aggregation" means different things in each camp, or the recognition that no further argument should be expected and why?
