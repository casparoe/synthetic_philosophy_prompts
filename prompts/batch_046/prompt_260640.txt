Write a technically precise dialogue between two equally capable interlocutors—call them the Probabilist and the Revisionist—about whether the triviality result for indicative conditionals undermines rational conditional belief. The Probabilist should defend the view that an indicative conditional expresses a proposition whose probability equals the corresponding conditional probability; she should argue that the triviality result therefore forces a choice between giving up standard probabilism for conditionals or accepting that agents with non-degenerate credences cannot hold interesting dependency beliefs. The Revisionist should defend the view that indicative conditionals do not express propositions at all but instead encode belief-revision policies; he should argue that the triviality result is harmless because it targets a misapplied belief-model, not a revision rule, and that treating conditionals as update instructions avoids the incoherence entirely.

Have them engage directly rather than talk past each other: the Probabilist must address the Revisionist’s claim that revision policies need not be assigned unconditional probabilities, and the Revisionist must address her charge that any policy with predictive content will, upon formalization, reproduce the very probabilistic constraints that generate the triviality result.

Inject a concrete, non-textbook case to ground the dispute: an automated epidemiological forecasting system that assigns credence 0.85 to “if vaccine rollout is delayed by two months, then winter mortality exceeds the baseline,” assigns credence 0.70 to “rollout will be delayed,” yet assigns only 0.25 unconditional credence to “winter mortality exceeds the baseline.” The Probabilist should argue that this triad is probabilistically incoherent if the conditional is a belief-object, and that the system must therefore be treating the conditional non-propositionally—perhaps inconsistently. The Revisionist should defend the triad as coherent by explaining how the conditional operates only as an update rule triggered in delay-worlds, and should reply specifically to the Probabilist’s objection that this “rule-only” treatment collapses into disguised probabilism once predictive accuracy is required.

Conclude with both identifying the precise further case that would settle the matter for them—whether a formal proof that revision policies can be fully embedded in a Bayesian framework without predictive loss, or an empirical demonstration that human reasoners track dependencies by assigning unconditional probabilities to conditionals.
