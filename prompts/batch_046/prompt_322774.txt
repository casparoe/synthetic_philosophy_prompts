My question concerns whether generational cohorts — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, and the like — constitute real social kinds or are merely artifacts of how we slice continuous demographic flows. For roughly half a century, one camp (working in a tradition that treats generations as historical locations shaped by shared formative events) has argued that cohorts possess a kind of collective identity and exhibit stable, non-reducible patterns in values, consumption, and political behavior. The opposing camp, drawing on age-period-cohort (APC) decomposition, insists that what looks like a cohort effect is usually an entanglement of life-cycle aging and historical period; they emphasize that because cohort, period, and age are linearly dependent (Cohort equals Period minus Age), the three dimensions are formally underidentified in standard regression frameworks, making pure cohort coefficients unstable and often unestimable without arbitrary constraints.

The debate has persisted through waves of survey data and qualitative history, yet neither side concedes ground. Realists respond to statistical critiques by saying that APC models miss the qualitative specificity of shared formative experience — the kind of thing that makes a cohort fungible across counterfactual demographic reshufflings only if the historical events are held constant. Critics reply that without identifiable, counterfactual-supporting parameters, generation collapses into a narrative overlay on age and period trends.

My own hypothesis is that the disagreement is partly a hidden metaphysical dispute about whether social kinds must support modal counterfactuals (the realist demand) and partly a methodological dispute about APC identifiability, but I suspect there is also a verbal divergence: one side uses generation to denote a demographic cohort defined by birth-year windows, while the other uses it to denote a historical subject with collective agency.
