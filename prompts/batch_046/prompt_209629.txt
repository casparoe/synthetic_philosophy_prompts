I am injecting a somewhat niche angle: I want to examine whether role-based obligations within collaborative inquiry generate reasons that belong to the wrong kind for maintaining a theoretical commitment, and whether those same obligations can also supply right-kind reasons for managing how that commitment is expressed socially.

Essay sketch:
The standard wrong-kind-of-reasons problem asks whether pragmatic or relational considerations can justify belief or admiration. I propose extending this framework to collective epistemic practices. Imagine a senior researcher who has endorsed a theoretical framework that a junior collaborator's career depends upon. New counterevidence emerges. On the one hand, there is a pragmatic reason to maintain her public endorsement: protecting the junior researcher's livelihood and the stability of the team. On the other hand, there is what I call a role-based reason: as a mentor and institutional leader, she owes consistency, reliability, and protection of dependents within the collaborative structure. This reason is not merely self-interested or prudential; it derives from the normative structure of the role itself.

The puzzle is that if she retains her belief in the framework primarily because of this role-based reason, she seems guilty of letting a wrong kind of reason govern her epistemic attitude—analogous to believing a proposition because it makes one happy. Yet abandoning the framework immediately might also violate her role obligations, now by destabilizing the very collaboration she is meant to sustain. Thus role-based reasons appear on both sides of the decision.

My proposed resolution distinguishes between reasons for entering or revising an epistemic state (where only truth-oriented considerations are of the right kind) and reasons for structuring the institutional expression or temporal management of that state (where role-based considerations are legitimate). The risk, however, is that this distinction looks ad hoc: without an independent account of epistemic agency that separates belief-formation from belief-management, we have merely relocated the problem rather than solved it.
