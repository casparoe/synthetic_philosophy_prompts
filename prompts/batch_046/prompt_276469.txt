I'm preparing a formal presentation for my philosophy class and want to extend Augustine's ethics of speech to a modern case he never addressed. Augustine holds that lying is always sinful because language is a divine gift meant for truthful communion with others. Now consider clinical 'therapeutic privilege,' in which a physician tells a terminal patient that she is 'still fighting,' not for personal gain but to preserve the patient's psychological coherence—an instance of what philosophers term 'epistemic paternalism,' which I will call 'existential scaffolding.' Would Augustine treat this benevolent falsehood as falling under his absolute prohibition in De Mendacio, or could he accommodate it through his distinction between harmful deception and playful joking (jocus)? More specifically, if the physician's aim is not to deceive for advantage but to maintain the patient's capacity for hope, does Augustine's theology of speech allow the duty of truthful disclosure to be reordered by charity, or does that collapse into the consequentialism he explicitly rejects? I need to know whether applying Augustine to this modern dilemma genuinely extends his framework or forces a distortion of his core commitments.
