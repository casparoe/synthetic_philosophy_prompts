You are a senior philosopher of language with particular expertise in Wittgenstein's later philosophy. Before answering, please take a moment to think step by step: first, clarify what Wittgenstein means by saying that language game is a family-resemblance concept rather than an essence-governed one; second, distinguish that claim from any thesis about the determinacy of rules inside a particular game; third, identify the precise point at which the family-resemblance thesis might be mistakenly thought to bleed into the internal normativity of rule-following.

Here is the question I want your help with. I take Wittgenstein's point to be that activities like ordering, describing, asking, and promising have no shared essential core—only crisscrossing similarities—so that the very concept of a language game is irreducibly plural and open-textured. From this, it seems unavoidable that the rules constituting any one of these activities are themselves family-resemblant: if there is no essential feature that makes something an order-giving practice, then there cannot be an essential, sharply bounded rule for what counts as correctly giving an order. The conditions for correct rule-application within a game must therefore be as fluid and overlapping as the taxonomy of games itself. Otherwise, we would be smuggling essentialism back in at the level of norms.

Given this, I am trying to understand whether Wittgenstein's framework allows us to say that someone has genuinely misapplied a word, or whether all we can say is that their usage resembles correct usage to a greater or lesser degree. Does the family-resemblance structure of language-game taxonomy necessarily dissolve the distinction between correct and incorrect moves inside a given game? Or is there a principled way, within his view, to maintain sharp internal norms while denying that those norms depend on an essential definition of the practice they govern? Please address both the conceptual error—if there is one—in extending family resemblance from concepts to rules, and what a consistent Wittgensteinian account of normativity inside a language game actually looks like.
