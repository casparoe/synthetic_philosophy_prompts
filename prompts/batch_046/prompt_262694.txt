I cover the intersection of science and philosophy for a general-interest magazine, and I am pitching a six-month independent research project—intended for a long-form feature paired with a conceptual white paper—on a specific tension within the ethics of advance directives. The question I need the proposal to state precisely is this: when an advance directive specifies a clinical condition, such as a state in which one no longer recognizes close family, and the now-competent patient in that very condition reports that life remains worth living, does the directive retain normative authority, or is it undermined by an epistemic asymmetry between the drafting self and the experiencing self? Existing accounts tend to frame the conflict either as a metaphysical problem about personal identity across time or as a legal problem about prior informed consent, but neither addresses the possibility that the earlier self made a well-informed value judgment about a phenomenological state it could not genuinely imagine. The proposal should set out an approach that treats the authority question as one about epistemic reliability rather than identity: it should map which features of future experiential states are accessible to present imagination and which are systematically opaque, then ask whether that opacity weakens the directive's claim. I want the deliverable to be a structured argument—no literature review—defining the conditions under which an earlier directive should be overridden, specifying what empirical or conceptual finding would cause the argument to fail, and outlining a realistic six-month timeline for a solo investigator. Crucially, the proposal must include a brief self-critical section identifying exactly what would change my mind—whether a demonstrated symmetry in first-person prognosis, or a normative argument that prior values necessarily trump present reports regardless of epistemic access—so that the project does not present itself as a foregone conclusion. Please write in a formal register suitable for a workshop convenor or small-grant program officer, avoid bibliographies or citation requests, and do not rely on highly specialized terminology that assumes familiarity with niche debates in philosophy of mind; inject any necessary conceptual framing directly into the text.
