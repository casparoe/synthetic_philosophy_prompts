As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of biology and philosophy of mind, I have become preoccupied with a strange inversion that Darwin and Turing seem to share. In evolutionary explanation, we derive the appearance of design without invoking a designer; in mechanized computation, we derive the appearance of cognition without invoking a comprehending subject. In both cases, global competence—achieving a structured, functional result—appears entirely divorced from local comprehension. I want to stage a 90-minute workshop for approximately fourteen adult learners, mostly scientists turned philosophers, that makes them feel this inversion in their bones rather than merely discuss it conceptually. Design a structured, runnable activity or game with explicit rules, assigned roles, necessary materials, and a precise timeline, in which the group collectively generates a complex, apparently purpose-built outcome through a process that is rigorously blind to that outcome. Structure the exercise so that one subset of participants acts as a stochastic selection mechanism—making local, myopic choices with no access to the global target—while another subset operates a deterministic, symbol-manipulating procedure that achieves an equivalent structural result without any interpretive layer. Include a payoff or incentive scheme that requires no real money but creates genuine, felt tension between local action and global outcome. The rules should be arranged so that participants first experience the success of the collective result, and only afterwards discover that no individual ever held the result in mind. In the debrief, map specific moments of the activity onto Darwin’s reversal of teleological reasoning and onto Turing’s separation of computation from cognition; be explicit about which feature of the inversion—such as the open-endedness of evolutionary time scales or the universality of mechanized reasoning—the rules necessarily fail to reproduce and must instead be supplied through guided discussion. Also provide an honest forecast of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how to recover without breaking the experiential force of the exercise.
