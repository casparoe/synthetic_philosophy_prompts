Here are the working notes for a short essay. I begin with Sen's liberal paradox: the demonstration that even a minimal liberal condition—giving each individual decisive authority over at least one pair of social alternatives—conflicts with both the Pareto principle and unrestricted domain once there are three or more agents, as illustrated in the canonical two-person, one-book case. Rather than treating this solely as a result in social-choice theory, I propose extending its logical structure to what I call "protocol liberalism" in decentralized network governance. In a federated architecture, individual nodes retain veto power over local rule-sets (the liberal condition), yet global interoperability demands that no node's local choice degrade connectivity for others in a way that all nodes would reject (a Pareto-like consistency condition). The essay argues that Sen's impossibility therefore carries over: any protocol guaranteeing both strict node sovereignty and universal interoperability must either constrain the space of admissible node preferences or relax one of the two conditions, typically by substituting a "rough consensus" or fault-tolerant aggregation rule for strict Pareto improvement.

I am unsure whether this extension is formally valid or merely metaphorical, and I want sharp analytical comments on three specific points. First, does network interoperability genuinely parallel social-state aggregation, or is it better modeled as a coordination game with different strategic properties? Second, have I misrepresented Sen's original concern—was it primarily about protecting individual rights from collective override rather than about institutional design constraints? Third, my argument assumes that local veto rights generate cross-node externalities analogous to preference conflicts in Sen's framework; is this assumption defensible without an explicit model of dependency between nodes? Please respond with objections, suggestions for tightening the analogy, and identification of any hidden premises; I am not seeking bibliographic recommendations or citations, but an assessment of whether the conceptual move holds.
