I am revising the syllabus for my graduate seminar in political philosophy and comparative constitutional design, and I have noticed that students tend to treat subsidiarity as a synonym for decentralization rather than as a contested normative principle with distinct moral and epistemic foundations. To remedy this, please compose a dialogue between two equally capable interlocutors—call them Dr. Alina Fischer and Professor Thomas Brenner—who debate a conceptual distinction I have been developing for the course. Fischer advocates what she terms "deep subsidiarity": the view that a higher-level authority may legitimately preempt a lower-level jurisdiction only when it can demonstrate not merely inefficiency or divergence in local outcomes, but a specific democratic failure within the locality itself—namely, that the local democratic process has systematically excluded or misrepresented minority interests that the locality claims to protect. Brenner, by contrast, defends "functional subsidiarity," according to which the legitimacy of central intervention should depend solely on whether the problem exhibits genuine collective-action or spillover characteristics that lower levels are structurally incapable of solving, regardless of the internal democratic quality of those lower levels.

Set their exchange in the context of a hypothetical constitutional reform for a multi-tiered federation that borrows structural features from the German Länder system, Swiss cantonal autonomy, and American cooperative federalism, but do not hinge the argument on any specific historical statute, court ruling, or treaty article. Fischer should ground her position in arguments about epistemic humility—the idea that central planners lack the contextual knowledge to judge whether local outcomes reflect genuine democratic preference or mere majoritarian domination—and in a moral claim about the non-instrumental value of local self-determination. Brenner should respond directly: he should challenge whether her "democratic failure" criterion is administratively discernible without itself requiring invasive central oversight, and he should press her on transboundary problems such as climate policy or digital-privacy harmonization, asking whether her model risks privileging the status quo of locally dominant groups under the guise of protecting minorities. Fischer, in turn, must address these objections specifically—if Brenner raises carbon leakage or data-flow spillovers, she should engage with those mechanisms rather than retreating to general praise of local knowledge. The dialogue should be philosophically rigorous, avoid straw-man representations, maintain genuine back-and-forth, and conclude without resolution but with the conceptual distinction—between subsidiarity as jurisdictional efficiency and subsidiarity as a safeguard against internal democratic failure—fully clarified. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid references to obscure doctrinal texts or highly specific historical provisions that a well-trained generalist model could not be expected to recall from standard training.
