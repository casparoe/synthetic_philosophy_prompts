I've been working through the idea that proper names and natural-kind terms are rigid designators—referring to the same object or substance across all possible worlds—and I'm struggling to see how this is meant to yield substantive necessary a posteriori truths rather than just relabeling empirical discoveries. If 'water' rigidly designates H2O, then the fact that water is necessarily H2O seems to depend entirely on what chemists discovered in the actual world: the modal profile is fixed by our local empirical identification. So the 'necessary' aspect looks like a semantic consequence of rigidity, while the 'a posteriori' aspect does all the metaphysical work of telling us which substance we actually named. The same seems true for 'Hesperus is Phosphorus': if I don't yet know that both names refer to Venus, I have no basis for judging the statement necessary, which suggests that necessity here is just co-reference in disguise. Is there a coherent way to maintain that these identities express genuine metaphysical necessities about other worlds, or does rigidity ultimately collapse the modal/empirical distinction by making every necessary truth about a rigidly designated kind depend on a prior empirical identification in our world?
