I'm working through my own commitments on peer disagreement—specifically when conciliationism is appropriate versus when steadfastness is justified—and I've found that my intuitive verdicts across concrete cases resist easy systematization. I want to know whether there is a coherent principle that captures most of them, which ones it forces me to abandon, and how it handles a situation I haven't resolved.

Here are my verdicts. First, two climate scientists with identical publication records and access to the same regional temperature datasets disagree on whether a particular feedback loop will trigger within fifteen years; one assigns 65% probability, the other 35%. My gut says they should both move toward 50%—they are genuine peers with shared competence and no hidden asymmetry. Second, two moral philosophers with equivalent training in normative ethics disagree about whether it is permissible to use a generative model trained on a deceased person's correspondence to produce consoling messages for bereaved family members; one treats this as a benign extension of memorial practice, the other as a deceptive exploitation of grief. Here I feel both should remain steadfast—the disagreement traces back to incompatible axiological commitments about authenticity and harm, not to any remediable epistemic failure. Third, two mathematicians examining the same lengthy proof of an open conjecture in combinatorics both understand every line, yet one identifies a hidden circularity in a lemma that the other regards as a harmless idealization. I think they should split the difference in their credence but neither should fully adopt the other's binary belief: the proof is neither clearly valid nor clearly invalid, and peer disagreement here should reduce confidence without forcing abandonment. Fourth, a senior oncologist with decades of clinical experience and a biostatistician with identical formal credentials disagree about whether a particular immunotherapy is effective for a rare cancer subtype; the physician weights qualitative patterns from bedside observation, the statistician weights a recent meta-analysis with small sample sizes. Despite their peer status, I think the physician should largely defer to the statistical assessment rather than split the difference. Fifth, two close friends with equal familiarity with a colleague's character disagree about whether that colleague intentionally misrepresented a project timeline in a meeting yesterday; only one was present. I think the witness should stay steadfast and the absent party should defer, rather than both converging on an intermediate confidence.

Is there a single principle—perhaps tied to the nature of the evidence, the structure of disagreement, or the domain of judgment—that explains why I want conciliation in the climate case, steadfastness in the moral and testimonial cases, partial conciliation in the mathematical case, and near-complete deference in the clinical-statistical case? Or do I need a family of principles? And what should I say about the unresolved case: two AI safety researchers with identical publication histories and access to the same architecture documentation disagree about whether a particular reasoning module in a large language model exhibits robust goal-directed behavior that could survive distributional shift; one interprets the behavior as instrumental convergence, the other as a spurious correlation with training-data patterns?
