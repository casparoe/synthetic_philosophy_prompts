Write a retirement address for a dean who served thirty years in a faculty now adopting algorithmic governance — AI-driven resource allocation and staff evaluation without human deliberation. The audience is mixed: humanists versed in Confucian ethics, administrators who think in terms of strategic leadership, and the engineers who built the system. The retiring dean has asked that you not celebrate her as an autonomous individual or visionary leader, but honor the relationships she sustained.

Structure the speech around Confucian role ethics: the idea that moral personhood is constituted by specific relationships (teacher–student, elder–junior, ruler–subject analogized to dean–faculty) and by the performance of ritual propriety (li) within them, rather than by inner autonomy or contractual obligation. This should constrain what you can say — avoid framing retirement as an exit into private selfhood, and avoid praising her for personal genius or strategic control. Instead, present retirement as a transformation of roles, from active administrator to elder mentor, and her legacy as the cultivation of reciprocal obligation (shu) within the institution.

Without endorsing or condemning the new system, use this framework to articulate what is at stake when relational judgment is replaced by functional assignment. Address the engineers and administrators in terms they can grasp — not rights or efficiency, but whether an algorithm can occupy a role in the Confucian sense, and what is lost when institutional bonds are reduced to data flows. Comfort those who fear the loss of human judgment; challenge those who see only optimization; and do not resolve the tension into a simple verdict. Keep the address suitable for oral delivery to a non-specialist audience, but let the philosophical structure do real work: if Confucian role ethics were false or irrelevant, the speech would collapse.
