I'm trying to determine whether virtual objects and virtual harms are genuinely real, and I'm testing whether my intuitions cohere. Here are my verdicts. If a virtual chair consists of virtual parts bound within a single data structure, it is one object, not a mere plurality; but if its parts are linked only through relations that themselves require further relations to be unified, I think Bradley's regress applies and there is no unified chair—yet I also find it unsatisfactory to halt that regress by positing a primitive "binding" relation. A virtual sword that is exactly copied becomes two distinct swords, not one object present in two locations. A harm experienced in virtual reality that causes genuine psychological trauma is real in its effects but not in its virtual form: the virtual insult is not itself harmful, only its real-world consequence is. An entity that resides in server data but has never been rendered exists virtually, though not phenomenally. Is there a coherent principle—say, that virtual reality is fixed by functional-causal integration rather than by relational composition—that delivers all five judgments without ad hoc primitives? If not, which verdict should I drop: the anti-regress commitment, the duplication intuition, or the derivative-status claim about virtual harms? And what does your best account predict about a virtual object whose internal binding program is itself decomposed into subroutines that require further binding?
