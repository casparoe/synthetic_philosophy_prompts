I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop next month honoring the retirement of my doctoral advisor, who spent thirty-five years in comparative defense studies with particular attention to Switzerland’s militia tradition, Sweden’s total-defense model, and Finland’s post-Cold War conscription politics. The audience is deliberately mixed: roughly half are defense scholars, constitutional lawyers, and military historians who will recognize every empirical reference; the other half are family members, university administrators, and students from unrelated fields who will not. I need the remarks—approximately six minutes—to treat armed neutrality not as a strategic footnote but as a philosophical problem about citizenship and the monopoly of violence.

Specifically, I want the speech structured around a framing I have been developing in recent conversations with colleagues: the idea that conscription within genuinely neutral states creates a “moral economy of shared risk,” in which democratic credibility depends on citizens personally bearing defensive burdens rather than outsourcing them to a professional class. This concept should do real structural work, not serve as ornament. It should make it impossible for me to romanticize conscription as pure democratic equality—since shared risk remains coercive and materially costly—while also preventing me from presenting professional armies as an unproblematic efficiency gain—since they sever the civic bond by alienating citizens from the conditions of their own security. The speech should remain accessible to non-experts without sounding patronizing to specialists, and it should avoid bibliographies, citation lists, or reading recommendations, as the occasion calls for spoken reflection rather than academic survey.

Because this framing leans on a contested interpretation of republican civic obligation—one that draws more on normative political theory than on standard strategic-studies literature—I ask that you append a brief reflective note to the drafted remarks. In that note, please indicate how confident you are in the speech’s central claim linking shared risk to democratic credibility, and single out the specific point—whether conceptual or empirical—where you believe the argument is least secure.
