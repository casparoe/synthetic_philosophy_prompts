I've been working through the tension between primordialist and modernist accounts of nationalism—specifically the way Hobsbawm describes traditions that present themselves as ancient being deliberately constructed in recent centuries, and the way Anderson frames nations as imagined communities held together by print-capitalism and a shared, horizontal sense of simultaneous time. For a philosophical-poetics workshop I'm helping organize, I want a poem that doesn't merely illustrate these theories but enacts their central contradiction through its own formal machinery, so that the structural logic of the verse is inseparable from the conceptual argument.

Write a villanelle—exactly nineteen lines, five tercets followed by one quatrain, with two refrains designated A1 and A2 and only two rhymes running through the entire poem. Let A1 be a statement of timeless, organic belonging voiced by the nation itself, along the lines of We are the people this valley has always named; let A2 be a precisely dated bureaucratic fact that undermines that timelessness, along the lines of The census first wrote our surname in 1871. Through the tercets, interweave archaic imagery—bone, stone, oral memory, unbroken lineage—with the modern institutional apparatus that actually produced national consciousness: railway timetables, census categories, standardized primers, rotary presses. Do not resolve the contradiction; let it accumulate, so that each return of the refrain feels more like a bureaucratic repetition than a mythic echo.

The rhyme scheme should forcibly marry the archaic register to the bureaucratic one—so that bone rhymes with telephone, or soil rhymes with oil-field—making sonic continuity perform the same ideological sleight-of-hand that the literature on invented tradition identifies. At the quatrain turn, bring both refrains together for the first time not to synthesize them but to reveal that the repetition of the refrain is itself the mechanism of invention: the nation persists precisely because it keeps asserting that it has always persisted. Use mostly iambic pentameter, but allow occasional metrical substitutions—stutters, administrative pauses—to suggest the machinery breaking through the mythic surface. The result should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the literature on invented tradition and imagined communities recognizes the conceptual architecture rather than just the proper names, and should work as a poem for a reader who knows none of that background. No bibliographic apparatus, no reading list—just the poem itself.
