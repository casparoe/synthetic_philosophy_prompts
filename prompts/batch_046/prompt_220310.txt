You are serving as an advisory philosopher of public ethics and memory studies to a municipal commission on shared heritage. Your task is to audit an analogy that has become common in recent debates about retaining statues and street names honoring controversial historical figures.

The analogy, often advanced by those who argue for retention together with added contextual signage, runs as follows: curating public monuments is like curating a museum exhibition. A museum does not display only morally flawless figures; it selects works and people who shaped history, frames them with explanatory plaques, and trusts visitors to understand complexity. By analogy, a city should not tear down statues or rename streets that honor flawed founders or leaders; it should keep them in place and add interpretive context, letting citizens learn from the full record rather than sanitizing the past.

Your audience is the commission: they need a rigorous conceptual audit, not a polemic. Before you answer, spend a few minutes brainstorming the structural features of museum curation—voluntariness, expert gatekeeping, spatial containment, aesthetic versus civic purpose, and the option of deaccessioning to storage—alongside the structural features of urban commemoration: ubiquity, navigation, daily involuntary encounter, symbolic endorsement embedded in address and orientation, and democratic co-ownership of shared space.

Please structure your response in exactly these sections, in this order, with approximate lengths as noted:

1. Orientation (approx. 150 words): Briefly state the analogy, identify the specific conclusion it is meant to license—namely, that retention plus contextual plaque or signage is generally preferable to removal or renaming—and flag the conceptual distinction between archival preservation (storing an artifact in a depot or museum) and ceremonial presence (occupying a place of honor in shared civic space).

2. Correspondence table (approx. 200 words): Lay out the explicit mapping between source and target. What corresponds to the curator, the gallery wall, the exhibition label, the museum visitor, the collection's thematic coherence, and the decision to deaccession? Be precise about both directions of the mapping and about what kind of "context" a plaque can provide.

3. Disanalogies that matter (approx. 250 words): Identify where the museum picture breaks in ways that directly affect the "retain with context" conclusion. Consider, at minimum: the difference between voluntary entry into a museum and unavoidable daily navigation (especially for street names used in addresses and directions); the difference between aesthetic appreciation and civic identity formation; the asymmetry between expert curatorial authority and democratic decision-making about shared space; and the fact that a museum can relocate an object to storage without "erasing" history, whereas removing a statue from a plaza or changing a street name often functions precisely as a form of removal from the ceremonial register, not a neutral relocation.

4. Disanalogies that are red herrings (approx. 150 words): Note points where critics might invoke museum power dynamics, funding biases, or the ability of visitors to skip rooms that, on closer inspection, do not actually damage the analogy's core logic about retention versus removal. Separate noise from signal.

5. Hidden imports (approx. 200 words): Analyze what the analogy quietly imports about the value of the whole versus the parts. Does it assume that the city's commemorative "collection" has an integrity that requires preserving every piece? Does it treat citizens as visitors rather than co-owners? Address the indexical versus symbolic function of street names—how a name operates not merely as a signpost but as an embedded coordinate in daily practical life and in the symbolic geography of belonging.

6. Verdict and better analogy (approx. 200 words): State clearly whether the museum-curation analogy survives your audit well enough to carry the conclusion that retention with plaques is generally the right policy for contested monuments and names. If it collapses or is severely weakened, propose a more apt analogy—perhaps drawn from contract, inheritance, constitutional amendment, or household decision-making about shared walls—and explain why it maps more cleanly onto the ethics of shared commemorative space.

7. Bottom line for the commission (approx. 100 words): Give a concise, actionable bottom line: should the commission treat "museum-style contextualization" as a sufficient response to demands for removal or renaming, or should it be treated as only one option among several, with removal or renaming remaining legitimate in some cases?
