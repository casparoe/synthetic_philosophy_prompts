I work as a senior backend engineer at a logistics platform company. After a recent multi-region outage, our reliability team is redesigning our post-incident analysis framework, and I proposed two competing documentation templates. Template A requires analysts to identify the general covering conditions—load thresholds, missing safeguards, correlation patterns—that characterize this class of failure, with the goal of feeding predictive monitors. Template B requires a chronological, agent-centered narrative that specifies who acted, in what sequence, with what information available, emphasizing the irreducible particularity of the context. My manager claims Template A is epistemically empty because every serious outage is structurally unique; a teammate claims Template B is operationally useless because it produces stories that do not transfer. I recently attempted a synthesis by mapping the narrative onto a causal-graph structure in which nodes represent specific actions and edges carry probabilistic dependencies, but I am unsure whether this genuinely integrates two explanatory forms or merely disguises a deeper ontological disagreement about whether an event is best understood through universal conditions or through its unique temporal unfolding. Could you advise me on how to evaluate these templates? In particular, I want to know whether there is a principled philosophical distinction between explaining an occurrence via general laws and explaining it via its particular sequence, and whether my causal-graph synthesis actually resolves that distinction or just collapses it without argument.
