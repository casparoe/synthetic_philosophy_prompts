As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am drafting a feature on moral psychology and institutional design. At a recent symposium in Edinburgh — where I had hoped to ask a question about AI governance, but the session overran — I observed a sharp exchange between two panellists, Dr Amara Okonkwo and Professor Julian Reeves, about whether Bernard Williams’s integrity objection, particularly the idea that saving a drowning spouse only after weighing lives introduces a corrupting extra step of reasoning often summarised as having one thought too many, can be extended to algorithmic triage protocols in hospitals. Their disagreement unfolded in five moves. First, Okonkwo argued that when clinical algorithms force doctors to justify life-saving interventions through an explicit utility calculus, they reproduce Williams’s corruption of integrity at the institutional level: the clinician ought to act from her vocational commitments without an additional impersonal check, so protocols should preserve role-based discretion rather than maximisation requirements. Second, Reeves replied that Williams’s example concerns intimate personal attachments, specifically spousal love, not professional roles; institutional ethics is constituted by universal obligations, so applying the objection to triage confuses integrity with partiality and risks legitimising unfair allocation. Third, Okonkwo countered that professional identity itself forms part of a practitioner’s practical identity; a doctor can preserve integrity by acting from role-based norms without reducing them to universal calculus, and the danger lies in the demand for an alienating justification, not in particular attachment. Fourth, Reeves responded that this strips integrity of its substance: within universal roles, integrity demands adherence to fair allocation norms, not deviation justified by vocational intuition; otherwise the concept becomes an empty licence for bias disguised as character. Fifth, Okonkwo concluded that the real distinction is not between partiality and universality but between two modes of justification — acting because this is what a good clinician does versus acting because the calculus permits it — and that algorithmic design should avoid imposing the latter as a necessary check without allowing personal favouritism.

Please adjudicate each of the five moves: say who is right about the specific point made, note anything both parties are missing, and indicate which argument or feature of the case is most likely to cause you to change your overall verdict. Present your response as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
