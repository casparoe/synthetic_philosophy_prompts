my roommate's been studying Nyāya logic for a while and she's been giving verdicts on whether specific belief-forming setups count as actual pramāṇa — not just seems true but genuinely valid by Nyāya conditions. here's her track record so far:

- seeing smoke on a distant hill and inferring fire: she calls it valid anumāna, but only because you've personally grasped the vyāpti (the invariable connection) from prior experience. if you'd never seen smoke with fire, she says it wouldn't count.
- recognizing a new plant as medicinal because it resembles a known one in the feature that actually causes the cure: valid upamāna. but if the resemblance is just in color or leaf shape with no causal link, she rejects it.
- accepting atomic theory just because a trusted physics professor said so, without understanding the reasoning yourself: she says that's not śabda for you. she insists śabda needs either direct comprehension of the reasoning or an authority whose trustworthiness is established in a way you can access; blind reliance on an expert chain breaks the conditions.
- feeling rain on your skin: straight pratyakṣa, no issues.
- knowing your location from a GPS blue dot on your phone: she says this isn't pramāṇa at all for you. you're not perceiving satellites or algorithms, so there's no direct perceptual or inferential link; it's ungrounded trust in a black box.

she also added two more rulings:
- if a compulsive liar tells you your keys are in the drawer and they happen to be right, it's still not śabda, because the speaker's authority/truthfulness is part of the condition, not just the result being correct.
- believing a health correlation from a big study you can't replicate or inspect: not anumāna, since there's no guaranteed concomitance with exclusion of other causes, just statistical association.

now the new case: there's a brain-computer interface that reads thermal drone data from ten miles away, processes it through an opaque neural network, and then directly stimulates your visual cortex so you experience a glowing red shape that your brain reads as fire on the ridge. you don't percieve smoke, you don't run any inference yourself, you don't understand the processing, and nobody told you verbally. do you think she'll rule this a pramāṇa?

i'm less interested in her yes/no than in the rule she's actually running — is it about direct causal contact with the object, about the believer's own cognitive access to the chain, about whether the process fits one of the four classical categories regardless of reliability? — and what other rules fit all her earlier verdicts just as well. also, what's the one further case that would best split those competing rules apart?
