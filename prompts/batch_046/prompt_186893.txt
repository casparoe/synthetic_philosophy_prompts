Since retiring from experimental physics I have been reading in philosophy of science, and I have constructed an argument about ontology and informational inaccessibility that now strikes me as suspicious because it eliminates ontology altogether. I should like to know precisely which step fails, why that step looked plausible, and what each alternative costs.

(1) If two theoretical frameworks are empirically indistinguishable in every domain to which we have, or in principle could have, observational access, then they do not represent genuinely different ontologies; they are merely notational variants of the same underlying reality.

(2) For any ontology that posits entities within an informationally inaccessible region—whether beyond a cosmological horizon, inside an event horizon, or within causally disconnected branches of a decoherent history—there exists a rival ontology that omits those entities entirely yet reproduces every observable fact within our accessible domain with perfect fidelity.

(3) Therefore, by (1), the presence or absence of entities in such regions makes no ontological difference.

(4) But the boundary between “accessible” and “inaccessible” is not absolute; it is always relative to a particular observer, instrument, or epoch. What is inaccessible to me now may become accessible with improved apparatus, and what I now observe is already inaccessible to distant observers.

(5) Apply (1) and (2) recursively, then, not only to cosmological boundaries but to every local boundary of present observational reach. For any entity I now claim exists, there is a rival framework that denies it yet reproduces all my current data, because my present data never extend to every possible verification.

(6) Hence, by repeated application, no ontological claim is ever genuinely committed to entities; all are dispensable conventional conveniences.

(7) Yet this applies even to the very entities—measuring devices, observers, causal structures—that I invoke to define “accessibility” in premise (1). The argument therefore undermines its own framework.

(8) The conclusion that all ontology collapses is absurd, so one of (1) through (5) must be rejected.

I am inclined to defend (1), because it seems to capture a sound principle of scientific realism: do not multiply entities beyond empirical reach. But if I keep (1), I must block the recursion in (4) and (5), and I do not see how to do so without abandoning the universality of empirical equivalence. If I reject (3) instead, I seem to allow ontology to float free of evidence, which feels like the very metaphysics I wished to avoid. Please identify the faulty step, explain why it appeared sound, and price the alternatives—particularly what I must pay if I wish to retain (1) and reject the recursion. Reply in British English, with a formal tone suitable for a philosophical memorandum.
