I am working through the classical framing of the problem of universals and have reached an impasse with the opening of Porphyry's Isagoge, as it comes down through Boethius's translation and commentary. The passage reads:

"I shall omit to speak about genera and species, as to whether they subsist (in the nature of things) or in mere conceptions only; whether also if subsistent, they are bodies or incorporeal, and whether they are separate from, or in, sensibles, and subsist about these, for such a treatise is most profound, and requires another more extensive investigation."

My difficulty is not merely lexical but architectural. Porphyry seems to present a stark binary—either universals have real existence in nature or they are bare mental conceptions—before branching into subsidiary questions about corporeality and separation from sensibles. Yet the Aristotelian position I take to be the intended middle ground holds that universals are genuinely in things (not mere conceptions) without being separable Platonic entities. I worry that by treating "mere conceptions" as the sole alternative to subsistence in rerum natura, Porphyry's framing implicitly collapses immanent realism into nominalism, thereby distorting the very view he claims only to be postponing. Could you help me understand whether this tripartite division is genuinely neutral, or whether it already encodes a metaphysical commitment? In particular, I am unsure how to parse "subsist about these"—whether it means "inhering within sensibles" or "existing separately with respect to sensibles"—and whether that ambiguity explains why Boethius later felt compelled to supply a resolution that Porphyry refused. I would like a reading that clarifies the conceptual structure of the omission itself, rather than a survey of later medieval positions.
