I am preparing the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in Philosophy of Collective Action and Risk. Students have encountered the standard literature on collective rationality and risk aggregation, but they are not decision-theory specialists. The exam runs 90 minutes; this essay question carries 35 marks and should be answered in approximately 900–1,100 words.

Please construct one essay question that meets the following constraints. First, it should require students to reconstruct the standard unilateralist’s curse—situations in which multiple independent actors share exposure to a risk or benefit, each can act unilaterally, no central veto exists, and the actor with the lowest action threshold determines the collective outcome—without allowing them to answer by mere summary. Second, it must introduce and deploy a modified variant that is not in the standard literature: call it the interpretive unilateralist’s curse. In this variant, members of a collective can each, at private cost, acquire ambiguous evidence about an emerging risk; disclosure is mandatory upon acquisition. Members differ not in willingness to deploy a technology, but in interpretive risk thresholds—the level of ambiguity at which they treat evidence as sufficient to trigger a binding precautionary protocol for all. The member with the lowest interpretive threshold acquires evidence first, finds ambiguity within their tolerance range, and activates the protocol unilaterally, while members with higher thresholds rationally free-ride on acquisition. The collective’s epistemic standard is thus set by its least epistemically cautious interpreter.

The essay question should ask students to do three things: (i) identify the two structural conditions that make the standard deployment-based curse inescapable; (ii) determine whether the interpretive variant genuinely extends the curse’s logic or collapses into a different collective-action failure (e.g., simple free-riding, a tyranny of caution, or a misapplication of the precautionary principle), with particular attention to whether interpretation can generate unilateral externalities analogously to physical deployment; and (iii) evaluate a proposed remedy called epistemic cost-pooling—shared financial costs of evidence acquisition, but retained individual authority over interpretation—assessing whether it resolves the interpretive curse or merely shifts the locus of unilateralism from acquisition to interpretation, and identifying any new pathologies such as strategic misreporting of thresholds.

The question should not be answerable by reciting labels; it should force students to apply structural logic to an unfamiliar epistemic domain. Do not include requests for bibliographies, named-author citations, or reading lists. Write the question in a formal, polished examination style.

Please provide:
1. The exam question itself, with clear instructions on length, marks, and the three parts.
2. A model answer of approximately 700 words.
3. A marking scheme totaling 35 marks that distinguishes an A-range answer from a B-range answer by naming the specific conceptual moves each must contain—for example, correctly distinguishing interpretation-from-deployment analogies, diagnosing the free-rider mechanism in acquisition, and identifying strategic misreporting under cost-pooling—and that lists specific mistakes that should cost marks, such as conflating the interpretive variant with uniform application of the precautionary principle or treating shared costs as resolving interpretation without addressing retained individual authority.
