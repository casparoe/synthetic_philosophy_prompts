I'm leading a ninety-minute seminar for first-year philosophy-of-mind graduate students—joined by one cognitive neuroscientist—on whether split-brain cases genuinely fracture the unity of consciousness or merely split access pathways. The students arrive with the pop-culture intuition that callosotomy creates "two people in one skull," and I want to dislodge that without simply asserting it is wrong. Rather than walking through the classic Sperry hand-drawing experiments, which they have already encountered in undergraduate surveys, I want to build the session around a constructed pedagogical case I'll call Case J: a hypothetical patient with a complete section of the corpus callosum but an intact anterior commissure, presented with a chimeric emotional display (fearful expression to the left visual field, neutral to the right) and then asked to rate the overall emotional valence verbally (left-hemisphere mediated) while selecting a matching image with the left hand (right-hemisphere mediated). The behavioral outputs diverge, yet the patient reports a single, stable emotional experience rather than two competing ones. I need a teaching plan—not the explanation itself—that lays out the dependency order: what conceptual distinction must be secured before Case J can do any philosophical work, and which idea depends on which. I also need guidance on the carrying example's necessary features and deliberate omissions—what Case J must include, and what it must lack (for instance, explicit language capacity in the right hemisphere or conscious awareness of conflict), so that it forces the distinction between access unity and phenomenal unity rather than collapsing into behaviorism or Cartesian dualism. Please map the misconceptions in the order they will arise—beginning with the "two minds" intuition, then the "language hemisphere is the real self" move, then the "if behavior diverges consciousness must split" inference, and finally the "the anterior commissure saves unity so there is no problem" dismissal—and indicate exactly where in the sequence each should be intercepted, with a reply that absorbs it without shutting down discussion. Include a check-for-understanding question that distinguishes students who have actually grasped the conceptual distinction from those who can only repeat the jargon "access unity" and "phenomenal unity," and specify what neuroanatomical detail and metaphysical baggage I should explicitly refuse to cover at this level. Conclude with a single line beginning "Teaching arc:" followed by the three dependency stages in order.
