At a recent meetup, two colleagues—neither trained philosophers, though both read widely—debated whether mental states can genuinely cause actions if every physical event already has a sufficient physical cause. I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background; I followed the exchange closely enough to be intrigued but not enough to referee. Their argument unfolded in five moves.

(1) Alex said the causal exclusion argument is decisive: if physicalism holds and the physical domain is causally closed, then for any mental event there is a complete physical cause, so the mental event is redundant and mental causation is impossible unless we accept systematic overdetermination.

(2) Blake replied that this treats mental and physical properties as competing inputs, but on a non-reductive view they stand in a realization relation—like a high-level algorithm and its compiled machine code—so there is not two causes but one pathway described at different levels of abstraction.

(3) Alex countered that realization does not eliminate redundancy: if the physical realizer does all the causal work, the realized mental property still contributes nothing of its own, leaving mental properties epiphenomenal in exactly the way non-reductive physicalists hoped to avoid.

(4) Blake then invoked a proportionality constraint on causation—the idea that a cause should match its effect in specificity—arguing that mental descriptions, such as refusing because one recognized an argument was fallacious, are often more proportionate to behavioral effects than neural descriptions, which shows mental properties are causally appropriate at their level rather than redundant.

(5) Alex answered that proportionality is at best a pragmatic criterion for selecting explanations, not an ontological proof that a realized property acquires independent causal powers; without an account of how the mental property adds efficacy beyond its physical base, the appeal merely relabels the exclusion problem.

Please adjudicate move by move in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification, and provide no introduction or conclusion.
