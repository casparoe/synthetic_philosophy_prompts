I'm preparing a consistency check on my views about confirmation and old evidence, and I'd like an audit rather than a lecture. My commitments are:

(1) The strong likelihood principle: the evidential bearing of data on a hypothesis depends solely on the likelihood ratio, with no independent weight given to predictive novelty, temporal ordering, or the inquirer's learning history.

(2) Old evidence can genuinely confirm: Mercury's perihelion (known since 1859) provided real, non-redundant confirmatory support for General Relativity over Newtonian gravitation in 1915, not merely psychological reassurance.

(3) Counterfactual reconstructions of prior belief—assigning probabilities as if one had not yet seen the evidence—are normatively illegitimate; priors must reflect actual epistemic states.

(4) Objective Bayesianism is correct for scientific reasoning: rational priors for physical theories are constrained by structural virtues (simplicity, symmetry, unification) rather than subjective belief states, and should not be revised retroactively.

(5) Post-hoc accommodation is vicious only when it introduces free parameters lacking independent theoretical motivation; deriving a known result from pre-existing formal principles is not ad hoc.

(6) If two theories are empirically equivalent with respect to all currently available data, then further reflection on that same data alone cannot rationally discriminate between them.

Which pairs or triples are genuinely incompatible, which conflicts dissolve on closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision? Rank the tensions by severity, specifying for each what would have to give and at what cost.
