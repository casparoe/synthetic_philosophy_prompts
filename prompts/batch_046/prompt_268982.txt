A recent interdisciplinary article summarizes Hume's essay "Of the Standard of Taste" through the lens of "sentimental calibration." On this reading, Hume holds that beauty is not a property of objects but a calibrated response of properly functioning observers, and that the standard of taste operates as a statistical convergence procedure: we identify ideal critics by checking whose judgments correlate reliably across cultures and training conditions, and we treat aesthetic divergence as evidence of defective calibration rather than legitimate disagreement. The author concludes that Hume is best read as an early social-epistemologist of aesthetics, for whom disputes about beauty are resolvable in principle by empirical investigation into observer reliability—much as we correct reports of color blindness—so that agreement among refined observers indicates objective convergence rather than shared sentiment alone. This strikes me as both illuminating and suspicious, especially the slide from sentiment to empirical correlation. Is this characterization fair? Please take a definite, committed stance: separate what Hume actually claims from what is imported by the calibration metaphor, assess whether the social-epistemic conclusion genuinely follows from his premises, and state clearly whether this reading improves on standard interpretations or distorts them beyond recognition.
