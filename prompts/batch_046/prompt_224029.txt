I have spent considerable time thinking about why arguments over vague terms—such as whether someone with a particular number of hairs is "bald," or whether a specific height counts as "tall"—never seem to reach a conclusion. As I understand it, one camp maintains that there is a precise, hidden boundary in reality—perhaps exactly 3,847 hairs—separating the bald from the not-bald, and our inability to locate it reflects only our ignorance, not any absence of fact. The opposing camp holds that no such unique boundary exists; rather, language permits many equally acceptable ways to draw the line, and statements about baldness lack a single correct truth-value until one of those precisifications is fixed. I understand these positions are often labeled epistemicism and supervaluationism, and that the literature contains long-running exchanges about whether hidden sharp boundaries are knowable in principle, about how to preserve logical consistency when borderline cases seem both true and false, and about whether truth-value gaps are acceptable. Despite this, the dispute appears frozen: the same objections about knowledge, contradiction, and logic recur, and neither side appears persuaded. I have developed my own working hypothesis, though I lack formal training: perhaps vagueness is neither a hidden feature of the world nor an indeterminacy in language, but rather a lack of standardized calibration in our measurement practices—similar to an unstandardized thermometer—so that epistemicists mistake the missing calibration for hidden knowledge, while supervaluationists mistake it for an absence of any correct reading. I am unsure whether this idea clarifies anything or merely restates the problem in different vocabulary. Could you diagnose the actual structure of this impasse? Is it fundamentally verbal—centered on competing uses of words like "vague" or "truth"—or is there a substantive disagreement about metaphysics, epistemology, or logic that remains unresolved? Might the two sides actually be addressing distinct questions—one about the nature of reality, one about the behavior of language—while treating them as one? Given your diagnosis, what kind of development—whether a formal logical result, an empirical study of linguistic usage, a conceptual distinction that separates the questions, or something else—would constitute genuine progress? Or is this a dispute driven by irreconcilable foundational intuitions about precision and meaning, in which case we should expect no resolution? In answering, please engage concretely with what each position actually claims about knowledge, contradiction, and logical law, rather than offering a general survey, and tell me frankly whether anything could actually advance it.
