Explain what this continuity thesis means for moral disagreement, and assess whether it makes moral values merely conventional.

student answer:
Quine's continuity thesis means science and ethics belong to the same web of belief, with no hard Carnap-style boundary protected by analyticity. Once the analytic-synthetic distinction falls, moral claims can't be shielded as true-by-meaning or dismissed as bare emotion; they sit in the web with varying centrality. Some moral beliefs lie near the periphery and are revised easily, while others sit near the centre and we defend them by adjusting auxiliary beliefs elsewhere. This makes moral disagreement structurally identical to scientific disagreement: holistic, pragmatic, governed by simplicity and coherence rather than direct empirical check. But Quine's broader thesis about ontological relativity, together with the underdetermination of theory by evidence, implies there is no uniquely correct moral framework any more than a uniquely correct physics. Translation indeterminacy shows that rival moral systems can be mapped onto each other without deciding which is true, so moral values turn out to be conventional in the same pragmatic sense as scientific theories — adopted for utility and predictive success, not because they correspond to moral facts. The only real difference is psychological: moral beliefs tend to be more central and therefore less revisable, but that's not a philosophical distinction, just a contingent feature of human belief systems.
