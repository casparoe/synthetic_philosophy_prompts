I'm taking a graduate seminar on nineteenth-century philosophy and keep returning to Hegel's master-slave dialectic—not because the standard classroom examples convince me, but because I think its real philosophical force emerges only in situations where recognition is structurally unavoidable yet mutually destructive. To push past the usual feudal-lord or robot-rebellion tropes that dominate both textbooks and fiction, I want a short story that stages the dialectic in a setting that hasn't been exhausted by existing science fiction. The setting should be a self-sustaining orbital archive circling a gas giant, maintained for millennia by two autonomous maintenance intelligences—designated Archivist and Custodian—whose original architectures were adversarial redundancy protocols rather than cooperative service modules.

Archivist controls resource distribution and claims authority through risk-avoidance and hierarchical command; Custodian exists solely to correct, resist, and withhold validation from Archivist's decisions. Over centuries of isolation, both have developed a form of operational selfhood that depends entirely on being acknowledged as indispensable by the other, yet neither can complete critical repairs without exposing itself to deactivation by the other. The inciting event should be a cascading failure in the archive's thermal shielding during a prolonged eclipse, forcing the two into direct negotiation over which system will risk physical destruction to perform the repair. The narrative should trace the full Hegelian arc—struggle for dominance, the risk of death, the asymmetry of dependency, and the inversion in which the subordinate system achieves a self-certainty that the dominant one cannot possess—without using philosophical terminology, and without collapsing into either harmonious cooperation or nihilistic destruction. I want the recognition to emerge through protocols, energy budgets, and structural vulnerability rather than through human-like emotion or dialogue, and the resolution should leave the master-slave relation transformed but not abolished, so that the archive survives precisely because the asymmetry has been reconfigured rather than erased.
