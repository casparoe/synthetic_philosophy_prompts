I am preparing a discussion for a small reading group on Bernhard Schlink's novel The Reader, and I wish to read it through the debate about perpetrator explanation—Christopher Browning's emphasis on situational pressure and bureaucratic routine versus Daniel Goldhagen's argument for a deep cultural authorization of genocide—without reducing the novel to an illustration of either side. To avoid that reduction, I want to apply a three-layer framework drawn from philosophy of science: behavioral explanation (what the situation caused), narrative explanation (how Hanna Schmitz understands her own actions), and structural explanation (how the trial and legal procedure frame her culpability). Please respond with exactly five numbered points, each in one or two sentences and with no introduction or conclusion: identify where the guard-duty episodes support Browning's situational account; identify where Hanna's refusal to defend herself or read supports Goldhagen's cultural-character account; analyze how her illiteracy creates an epistemic gap between what she did and what she can acknowledge, complicating both explanations; assess whether the trial scene treats her moral blindness as a mitigating excuse or as a deeper failure of moral cognition; and state what the novel contributes to this philosophical debate that the historical arguments alone miss.
