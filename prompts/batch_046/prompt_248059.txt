I am preparing an essay for my philosophy of science seminar on the demarcation problem and falsificationism, and I want to move beyond the standard introductory comparison with verificationism. I propose to treat the 1989 Fleischmann-Pons cold fusion announcement and the rapid replication crisis that followed as a live case study for Popper's framework.

The episode is familiar in outline: researchers at the University of Utah reported anomalous excess heat in palladium-deuterium electrolysis; within months, multiple laboratories reported failure to replicate; the claim was largely abandoned by mainstream physics, though a small literature on low-energy nuclear reactions persists. For this analysis, please work with that common outline and do not depend on obscure archival details.

Specifically, examine the crisis through Popper's demarcation criterion and his associated normative methodology, paying particular attention to two elements often glossed over: first, his concept of conventionalist stratagems, by which a core claim can be immunized against apparent refutation through adjustments to auxiliary assumptions; and second, the problem of the empirical basis, especially the theory-ladenness of the observation reports that replication teams produced when they detected no excess heat under conditions they believed matched the original protocol.

With these tools, provide a disciplined, two-directional analysis:

1. Mapping the actors and options. Identify the relevant scientific community, the core claim, and the available responses for Fleischmann-Pons and for the replication groups. By the lights of Popper's normative falsificationism, what should each party have done upon encountering ambiguous or negative results?

2. Explanatory successes and failures. What does falsificationism illuminate well—for instance, the speed with which mainstream physics treated the claim as refuted? Where does it misdescribe or remain silent? In particular, discuss whether the persistence of a minority of researchers claiming positive results for decades, despite near-universal replication failure, is best understood as a conventionalist stratagem, as a legitimate open problem, or as something the demarcation criterion is not designed to adjudicate.

3. Normative versus logical demarcation. Does this episode support treating falsifiability as a guide to how scientists ought to behave in real time, or does it show that the criterion functions only as a logical boundary between science and non-science? Consider especially whether reported reluctance to disclose precise experimental parameters—which complicated immediate replication—should be evaluated as a methodological failure by Popperian standards, or whether it reveals a practical gap between falsifiability in principle and falsifiability in practice.
