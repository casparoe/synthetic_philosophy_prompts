For a feature I am drafting on collective decision-making and economic fairness, I want a philosophical reading of Sidney Lumet’s 1957 film *Twelve Angry Men* through the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution. Treat the jury’s initial eleven-to-one split as a cooperative game: the disagreement point is a hung jury leading to mistrial, and the utopia point is unanimous acquittal. Does Juror 8’s strategy of incremental concession—trading openness on one piece of evidence in exchange for scrutiny of another—track the K-S criterion of maximizing the weakest party’s gain relative to the disagreement payoff, without pushing anyone below it? Or does the deliberation instead reflect a Nash-style proportional division of conviction? I want the analysis to take both the film’s dramaturgy and the axiomatic structure seriously: point to specific scenes where a juror’s refusal to budge either respects or violates monotonicity, and say whether the screenplay treats the negotiation as strategic bargaining or as epistemic reasoning about reasonable doubt, and where the philosophical framing illuminates the drama rather than reducing it to an illustration.
