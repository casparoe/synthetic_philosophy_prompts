I have been observing a recurring argumentative structure in discussions of whether the phrase "Western values" denotes a coherent moral category. The pattern involves a single speaker advancing the same normative claim—such as gender equality, secular governance, or broad protections for dissent—under two incompatible status descriptions. First, the speaker presents the claim as a contingent, historically constructed achievement specific to certain societies, using this framing to resist demands that it be imposed rapidly elsewhere or to defend it against internal accusations of false universality. Then, often within the same exchange and without acknowledging a shift, the speaker treats the identical claim as a universal moral standard that any society can and should be judged by, deploying it to criticize other societies for falling short. I have noticed this with norms governing religious neutrality in public life and with doctrines of free expression. The move is not merely strategic hypocrisy, since each framing is developed with some conceptual care; nor is it standard equivocation, because the normative content does not change, only its metaphysical status—heritage versus universal law. Is there an established concept in political philosophy, the study of ideology, or cross-cultural ethics that captures precisely this oscillation between contingent-particular and universal-normative registers? I am especially interested in how it relates to, and differs from, strategic essentialism, ordinary inconsistency, and the kind of qualified universalism that allows for culturally variable implementation of shared principles. If no established term exists, I would like a proposed name together with a precise definition that includes boundary conditions: for instance, whether the speaker must genuinely hold both beliefs, or whether the pattern can be purely rhetorical; and whether the two registers must concern the exact same normative content, or whether partial overlap is sufficient.
