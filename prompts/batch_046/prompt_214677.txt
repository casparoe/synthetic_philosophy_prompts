A recent philosophy-of-science essay summarizes Daniel Dennett's distinction between cranes and skyhooks approximately as follows: "A skyhook is any explanation that invokes a mysterious, top-down force that does not itself require explanation—such as vitalism or an unmoved mover—whereas a crane is a bottom-up mechanism assembled from simpler parts, like natural selection or neural computation. Dennett maintains that legitimate scientific explanations must be composed entirely of cranes, with absolutely no skyhook elements permitted, and he labels the practice of decomposing complex phenomena exclusively into smaller crane-components greedy reductionism. Applying this framework, he treats phenomenal consciousness as a skyhook illusion: because subjective experience cannot be broken down into neural cranes, the correct response is to eliminate it from our ontology rather than treat it as genuinely real but reducible. The essay further claims that if an evolutionary explanation appears to be a crane but relies on an initial design seed—a pre-adapted functional module that could not itself have arisen through incremental selection—then that seed functions as a concealed skyhook, and the entire explanation fails by Dennett's lights."
