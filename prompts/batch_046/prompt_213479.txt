I've been thinking a lot about Douglas Hofstadter's essay that people call "Person Paper" — the satire where he reverses gendered language to show how one group gets treated as the invisible standard. I don't have any background in linguistics, but the logic stuck with me: whatever gets labeled is the exception, and whatever stays unlabeled is quietly running the show. Lately I noticed the same pattern in the manual for my old blender. Every diagram shows a right-handed person standing alone at a counter; left-handed use, seated operation, or two people working together are all flagged as "alternate configurations" with special warning symbols. I want you to write a deadpan parody of a 1970s kitchen-appliance instruction manual, about 600 words, that applies Hofstadter's reversal technique to this hidden physical default. In your version, treat the right-handed, standing, solitary user as the marked category: invent a suffix like "-dex" that must be appended to every reference to them, add footnotes explaining their special spatial needs, and present left-handed, seated, or cooperative users as the ordinary, unmarked case that requires no explanation or labeling at all. Keep the voice perfectly straight-faced and bureaucratic — a technical writer who genuinely believes this framing is neutral — so that the absurdity comes only from the language itself, not from any wink to the reader. Don't include citations, bibliographies, or references to other scholars. The piece should show that you actually understand what Hofstadter was doing — that it's about forcing a hidden norm into the open by making it carry all the linguistic baggage — rather than just swapping words around. Make sure it reads like a real document that accidentally reveals its own bias.
