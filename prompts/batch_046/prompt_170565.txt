I am revising the syllabus for an advanced undergraduate seminar in rational choice theory, and I have been turning over a persistent confusion about the two-envelope exchange problem that I want to address directly with my students rather than gloss over. The standard presentation runs as follows: you are given two envelopes, one containing twice as much as the other; you open one and find a specific amount, call it X, then compute that the other envelope contains either 2X or X/2 with equal probability, yielding an expected value of 5X/4, so you should switch; but since the reasoning is symmetric, you are driven to switch back and forth indefinitely. Most textbook resolutions dissolve the paradox by observing that no probability distribution over positive amounts can be uniform and also satisfy the doubling condition, which makes the conditional 50/50 assignment illegitimate once you observe a particular value. Yet this resolution strikes me as suspiciously quick: it seems to block the paradox only by denying that there is any well-defined prior over the envelope values, which feels like assuming away the very scenario the puzzle describes rather than explaining why a rational agent should not switch.

What complicates matters further is that if one tries to salvage the setup by imposing a proper Bayesian prior with a finite mean—say, an exponential or Pareto distribution—then the switching argument indeed collapses for large observed values, but only because the prior assigns vanishing probability to arbitrarily high payoffs. This looks circular: the paradox is blocked precisely by the assumption that infinite expectations are impossible, which is essentially what the paradox was testing. So I find myself wondering whether decision theorists have genuinely solved the problem or have merely relocated the tension into the choice of prior. Given all this, I want to press the question in its strongest form: does the two-envelope paradox reveal a genuine structural vulnerability in expected-utility maximization when applied to unbounded choice sets, or is the apparent contradiction entirely an artifact of an incoherent probabilistic model? I am asking for a committed assessment—take a definite stand on whether the standard resolution is sound or question-begging, explain exactly where the false step lies in the switching reasoning, and address whether any non-ad hoc prior can coexist with the intuition that, for any observed amount, there is a determinate fact about whether the other envelope holds double or half. Please do not simply catalogue competing interpretations; I want to know which view holds up and why.
