Rank the following five reference-class strategies for applying an outside-view forecast to the long-run stability of a technologically mature, continent-scale federal republic with permanent overseas military commitments, ordered from the framing you would weight most heavily to the one you would weight least. Assign each a tier—S, A, B, C, or D—rather than a pure ordinal rank, and in two sentences per tier explain both why it belongs there and the hidden structural assumption that makes it either robust or fragile. The five strategies are: (1) the classical territorial-empire duration base rate since 1500, weighted by peak land area; (2) the maritime-commercial hegemony cycle, comparing states that once held more than a quarter of global GDP while sustaining overseas force projection; (3) the constitutional federal-republic class, restricted to large polities with universal suffrage, written constitutions, and professional standing armies since 1789; (4) the institutional-sclerosis analog, matching states by the ratio of bureaucratic-expansion velocity to constitutional-amendment frequency after reaching technological maturity; and (5) the strategic-monoculture model, treating dominance in a single innovation cluster as analogous to the resource-curse dynamics observed in extractive economies. Be committed: do not treat the tiers as interchangeable; explicitly identify which of these five framings is the most overused by forecasters who invoke "the fall of empires," state the specific weakness in your own top-tier choice that a skeptic of base-rate reasoning could press, and conclude with the single reference class you think should dominate a 30-year forecast even if it feels counterintuitive.
