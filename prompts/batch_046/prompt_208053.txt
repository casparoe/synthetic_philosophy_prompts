I'm on the oversight board for our city's pilot participatory-AI thing. There's a proposal sitting in front of us for a "public-value arbitration module" that'd sit inside decision-support tools — zoning appeals, public-health messaging, whatever comes up. The mechanism works in two stages. First, stakeholder inputs get sorted: anything tied to a "comprehensive doctrine" — thick religious worldviews, metaphysical commitments, etc. — gets moved to a background constraint layer rather than entering the aggregation pool. Then what's left gets weighted by something they call a "reflective revision score": basically you gain aggregation weight proportional to how much you've revised your initial preference after being exposed to counter-arguments in the deliberation forum.

Some board members think this is exactly what liberal neutrality requires — the state shouldn't rank doctrines, so walling them off as constraints is correct, and the revision score rewards genuine reflection rather than raw preference intensity. Others think it's elitism in disguise, since working-class residents don't have hours to hang out in forums revising thier views, and that shunting religious inputs to the background still lets secular liberal reasons dominate the pool. We need a memo for thursday recommending approve pilot funding, approve with conditions, or reject, with a concrete implementation timeline if we move forward. Please take the neutrality argument seriously rather than just dismissing it as bias, but also deal honestly with the worry that this mechanism might systematically underweight people with less leisure time for deliberation. Don't just lean on standard social-choice literature; work through the logic of this specific two-stage filter and say which side you think actually wins.
