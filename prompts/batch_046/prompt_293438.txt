After nearly a year working inside a government ministry, I have found myself returning to a problem that the standard philosophical literature treats as a solitary crisis: the politician who must choose between moral purity and effective action. In my office, however, the morally compromising acts are rarely performed by the minister herself; they are designed into protocols, delegated to career staff, and distributed across committees so that no single person executes the wrong. This structural displacement has made the classical problem of dirty hands feel inadequate, yet I am unsure whether the inadequacy is real or merely apparent.

I am asking for a one-page research proposal—suitable for a six-month independent study fellowship—on what I will call architected dirty hands: cases in which a political leader does not personally commit a morally questionable act but deliberately constructs an institutional mechanism (a delegated authorization chain, a classified decision protocol, or a bureaucratic harm-reduction procedure) that predictably requires subordinates to perform it. The precise question is whether this architectural form of agency generates a moral residue that is quantitatively different (shared or diluted), qualitatively different (a distinct kind of stain), or structurally identical to direct dirty-handed action; and whether the answer turns on the degree of autonomy retained by subordinate agents or on the leader’s capacity for retrospective transparency. Existing treatments—whether they treat dirty hands as an inescapable feature of individual political responsibility or as a problem soluble through democratic accountability—assume that the agent and the act are co-located. They therefore leave open whether institutional mediation transforms the moral phenomenology of the stain, or whether it functions merely as a distribution mechanism with no distinctive philosophical significance.

The proposal should state the question in a form that admits a determinate negative answer, outline a conceptual approach that distinguishes architected from direct and collectively shared dirty hands without collapsing into standard theories of group responsibility, specify what a successful result would look like (a taxonomy of delegation structures paired with conditions for moral residue attachment, plus an argument about whether transparency operates as moral amortization or as confession without redemption), and acknowledge what would count as failure—specifically, if the analysis reveals no feature beyond ordinary complicity. Given the six-month scope, the deliverable should be a philosophical framework rather than an empirical study, and the proposal should explain why this framing matters for assessing legitimacy in bureaucratic governance. Please do not include a bibliography, citation list, or literature review by reference; I will integrate sources myself. I want the proposal to argue that the work is tractable, genuinely open, and worth funding precisely because it moves the debate from individual moral psychology to institutional design.
