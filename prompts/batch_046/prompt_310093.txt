I have been reading Machiavelli on my own, without any academic training, and I keep returning to his image of fortune as a river and of human capacity—virtù—as the dikes that hold it back. Most accounts I encounter treat virtù as a single blended trait: boldness mixed with foresight and flexibility. Yet I suspect there is an unresolved tension in the texts between two very different activities—reshaping conditions before they arrive, and reacting inventively when they suddenly shift—and that treating them as one thing obscures why he claims fortune is more easily resisted by the young and vigorous. I want a proposal for a three-month independent study, conducted part-time without access to archives or rare editions, asking whether Machiavelli’s theory of successful leadership becomes more coherent if we split virtù into these two modes—preparatory structuring and situational improvisation—rather than keeping it unified. The proposal should state the precise question, explain why the standard unified reading leaves a specific gap in explaining the relationship between the advice in The Prince and the historical examples in the Discourses, outline an approach that compares particular episodes across both works using only widely available translations, describe what a positive result would look like and what would count as failure, and give an honest assessment of whether this is realistic for a non-specialist working alone. I will supply my own references; please do not include a bibliography or citation list.
