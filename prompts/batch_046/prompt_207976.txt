Notes for the essay:

The standard Rylean opposition frames intellectualist accounts—those that reduce knowing-how to knowing-that—against an anti-intellectualist insistence that skill is manifested in intelligent performance rather than in prior contemplation of propositions. More recent dissolution attempts, whether through dispositional analyses or through computational models of skill acquisition, tend to share a hidden premise: if the causal structure of a skill can be mapped onto propositional representations, the distinction collapses.

I want to defend a qualified anti-reductionist position. Knowing-how involves a mode of attention that is structurally different from propositional knowing. When a skilled practitioner acts, awareness is directed not at the rules governing the action but at situational affordances that call for specific responses. This is not merely a difference in content—knowing that X versus being able to do X—but a difference in logical form: knowing-how is indexical and non-propositional in a way that resists full translation into a set of truths about the world.

But a serious complication follows. If knowing-how is genuinely non-propositional, then it seems inarticulable, resistant to explicit verbal instruction, and immune to correction by argument. Yet we clearly do teach skills through verbal explanation and correct mistakes through reasoning. This suggests that knowing-how must possess some propositional interface, even if its core is non-propositional.

My tentative resolution is that knowing-how is a hybrid state. It has a non-propositional core—the embodied, indexical orientation toward affordances—and a propositional periphery comprising the explicit rules that guide learning, error correction, and pedagogical transmission. The intellectualist errs by identifying the core with the periphery; the pure anti-intellectualist errs by denying any propositional structure whatsoever.
