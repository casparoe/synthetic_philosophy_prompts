I'm preparing a position paper for a seminar on suffering-focused ethics and want a consistency audit of my own commitments before I present them. Here is roughly where I've landed: (1) I endorse a strong form of negative utilitarianism, holding that the reduction of suffering has not merely greater weight but structural priority over the promotion of happiness, so that a world with less suffering is better regardless of aggregate well-being outcomes. (2) I accept the asymmetry thesis common in anti-natalist arguments: there is a serious moral reason not to bring suffering beings into existence, but no corresponding reason to bring happy beings into existence, and the absence of pleasure in a life that never exists is not a deprivation. (3) I regard wild animal suffering as one of the largest reservoirs of disvalue, and I hold that large-scale ecological intervention—such as reducing predation through genetic or immunological means—is in principle morally required if technically feasible and stable. (4) At the same time, I maintain that the badness of suffering is partly constituted by its relation to a subject's narrative identity: suffering that is radically alien to the being's self-conception and ongoing projects is worse, other things equal, than suffering that arises organically from those projects. (5) I deny that wildness, naturalness, or the preservation of specific ecological roles carries any intrinsic moral value for the beings involved; nature has no normative authority over us. (6) Consequently, I think we ought to pursue humane wildlife modification—such as rendering predators herbivorous through gene editing—provided the transition avoids producing the kind of existential dislocation that would violate (4).

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict, and how severely. In particular, examine whether (1) and (4) are compatible given that narrative mediation seems to condition the urgency of suffering in a way that structural priority might not allow, and whether (3) and (4) pull against each other over the identity costs of ecological engineering. I also want you to address the apparent tension between (2) and (6): one might think that radically altering an existing being's nature is closer to creating a new being than to preserving an old one, which could trigger asymmetry concerns about generating a being with a disrupted identity. Does that problem dissolve on closer inspection, or is it a real crack in the package? Rank the actual tensions by severity, and for each specify the cheapest revision—whether it requires weakening my negative utilitarianism, constraining my interventionism, or qualifying my narrative-identity view—and say exactly what that revision costs me. I do not know which commitment is the weakest link, so do not treat any of them as the obvious one to drop. Please answer in American English, keep the analysis focused on consistency rather than delivering a general survey of suffering-focused ethics, and do not include bibliographies or citations.
