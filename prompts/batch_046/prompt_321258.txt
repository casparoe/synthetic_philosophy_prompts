I've been convinced that Whitehead's rhythm of education—romance, then precision, then generalization—plus his warning about inert ideas actually entail something pretty specific about curriculum design. I think they imply that any subject taught primarily through standardized problem sets before students have developed a living, active connection with the material is not just inefficient but conceptually broken, because precision is being smuggled in before romance has done its work. So here's my position: in Whiteheadian terms, math and physics should never be introduced via formal drill in the first two years of secondary school, full stop. What would actually count against this? I want defeaters at every level—empirical studies showing drill-first works, conceptual arguments that romance and precision can overlap in time rather than being strictly sequencial, maybe even internal tensions in Whitehead's own writings where he praises early discipline. Rank them from fatal to awkward, and tell me honestly if I've built in enough escape hatches that the view is unfalsifiable, and whether thats a problem for a pedagogical claim like this one.
