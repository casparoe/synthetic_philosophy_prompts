Here's an argument my friend cooked up in a reading grp and I think it's fishy but can't pin down were.

(1) If you and someone are epistemic peers and disagree about p, conciliationism says you both should shift your credences closer together.
(2) Peerhood is symmetric, and in practice it's also transitive: if I'm calibrated like you and you're calibrated like them, then I'm roughly calibrated like them too.
(3) So in any big enough community there's a chain of peers linking me to someone with the opposite view.
(4) Apply conciliationism repeatedly along that chain and my credence gets dragged toward the community mean, which for hard questions is around 0.5.
(5) But conciliationism also applies to beliefs about who counts as a peer, so I can't treat (2) or (3) as fixed.
(6) That sets up a regress: I need peer-beliefs to conciliate, but conciliating undermines those very beliefs.
(7) So either I end up with credence 0.5 on everything (no beliefs left) or the view collapses.
(8) Therefore conciliationism is self-defeating and steadfastness wins by default.

Where's the weak link? I'd rather save (1) and (6) than (7), but I want to know what I'm giving up if I reject (3) or (5). What's the standard conciliationist response to the chain/transitivity worry, and what does the steadfastist have to say about (6) looking like a feature not a bug?
