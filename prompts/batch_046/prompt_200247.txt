I'm preparing a conceptual exercise for a discussion group on institutional ethics, and we kept returning to the problem that corporate codes of conduct routinely demand employees "go above and beyond" without recognizing the philosophical incoherence of making the optional mandatory. I'd like you to write a deadpan satirical memo—around 600 words, formatted with formal sections, subsections, and a sidebar—that treats supererogation as a regulatory compliance issue rather than a moral category.

The piece should be titled "Interim Directive on the Quantification and Mandatory Reporting of Supererogatory Conduct," issued by the fictional Department of Moral Accounting within a large institution. The voice must be that of a risk-management administrator who has fully absorbed the philosophical distinction between obligation, permission, and acts beyond duty, but who translates it into bureaucratic machinery. Invent a specific institutional mechanism to keep the satire from being generic: employees must file Form S-19 for any act exceeding contractual moral obligations, accumulating "merit surplus" points that are taxed at forty percent by the organization; those who perform only their baseline duties are placed on a "moral neutrality watch list"; and the memo should include a deadpan footnote addressing the "paradox of praised omission"—that is, the tension between praising someone for not donating a kidney and penalizing them for not working overtime. The target is the practice of systematizing supererogation in organizational ethics codes, not any named individual.

The humor must come from an internal contradiction that the author never notices: for example, a clause stating that supererogatory acts are strictly voluntary, followed immediately by a provision that refusal to perform them may trigger a "compassion audit." By the end, a reader familiar with the three-tiered structure of moral evaluation should recognize that the bureaucratic framework has collapsed the category of the supererogatory into obligation without ever breaking character. Do not include citations, bibliographies, named philosophers, or references to living persons; the critique should emerge entirely from the logic of the form and the invented administrative details. Keep the tone sustained, legible, and deadpan—no winking.
