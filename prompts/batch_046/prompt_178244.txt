As a novelist currently negotiating the subsidiary rights for a manuscript I have spent four years completing, I find myself confronted with a sequence of proposed contractual amendments that disturb me in a manner I cannot fully articulate. The publisher first offers a premium for audio rights; then, in a separate round, a larger sum for digital serialisation rights; finally, a further payment for permission to include the work in a corpus used to train synthetic-language models. Each concession, examined in isolation, appears advantageous—the premium addresses immediate financial pressure, and the right relinquished seems, at that moment, peripheral to the book's core value. Yet, having mentally accepted all three, I recognise that the bundle of rights I now retain is one I value less than the original unified rights, and I would willingly forfeit the accumulated premiums to restore my initial position, despite having endorsed every individual step. I am uncertain whether this reveals a genuine incoherence in my preferences across time, or simply the unavoidable cost of adapting to circumstance. I should be grateful if you could advise me, writing in British English, on how to diagnose such patterns before they occur, and on what practical discipline I might impose upon myself to ensure that my successive judgments in negotiations remain consistent over time, without forcing me to reject all incremental compromise outright.
