Consider an argument about gradual mind uploading that seems to show functional continuity is incompatible with the uniqueness of personal identity.

(1) If two successive cognitive states share the same active functional architecture—same information flow, same processing dispositions, same integrated dynamics—then they are stages of one and the same continuing person.

(2) In a gradual replacement procedure, each synthetic neuron is designed to replicate exactly the input-output profile and synaptic connectivity of the biological neuron it replaces, so that at every intermediate step the hybrid system possesses the same active functional architecture as the immediately preceding state.

(3) Therefore, by successive application of (1), every intermediate hybrid state is the same person as the original biological state, and by transitivity the final fully synthetic system is also that same person.

(4) Now suppose the procedure is executed in parallel on two independent synthetic substrates, each replacement synchronized step-by-step with the biological original so that each parallel chain also satisfies the functional-architecture condition in (2) relative to its predecessor.

(5) By the same reasoning as in (3), both final synthetic systems are identical to the original biological person.

(6) But the two final systems are not identical to each other: they occupy distinct physical locations, accumulate different experiences immediately after separation, and can be destroyed independently.

(7) Personal identity is a one-one relation; one person cannot be identical to two distinct entities that are not identical to each other.

(8) Hence at least one of (1) through (7) must fail, even though each appears independently plausible.

The conclusion I resist is that gradual uploading is impossible without either violating uniqueness or abandoning functional continuity. If I must choose, I would rather preserve (1) and (2)—that perfect functional replication preserves stage-to-stage identity—than deny (7). Where exactly does the argument slip? Is the error a hidden equivocation in what counts as the same functional architecture, a modal slide from possible continuity to necessary identity, a failure of transitivity over branching structures, or is the argument valid and the real cost accepting that identity can split? Diagnose the weakest step, explain why it initially looks sound, and price the alternatives: what does rejecting transitivity cost a psychological-continuity theory, what does denying uniqueness require, and what is lost if we instead claim that synthetic replication never fully replicates the relevant architecture?
