For a feature I am writing on the conceptual architecture of suffering and well-being, I need a taxonomy—not a prose comparison—of how philosophers and moral psychologists have tried to relate pleasure and pain, especially within negative-utilitarian frameworks. Please construct it as a decision tree rooted in logical space rather than in a list of named schools. The root node should ask whether pleasure and suffering are commensurable at all, with branches separating total cardinal incommensurability, ordinal-only comparison, and full quantitative trade-off. From there, split on whether any amount of pleasure can ever offset suffering; whether there is a sharp or fuzzy phenomenological threshold—say, between discomfort and what might be called extreme agony—below which trade-offs are permitted but above which suffering acquires lexical priority; whether that lexical priority applies only to experienced welfare or extends to potential beings; and whether ecological or evolutionary asymmetries (e.g., that suffering is often more intense and more durable than pleasure) should enter the moral arithmetic independently of phenomenology. Each leaf must carry either a recognized position name or be marked “unnamed,” followed by one sentence stating its most awkward commitment—such as being forced to reject intuitively acceptable small pleasures or being unable to justify preventing low-grade suffering. Where the tree produces a combination of answers that no one seems to defend, flag the quadrant and briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored. I am particularly interested in whether your tree can genuinely separate a threshold-lexical negative-utilitarian view from an extreme risk-averse classical-utilitarian view, and whether the quadrant that accepts full cardinal commensurability but rejects both linear weighting and lexical asymmetry contains a stable position or collapses into one of its neighbors. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for further sources; build the instrument entirely from the diagnostic questions and the logical combinations they allow. Frame every node as a concrete claim or case judgment that someone could answer without knowing which label it corresponds to, and keep the audience in mind: educated general readers of science-and-ideas coverage who have not necessarily studied formal ethics.
