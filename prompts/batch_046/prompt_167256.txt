In my philosophy of mind seminar, we have been examining accounts that treat phenomenal consciousness as a user illusion—roughly, the view that first-person experience is not a direct presentation of physical or mental reality but a kind of compressed virtual interface generated for the purposes of self-modeling and action-guidance. Before I try to adjudicate between particular versions of this view, I want to establish what any adequate theory in this family actually has to deliver.

Write the full desiderata: the phenomena such a theory must explain or accommodate, the conceptual distinctions it must preserve, and the constraints—empirical, evolutionary, ontological, or moral—it must satisfy. For each requirement, provide the motivating case or consideration behind it, and mark whether you take it to be non-negotiable or specific to one camp. Relevant distinctions and cases should include, but are not limited to: the apparent immediacy of first-person access; the binding of qualitative features into a unified field; selective disruption of particular phenomenal qualities by localized neural damage without collapse of self-modeling; evolutionary utility of a compressed self-representation; empirical consistency with cognitive neuroscience; preservation of phenomenal transparency, in the sense that experience seems to present the world rather than an internal representation; avoidance of panpsychist commitments; and preservation of moral status for beings with conscious experience.

Crucially, work with and refine a distinction I am calling the thin-thick interface divide. On the thin interpretation, consciousness is analogous to a desktop icon: a useful fiction with no corresponding virtual object, where all causal work is done by sub-personal processes and the interface itself is causally inert. On the thick interpretation, consciousness is analogous to a running software instance: a genuinely existing virtual object constituted by functional organization, with its own causal dynamics and ontological status at the level of representation, even if not identical to any neural substrate. Specify which desiderata apply differently across this divide—does evolutionary utility require only thin-interface functionality, or does it demand thick-interface causal efficacy? Does phenomenal transparency favor one side? Does the possibility of selective qualitative disruption require a thick virtual object with internal structure?—and indicate whether satisfying all requirements forces a collapse of the distinction or renders one horn unstable.

Also identify any subset of requirements that appears jointly unsatisfiable given standard results—perhaps full ontological elimination of phenomenal properties combined with the demand that the illusion have evolutionary utility, or the combination of total causal inertness with the need to explain selective disruption of qualitative features by localized brain damage. Keep the primary weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring particular theories, though you may briefly illustrate one or two requirements by referencing how a Dennett-style eliminativist account or an interface-theoretic approach might fare.

No bibliography or citation list is needed—just the specification.
