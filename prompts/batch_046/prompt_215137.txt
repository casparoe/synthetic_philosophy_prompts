I am drafting a philosophical novel set in the city-state of Kallipolis, where a governing council allocates resources not according to equality of welfare but according to a prioritarian calculus modified by what my characters call "depth-weighted well-being"—a metric that treats a life as an extended narrative whose value depends partly on the distinctiveness and continuity of its arc across generations, not merely on the sum of its momentary satisfactions. The central conflict arises when a faction argues for reducing the prosperity of the most advantaged citizens—not to transfer gains to the worst off, who remain unchanged, but to prevent what they call "narrative crowding," the dilution of distinct life-stories into a homogenized collective biography. Their opponents invoke the leveling-down objection, insisting that any principle permitting harm to some without benefit to others is morally grotesque, regardless of whether the harm is measured in utility or in narrative depth. I want this debate to avoid the usual textbook stalemate, so I am asking: within the broader space of prioritarianism and its critics, which open questions are genuinely upstream of the others? Specifically, does resolving the leveling-down objection require first settling whether prioritarian aggregation must respect the separateness of persons, or whether it can be addressed locally by refining the baseline of comparison? And if one introduces a non-standard metric like depth-weighted well-being—one that makes the harm of leveling depend on intertemporal narrative continuity rather than on instantaneous welfare—does that shift the tractability of the problem, or does it merely relocate the same conceptual instability into the philosophy of personal identity over time?

Furthermore, I am trying to decide where to direct the limited intellectual energy of my protagonist, a young ethicist who has perhaps three years of serious study before she must take a public position. Given that she cannot pursue every line—whether investigating asymptotic aggregation rules, the metaphysics of narrative identity, or empirical work on how people actually weigh future versus present welfare—which agenda should she prioritize? Which unresolved issues, if settled, would collapse the most downstream disagreements about distributive policy, and which are currently tractable with existing conceptual tools versus those that remain speculative? I want a defended prioritization: state the criteria you are using—dependency, tractability, neglectedness, or practical consequence for governance—rank the questions accordingly, and be explicit about which ones you would deprioritize because their resolution would not alter the practical outcome for a council like Kallipolis's. I am not looking for a bibliography or a survey of authors; I want to know where the conceptual work actually lives, and which crux, if moved, moves everything else.
