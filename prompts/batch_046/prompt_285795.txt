Consider the Pasadena game, in which a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads for the first time, and the payoff on the nth toss is 2^{n-1} dollars if n is odd and negative 2^{n-1} dollars if n is even. The expected value is a conditionally convergent series with no well-defined sum, so standard expectation-based decision theory offers no definite recommendation. Now imagine a decision-maker who can choose between playing this standard Pasadena game and playing a structurally symmetric "reverse-sign mirror" in which the rewards and penalties are swapped—odd tosses bring penalties of magnitude 2^{n-1}, even tosses bring rewards of the same magnitude—so that its expectation is equally undefined, but the temporal ordering of large risks and large gains is inverted.

Please argue that a rational, risk-averse agent with a finite capital reserve should strictly prefer the reverse-sign mirror to the standard Pasadena game, and that this preference reveals a deeper failure in expectation-based decision theory: not merely that some expectations are undefined, but that the framework lacks the resources to discriminate between risk paths that share the same formal expectation properties but differ in the sequencing of catastrophic exposure. In developing your argument, defend a truncation-based comparison criterion—evaluate each game only up to the agent’s capital limit, rank them by the worst reachable net position within that horizon, and reject any attempt to resolve the comparison by reordering the conditionally convergent series. Address the objection that without a definite expectation there is simply no rational basis for preferring one game to the other, and explain why path-dependent risk structure should matter normatively even when probabilistic aggregation collapses.
