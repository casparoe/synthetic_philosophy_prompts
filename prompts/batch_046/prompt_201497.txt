I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind, and I am constructing a diagnostic instrument for a graduate seminar on self-location rather than a conventional literature survey. I want a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts actual commitments about personal location in brain-transplant scenarios, but I am deliberately avoiding Dennett’s canonical Hamlet-and-Yorick framing because every student in the course can reproduce the standard answers from introductory texts from memory. Instead, I want the tree built around a hypothetical family of cases I have been developing: a gradual, module-by-module replacement of a subject’s cortical tissue with functionally identical synthetic equivalents, while the original biological brain remains intact and linked to the synthetic system via a high-bandwidth, real-time bidirectional interface. At intermediate stages—approximately thirty percent, seventy percent, and full replacement—there exist two physically separated cognitive systems, one embodied with biological tissue and one differently embodied with synthetic tissue, both maintaining access to a shared working-memory buffer but exhibiting differential latency in episodic retrieval.

The tree should begin with concrete decision nodes that do not mention philosophical labels. For instance, one node might ask: if both systems simultaneously issue a first-person claim to be the original subject, do you locate selfhood with the biological substrate, the synthetic substrate, both, or neither? Another might probe whether phenomenological continuity without direct causal integration is sufficient for co-location. The branches should eventually map onto at least five named positions: a strict brain-criterion view, an embodied-cognition view that denies disembodied selfhood altogether, a psychological-continuity view both with and without a no-branching clause, a narrative or socially mediated recognition view, and a deflationary option that treats the indexical question as malformed. I also want the logical space explicitly mapped, with unoccupied combinations noted and briefly diagnosed—whether the gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Crucially, no question should ask the respondent to self-identify with a label such as animalism or psychological-continuity theory; the instrument should classify solely by answers to the concrete cases. Could you design this taxonomy, specifying the exact questions at each branching point, the answer combinations that define each path, and a concise diagnosis of the empty branches?
