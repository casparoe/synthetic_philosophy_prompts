Consider the following argument about technological maturity and institutional form. (1) Define a singleton not by formal hierarchy but by a decision-integration threshold: whenever the correlation between local choices and global outcomes exceeds 0.95, the system functions as a single agent regardless of internal pluralism. (2) Surviving planetary-scale existential risks—such as misaligned autonomous systems or engineered biological threats—requires suppressing local deviation probabilities below 0.05. (3) Therefore any civilization that reaches technological maturity necessarily exceeds the integration threshold. (4) Hence every mature civilization is a singleton, even those retaining democratic or federal appearances. (5) This collapses the distinction between a singleton and a mature pluralistic world, turning the hypothesis into a tautology about risk management rather than an empirical prediction about institutional convergence. (6) If it is a tautology, it cannot explain why we observe no other mature civilizations, since pluralistic singletons could be widespread yet observationally identical to dispersed ones. (7) So the argument proves too much: either maturity is identical to being a singleton, which makes the concept unfalsifiable, or the integration requirement is false, which undermines the standard rationale for expecting convergence. Which step should be rejected? Specifically, is there a coherent account of how a pluralistic civilization could suppress global-risk deviations without crossing the decision-integration threshold? And what does giving up that suppression-requirement cost for explaining long-term survival?
