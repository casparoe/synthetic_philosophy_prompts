I need a decision tree for a philosophy-of-social-science seminar that sorts people on whether culture can actually be a cause, using Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis and the problem of explaining with values as the backbone. Dont just ask 'are you a weberian?' — build it from concrete branches that people can answer without knowing the labels.

Start with this case: two regions in 1600 have the same legal codes and resource access, but only one adopts Calvinist predestination theology and then develops sustained capital reinvestment and rational accounting. Does the theology explain the divergence?

First branch: what kind of explanation is it? (A) Direct motivation — salvation anxiety literally drives harder work and saving; (B) Elective affinity — the doctrine and capitalist rationality happen to fit together but neither forces the other; (C) Institutional selection — the doctrine makes certain economic rules feel legitimate so they survive; (D) Post-hoc rationalization — the theology came after the economic shift and justifies it.

Second branch: overdetermination. If historians later find the capital-reinvestment region also had a unique inheritance law, does that eliminate the cultural explanation, weaken it partially, or not affect it? This should split people who demand culture be the sole cause, those who accept configurational causation, and those who think any rival factor kills the cultural claim.

Third branch: the nature of values. Are values being used as efficient causes (like a physical push), as constitutive conditions (like rules that make an outcome possible), or as interpretive frames that just make actions meaningful? This separates people who reject value-explanation for lacking efficient causation from people who accept non-physical causal talk.

Fourth branch: reverse influence. Once capitalist institutions are locked in, do they retroactively reshape the religious values that originally aligned with them — say, turning the Protestant work ethic into secular utilitarianism? People who deny this co-evolution should land in a different leaf from those who think culture and economy mutually stabilize each other.

The leaves should include at least: strict materialist (culture is always superstructure), Weberian elective affinity (mutual fit, neither dominates), institutional-selection view (culture picks which rules stick), strong idealist (values drive material change on their own), and interpretive anti-causalist (culture gives meaning but doesnt cause outcomes in any scientific sense). Note any empty combinations — for example, can someone consistently hold that culture is only post-hoc rationalization but also that values have direct efficient-causal power? Flag that as incoherent. Also flag which pairs your tree cant really separate: maybe the institutional-selection view and a weak Weberian view overlap too much on these four questions, so say so.

Make it about eight to ten decision nodes, phrased so a grad student who has read some Weber but not the whole secondary literature can answer without knowing sociology jargon. Keep it casual — one typo is fine.
