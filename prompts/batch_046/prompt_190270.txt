I'm working through some conceptual difficulties in Adam Smith's moral psychology—specifically, the relationship between sympathy and the impartial spectator—that I think deserve more structured attention than they typically receive in either historical scholarship or applied ethics. Before I commit a seminar or a research group to one thread, I want a defended prioritization of which open lines of work matter most, and for whom.

To frame the problem concretely: in Smith's account, we calibrate our sentiments by observing how an impartial spectator responds to our passions, yet that spectator's own judgments seem to depend on prior sympathetic calibration within a community. This generates what I will call the recursive calibration problem—either an infinite regress of observers-observing-observers, or a need for some non-sympathetic ground for the first standard. Alongside this, there remain unsettled questions about whether sympathy in Smith is primarily an affective matching mechanism or an imaginative-cognitive projection; whether the impartial spectator should be read as an internalized psychological structure or as an idealized social other; and whether the framework can accommodate deep moral disagreement without collapsing into either relativism or an implausible universalism.

Assume my aim is not historical exegesis but using Smithian resources to address contemporary debates in moral psychology and normative ethics—particularly questions about emotional regulation, the social construction of moral norms, and the limits of intersubjective justification.

Please defend your ordering with explicit criteria—tractability, downstream impact, neglectedness relative to current literature, and fit with the practical aim I described.
