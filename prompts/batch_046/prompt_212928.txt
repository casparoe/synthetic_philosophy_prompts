Assume the dual role of a novelist who believes poetic form should perform philosophical labor and of a philosopher of mind familiar with Douglas Hofstadter's model of creativity as knob-twiddling in conceptual spaces, where a theme is not replaced but continuously transformed by adjusting its underlying parameters. Before drafting, engage in structured brainstorming: identify six conceptual dimensions—knobs—of a single theme such as recognition, mourning, or the boundary between pattern and noise, ensuring that at least one parameter is not a standard emotional or aesthetic category but something more granular like the slack between analogies or the error term that reorganizes perception. Then write a sestina: six stanzas of six lines, each stanza ending with the same six words rotated according to the classic pattern, followed by a three-line envoi in which all six end-words appear together. The rotation must do the conceptual work: each successive stanza should present the same thematic space with one parameter turned further, so that by the fourth stanza the original theme is still identifiable but structurally estranged, and the envoi should hold all six dimensions in suspension without resolving them into a thesis. The poem should be accurate enough that a reader acquainted with Hofstadter's mechanics of analogical thinking recognizes the operation in the form itself rather than in mere vocabulary; do not cite sources, list readings, or include bibliographic notes, since the exercise depends on first principles alone. Write with formal precision, allowing the sestina's repetitions to feel like a conceptual space being slowly reconfigured rather than like a refrain.
