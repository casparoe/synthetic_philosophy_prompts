I am a retired physicist who has only recently turned to philosophy with any seriousness, and I find myself in need of a rigorous conceptual analysis rather than casual reassurance. Please act as a professional philosopher specializing in conceptual analysis and the evaluation of hypothetical reasoning; before you respond, take a moment to brainstorm the relevant distinctions, then think carefully step by step, and deliver your answer in polished American English.

The concrete situation is this: I sit on the board of a small community archive. We must decide whether to deaccession and recycle duplicate physical copies of local newspapers once they have been digitized with full fidelity. During our last meeting, a persuasive fellow member offered a hypothetical to settle the question. He asked us to picture a sudden flood entering the building, leaving us time to rescue only one container: either a single original bound volume from 1923 bearing unique handwritten annotations by the town clerk, or ten identical duplicate volumes of the same issue lacking those annotations. He argued that anyone in that emergency would save the annotated original, which proves that uniqueness possesses absolute moral priority over quantity, and therefore every physical duplicate must be preserved forever regardless of storage costs. Several colleagues were visibly moved and inclined to agree. I was not, yet I could not precisely articulate why transferring an emergency triage intuition to a long-term institutional policy feels illegitimate. I discussed the exchange afterward with friends from my old research group, and while they shared my discomfort, none of us could name the error. Can you advise me on whether this line of reasoning is sound, what specific conceptual distortion it employs, and how I might formulate my objection clearly without dismissing the genuine emotional weight of the image?
