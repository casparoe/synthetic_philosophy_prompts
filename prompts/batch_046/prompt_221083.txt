You are a professional philosopher of law specializing in twentieth-century jurisprudence. Before giving your audit, briefly map the logical dependencies among the commitments listed below.

Here is my package of views on legal validity, interpretation, and morality. I want a consistency check—specifically which commitments genuinely pull against each other, which only appear to, and what the least costly revision is in each case.

(1) I accept the separation thesis in its standard form: law's existence and content are conceptually independent of its moral merit.

(2) I accept that any functioning legal system must include minimal rules protecting life, property, and promise—content Hart treated as a minimal natural law necessary for social survival, not as a moral constraint on law.

(3) I interpret Fuller's eight principles of legality—generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, consistency, feasibility, stability, and congruence between rule and official action—as purely functional prerequisites. A system violating them fails as law not because it is immoral but because it cannot perform the coordinating function definitive of legal institutions.

(4) In hard cases, where the core meaning of a rule is exhausted, I think judges necessarily engage in practical reasoning that draws on moral considerations, and I treat this as part of legal interpretation rather than an external moral correction.

(5) I hold that the rule of recognition is a conventional social rule grounded in the shared practices of officials; its criteria of validity contain no moral conditions.

(6) I think that in transitional justice contexts, courts may correctly deny legal status to formally enacted rules that enforce severe group-based subordination—treating them as nullities rather than valid but evil laws—without this judgment being reducible to moral disapproval.

(7) I also hold that the core/penumbra distinction applies not merely to linguistic meaning but to institutional authority itself: there is a conventionally fixed core of authority and a penumbral zone where authority is indeterminate, but filling that zone requires reasoning about the institutional point of authority, not moral reasoning about justice.

Audit this package. Rank the tensions by severity. Identify which pairs are genuinely incompatible and which are only apparently so—for instance, whether (3) and (4) conflict once (7) is in place. For each genuine tension, state the least costly revision and what it costs me elsewhere in the set. Be specific about whether (6) forces me into Fuller's natural-law position or whether a stable intermediate view—distinguishing institutional dysfunction from moral failure—can preserve both (1) and (6). Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Start with a brief dependency map, then the ranked audit, and think carefully about whether my reasoning in (7) is doing hidden work that undermines (5).
