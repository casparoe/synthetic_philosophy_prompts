hey so i've been chewing on Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment and i think i built an argument that either makes democratic constitutions totally illegitimate or lets founders lock in literally everything. can you tell me where it actually breaks?

(1) A Ulysses contract is rational only when you now have stable, reflective preferences and you know your future self will have distorted ones, so you bind yourself in advance.

(2) Constitutional precommitment — entrenched rights, supermajority rules, that kind of thing — works the same way at scale. The founding generation is just running a collective Ulysses contract, protecting future citizens from their own temporary, impulsive majorities.

(3) The justification here is structural, not about any particular right. Wherever future voters might act on short-term passion rather than long-term value, precommitment is in principle justified.

(4) So there's no principled line between entrenching free speech and entrenching, say, a specific tax rate or immigration quota, as long as teh founders sincerely believe later generations would regret changing them.

(5) In fact, if the whole point is to stop future reopening, then a "closure clause" that makes the precommitment itself unamendable is actually teh purest expression of this logic — it protects future people even from teh impulsive desire to amend.

(6) But if closure clauses are legitimate, democratic self-correction is dead. The founding moment decides everything permanently, and later generations are just living inside someone else's Ulysses contract.

(7) That can't be right. We think constitutions should be amendable, and we think democracy means future generations actually get a real say, not just a choice inside pre-set walls.

(8) So either collective Ulysses reasoning collapses entirely — which kills (2) — or democratic constitutions are basically a sham.

If i have to choose i'd rather keep (2) and (3), because constitutional precommitment for basic rights still feels okay. But then what's stopping the slide to (5) and (6)? Is there a hidden equivocation between binding your own future self and binding strangers who don't share your identity? Or is the mistake somewhere in (3) about treating structural justification as uniform across domains? I want to know what i'm paying if i hold onto the parts i like.
