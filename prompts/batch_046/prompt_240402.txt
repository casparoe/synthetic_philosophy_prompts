I've been thinking about that argument where people say consciousness is basically an illusion—like, your brain tells you there's this rich movie playing inside, but actually it's just computing outputs without any real inner show. I keep running into one specific version of the pushback and I'm not sure if it has it's own name.

The setup is this: the illusionist says there's no phenomenal experience, just cognitive machinery that produces false self-reports—kind of like a broken thermometer that displays '72 degrees' but there's no actual temperature being measured, just a malfunctioning needle. But then the critic asks: okay, but an illusion usually needs someone to be fooled. If I think there's a pink elephant in the room, I'm the one being deceived. So if there's genuinely no subject having experiences, then what's being deceived? The machinery isn't 'experiencing' a false movie; it's just generating judgments. That seems less like an illusion and more like... I don't know, a mislabeled file? Then the illusionist responds that the 'someone' being fooled is just another layer of representation—there's no homunculus, just more machinery misrepresenting the first layer. But then the critic comes back with: if every layer is just machinery with no inner life, then you've explained away the audience entirely, and 'illusion' stops being the right metaphor altogether.

I've seen this called the 'no audience' problem or the reflexivity objection, but those feel like broad labels. Is there a more precise term for this specific move—where the illusionist denies the very subject required for an illusion to occur, and critics say that collapses illusion into mere error? And how does it relate to debates about whether introspection can be systematically wrong without being experienced as wrong? I don't want just a bare name; I want to know if this knot has been carved out with boundary conditions, or if I'm just reinventing a well-known wheel with a broken thermometer analogy.
