I am applying for a one-semester independent-study fellowship (approximately 4,000 words, four months) in philosophy of science, and I need a polished two-page proposal. My core idea is that the classical problem of verisimilitude—whether one false scientific theory can be closer to the truth than another—has stalled because every formal reconstruction treats a theory as a set of propositions and truth as correspondence to a complete state-description. I want to investigate whether the problem can be reopened if we reject that ontology and instead model theories as constraint-structures: rules that restrict the space of possible observations rather than lists of true or false sentences. The precise research question is whether there exists a coherent, non-trivial metric of proximity between false constraint-structures and the true one, defined not by counting shared true propositions but by measuring the overlap, within an appropriate observation-space, of the regions each structure permits. Specifically, can this metric recover intuitive judgments about scientific progress—for example, that Newtonian mechanics lies closer to general relativity than Aristotelian physics does—without smuggling in hidden value judgments about which constraints are epistemically central?

Why existing answers leave this open: Popper’s logical account and its successors all presuppose a propositional framework, which makes them vulnerable to well-known impossibility results (that any measure defined over sentences either rewards falsehoods or collapses into trivial ordering) and also renders them conceptually unable to distinguish theories that share predictive profiles but differ in explanatory architecture. By shifting the unit of analysis to constraint-spaces, the project tests whether those impossibility results depend essentially on the propositional premise rather than on the underlying intuition about progress itself.

The approach, month by month: Month one, formalize toy constraint-structures for three simple physical domains—projectile motion, heat flow, and orbital dynamics—and define an intersection-based distance metric on the observation-spaces they generate. Month two, apply the metric to order false theories within each domain and check whether the ordering aligns with pre-theoretic scientific intuition. Month three, examine whether the reconstructed metric avoids the standard impossibility arguments by violating their additivity-over-sentences premise, and identify whether any implicit normative weighting of particular constraints is required to preserve the ordering. Month four, draft the conceptual defense, including an explicit failure condition: if the metric collapses so that all false theories are equidistant, or if it turns out to depend on an unstated judgment about the importance of specific constraints, then the problem has not been solved but merely relocated.
