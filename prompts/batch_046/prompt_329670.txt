As a novelist working on a narrative about lost archives, I find my own judgments about forgeries, copies, and reconstructed artifacts keep shifting in ways I cannot justify. Here are my settled verdicts, followed by the case I keep revisiting.

A museum displays a flawless 20th-century copy of a destroyed 17th-century painting, honestly labeled. I think it should remain on the wall: its power to move viewers is intact, and nothing in the aesthetic experience depends on the painter's hand having touched the canvas. A scholar forges a "newly discovered" poem in the exact voice of a dead poet, using only imagery and meter authentic to the period; before exposure, critics read it as part of the poet's oeuvre. Once revealed, I feel its value collapses—not merely its price, but its entitlement to be interpreted as extending that poet's mind. The words are unchanged, yet they no longer belong to the tradition they imitated. A biographer reconstructs a conversation that never occurred, stitching together sentences both figures actually wrote elsewhere, and prints it in quotation marks as if it were a transcript. I think this is graver than writing an openly fictional scene, because it borrows the epistemic authority of direct speech without warning. By contrast, a novelist publishes a "found manuscript"—letters and diaries entirely invented—clearly marked as fiction; I regard this as not only permissible but capable of reaching a historical truth that archives obscure. Finally, a restorer fills missing sections of a damaged fresco with new pigment matched to the original, leaving the intervention visible only to experts; I still call the result the original work, though wounded. But if the restorer replaced a missing central panel with a new painting that completed the composition, I think we now possess a hybrid, not a restored original.

The tension I cannot resolve concerns a sound engineer who reconstructs a "lost" symphony from a dead composer's fragmentary sketches, composing roughly seventy percent of the orchestration in the composer's documented style. The recording is released under the composer's name, with only a footnote about the reconstruction. Sometimes I treat this as a legitimate artistic completion, analogous to my acceptance of the honest copy or the labeled fiction; other times it feels like the biographer's false quotation, appropriating an authority that does not belong to the reconstructor. I keep reversing myself.

Is there a single principle—perhaps distinguishing substitution for absence from contamination of presence, or separating aesthetic value from evidential entitlement—that captures my first four verdicts and also resolves the symphony case?
