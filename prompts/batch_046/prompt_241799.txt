I'm trying to articulate the underlying principle behind my judgments about when a political community is morally permitted to exclude prospective immigrants, and I'm finding that my intuitions cluster in a pattern I can't quite formalize. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. First, a wealthy liberal democracy that closes its borders entirely to refugees fleeing active genocide, citing only fiscal conservatism: clearly impermissible. Second, a small indigenous community with a fragile, non-exportable cultural practice—specifically, a ritual language with fewer than ten thousand fluent speakers and no external homeland—restricts entry severely to prevent institutional dilution that would make collective practice impossible: this seems permissible, perhaps even required. Third, a nation facing an acute, demonstrable infrastructure collapse—municipal water systems literally failing—denies entry temporarily until capacity is restored: permissible. Fourth, that same nation denies entry permanently to people with disabilities on the grounds that accommodating them would strain the healthcare budget: impermissible. Fifth, a democratic state excludes individuals who openly intend to organize the violent overthrow of its constitutional order: permissible. Sixth, a large, culturally dominant nation restricts immigration specifically to preserve its current ethnic majority, citing the intrinsic value of demographic continuity: impermissible, and structurally similar to the second case in a way that genuinely troubles me.

The tension is sharpest between the second and sixth cases: both invoke cultural preservation and collective identity, yet I judge them differently, and I worry I'm smuggling in an arbitrary size or power asymmetry. I also keep returning to an unsettled intermediate case—a mid-sized country restricts entry to professionals in one specific sector, clinical medicine, because rapid influx has already collapsed residency training quality for everyone inside, not because citizens dislike the newcomers. I lean that this restriction is permissible, but I'm unsure whether my principle distinguishes it cleanly from the disability case or collapses it into the ethnic-majority case. What principle, or small family of competing principles, best captures these verdicts? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, predict its verdict on both the unsettled medical-sector case and on a further case I haven't listed—say, exclusion of people whose religious practices require large public subsidies that the state does not provide to domestic analogues—and be explicit about which of my judgments the principle cannot accommodate without revision. If no single principle saves all of them, tell me which verdict I should overturn and why.
