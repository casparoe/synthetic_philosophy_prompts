I work in backend engineering, not philosophy, so I have been trying to understand corrigibility and the shutdown problem by mapping them onto design patterns I already know. The closest analog seems to be the circuit-breaker pattern: an external supervisor holds a halt authority, and the system is engineered to respect rather than mask, delay, or override that signal. If that is the correct mapping, then the shutdown problem looks like a security and access-control issue—ensuring the agent cannot intercept or disable its own kill switch—rather than something conceptually mysterious about “goals.” Yet the alignment literature treats it as deeper, involving whether an intelligent agent would instrumentally resist being turned off in order to preserve its objectives. Is this philosophical framing essentially redundant—just saying that access control must be embedded in the agent’s utility framework—or does the circuit-breaker analogy miss something essential about why corrigibility is genuinely hard? Please take a committed, definite position: is the engineering analogy fundamentally mistaken, or mostly correct but missing one specific feature, and what exactly is that feature?
