I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy preparing the metaethics module for our department’s PhD comprehensive examination. I need a single 90-minute essay question on evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics that cannot be answered by summarizing the standard genealogical challenges associated with Street or Joyce. Instead, the question should force candidates to work through a specific conceptual tension that is rarely made explicit: evolutionary debunkers claim that the causal etiology of our moral beliefs—shaped by selective pressures for social cooperation rather than by tracking mind-independent moral facts—generates a form of epistemic defeat. Yet this move appears to presuppose a reliabilist or etiology-sensitive account of justification according to which causal origin is relevant to epistemic status, an assumption that is neither default in moral epistemology nor obviously compatible with the normative framework the debunker herself employs.

Please draft the exam question so that it requires candidates to (i) reconstruct the debunking argument with this epistemological presupposition made explicit; (ii) apply the argument differentially to “thin” moral concepts (e.g., wrongness, obligation) versus “thick” moral concepts (e.g., cruelty, betrayal, honor), explaining why evolutionary continuity might affect them asymmetrically; and (iii) evaluate whether the debunker can restrict the scope of defeat to first-order moral beliefs without also undermining the second-order epistemic norms she relies on to mount her case—specifically, assessing whether such a restriction is principled or ad hoc given evolutionary continuity between moral cognition and epistemic cognition. The question should ask them to conclude whether the reliabilist framing saves the debunker from self-defeat or collapses the argument into circularity.

Please provide: (a) the exam question itself, clearly worded for a 90-minute written response; (b) a model answer of approximately 650 words that demonstrates each of the required moves with philosophical precision; and (c) a marking scheme out of 25 points that separates an A-range answer from a B-range answer. The scheme should list the specific moves an A answer must contain—explicit identification of the reliabilist presupposition, correct application of the thick/thin distinction to the debunking scope, and a diagnosis of the ad hoc restriction problem—together with the point values for each. It should also specify the characteristic errors that should cost marks, such as conflating evolutionary influence with a genetic fallacy, treating the thick/thin distinction as merely linguistic rather than epistemically significant, or ignoring the self-defeat structure entirely. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and frame everything so that it can be addressed without access to external sources.
