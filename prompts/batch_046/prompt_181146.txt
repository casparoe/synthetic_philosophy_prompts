As someone with a background in software engineering and no formal training in philosophy or international law, I want to understand the 1994 Rwandan genocide as a case study in what I would call a classification-boundary failure. Raphael Lemkin originally conceived genocide not as a single physical act but as a coordinated plan operating across multiple domains of group life—political, social, cultural, economic, biological, physical, religious, and moral—with the aim of destroying the essential foundations of a national group. The 1948 UN Genocide Convention, by contrast, adopted a far narrower schema: it requires proof of specific intent to destroy a protected group, in whole or in part, through killing, serious bodily harm, or closely related physical measures.

Analyze the international response during April and May 1994 through this tension. At the time, UN officials and several member states systematically avoided the genocide label, describing the violence instead as tribal warfare, spontaneous civil conflict, or reciprocal ethnic fighting—classifications that did not trigger the Convention's mandatory obligations. Was this refusal purely strategic evasion, or did it also reflect a structural mismatch between the observable events and the Convention's required fields? Consider that the killings were rapid and ethnically targeted, yet contemporaneous arguments emphasized the absence of centralized state documentation, the presence of armed Hutu resistance, and the difficulty of isolating intent to destroy the group in its own right from broader political violence.

Then examine the reverse direction: what does Rwanda reveal about the politics of naming atrocities? Does the Convention's narrow physical-destruction schema create a predictable false-negative problem, allowing group destruction to proceed through methods—such as the systematic dismantling of political institutions, cultural practices, and economic infrastructure—that fall outside the validated category? And does the eventual ICTR jurisprudence correct this by genuinely expanding the interpretive framework, or does it merely stretch the concept of intent within the same restricted schema without recovering Lemkin's broader taxonomy?

Finally, assess whether the episode supports Lemkin's claim that cultural and political destruction are not moral embellishments but empirically necessary conditions for recognizing how group annihilation actually operates, or whether Rwanda ultimately demonstrates that a tightly bounded legal definition, despite its false-negative risks, remains the only enforceable standard.
