I am constructing a novel built upon nested unreliable narrators who recount successive episodes of irrecoverable loss, and in preparing its emotional architecture I have returned repeatedly to the philosophical problem commonly designated the paradox of tragedy: why audiences deliberately seek artworks that elicit grief, dread, and sorrow when those same affective states are systematically avoided in practical life. Through my own reflection, I have arrived at what I term a containment thesis — the claim that negative affect is transmuted into aesthetic reward only under conditions of strict diegetic quarantine, wherein the emotional response is sealed within a recognized fictional frame that prevents behavioral spillover and practical commitment. From this premise, I draw a further inference for narrative craft: that any technique which erodes this boundary — whether autofictional disclosure, direct second-person interpellation, or interactive branching that assigns agency to the reader — must necessarily dissolve the paradox itself, converting tragic pleasure into mere unmediated distress. I wish to test this reasoning before finalizing the structure of the manuscript. Does the containment thesis accurately describe the mechanism by which tragedy produces enjoyment? And if the diegetic frame is genuinely porous, does that porosity destroy tragic affect, or might it generate a different, equally valid modality of aesthetic experience?
