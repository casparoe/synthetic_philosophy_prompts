I am drafting a workshop for fellow novelists—mostly literary-fiction writers with little formal philosophy—who are developing historical novels about the collapse of empires. The conceptual knot I want them to grasp is what it means to hold a nation responsible for a war, using the First World War and the Fischer controversy over German war aims as the working case. I need them to see that attributing blame to "Germany" is not a scaled-up version of blaming a single person, and that the Fischer thesis raises problems of institutional persistence rather than collective guilt.

Plan the session's explanatory arc: what idea has to be in place before the question of national blame even makes sense—perhaps the distinction between individual moral agency and what we might call diachronic institutional continuity? In what precise order should the pieces come: the Fischer claim about state-level war aims, the objection that a nation is merely an aggregate of individuals who have long since died, and the intermediate framework that allows the two to meet without collapsing into either?

Identify the one concrete historical scenario that should carry the discussion—something specific enough to ground the abstraction but stripped of details that would turn the session into a historiography seminar. Specify what this example must include (for instance, the persistence of administrative structures across regime change) and what it must deliberately lack.

Then map the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring: they will likely conflate national responsibility with collective punishment, assume that if no individual citizen intended the war then no blame attaches, or treat Fischer's argument as a claim about popular sentiment rather than state structure. For each misconception, specify exactly where in the sequence to intercept it—before introducing Fischer, immediately after, or only once the institutional framework is in place—and how to absorb it without losing the narrative thread.

Finally, suggest one question I can ask at the end to determine whether they have grasped the conceptual structure rather than merely adopted a posture—perhaps requiring them to apply the framework to a case where institutional continuity is broken, or to identify precisely what would cause the attribution of national responsibility to fail. And be explicit about what I should openly refuse to explain at this level—likely the finer points of just-war theory, the full historiography of the Fischer debate, and any reading list—and say so outright.
