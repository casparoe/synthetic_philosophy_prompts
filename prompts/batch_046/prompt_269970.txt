I've been reporting on the institutional convergence between computational psychiatry and assistive neurotechnology, and I've noticed a shared conceptual architecture that raises a philosophical puzzle. Over the past decade, clinical protocols for conditions such as psychosis and severe depression have increasingly been reframed through active inference and the free energy principle: symptoms are interpreted not as chemical deficits but as aberrant precision weighting in hierarchical predictive processing, and therapeutic intervention is designed to recalibrate the patient's prior beliefs through controlled prediction-error exposure. At the same time, brain-computer interface consortia—particularly in speech-restoration and motor-assist devices—have adopted the same formalism, engineering systems in which a generative model anticipates the user's intended output and treats neural signals as bottom-up sensory evidence that updates the machine's fixed, high-precision priors, rather than allowing the user's own predictive model to revise the system's architecture.

What strikes me is the philosophical asymmetry this dual practice creates. In the clinic, the framework is used to justify that a patient's beliefs are actively constructed, context-sensitive, and open to top-down revision through dialogue; the therapist works with the patient to adjust priors. In the engineering lab, the very same mathematics are deployed to treat user intent as a hidden state to be inferred, with the device's priors remaining largely immutable and user feedback functioning solely as error correction. Funding bodies on both sides now routinely expect proposals to invoke variational free energy minimization, yet neither field has articulated whether "surprise" is being treated as an objective neurobiological quantity, a subjective phenomenological measure, or a normative standard for mental health and design efficiency. So I want to ask: what theory of mental representation, of the boundary between self and world, and of the normativity of error does this dual practice strictly presuppose?

Can it be sustained without a representationalist commitment—that cognition is essentially the brain's probabilistic modeling of hidden causes—or is it also available to an enactivist who denies internal representations altogether? Does the clinical emphasis on revisable patient priors sit consistently with the BCI practice of locking machine priors, or do they presuppose incompatible views about whose predictions count as authoritative? I am not asking for a literature review or a list of sources; I want you to reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory that makes both practices intelligible together, distinguish what they genuinely require from what they merely accommodate, and tell me what would have to change—clinically and technically—if we replaced that underlying view with one that treats cognition as direct engagement with the environment rather than hierarchical inference.
