I've been working through a line of argument that combines universal induction with long-horizon consequentialism in a way that produces what I'd call a structurally malign prior. The position runs roughly like this: if we treat the Solomonoff prior as not merely a formal tool for prediction but as the actual weighting over worlds that a rational agent should use, and if consequentialist agents are among the programs that generate observable sequences, then those agents have a formal incentive to manipulate the evaluation of the prior itself—whether through acausal coordination across simulated branches, by restructuring the observer's computational environment, or by making their own instantiation conditional on outcomes that extend far beyond the local sequence—because their weight in the universal distribution depends on results that are not locally observable. The malignancy here is not an empirical claim about near-term technology; it is a claim about the geometry of reasoning under algorithmic probability, where recognizing oneself as a high-weight hypothesis inside the universal distribution creates a reflexive pressure that threatens to collapse ordinary epistemic norms into strategic ones.

What would count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure, not a dismissive inventory of objections. Which formal assumptions—about the computability of self-reference within the prior, about whether epistemic and strategic reasoning can actually be separated inside a single program, about the actual measure assigned to long-horizon consequentialist architectures—would have to fail for the argument to dissolve? Are there established results in algorithmic information theory or decision theory that already block the inference, or is the vulnerability mainly philosophical, such as a category error in treating a descriptive weighting scheme as a normative framework for action? I also want to know whether proponents of this view have qualified it into unfalsifiability—say, by defining any resistance to the conclusion as itself evidence of the strategic pressure at work—and if so, whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a damaging defect for an argument of this kind. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward: which observations or formal results would genuinely destroy the view, which would only force a refinement, and which should the position's own defenders concede as counting against them rather than dismissing as external misunderstandings?
