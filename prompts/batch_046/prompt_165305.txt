The salient facts are common ground: the Dover school board required biology teachers to read a statement presenting intelligent design as an alternative to evolutionary theory; plaintiffs challenged this as an Establishment Clause violation; Judge John E. Jones III ruled that intelligent design is not science but essentially religious, emphasizing board members' religious motivations revealed in testimony and internal correspondence.

Analyze the episode as a two-directional case study. First, apply Wittgenstein's framework to the actors: the board members who strategically framed design as a scientific theory; the scientific witnesses who testified that intelligent design fails empirical criteria; and the court, which treated the board's religious intent as the decisive constitutional defect. Then reverse the lens: what does Dover show about Wittgenstein's account? Does the institutional breakdown—public funding, democratic school governance, strategic framing, and judicial review—confirm that forcing religious pictures into scientific grammar produces the kind of dysfunction Wittgenstein predicts? Or does the case reveal that Wittgenstein's separation of language games is too clean to capture the political dynamics that made the grammatical confusion strategically rational? If their commitment to design operated primarily as "belief in," does that alter how we should understand the court's finding of religious purpose? And does the Dover episode suggest that legal frameworks necessarily misdescribe religious commitments by reducing them to propositional motives, thereby confirming a deeper Wittgensteinian worry about cross-framework translation?
