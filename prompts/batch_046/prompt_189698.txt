In my work as a doctoral student in moral philosophy with a focus on comparative psychology, I have been struck by how little progress has occurred in the debate over whether cross-cultural differences in folk morality—particularly the well-documented divergence between WEIRD and non-WEIRD populations in the weighting of harm, purity, loyalty, and authority—have any bearing on the objectivity of moral claims. The empirical patterns themselves are not in serious dispute: both sides acknowledge that Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic samples tend to privilege individual harm-based reasoning, while many other populations distribute normative weight more evenly across relational and group-binding concerns. Yet for years, moral realists have cited cross-cultural convergence on basic harm prohibitions as evidence of a universal moral substrate, while relativists and pluralists have cited the same divergence in purity and loyalty judgments as proof that moral cognition is locally constructed. Despite this shared empirical ground, neither camp has modified its position in response to the other, and the literature feels less like an advancing argument than a stable partition.

My hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily about empirical facts or about the definition of objectivity, but about a hidden, largely unexamined commitment I will call normative calibration: the assumption that philosophical moral theory ought to be calibrated against the statistical distribution of folk moral judgments across cultures, and that the suitability of such calibration is what is actually at stake in the objectivity debate. On this reading, realists are defending the claim that there is a stable, culture-transcendent target—centered on harm—against which theories can be calibrated, while anti-realists are denying that any such stable distribution exists beyond local convention. But if this is the real disagreement, then both sides may be answering different questions under one heading: one about whether moral truths exist, the other about whether folk intuition is an appropriate evidential base for normative philosophy.

Please diagnose this impasse. Is it verbal, substantive but masked, or a case of two disciplines—descriptive psychology and normative ethics—addressing different questions with the same vocabulary? Do the sides differ about facts, about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data for moral theory, or about bedrock intuitions regarding the authority of common moral sense? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question—perhaps about the evolutionary or functional basis of moral cognition—that is actually doing the work? And given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction that separates calibration from objectivity, an empirical finding that both sides would have to accommodate, a formal argument showing that calibration fails for independent reasons, or a demonstration that the question is ill-posed?
