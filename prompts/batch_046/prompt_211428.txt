I'm working through a set of moral-psychology cases with a colleague who is quite consistent but reluctant to articulate her general principles. She has rendered clear verdicts on four constructed scenarios involving a runaway trolley and five people at risk. First, she approves pulling a lever that diverts the trolley onto a side track where one person stands. Second, she rejects pushing a large bystander off a footbridge to stop the trolley directly. Third, in a loop variant—where the side track curves back toward the main line and the single person on it is heavy enough that the trolley would strike them and stop—she still approves pulling the lever. Fourth, she rejects pressing a remote button that opens a trapdoor beneath an unsuspecting person standing over the track, causing them to fall and block the trolley, even though no lever touches the tracks.

I would like you to treat these four judgments as a track record and diagnose the underlying rule structure, not merely deliver a yes-or-no answer. Specifically, identify at least two conceptually distinct rules that jointly accommodate all four verdicts—one of which should engage with something like the distinction between redirecting an existing threat and introducing an independent obstacle, and another that should engage with the doctrine of double effect or with whether the victim's body is being used as the specific causal mechanism of rescue. Then apply both rules to a fifth case: there is a switch that sends the trolley onto a dead-end spur; the spur has no natural stopping mechanism, and the trolley will continue through and eventually reach the five unless it strikes the one person who happens to be standing there, whose body is the only thing that can halt it. You know this and pull the lever specifically so that the impact with that person will serve as the brake. For each rule, state whether she should find this permissible or impermissible; then offer your best-guess verdict about what she actually would say, with reasons for preferring that rule over the other; and finally propose one further hypothetical case—structurally different from all five above—that would most sharply discriminate between the two rules you have identified.

Please keep the analysis focused on the logic of her judgments rather than on historical citations, bibliographic references, or empirical surveys; I am interested in the conceptual architecture of the disagreement itself and in how the rules you identify would extend to genuinely novel structural variations.
