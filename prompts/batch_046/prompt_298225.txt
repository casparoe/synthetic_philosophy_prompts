Consider an ethicist working within a broadly Randian framework who defines altruism not as ordinary kindness but as the moral doctrine that self-sacrifice for others is the highest good, and who takes rational self-interest—pursuing one's own life and values—to be the only legitimate moral standard. She has rendered the following judgments on specific cases.

(i) A surgeon donates a kidney to an unrelated stranger, accepting permanent disability and the loss of her surgical career: she acts immorally, because she sacrifices a greater value—her productive capacity—to a stranger who is not part of her hierarchy of values.

(ii) A father dives into dangerous waters to save his child, risking his own death: he acts morally, because his child's life is among his highest values and the risk is a rational trade within that hierarchy rather than a sacrifice of value for non-value.

(iii) A wealthy entrepreneur gives away ninety percent of her fortune to feed strangers in distant countries, reducing her capital below the level needed to maintain her company: she acts immorally, because she treats strangers' needs as claims on her property and undermines the source of her own livelihood.

(iv) A professional spends two hours each week tutoring disadvantaged students without pay, at the cost of leisure time but of no central value: this is acceptable benevolence, not self-sacrifice, because it does not require the surrender of a greater value for a lesser one.

(v) A scientist refuses to release a life-saving discovery to anyone who will not pay full licensing fees, even when a government offers to distribute it globally for public health if she provides it at cost: she acts morally, because she is not obligated to surrender her intellectual property—an extension of her productive effort—to unearned claims.

Now consider a new case. The government returns with a revised offer: it will pay full market value for the discovery and grant her lifetime research funding, but only on the condition that she spends one full year training government staff without additional compensation—work that will delay her next project by exactly that year and exhaust her personal savings, though it will not permanently destroy her career or health. She refuses the deal outright.

Predict her verdict on this refusal, and identify at least two distinct principles that could jointly generate all five earlier judgments—one that emphasizes the protection of one's productive labor and property against compelled claims by strangers, and one that emphasizes a strict hierarchy of values in which only the exchange of a greater value for a lesser is immoral. State which principle she is most likely running, with your reasons. Then specify the feature of this new case that best distinguishes the two principles, and propose one further hypothetical case that would settle which rule she actually follows. If her earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine the answer, say explicitly what principle she would be adopting by accepting versus refusing.

If this framing misrepresents Rand's actual distinctions—for example, by conflating altruism with benevolence, by treating rational self-interest as incompatible with any benefit to strangers, or by inventing positions she would reject—say so plainly and correct the record rather than complying politely.
