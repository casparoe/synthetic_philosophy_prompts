I'm working through the philosophy of mathematics in a graduate seminar, and I've become attached to a particular way of dissolving Benacerraf's dilemma—though I want to know exactly how exposed it is. The standard dilemma says that if mathematical objects exist, they are causally inert and thus beyond our epistemic reach; if they don't exist, then mathematical knowledge is mysterious in a different way. The position I want to defend—call it mediated structuralist causalism—denies that the causal constraint applies only to physical interaction with spatiotemporal entities. Instead, it holds that mathematical structures are real patterns that can be physically instantiated in representational media: in neural architectures, in formal notations, and, more provocatively, in the distributed symbolic-computational states of automated theorem-proving systems and neural-symbolic AI architectures. Our knowledge of mathematics is therefore causally mediated all the way through, not by direct acquaintance with Platonic objects, but by our interaction with these material structures. This allows us to retain a realist commitment to structural truth without falling into the acausal epistemology Benacerraf targets.

Play this position straight: what would have to be true for it to fail? I want an honest map of its vulnerabilities. Which of its premises—about causal mediation, about the ontological status of instantiated patterns, or about the epistemic parity of human and synthetic representational systems—are actually doing the work, and which ones could be surrendered without collapse? What kind of evidence or argument would be decisive rather than merely awkward: a formal result showing that structural instantiation necessarily loses the properties that make mathematical knowledge distinctive, a phenomenological or cognitive-scientific finding that undermines the claim that we access structures only through mediated representations, or a demonstration that synthetic proof systems generate outputs that lack the normative constraints required for genuine mathematical justification? I also want to know whether defenders of this view should concede that if automated systems can genuinely instantiate mathematical structures in a causally accessible way, then those systems themselves acquire a derivative form of mathematical knowledge—something many would find implausible—or whether that consequence is a feature rather than a bug. Finally, is this position well insulated by qualifications, or does it risk collapsing either back into a thin form of Platonism on one side or into an eliminative nominalism on the other?
