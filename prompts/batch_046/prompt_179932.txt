The debate over Avicenna’s flying-man thought experiment—where a person suspended in void, stripped of all sensory, proprioceptive, and mnemonic contact with a body, would nonetheless affirm his own existence—has divided interpreters for decades into two camps that never seem to converge. One camp reads the scenario as an argument for ontological independence: the soul is a substance (jawhar) that can, in principle, exist and be aware of itself without any corporeal substrate, so the flying man anticipates a form of substance dualism. The other camp reads it as an argument for epistemic priority only: Avicenna intends merely to show that first-person self-awareness is immediate and non-inferential, not mediated by bodily observation or sensory channels, without committing himself to the modal claim that the soul can actually subsist apart from the body. Both sides cite the same metaphysical and psychological writings, both acknowledge that Avicenna uses substance-language elsewhere, and neither has persuaded the other in recent scholarship.

I want a diagnosis of this stall rather than another adjudication. I hypothesize that part of the impasse is verbal—centered on an ambiguity in what “independence” is meant to prove—while another part is substantive, hiding the fact that the two camps are answering different questions under one heading: one about the modal ontology of the soul, one about the structure of self-consciousness. But I also want you to consider a less-discussed possibility: that there is a hidden formal question doing the work, namely whether the argument tacitly assumes a principle of identity across modal variations (that the “I” affirmed in the suspended state is identical to the embodied “I”), and that resolving the debate requires not more textual commentary but a decision about whether that principle is defensible within Avicenna’s broader hylomorphism. So tell me, with commitment: is the disagreement mainly verbal about “independence,” mainly a case of two groups answering different questions, or mainly an unacknowledged formal dependency? Support your diagnosis by specifying exactly what each side says—about substance, about sensory mediation, about the scope of the conclusion—that supports your reading. Then say what would actually count as progress given your diagnosis: a textual discovery, a formal reconstruction of the argument’s suppressed premises, a conceptual distinction that splits the question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. Take a definite position; do not merely map the options. Finally, inject and assess one niche modification: suppose the flying man retains a minimal interoceptive awareness of spatial orientation but no exteroceptive or bodily-image awareness—does this variant help one camp, hurt the other, or reveal that both have been assuming an overly binary model of embodied versus disembodied awareness?
