I'm a PhD student in metaphysics and philosophy of language, and I've been asked to deliver the wedding toast for my older sister and her partner. The audience will be family and close friends—mostly non-philosophers—but there will be a few academics present who will catch a precise reference. The couple has asked that I avoid generic sentimentality; they want something that takes the idea of their union seriously rather than decorating it. I want the toast to be structured by Bradley's regress and the problem of the unity of the proposition—not as an illustrated lecture, but as a live constraint on what can be honestly said. Specifically, praising a marriage risks either treating the partners as two isolated relata with an external relation tacked on (which feels metaphysically false to the experience of long commitment) or launching into an infinite regress of explanations for what binds them together (the love binds them, but what binds the love to them, and so on). I need a five-minute toast that navigates this tension: it must affirm a genuine unity without reifying that unity as a separate third thing, and it must avoid the regress of ever-deeper explanations. The speech should comfort a room that does not know Bradley, yet it should not say anything that a metaphysician in the room would find careless or that the couple would hear as reducing their bond to a relation. Please write it without citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts; the idea should be lived in the speech rather than footnoted.
