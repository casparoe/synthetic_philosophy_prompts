I've been thinking about moral learning from biograpy - not just saints' lives but also modern autoficton and political memoir. There's this tension between Plutarch's parallel-lives method (compare virtues across subjects) and the worry that hagiography flattens people into moral cartoons. If you were advising a grad student with three years, which open questions in this space are actually worth the time?

Specifically, I'm torn between three lines: (1) the psychology of narrative identification - do readers learn by seeing themselves in the subject or by critically comparing? (2) whether moral improvement requires "flaw visibility" - seeing the subject actually fail - and whether hagiography's suppression of flaws kills the pedagogical effect; and (3) this weird phenomenon I call "hagiographic drift," where even initially critical biograpies get read as devotional texts over generations, which might mean the form itself corrupts the content.

Which of these is upstream? Like, if we nailed whether emulation or comparison is the mechanism, would that settle the flaw-visibility question, or are they independant? And does any of it matter for current virtue-ethics debates about moral exemplars, or is that literature running on parallel tracks?

Also, there's the tractability issue. The identification mechanism feels like it needs empirical psychology, not philosophy. The drift phenomenon feels like reception history - tractable with archives but maybe just antiquarian. Where should a philosopher put effort?

I want a real prioritization with criteria: importance, tractability for a philosopher without a lab, and how many other questions depend on this one. And tell me what evidence over the next few years would move one of these up or down. Don't just list them - defend an ordering.
