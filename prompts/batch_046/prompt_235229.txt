I am grading take-home exams for a graduate seminar on rationality and institutional design. One question asked students to explain Derek Parfit's hitchhiker case—specifically, how a rational agent might need to cultivate a disposition that is directly irrational in particular circumstances in order to secure a larger strategic benefit, and why this does not reduce without remainder to ordinary indirect rationality. The second part directed students to apply that framework to the institutional design of central-bank independence, with explicit instructions to treat the democratic government as the hitchhiker, international bond markets as the driver that grants credibility only in exchange for promised non-interference, and the constitutional delegation of monetary authority as the promised payment that becomes tempting to withhold once credibility is secured. Students were warned not to confuse the analogy with a prisoner's dilemma, a repeated-game reputation model, or a simple principal-agent problem.

Here is one response, which strikes me as B-range work but contains slips I want precisely identified. The student writes: "Parfit's hitchhiker is picked up by an alien driver who demands payment after being dropped off. Once arrived, the hitchhiker has no reason to pay; anticipating this, the driver will not stop unless she believes he is the kind of person who pays even when it is against his interest. Parfit argues that it can be rational to cultivate exactly this irrational disposition—being disposed to pay despite lacking reason—because the strategic benefit of being picked up outweighs the cost. This is rational irrationality: rational adoption of direct irrationality. Applying this to central-bank independence, the government is the hitchhiker that wants the ride of low-inflation credibility, and the democratic electorate is the driver that grants legitimacy only if it expects the government to honor its commitment not to interfere. Once credibility is established and inflation is low, the government faces direct incentives to intervene for electoral gain, just as the hitchhiker faces direct incentives not to pay. To solve this, the government should make itself institutionally irrational—delegating authority to an independent bank—so that markets and voters believe it cannot interfere. This is structurally identical to the hitchhiker's psychological irrationality, except distributed across rules rather than a single mind. One caveat is that democratic voters have cyclical, non-unitary preferences, so the driver lacks a single preference ordering; nevertheless, the core commitment logic survives intact."

Please grade this answer with conceptual discipline. Identify what the student correctly grasps about Parfit's distinction, pinpoint exactly where the mapping to central-bank independence slips—particularly regarding the identity of the driver and whether institutional self-binding genuinely replicates the psychological structure of direct irrationality rather than approximating it through indirect rationality—and flag any subtle misstatements about the conditions under which the hitchhiker's solution succeeds.
