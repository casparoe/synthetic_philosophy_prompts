I am serving as a teaching assistant for an advanced undergraduate seminar on rational choice theory, and I recently encountered a modified St. Petersburg problem on a problem set I am grading. The exam question presented a variant in which a fair coin is tossed until tails appears; if tails first appears on toss n (with n beginning at 0), the gross payout is 2^n, but a progressive tax applies: payouts for n ≤ 3 are untaxed, for 4 ≤ n ≤ 7 they are reduced by 25%, and for n ≥ 8 they are reduced by 50%. The entry fee is F. The problem asked two things: (a) compute the expected net payout before subtracting F, and discuss whether this tax structure resolves the classic paradox of infinite expected value; (b) assuming the player evaluates outcomes with utility u(x) = ln(x + 100) over net change in wealth, determine whether there exists a positive fair entry fee and justify the reasoning precisely.

One student submitted the following answer.

"(a) Without any tax, the expectation is Σ (1/2^{n+1})2^n = Σ 1/2, which diverges to infinity. Applying the tax, I divide the sum into three ranges. For n = 0 through 3, each term contributes (1/2^{n+1})2^n = 1/2, so the total is 4 × 1/2 = 2. For n = 4 through 7, the payout is (3/4)2^n, so each term contributes (3/4)(1/2) = 3/8, and four terms give 4 × 3/8 = 1.5. For n ≥ 8, the payout is (1/2)2^n = 2^{n-1}, so each term contributes (1/2^{n+1})2^{n-1} = 1/4. Because there are infinitely many terms in this range, their total contribution is infinite. Therefore the expected payout remains infinite, and the tax structure does not resolve the paradox.

(b) To find the fair fee, I compute the expected utility of the gross payout: EU = Σ (1/2^{n+1}) ln(P_n + 100), where P_n denotes the taxed payout. For large n, ln(P_n + 100) behaves like (n-1)ln 2, while the probability weight decays like 1/2^{n+1}, so the tail is exponentially damped and the series converges to a finite value, approximately 4.2. I then solve u(CE) = EU for the certainty equivalent CE, yielding CE ≈ e^4.2 - 100 ≈ 56.7. Hence the player should pay no more than about $57, which is positive, so a fair entry fee exists. Because risk aversion bounds the value of the game even though the monetary expectation diverges, the paradox is fully resolved."
