I'm applying for a one-semester graduate research fellowship (ten weeks, deliverable: one 5,000-word paper) in philosophy of social science. The topic is the gender-equality paradox: in societies with stronger formal gender equality and expanded opportunity structures, gender differences in career and academic choices—especially in STEM versus care-oriented fields—tend to be larger rather than smaller.

The standard debate asks whether this pattern reveals stronger biological predispositions, hidden cultural forces, or measurement artifacts. I want to avoid that framing. My precise question is: does the paradox undermine the liberal-egalitarian assumption that removing structural barriers should yield roughly gender-neutral preference distributions? More specifically, if an expanded feasible set produces more pronounced gendered patterns, should we read this as (a) revealed authentic preference that justice must respect, (b) an "adaptive preference in reverse" where security allows gendered expression previously suppressed by constraint, or (c) structurally channeled choice that operates through identity-formation rather than through explicit barriers?

Existing answers leave this open because empirical sociologists treat the pattern as a causal puzzle, while political philosophers usually assume that unbiased choice produces parity in outcomes; neither camp has developed the conceptual taxonomy needed to determine what normative weight gendered preferences should carry under expanded opportunity.

My approach will be conceptual, not empirical. I will construct and defend the three readings above, then test each against three standard objections: the "natural differences" objection, the "false consciousness" objection, and a "metric invariance" objection about comparing preferences across welfare regimes. I will use hypothetical case constructions rather than new data, treating the cross-national pattern as a stylized fact. I will argue that reading (b) is the most coherent but forces egalitarians to abandon outcome-parity as a benchmark of justice, replacing it with a procedural criterion tied to autonomy-under-expanded-choice.

Success would be a stable taxonomy showing that the paradox is neither automatically good news nor bad news for egalitarianism, but forces a choice between competing conceptions of autonomy. Failure would occur if the taxonomy collapses back into the nature-versus-culture dichotomy, or if maintaining the distinctions requires empirical resolution beyond a conceptual project. The main scope risk is overreaching into empirical sociology; I will guard against it by framing all empirical claims conditionally on the stylized description and restricting citations to those I add myself after drafting.
