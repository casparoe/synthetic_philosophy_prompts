For my philosophy seminar, I am asking for a strict villanelle—nineteen lines, with refrains A1 and A2—on whether white lies are wrong, framed by Dutch directness and Japanese indirectness. The first refrain should be "Say it plainly; kindness never needs a false word," and the second "Keep the air whole; truth can rest upon harmony." Use the opening tercets to stage the conflict: one voice should defend Dutch bespreekbaarheid, treating bluntness as respect for the listener's rational autonomy, while the other should defend Japanese kuuki wo yomu, treating indirectness as the preservation of wa, the shared space in which meaning is co-constructed rather than delivered. At the volta, which should occur in the final tercet, introduce the idea that the moral failure of the white lie is neither deception nor harm to social harmony, but rather an autonomy-blocking act: by offering a comfortable falsehood, the speaker denies the hearer the information required either to respond with full knowledge or to read the air for themselves. The closing couplet should state precisely what must be relinquished—directness, indirectness, or the lie itself—to restore that autonomy, without resolving which cultural form is correct. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with politeness theory recognizes the structure, yet it should work as poetry for a reader who knows none of the terms.
