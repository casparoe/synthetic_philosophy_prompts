I have been following a recurring pattern in philosophical discussions of whether Buddhist ethics should be classified as consequentialist, and I am unsure whether it has an established name or falls cleanly under a broader category. The pattern works like this. First, a defender points to context-sensitive rules—such as the allowance that a monastic may consume meat if it was not killed specifically for him, justified by the claim that refusing might generate greater aversion or harm in the donor—as evidence that Buddhist moral reasoning tracks outcomes rather than rigid prohibitions. A critic then responds that the tradition explicitly denies that liberation depends on the fruits of action, emphasizing instead the purification of intention and the absence of craving for results. The defender counters by invoking the bodhisattva aspiration to liberate all sentient beings, which seems to presuppose a maximization of welfare across an indefinite future. At this point the critic notes that the vow is described as requiring countless lifetimes and that no individual practitioner expects to complete it, suggesting the language is aspirational or character-forming rather than genuinely outcome-oriented. The defender then asks why the aspiration is framed in such expansive, world-encompassing terms if the actual achievement of universal liberation is not part of the moral evaluation. The exchange tends to stall there, but what interests me is not any particular author's position; it is the structural move by which the framework resists assimilation to consequentialism by appealing to internal mental states, and resists assimilation to deontological or quietist positions by appealing to cosmic-scale welfare goals. I have seen this in discussions of karma, skillful means, and the bodhisattva path across several commentators. Is there a recognized concept in the literature—perhaps in comparative ethics or Buddhist philosophy—for this specific two-way insulation, where outcome-language is used for motivation and scope while state-language is used for evaluation and justification? If there is no precise term, could you propose one, together with a definition that distinguishes it from ordinary moral pluralism, from strategic ambiguity, and from a straightforward hybrid of consequentialism and virtue ethics? I am particularly interested in the boundary conditions: at what point does this structure collapse into one of the simpler categories it avoids?
