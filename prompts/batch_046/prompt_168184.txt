I am grading final exam responses for an advanced philosophy of mind seminar focused on personal identity and non-standard cognitive architectures. One essay question asked students to explain Douglas Hofstadter's argument that an individual "I" can be distributed across separate brains, with particular attention to his claim that intimate others may become carriers of one's symbolic self-pattern after death. Students were expected to clarify why this is not merely a metaphor for memory or influence, and to distinguish Hofstadter's strange-loop framework from generic functionalist accounts of psychological continuity.

Please evaluate the following student answer with a specific letter grade and granular, point-by-point commentary that justifies the score. Your assessment should identify which claims correctly capture Hofstadter's view of the self as a high-level, substrate-independent pattern, and which involve subtle errors—such as misattributed mechanisms, conflations with other philosophers' positions, or failures to distinguish passive pattern preservation from active loop continuation within a coupled living system. Do not provide a broad impressionistic summary; I need precise notes on where the reasoning is sound and where it slips conceptually.

Student answer: Hofstadter argues that the self is not a localized physical object but a dynamic pattern of symbols and causal loops running in neural tissue. Because patterns are substrate-independent, the "I" is not confined to one skull; after death, a spouse or close companion can internalize enough of one's symbolic habits—values, verbal styles, decision heuristics—to become a genuine continuation of that self. This is essentially the functionalist view shared by thinkers like Parfit, where identity depends on psychological connectedness rather than bodily persistence. The mechanism involves what Dennett calls symbol-activation patterns being copied into a new brain, generating a subsidiary strange loop that runs the same cognitive software. Therefore, any sufficiently accurate biography or digital archive could in principle serve the same role, since all that is required is that the pattern be preserved and activated elsewhere.
