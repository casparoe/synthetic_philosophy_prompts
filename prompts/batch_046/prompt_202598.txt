I'm participating in a philosophy of mind reading group that has spent several months stuck on a particular application of the extended mind thesis, and I'm trying to understand whether our impasse is substantive or structural. The case concerns a composer who has worked with the same modular analog synthesizer for over two decades; she reports that she doesn't merely operate the instrument but "reasons through the patch bay," arranging voltage paths as if they were intermediate steps in a proof. When the synthesizer was recently removed for repairs, her compositional process didn't just slow—she described a qualitative fragmentation, as though the space of possibilities she could entertain had collapsed, not because she lacked sound, but because the external topology she used to navigate harmonic relations was absent. One faction in our group treats this as a textbook case of extended cognition: the synthesizer is tightly coupled, automatically available, and informationally integrated in ways that satisfy parity conditions, and the disruption demonstrates constitutive dependence rather than mere causal inconvenience. The opposing faction grants all the functional descriptions but denies extension, arguing that the instrument lacks the requisite reciprocity or "mutual manipulability" for cognitive status, and that her reported fragmentation is better explained as the disruption of a highly automatized skill—analogous to a mathematician who feels lost without her whiteboard, without the whiteboard being part of her reasoning.

What makes the stall puzzling is that both sides accept the same behavioral and phenomenological data, invoke the same canonical distinctions (integration, automaticity, parity), and still arrive at opposite verdicts. My suspicion is that beneath the functional debate lies an unacknowledged disagreement about what counts as authentic mental ownership—whether extending cognition into an artifact threatens a normative boundary around the self—or perhaps that the two sides are answering different questions: one about the ontology of cognitive processes, the other about the conditions for personal-level agency. Given that diagnosis, I want to ask: is this disagreement best understood as a hidden normative clash about authenticity, a verbal dispute over "part of" versus "tool for," or an empirical gap that could be closed by distinguishing constitutive from causal dependence in disruption studies? And if the diagnosis is correct, what kind of move—formal, empirical, or conceptual—would actually advance the discussion rather than repeating the same circular exchange?
