Since my mother was recently diagnosed with a neurological condition that clinicians describe as pain asymbolia—she reports intense nociceptive sensations yet shows no withdrawal reflex, no emotional distress, and no behavioral avoidance—I have been trying to understand whether the sensation itself carries negative value or whether its badness is entirely carried by its usual motivational consequences. In the philosophical discussions I have encountered, I keep noticing a specific dialectical pattern, and I want to know whether it has an established name.

The pattern works as follows. A speaker cites an asymbolic case—perhaps a patient who feels burning without flinching, or a subject under dissociative anesthesia who describes vivid nociceptive qualities without suffering—to argue that the sensory and affective dimensions of pain are fully separable, and that intrinsic badness belongs only to the latter. An interlocutor then points out that these subjects still seek relief, still rate the experience negatively, or still exhibit subtle physiological stress responses. Rather than conceding that the raw sensation possesses some negative valence, the original speaker relocates the residual negativity into an adjacent category: it is reclassified as anxiety about tissue damage, cognitive alarm, or disruption of bodily integrity, and the speaker insists that the pure nociceptive quality itself remains axiologically neutral. I have seen versions of this move applied not only to classic asymbolia but also to certain congenital insensitivity variants and to morphine-induced dissociation cases. In each instance, a dissociation is initially used to bifurcate sensation from suffering; when suffering proves inseparable from the report, it is taxonomically expelled from pain proper in order to preserve the neutrality thesis.

This is not quite the motte-and-bailey, since the two positions are not a defensible core and an indefensible slogan-extension but rather competing taxonomies of the same state, and the speaker shifts between them defensively. It is also not plain equivocation, because the redefinition is systematic rather than accidental. Is there an established concept in philosophy of mind or axiology that captures this exact maneuver—this protective reclassification of residual affect to safeguard the claim that a sensory modality is intrinsically value-neutral? If no precise term exists, can you propose one, together with a formal definition and clear boundary conditions that distinguish it from definitional retreat, taxonomic gerrymandering, and ordinary ad hoc rescue?
