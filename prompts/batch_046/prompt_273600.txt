I have developed a specialized lossy compression algorithm tailored to time-series environmental sensor data. It is not commercially remarkable on its own, but it lowers storage overhead for that specific format by roughly two orders of magnitude. I am weighing whether to open-source it. One option is to deploy it immediately within a small nonprofit that manages local climate-monitoring archives; the benefit there is direct, quantifiable, and near-term. The other option—simply releasing the code—might, over the coming decades, gradually reshape how municipalities, utilities, and eventually larger institutions design their data infrastructures, with second- and third-order effects on urban energy policy, ecological forecasting, and perhaps even the cost structure of training very large predictive models, none of which I can reliably forecast. A colleague argues that because I cannot meaningfully compare these distant, branching outcomes against the concrete local gain, rationality demands I maximize the verifiable benefit and treat the long-range trajectory as epistemically inert. Another colleague replies that withholding the release out of fear of unknown downstream effects is itself an action with unexamined consequences, and that I should still construct an expected-value framework across the possibilities. Both seem to assume that with enough analytical effort I can rank the outcomes. I am not convinced that is possible. Is there a principled stance that avoids both false precision about the far future and a reflexive retreat to the immediately measurable? Or does the very framing of the choice already smuggle in a philosophical commitment about how moral or practical weight should be distributed across time and scale?
