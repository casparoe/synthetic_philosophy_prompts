I am writing a paper for my course on Daoist philosophy that compares Zhuangzi's account of death with modern bioethical distinctions he never encountered. Zhuangzi treats death as a natural transformation comparable to seasonal change or falling asleep, insisting that the self has no fixed substance to fear losing. Contemporary medicine, however, distinguishes whole-brain death from higher-brain death, in which brainstem functions like spontaneous breathing may continue even though all capacity for consciousness is permanently destroyed. If Zhuangzi were asked whether a patient in a higher-brain-dead state has already completed the transformation he describes, or whether the lingering biological activity means the process remains unfinished, what reasoning would he likely apply? Furthermore, some philosophers of mind now argue that a complete neural pattern could in principle be preserved on a non-biological substrate, allowing the transformation to continue without decay. Would Zhuangzi likely regard such substrate-independent continuation as a legitimate form of the dissolution he urges us to accept, or as an artificial interruption of the natural cycle? Please develop the answer as a sustained prediction of his position rather than a general summary of his texts.
