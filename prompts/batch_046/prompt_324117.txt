As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of science, I have constructed an argument that seems to push the pessimistic meta-induction past its usual target and into a self-undermining conclusion about scientific realism itself. I present it in steps, and I would like to know where it fails—specifically, which premise looks plausible only because of a hidden equivocation or scope shift, and what giving it up costs.

(1) Scientific realism explains the striking predictive success of mature science by holding that our best theories are approximately true descriptions of entities and processes we cannot directly observe.

(2) The pessimistic meta-induction notes that nearly every mature scientific theory in history—phlogiston chemistry, caloric thermodynamics, the luminiferous ether—eventually turned out to be strictly false, despite having enjoyed comparable predictive success and empirical support.

(3) By straightforward inductive reasoning, we should therefore expect our current mature theories—quantum field theory, general relativity, evolutionary biology—to be strictly false as well.

(4) But if these theories are strictly false, then realism cannot invoke approximate truth to explain their success; we are left with either a cosmic coincidence or an unexplained miracle.

(5) A realist might reply that our current theories are exceptional: they have survived far more severe testing, and they exhibit structural continuity with past theories in ways that justify belief in their core ontology.

(6) Yet every past false theory also survived the most rigorous testing available in its epoch, and structural continuity—such as the preservation of Maxwell's field equations across the abandonment of the mechanical ether—also holds between false predecessors and their successors, without preserving the ontology that was later rejected.

(7) So the exceptionalist reply collapses: we have no non-circular reason to exempt current theories from the induction.

(8) Therefore scientific realism is self-defeating. It offers approximate truth as the explanation for scientific success, but the meta-induction shows that success does not track approximate truth, leaving realism with no explanation and rendering continued belief in our best theories irrational.

The conclusion strikes me as absurd—certainly stronger than the standard anti-realist conclusion—because it seems to make my own laboratory experience of relying on quantum mechanics irrational rather than justified. I am reluctant to surrender premise (1), since the no-miracles intuition feels compelling from practical scientific work, and I am equally reluctant to deny (2), which seems like plain historical induction. So which step should I reject? If the argument is valid and I must choose, I would prefer to keep (1) and (2) intact, but I want to understand precisely what I am paying—whether it is a restriction on the scope of induction, a revision of what "approximately true" allows, or a hidden slide between theoretical ontology and predictive structure.
