I'm giving the retirement toast for my doctoral advisor, Professor Elena Vasquez, at a dinner where about half the guests are moral philosophers who have followed her career and the other half are family members and university staff who know her mainly as a warm, somewhat scattered presence in the department. For forty years she worked on a question that sounds abstract but shaped her life: whether being maximally morally good is actually a desirable state for a human being. She accepted the familiar critique—developed in the late twentieth-century literature on so-called moral saints—that a life of relentless self-sacrifice tends to crowd out the non-moral perfections that make a life rich and varied, from artistic playfulness to deep friendship to intellectual curiosity pursued for its own sake. But Vasquez added a distinction that became the spine of her later work: the problem is not moral excellence itself, but its concentration. She contrasted "moral saints," individuals who absorb moral demands into their entire identity, with "moral architects," people who achieve comparable or greater moral impact by redesigning institutions, practices, and social expectations so that moral labor is distributed rather than hoarded by one self-erasing person.

The difficulty for this toast is that Vasquez herself never presented herself as either figure. Her family would laugh at the idea of her as a self-abnegating saint—she forgot birthdays, loved obscure cinema, and was notoriously bad at answering administrative emails—yet her institutional reforms in our university's ethics review process and her founding of a distributed-mentorship program for first-generation students achieved moral outcomes far larger than any single person's direct charity could have produced. The philosophers in the room will want to hear that her life exemplifies her own theoretical distinction; her relatives need to hear that she was honored precisely for not becoming the kind of person her philosophy warned against. Write a five-minute toast that uses her "architect versus saint" framework as genuine structural scaffolding, not decorative jargon—explain why praising her as a "saint" in the traditional sense would actually misrepresent both her philosophy and her character—while remaining fully accessible to the family side of the table. It should land as affection and intellectual honesty rather than as a lecture, and it should leave both halves of the room feeling they understand something true about her.
