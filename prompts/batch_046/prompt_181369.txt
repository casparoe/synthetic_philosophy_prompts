You serve on the University Faculty Retirement Oversight Committee, which must vote by next Friday on whether to adopt university-wide the Dynamic Lifecycle Allocation Program (DLAP) for our 403(b) plan. DLAP requires participants to commit now to a binding sequence of portfolio shifts—high equity through age 45, a graduated glide to bonds by 60, and full annuity lock-in at 65—with a single permitted revision point at age 55 governed by an intertemporal arbitration clause (the “Bridgeman provision”). Under that provision, a participant may alter the glide path only by posting a non-refundable consistency bond and demonstrating to an independent trustee that the requested change is not the product of a locally advantageous but globally destructive preference reversal.

The philosophical issue is diachronic rationality and money-pump vulnerability. Critics on the committee argue that DLAP treats faculty as “resolute choosers” who must honor an ex ante plan even when their risk preferences have genuinely evolved; if preferences change rationally over time, locking in an old plan makes participants exploitable by a sequence of counterparty offers—each stage locally rational, collectively leaving them poorer than a consistently updated strategy would. Defenders reply that “sophisticated choice” justifies the architecture: rational agents anticipate future preference shifts, and the consistency bond is precisely the mechanism that prevents a diachronic money pump by making deviation costly enough that only authentic, globally coherent revisions survive.

The concrete trigger is Dr. Elena Vasquez, a 54-year-old pilot participant who has invoked the Bridgeman provision to halt her equity drawdown and freeze allocations at their current 60/40 mix, citing a permanent change in her risk tolerance following a recent family health crisis. The trustee has preliminarily ruled that her case meets the provision’s standard, but three committee members contend that accepting her revision undermines the anti-pump logic of the program and that we should either reject her request and keep her on the glide path, or suspend DLAP enrollment until the arbitration mechanism is redesigned.

Draft the committee’s decision memo—addressed to the Provost and the Board of Trustees—with a clear bottom-line recommendation (approve the program with conditions, reject it, or approve Vasquez’s revision and retain DLAP) and a concrete implementation timeline if adopted. The memo should treat the strongest argument for the losing position with genuine seriousness rather than dismissing it, should avoid treating legal definitions as dispositive, and should not request further study in lieu of a decision. Begin the response with the memo text itself, with no preface, no commentary, and no restatement of the assignment.
