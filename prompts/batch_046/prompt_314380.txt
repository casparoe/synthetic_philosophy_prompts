The debate over the Ellsberg paradox has been largely frozen since its formulation: one camp treats ambiguity aversion as a rational safeguard against unknown probabilities, the other treats it as an irrational violation of Savage’s sure-thing principle, and both sides continue to recycle the same two-urn examples together with the charge that the opponent is merely disguising risk aversion. I suspect the real deadlock lies not in the examples but in a deeper disagreement about what rationality demands—specifically, whether coherence must be assessed relative to one’s actual evidential partition, or relative to a counterfactual partition in which the ambiguity is resolved by a learning rule that the agent does not currently possess and may never apply. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a hidden formal disagreement doing the work—for instance, over whether subjective expected utility can be extended consistently to non-unique prior sets without collapsing into maximin rules? More generally, what kind of stalemate is this: a verbal dispute about the word “rationality,” a clash of bedrock normative intuitions about caution, or a substantive disagreement masked by shared vocabulary?
