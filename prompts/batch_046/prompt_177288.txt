The question asked was this: consider a case of distributed design—two architects, M and N, commissioned separately by a municipality to redesign a public plaza, who have never met, spoken, or been introduced. Their only interaction is through a shared digital repository of annotated blueprints: each uploads revisions, reads the other’s annotations, and adjusts her own structural and aesthetic choices accordingly. Both intend that they together produce the final plaza design; each takes the other’s annotations into account in planning; and each is prepared to adjust her work if the other’s annotations reveal conflicts. However, neither regards the other as bound by promise or obligation: if N withdraws, M experiences only practical inconvenience, not the specific sense that N has wronged her or violated a shared bond. Analyze whether this is joint action under Bratman’s shared cooperative activity framework and under Gilbert’s joint commitment framework. In particular, address whether mutual responsiveness and interlocking intentions can be satisfied through purely asynchronous, document-mediated exchanges, and whether the absence of felt normative obligation tells more against one account than the other.

The student wrote:

Under Bratman’s shared cooperative activity account, joint action requires three interlocking conditions: (i) each participant intends that they perform the joint action together; (ii) each intends that they do so via meshing subplans that each knows about and is responsive to; and (iii) there is common knowledge among them of these intentions and of the meshing subplans, sustained by mutual responsiveness in execution. In the plaza case, M and N clearly satisfy (i): both intend that they together redesign the plaza. They also satisfy (ii): each adjusts her annotations and design choices in light of the other’s uploads, so their subplans mesh. Because the repository is shared and both regularly consult it, they have common knowledge of each other’s intentions and adjustments, satisfying (iii). The fact that all this happens asynchronously through documents rather than face-to-face interaction does not matter, because Bratman’s conditions are functional, not phenomenological: responsiveness requires only that each takes the other into account in action and planning, not co-presence. The absence of felt obligation is also consistent with Bratman’s view, since his account is grounded in intentions and mutual responsiveness, not in normative bonds.

Gilbert’s joint commitment account requires that the parties form a plural subject through a joint commitment: a commitment of the will that is jointly created and that binds each party to the others, generating obligations that can be rightfully demanded and for which one can be blamed if violated. In the plaza case, there is no joint commitment because M and N never entered into any agreement—explicit or implicit—to form a plural subject. The absence of felt obligation upon withdrawal is decisive evidence here: if N were genuinely bound by a joint commitment, M would have standing to reproach N for breaking faith with the plural subject “we.” Since M feels only practical frustration, no plural subject was ever formed. Therefore Gilbert would deny that this is joint action.

One might object that Bratman’s account is too permissive, since it counts mediated coordination between strangers as joint action. But this is a feature, not a bug: it allows Bratman’s framework to cover large-scale, distributed cooperation like open-source development or distributed scientific collaboration. Conversely, Gilbert’s account demands a social ceremony of commitment-formation that excludes many real cooperative practices. Thus the case supports Bratman’s functionalism and reveals that Gilbert’s normative requirement is overly restrictive.
