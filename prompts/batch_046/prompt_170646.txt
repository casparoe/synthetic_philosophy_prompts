I'm noticing a recurring argumentative pattern that I can't tell whether it has a standard name or is just an informal family of mistakes. The pattern involves taking a minimal first-person certainty — the bare fact that, right now, some thinking or experiencing is occurring — and attempting to leverage that minimal certainty into much stronger conclusions about what the thinker is, how long it lasts, whether it is embodied, or whether it stands in reliable epistemic relations to an external world. Each attempted leverage point seems to require a separate premise — about identity over time, about the reliability of sensory channels, about causal connections between mental and physical states — that wasn't present in the original minimal move. I see versions of this in attempts to build substantial metaphysics from Cartesian-style self-certainty, in phenomenological arguments that move from the immediacy of experience to claims about a unified transcendental ego, and in analytic philosophy of mind when self-knowledge is treated as immediately given but then loaded with content about mental architecture. The result is that the minimal certainty proves far less than its users need, yet they continue to treat it as a foundation for those heavier claims. Does this pattern have an established label — perhaps in the literature on the scope or limits of first-person authority? If not, how should its boundaries be drawn: does it apply only to metaphysical extensions of self-certainty, or also to epistemological extensions like claims about direct access to mental content? In answering, please distinguish what is broadly settled about the actual reach of minimal self-certainty from what remains genuinely contested about how far it can be extended without smuggling in additional premises.
