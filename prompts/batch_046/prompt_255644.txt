People have been arguing for ages about whether honoring sunk costs is ever rational, and it feels like the debate just spins without anyone conceding ground. Economists tend to treat it as a fallacy by definition — you maximize expected utility from now on, full stop. Meanwhile philosophers and some behavioral folks keep pulling out edge cases where walking away feels wrong for reasons that don't look like simple confusion, like maintaining a diachronic plan or signaling commitment. My hunch is the stall isn't really about psychology or math at all — it's that "rational" is doing double duty: one camp means instrumentally optimal at the decision point, the other means consistent with a long-term self or identity. But maybe I'm wrong and there's actually a hidden empiricall question about how people model past commitments. I want a diagonsis, not a literature reveiw: is this mostly verbal, is there a substantive disagreement about norms or facts hiding inside, are the sides answering different questions under one name, or is the impasse a sign the question is ill-posed? Be concrete about what each camp actually says and where they talk past each other. And tell me honestly — could a new formal distinction, a conceptual move, or even an experiment actually break the deadlock, or is it just a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? One niche thought I keep returning to: maybe sunk costs function like credible commitments in repeated games with yourself, so ignoring them isn't irrationality but a failure of intertemporal bargaining. Does that idea help the diagnosis, or is it just injecting noise? I want the structure of the disagrement mapped out, plus what progress would actually look like given that map.
