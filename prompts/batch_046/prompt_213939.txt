You are a philosopher of technology with expertise in conceptual analysis and research design. Before you begin drafting, take a few minutes to brainstorm quietly: what distinct conceptual fractures might be hidden inside the common phrase the alignment problem, and which of them genuinely depend on one another?

Write a short research proposal—approximately 800 words, appropriate for an interdisciplinary fellowship or a workshop pitch—addressing the question: Is artificial-intelligence alignment a single problem with varied symptoms, or is it an accidental cluster of separate problems that share only a steering metaphor?

Frame the proposal from the perspective of a novelist researching a long-form narrative about artificial intelligence and institutional control; maintain philosophical rigor, but attend to how different conceptions of alignment generate different dramatic structures and different kinds of narrative failure.

State the question with precision. Identify at least three candidate problems that might be conflated: value-specification failure, institutional-delegation risk, and what I will call semantic inertia—the gradual, evaluation-invisible drift between a system's training-objective semantics and its deployment-context pragmatics. Explain why existing answers leave the issue open: technical literature tends to treat alignment as an optimization challenge, while critical scholarship tends to treat it as a governance challenge, yet neither tradition has systematically tested whether these are coupled failure modes or independent ones.

Propose an approach: a conceptual-mapping analysis that specifies, for each candidate problem, its necessary conditions, its characteristic failure signatures, and the class of interventions that resolve it without resolving the others. Defend this approach against the obvious alternatives—pure empirical benchmarking, which presupposes a unified metric, and broad historical analogy, which risks false parallels.

Specify what a result would look like: either a defensible taxonomy showing that the problems do not necessarily intersect, or a unifying framework demonstrating that they are structurally coupled. State honestly what would count as failure—for instance, if the distinction collapses into a merely verbal dispute without practical consequence.

Give a realistic scope assessment: this is tractable for a single-semester or six-month fellowship if restricted to three candidate problems and if it avoids constructing formal architectures; it becomes unmanageable if it attempts to adjudicate empirical disagreements about specific model behaviors.

Conclude with a brief epistemic self-assessment: identify the specific argument or observation that would change your mind and convince you that alignment is genuinely singular rather than plural, and flag the point in your reasoning where you are least certain and where the proposal is most vulnerable. Think carefully, and present the proposal in a polished, formal register suitable for a review committee that values both conceptual clarity and honest limits.
