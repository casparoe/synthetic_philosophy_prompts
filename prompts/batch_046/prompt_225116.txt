Having spent three decades in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have recently turned to philosophy of science and am now preparing a proposal for a six-month independent research fellowship. The problem arises from a tension I encountered constantly in the laboratory but never resolved conceptually: Good's theorem shows that, for an agent with a well-defined decision problem and cost-free access to information, acquiring that information is always rational, since acting after belief-updating can never lower expected utility. Applied to science, this seems to imply that an investigator should never refuse an additional measurement if it can be obtained without direct expense. Yet working scientists routinely design protocols that limit sampling, halt experiments before all observable variables are recorded, and treat certain data streams as noise to be excluded rather than incorporated. The standard explanations reduce these refusals to hidden costs—time, equipment wear, opportunity cost—or treat them as pragmatic lapses from ideal rationality. I want to ask whether there is a philosophically coherent account of rational refusal of information in scientific practice that does not collapse into hidden-cost reasoning, and whether such an account requires modifying the decision-theoretic framework itself.

More precisely, in open-ended inquiry the space of possible actions and the space of relevant hypotheses are not fixed in advance, as Good's formalism requires. If an experimenter does not yet know what theoretical framework will organize results, can it be rational to decline a measurement that might destabilize an emerging model without offering a determinate alternative? I propose to examine whether the failure of Good's conditions—specifically, the absence of a closed action space and the presence of what I call epistemic fragility—marks a benign boundary condition or reveals a substantive limit to decision-theoretic epistemology. The method will be conceptual modeling rather than empirical survey: I will construct a representation of exploratory inquiry in which hypotheses are generated dynamically, apply Good's reasoning to locate exactly where it breaks down, and then evaluate three resolutions—expanding utility to include model-stability values, reconceiving information as context-relative, or insisting that rational refusal is always apparent and masks a hidden cost. I will test these against two cases: stopping rules in adaptive clinical trials, where refusal is institutionalized, and the decision to terminate a long-baseline physics measurement when preliminary patterns suggest instrumental drift.

The deliverable will be a taxonomy distinguishing rational refusal (grounded in the structure of inquiry) from practical refusal (grounded in resource limits), together with an honest verdict on whether decision theory can absorb the distinction or must be supplemented. The project fails if the distinction collapses—if every case can be reconstructed as hidden-cost reasoning—or if formal modeling requires specifying an action space that exploratory science genuinely lacks, rendering the exercise circular. Given six months and my background in experimental design rather than formal logic, I will restrict analysis to qualitative structural arguments with minimal notation, avoiding full axiomatization. I seek support not to settle a debate but to determine whether a practitioner's intuition—that scientists sometimes protect understanding rather than maximize expected utility—can be made philosophically precise without inventing citations I could not verify.
