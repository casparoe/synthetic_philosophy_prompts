Please construct a philosophical argument for the view that decisions about retaining or removing statues, monuments, and street names ought to depend primarily on whether the object presently functions as an active normative endorsement rather than as a passive historical index, and not on a comprehensive moral assessment of the figure commemorated. You should begin by distinguishing commemorative presence — in which public space is used to affirm values the community currently holds — from archival presence — in which the object serves mainly to record that a particular valuation once existed — and you should explain why this functional distinction carries normative weight. Next, address the objection that any prominent retention necessarily signals present-day endorsement, and show how your distinction survives that challenge without collapsing. Then apply the framework specifically to street names that have become cartographic habits: designations used for navigation with minimal or no living association with the original honoree. Here, introduce and deploy the notion of mnemonic dependency — the degree to which residents' practical and emotional orientation in urban space relies on the existing name — and argue that this dependency creates a genuine ethical asymmetry between renaming a street and removing a statue. Your answer should be written in British English, should proceed by conceptual analysis rather than empirical survey, and should not cite named scholars, specific texts, or particular historical cases except in purely descriptive terms where unavoidable.
