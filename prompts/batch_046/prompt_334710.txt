Here is an argument I built after working through the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique together with some evolutionary-developmental literature, and it now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to dissolve the adaptation/spandrel distinction altogether.

(1) To classify a trait as a genuine adaptation rather than a spandrel or exaptation, one must establish that the trait’s specific form was causally necessary for the fitness advantage it conferred—if an alternative form could have produced the same functional gain, then selection did not specifically favor this form, only the outcome.

(2) Given developmental bias, high-dimensional phenotypic space, and the integrative architecture of organisms, for any actual trait T we can construct a plausible counterfactual evolutionary pathway in which an equivalent functional outcome is achieved without T, or with T replaced by a structurally different feature playing the same role.

(3) Therefore, for every trait, its specific form was not causally necessary for its fitness contribution.

(4) So no trait satisfies the condition in (1); every biological feature is either a spandrel or an exaptation.

(5) Yet evolutionary biology routinely explains complex traits—such as the mammalian middle-ear ossicles derived from ancestral jaw bones—as adaptations shaped by selection for auditory sensitivity, not as accidental architectural byproducts.

(6) Hence the adaptationist program systematically misclassifies spandrels as adaptations and is therefore incoherent.

The conclusion is clearly too strong. Where does the reasoning slip? If I have to choose, I want to preserve (1), since it captures the intuition that calling something an adaptation requires more than noting that it is useful, but I am willing to surrender (2) if that is the least costly move. Which step should I reject? Explain why it looked defensible—perhaps drawing on the idea that organisms are integrated wholes in which selection acts on the total phenotype, so that isolating one trait’s causal contribution is always problematic—then price the alternatives. In particular, does giving up (2) require me to deny that developmental bias and phenotypic plasticity can generate functional equivalents, or only to restrict that claim to counterfactuals that were actually available in the lineage? And what does the standard adaptationist pay if (1) is abandoned instead—must they accept that any currently useful trait counts as an adaptation regardless of its historical origin? Finally, say what your diagnosis predicts for a case not listed here: a trait that is clearly a byproduct of selection for body size, yet now serves a critical sensory function that its original form did not support.
