You are a senior policy analyst for a mid-sized city council, and you review roughly four proposed ordinances each week—zoning variances, vendor-licensing rules, public-space access restrictions, and data-collection mandates. Your council has adopted a republican-liberty framework for evaluating state action: a regulation is objectionable not merely when it interferes with choices (negative liberty) or when it fails to provide capacities (positive liberty), but when it subjects citizens to arbitrary power, including power that is never exercised but remains available.

Design a reusable, thirty-minute evaluation procedure—structured as an ordered sequence of tests with branching conditions—that distinguishes three failure modes: (1) direct interference reducing option sets; (2) missing enabling conditions that make formal rights empty; and (3) domination, both “procedural” (discretion applied arbitrarily to specific groups despite facially neutral rules) and “structural” (rules that are non-arbitrary in content but create enduring dependency relationships leaving citizens vulnerable to future arbitrary revision).

Your procedure must include:
- The specific questions to ask at each step, including what counts as evidence of arbitrariness when the ordinance text is facially neutral.
- How to handle conflicts between negative and republican assessments—for instance, a rule that expands practical freedom for one group while placing another under the discretionary authority of a licensing board with no appeal mechanism.
- Explicit markers for judgment calls that cannot be eliminated, such as weighing dependency against security benefits, or deciding whether a sunset clause genuinely removes domination or merely postpones it.
- Three worked applications: (a) a zoning ordinance restricting multi-family housing but granting variances through an appointed board; (b) a public-transport subsidy funded by a targeted fee on ride-share services; and (c) one case you construct yourself where the procedure yields a conclusion you personally dislike—explain what the procedure demands and why you resist it.
- A brief statement of what this procedure cannot do: which philosophical disputes about the metric of liberty it leaves unresolved, and which institutional features it assumes are present (for example, judicial review) without verifying them.

Keep the language precise but usable by a non-philosopher who must sign off on a memo by noon. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
