I have recently begun tracking my own conduct with Benjamin Franklin’s thirteen-virtue scheme—Temperance, Silence, Order, Resolution, Frugality, Industry, Sincerity, Justice, Moderation, Cleanliness, Tranquility, Chastity, and Humility arranged in the precise sequence he prescribed, on the belief that each virtue prepares the ground for the next—only to find that the grid exposes failures faster than it corrects them. Write a poem of exactly thirteen lines in unrhymed iambic pentameter, assigning one line to each virtue in Franklin’s order with the virtue named at the line’s opening. The first twelve lines should build grammatically as a single continuous sentence describing an ideal self; at the thirteenth line—Humility, which Franklin appended only after being accused of pride—the syntax should fracture and fail to close. The poem’s final phrase must turn back on the speaker to observe that the chart lists every fault except the fault of the one who keeps the chart, leaving the project of moral perfection structurally open. Do not resolve the tension into consolation or sermon; let the broken form do the work.
