In arguments over the restitution of the Benin Bronzes and the Parthenon Marbles, a frequent defense of retention holds that a universal museum functions like an encyclopedia: it assembles entries from every civilization so that any visitor can consult the complete narrative of human achievement, and dispersing its holdings would be like tearing out pages and scattering them to private shelves where the cross-references are lost. Critics respond that an encyclopedia is made to be reproduced and shared, not monopolized, and that its entries have no rightful single owner, whereas these objects were physically seized and remain rivalrous in possession—so the analogy imports a false picture of both ownership and harm. Audit this comparison explicitly: provide the correspondence table (the entry, the binding, the reader, the publisher, the missing pages, the act of tearing), identify the disanalogies that matter for the restitution question—particularly the difference between non-rivalrous knowledge and rivalrous material presence, and between a reference work designed for diffusion and a collection built through extraction—and determine whether the conclusion that dispersal damages global understanding survives once those breaks are registered, or whether the analogy was carrying that conclusion by smuggling in premises about neutral aggregation. If the encyclopedia picture fails, suggest whether a different analogy—for instance, a library with distributed copies governed by common-pool rules—could support retention, or whether no analogy can bridge the gap between universal access and physical custody.
