I'm writing for a publication that tracks conceptual shifts at the boundary of behavioral economics and philosophy of action, and I'm drafting a brief for a one-semester independent research proposal—roughly two thousand words, no empirical data collection required—on a framing problem that standard treatments of weakness of will and hyperbolic discounting tend to obscure.

Here is the half-formed idea I want developed into a formal proposal. Behavioral economists model intertemporal choice with hyperbolic discount curves that predict preference reversal: an agent prefers a larger-later reward at distance, but switches to a smaller-sooner reward as proximity increases. Philosophers model akrasia as a failure of practical reasoning in which an agent acts against her own best judgment. Both literatures usually preserve a unitary agent—one whose preferences may conflict across time, but who remains the same evaluator. I want the proposal to investigate whether there is a distinct phenomenon, which we might call structural akrasia, in which the agent's evaluative framework itself discontinuously shifts: not merely that she discounts the future steeply, but that the criteria by which she judges "better" are not stable across the temporal gap. In such cases, commitment devices—what some call Ulysses contracts—might bind future behavior without resolving whether the future self being bound shares enough normative continuity with the present self to make the binding intelligible as self-governance rather than external coercion.

The proposal should state the precise question: Can we articulate a coherent concept of weakness of will that applies when there is no single "better judgment" being overridden—only incompatible frameworks of evaluation separated by temporal distance—and if so, does this concept fall within the scope of hyperbolic discounting, or does it reveal a limitation in that model's assumption of a continuous utility function? It should explain why existing philosophical accounts—Aristotelian, Davidsonian, and contemporary planning-theoretic approaches—and existing economic models leave this specific gap open, particularly by treating either the unity of reason or the unity of the agent as non-negotiable.

The method should be conceptual and analytical: distinguishing predictive identification (expecting one's future preferences) from normative identification (endorsing them), examining whether these can come apart without collapsing into either pure preference reversal or pure alienation, and testing the resulting distinction against cases where agents deliberately engineer discontinuities in their own decision-making structures.

The deliverable should specify what counts as progress—either a reconstructed account of structural akrasia with clear conditions of application, or a rigorous argument that any such reconstruction must choose between preserving agential unity and preserving the phenomenon. The proposal should include an honest scope assessment: what simplifying restrictions, for instance excluding addictive or compulsive cases or restricting analysis to voluntary long-range planning, make the project feasible for one semester, and what would make it fail—whether conceptual circularity, empirical overreach, or collapse back into standard discounting.

Write this as a polished proposal document. Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; references may be inserted later. The framing should be precise enough that someone unfamiliar with the specialized literature can evaluate whether the conceptual distinction holds.
