I have been thinking, outside any formal study, about whether the self—the "I" that experiences things—must be confined to one brain, or whether it could genuinely be spread across two or more brains linked closely enough. I encountered Douglas Hofstadter's argument that the self is not a hidden soul but a kind of pattern or strange loop that runs on neural hardware; if the pattern is what matters, then the hardware could in principle be distributed. To push this past familiar thought experiments, imagine a concrete case: two adults are connected by a sustained inter-cortical lattice that does not merely transmit signals but integrates working memory and emotional processing in real time, so that each can think with the other's neural resources and neither can tell, from the inside, where one mind ends and the other begins.

From this starting point, I want a taxonomy—a branching decision tree—that sorts the philosophical positions on what, if anything, happens to the "I" in such a case. Build it from the logical space first: start with the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then place the actual views (named or unnamed) at the leaves. Note any unoccupied branches and briefly diagnose why they are empty—whether incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The tree must split on at least these concrete issues, phrased as questions anyone could answer without knowing the jargon:
- Must an "I" have exactly one continuous biological body to exist at all?
- If the bridge creates shared experience, is there now one self with two bodies, or two selves with an unusually deep overlap?
- If the bridge is suddenly cut, does one self split like an amoeba, do two separate selves emerge, or does one survive while the other dies?
- Is the distributed state genuinely shared identity, or is it only an extreme form of empathy and shared information with no real change in who is who?

Please include among the leaves the singular-substrate view, a full-fusion view, a partial-overlap view, and the "no real identity change" view. Also flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate. Keep clear what is broadly settled—for instance, that most people start with a single-brain assumption—from what is genuinely contested among people who think seriously about this, and do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus.
