Question: In Carroll's 1895 dialogue 'What the Tortoise Said to Achilles', the Tortoise forces Achilles into an infinite regress by refusing to accept Z from premises A and B unless Achilles also accepts the conditional linking them as an explicit premise. Some recent work frames this not as an epistemic problem—how we know deductions are valid—but as a problem of indirect normativity: logical rules govern reasoning not as beliefs but as structural constraints on rational belief revision. Explain Carroll's regress precisely. Then evaluate whether the indirect-normativity framing actually solves the puzzle, or just relocates the need for an unjustified stopping point. Be specific about whether Carroll intended the dialogue to illustrate general skepticism about logic, or something narrower.

Student answer:
Carroll's dialogue uses Euclid-style premises—things equal to the same are equal, etc.—to show that if you treat a logical rule like modus ponens as just another premise, you need another rule to apply it, and so on forever. The Tortoise accepts A and B but won't accept Z without also accepting C: if A and B then Z. Then he demands D, and the regress never ends. This demonstrates that deduction can't be justified from within, much like Hume's problem of induction shows empirical reasoning lacks a rational foundation. Once we see this applies to every inference rule—not just hypothetical syllogism but also conjunction introduction and quantifier rules—the problem becomes general.

The indirect-normativity view says modus ponens isn't a premise to be justified but a filter on permissible belief updates in idealized deliberation. That sounds right, but it only renames Carroll's primitive. The Tortoise can still ask why we should use this filter and not a paraconsistent one. Carroll's own solution is that Achilles eventually admits defeat and accepts logic needs an unjustified axiom, which Carroll treats as acceptable conventionalism—anticipating Carnap's later view that logical frameworks are chosen by convention. So the framing helps us see the issue is normative authority, not epistemic access, but it doesn't eliminate the stopping point. We still accept modus ponens without derivation, which is exactly what Carroll's Achilles was forced to do.
