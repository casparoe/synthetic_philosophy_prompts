As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently turned to philosophy of rational choice, I have become preoccupied with Newcomb's problem—not in its standard form, but in a variant that seems structurally analogous to quantum decoherence. I ask you to write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors: a causal decision theorist and an evidential decision theorist, standing before the two boxes in a scenario where the predictor is not a deterministic computer but a quantum apparatus. This apparatus prepares the contents of the transparent box by performing an entangled measurement whose outcome is decohered long before either agent or predictor acts; the correlation between prediction and choice thus exists in the statistical structure of the decohered branches, but there is no causal pathway by which the agent's present selection can influence the past preparation. The causal theorist should argue that decoherence eliminates any common-cause link susceptible to present influence, rendering evidential reasoning inert and making one-boxing irrational. The evidential theorist should respond that rational choice tracks the conditional probabilities embedded in the branch structure regardless of causal accessibility, and should address whether treating the agent's choice as a branch-selection rather than a causal intervention undermines the causal theorist's objection. Their exchange should be genuinely dialectical: each must reply to the specific point just made, not deliver a prefabricated monologue. They should also consider whether quantum indeterminacy compromises the predictor's reliability and whether the principle of maximizing expected utility must be indexed to causally accessible worlds or to the full belief-state of the agent. Please present this as a polished, technically precise philosophical dialogue, without bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, reasoning entirely from first principles.
