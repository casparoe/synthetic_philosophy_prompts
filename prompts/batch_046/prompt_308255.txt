Read Philip K. Dick's short story "The Minority Report" through the lens of Molinism and middle knowledge, with particular attention to the grounding objection for counterfactuals of creaturely freedom. The Precrime Division arrests people for crimes they have not yet committed, relying on visions from three precognitive mutants; one mutant occasionally issues a contradictory "minority report." Treat these visions not as mere predictions but as knowledge claims about what free agents would do: does the system assume they are true in virtue of actual future events, or in virtue of counterfactual truths about the agents' choices in various circumstances? Examine how Anderton's discovery of his own name in a report, and his subsequent decision to act differently, relates to the Molinist claim that God knows, prior to any creative decree, exactly what every free creature would do in every situation. Is the story dramatizing libertarian free will that defeats middle knowledge, or does it tacitly presuppose a deterministic branching model that Molinism explicitly rejects? Analyze whether the "minority report" functions analogously to a Molinist counterfactual that is not actualized, and whether the narrative takes an implicit stand on whether such counterfactuals must be grounded in something independent of the agent's will. Where the work resists this philosophical framing, note it directly. Finally, say what Dick's institutionalization of precrime adds to the debate over middle knowledge that purely abstract argument misses—particularly regarding the moral hazard of treating counterfactual knowledge as sufficient justification for coercive intervention.
