I have become convinced by a line of reasoning—associated, I think, with Rietdijk and Putnam—that special relativity eliminates any objective present moment. Because observers in relative motion disagree about which distant events are simultaneous, and physics offers no privileged frame to settle the dispute, it seems past, present, and future must all exist in the same static way, like positions in space. I am not asking for sources or a reading list, but I want to understand what would genuinely count against this block-universe conclusion. In particular, is the argument vulnerable at the step where every reference frame is treated as ontologically equal? Could a physically distinguished foliation—grounded not in hidden dynamics but in boundary conditions, causality, or the structure of experience—restore a real flow of time without contradicting relativity? I also wonder whether the view's implication that my future states are as fixed and real as my past ones is a fatal problem or merely an uncomfortable feature. Please rank the strongest defeaters: empirical results about temporal asymmetry, formal results in causality or quantum mechanics, philosophical arguments about open futures or agency, and whether defenders of the block view can absorb each by adding qualifications. I want an honest assessment of whether the position risks anything concrete, or whether it has been insulated into unfalsifiability—and if so, whether that insulation is itself a weakness.
