I was talking with a friend the other evening about causation — not in any technical way, just wondering whether we ever really see one thing make another happen, or only see them show up together like clockwork. That led me to this medieval thinker Al-Ghazālī, who apparently argued that fire does not actually burn cotton; what we call cause and effect is just God creating both events together so reliably that we mistake the pairing for a power residing in the flame itself. I am not trained in philosophy, but the image stuck with me: the cotton blackening, the flame nearby, and somewhere in between nothing we could point to and call the push.

I would like you to write a villanelle about this — the form with the two refrains that keep turning and finally meet in the last two lines. Let one refrain be in the voice of Fire claiming its own agency, and the other in the voice of Cotton denying it has been acted upon by anything but the direct will behind both. The rotation of the lines should mirror the occasionalist claim: every stanza brings the same conjunction, yet the rhyme and refrain structure should make it clear that no necessary link is being established, only a witnessed habit. By the closing couplet, where the two refrains stand together without resolving, the poem should land exactly on Al-Ghazālī's point — that what looks like nature's law is really a sustained coincidence we have learned not to be surprised by. Keep it precise enough that someone familiar with occasionalism recognizes the distinction between habitual conjunction and real causation, but write it as poetry first, not as a textbook explanation.
