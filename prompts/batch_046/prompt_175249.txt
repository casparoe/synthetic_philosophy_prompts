I sit on the joint governance and ethics committee of an international research consortium that reviews proposals for large-scale climate-intervention studies. A delegation from one member state has tabled an emergency protocol arguing that, because any single national laboratory could unilaterally initiate stratospheric aerosol injection without multilateral consent, our consortium should preemptively authorise its own tightly regulated 'counter-deployment' pilot — accepting a localised ecological risk in order to forestall a far more dangerous unilateral release by a less cautious actor. The opposing delegation contends that this reasoning exemplifies the unilateralist's curse: it converts the mere possibility of reckless unilateral action elsewhere into sufficient justification for our own risky preemption, thereby dissolving the collective-restraint norms that prevent competitive escalation.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate ahead of next Thursday's plenary. It should recommend a concrete course — approve, reject, or approve subject to defined conditions — with an explicit implementation or review time frame. The memo must engage the opposing delegation's position as a serious philosophical objection rather than treating it as procedural obstruction, and it should reconstruct the implicit theory of collective moral responsibility that the first delegation's argument presupposes. Specifically, consider whether that theory can draw a principled line between legitimate preemptive governance and the self-justifying escalation pattern characteristic of the curse, or whether it collapses into a licence for unilateralism by the most risk-tolerant party. The response should be written in American English; please treat this prompt itself, composed in British English, accordingly.
