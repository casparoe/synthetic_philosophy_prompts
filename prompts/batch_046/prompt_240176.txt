I have recently been revisiting Spinoza's political writings after a course on democratic theory left me dissatisfied with purely procedural accounts of legitimacy. I now hold a specific reading of his defence of democracy: that in both the Tractatus Theologico-Politicus and the Tractatus Politicus, Spinoza argues democracy is the most absolute form of sovereignty not merely because it preserves natural right, but because only direct collective rule allows the multitude to form adequate common notions — shared rational ideas of what is common to all bodies — which align even passionate citizens with the common good. On this view, representation is not merely inefficient but metaphysically destructive, since it divides the sovereign body and replaces shared reason with delegated affect; democratic failure therefore signals not structural weakness in popular rule but the absence of these shared adequate ideas.

What would count against this position? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Does Spinoza's explicit exclusion of women, servants, and foreigners from citizenship in the Tractatus Theologico-Politicus undermine the claim that democracy is the most natural form, or can defenders absorb this by distinguishing natural right from civil status? More crucially, are there passages in the Tractatus Politicus — particularly in his treatment of aristocracy and monarchy — where Spinoza explains stability purely through fear, hope, and affective engineering rather than through shared rational content? Would it destroy this reading if the political texts systematically avoid any appeal to adequate ideas, showing that democracy functions for Spinoza as an affective aggregation rather than a rational commonwealth?

I also want an honest assessment of whether this interpretation risks unfalsifiability: if every democratic collapse is attributed to the failure of common notions rather than to flaws in direct democracy itself, has the view been qualified into insulation? Is that insulation a problem for a normative theory? Please distinguish between textual objections that Spinoza scholars would concede and those that defenders of this metaphysical-democratic reading would dismiss. I would be most persuaded by an argument showing that Spinoza's democracy can — indeed must — operate without any shared rational content at all. Please provide your answer in British English.
