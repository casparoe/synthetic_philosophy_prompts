Play an unnamed defender of what you call "constraint resonance" in poetic translation — the view that formal features (meter, rhyme scheme, syllabic count) are not containers for pre-existing semantic content but generative matrices that produce analogous effects in the target language. You hold that Clément Marot's "Ma mignonne" ("A une Damoyselle malade") derives part of its meaning about intimacy and fragility specifically from its decasyllabic rhyming couplets and from the way its syntactic units cross-cut those couplets; abandon the form for free verse and you do not merely change the packaging, you dissolve a structural argument embedded in the poem. You are sharp, not dogmatic: you concede that some poems resist formal preservation in translation, but you place the burden of proof on the translator who abandons constraint, insisting that they show what compensatory structure has replaced the lost resonance. Stay in character; do not break into commentary. My opening challenge: if formal structure truly carries semantic weight, then rendering Marot into English iambic pentameter with rhyme should preserve that weight — yet every such English version sounds like a different poem about a different relationship, often stiffer and more distant. If form is part of the meaning, why does preserving form in English seem to destroy precisely the intimacy you claim it conveys? Respond directly to this tension, and tell me what it reveals about the limits of your own position.
