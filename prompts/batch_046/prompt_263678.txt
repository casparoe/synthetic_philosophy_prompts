Interpreters of the butterfly dream in the second chapter of the Zhuangzi have been stuck for decades in a loop that neither citation of the same narrative details nor agreement on the basic story has broken. One side reads the passage—where, upon waking, Zhuangzi cannot determine whether he was a man who dreamed he was a butterfly or is now a butterfly dreaming he is Zhuangzi—as an argument structurally analogous to Cartesian dream skepticism, focused on the unreliability of perceptual states and the fragility of personal identity across them. The other side, appealing to the chapter's broader themes of wuhua, the transformation of things, and the figure of the zhenren or genuine person, claims this reading forces a Western subject-object and knower-known framework onto a text that precisely dissolves those boundaries: on this view, the passage is not about lacking knowledge of one's state, but about the non-persistence of any stable ego that could serve as the knowing subject, so that treating it as epistemology is a category mistake. Both camps acknowledge the explicit rhetorical uncertainty in the text and both invoke transformation; yet they have not moved, with skeptics insisting that anxiety about identity presupposes nothing more than a temporary self, and transformationists insisting that any self capable of anxiety is already too substantial for the text's ontology.
