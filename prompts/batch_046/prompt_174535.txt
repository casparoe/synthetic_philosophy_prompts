I am a journalist covering science and ideas, and I am preparing an interview with Dr. Marcus Chen, a philosopher of social norms who defends a modified version of the thesis—associated with Bernard Williams and developed through Kwame Anthony Appiah's analysis of honor worlds—that moral revolutions such as the decline of dueling and the abolition of slavery occur neither through pure rational persuasion nor through bare material interest alone, but through a structural shift in what Chen calls the "honor economy." His specific contribution is a mechanism he terms "reputational escrow," in which a norm persists because unilateral defection imposes a status cost monitored by dense social networks, and collapses only when network density or the technology of monitoring falls below what he calls a "displacement threshold." He treats modern analogues such as extreme overtime culture and karoshi as comparable cases of honor-coded persistence rather than as identical moral revolutions. I want eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond restating this view. Please include at least one question pressing on the boundary between honor and ordinary social sanction; at least one asking how his displacement threshold differs from standard collective-action or tipping-point explanations in sociology; and at least one asking what his framework says about norms that appear to dissolve through explicit moral argument—such as the abolitionist campaigns—without any prior collapse in monitoring capacity. For each of these three, include a follow-up keyed to the answer Chen is most likely to give. Please separate what is well-established in the relevant historical and philosophical literature from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present Chen's particular synthesis—including reputational escrow and the displacement threshold—as if it represented the consensus view.
