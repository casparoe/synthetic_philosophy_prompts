I've been arguing with my roommate about whether you can call a painting good if the painter was a terrible person, and I think I built a reductio but it feels slippery. Here's the chain I came up with after a few beers:

(1) If an artwork's value depends partly on the moral character of its maker, then any feature of the work that causally traces back to that character is aesthetically tainted. (2) The emotional tone, color choices, and compositional balance of a painting causaly trace back to the painter's psychology. (3) So if the painter was cruel or deceitful, those traits infect the work and diminish its aesthetic value. (4) But the same causal tracing applies to the materails: if the canvas was made by exploited labor, or if the pigments were mined unethically, or if a later restorer who was a criminal touched the canvas, then those moral flaws also infect the work. (5) And if the work later inspired a bad person to do something good, or a good person to do something bad, those causal chains also affect its value. (6) So on this view, evaluating any artwork requires investigating its entire causal history, which is impossible, and almost every work has some moral stain somewhere in its chain. (7) Therefore either we can never call any artwork good, or (1) is false and the maker's morality doesn't affect the work's value.

But I'm not convinced (7) actually follows, and I actually want to keep something like (1) but not in this extreme form. Where did the slide happen? Is it in (2) treating psychological traits as aesthetically relevant in the first place? Or in (4) extending the contamination to materails and restoration? Or is there a hidden equivocation between 'moral evaluation of the work' and 'moral evaluation of the maker'? I want to keep the intuition that knowing an artist was monstrous can change how you experience the piece, but I don't want to commit to investigating every supply chain. Which premise do I drop, and what does dropping it cost me?
