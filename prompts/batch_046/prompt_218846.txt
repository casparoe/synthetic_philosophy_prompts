As a retired biologist who has recently begun studying philosophy of art, I wish to see whether two camps that often appear to talk past one another can produce a genuinely shared document. The two positions are, first, the "integrationists", who hold that moral defects—whether in the represented world of the work or in the known conduct of its creator—can diminish artistic value, because aesthetic engagement is not modally insulated from ethical cognition; and second, the "modal separatists", who hold that artistic evaluation operates within a counterfactual frame of engagement that brackets real-world moral facts, though they concede that certain works fail artistically precisely because they cannot sustain that frame.

Please draft the adversarial-collaboration statement both camps could sign. I require four sections, in this exact order and with these approximate lengths:

1. Agreed claims (a numbered list of propositions both sides accept; roughly 250–300 words). Include agreement about the nature of artistic value, about whether biography can ever be aesthetically relevant, about whether a clash between formal structure and moral content—what I shall call "ethical dissonance"—can function analogously to musical dissonance, and about the limits of audience autonomy in framing engagement.

2. Residual disagreements (a table with three columns—point of divergence, formulation the integrationists accept, formulation the modal separatists accept; roughly 250 words). Each formulation must be worded so that its own camp would recognise it as accurate. Cover at least: whether moral knowledge conveyed by the work counts as an artistic merit in itself; whether learning of an artist's immoral behaviour necessarily alters the work's aesthetic properties; and whether ethical dissonance is ever a formal virtue rather than a defect.

3. Cruxes and predictive cases (roughly 350 words). Identify the sub-questions that, if settled, would move at least one camp. I take two likely cruxes to be: (i) whether the "ecological validity" of a moral response—its being the kind of response we would actually have in real life—carries independent aesthetic weight; and (ii) whether requiring an audience to adopt a counterfactual moral stance in order to appreciate a work imposes an artistic cost. For each crux, present one concrete case or argument both sides agree is relevant, and record in advance what each camp predicts about it.

4. Verbal versus substantive disputes (roughly 150 words). State plainly which parts of the disagreement, if any, turn merely on how broadly "artistic value" is defined, and which parts represent genuine divergence about normative practice or about how human cognition works.

Write in British English (for instance, "behaviour", "centre", "analyse" where appropriate). Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote any real author or text verbatim; if you need to attribute a claim, paraphrase without quotation marks. Finally, if my framing rests on a confused premise—perhaps that these two camps can collaborate meaningfully, or that "ethical dissonance" is a coherent notion—or if the task cannot be executed well without distorting one side, say so plainly rather than forcing compliance.
