I've been retired from biochemistry for about three years now and have taken to reading philosophy instead of journal articles—mostly Dostoevsky, since a neighbour told me The Brothers Karamazov is basically an ethics textbook disguised as a novel. Anyway, I was chatting with a friend over tea and we sketched out this argument about the Grand Inquisitor that now feels like it's running away with itself, and I can't quite find the exit.

(1) The Inquisitor claims human beings are fundamentally too weak for freedom; given the choice, they will always exchange it for bread, miracle, and authority. (2) If freedom is valuable only instrumentally—because it lets us reach happiness—then when surrendering it produces more happiness, we ought rationally to surrender it. (3) That reasoning doesn't stop at religious belief; if autonomous choice itself is a source of suffering, it applies just as well to careers, relationships, moral judgement, even aesthetic taste. (4) So the logical endpoint of the Inquisitor's compassion is a system that makes every meaningful choice for us, leaving us blissful but entirely passive—almost like a benevolent mechanism running our lives from the inside. (5) Yet the Inquisitor presents himself as one who has taken on the terrible burden of authority precisely so that ordinary people don't have to suffer the weight of choice. (6) But if (1) is true, his own suffering is unnecessary; he should be the first to hand over his will, and in fact nobody should bear authority at all—everyone should just recieve it. (7) Therefore either people really can bear freedom after all, which contradicts (1), or the Inquisitor's position collapses into total passivity for everyone, including himself, which makes his very act of condemning Christ incoherent.

I feel like there's an equivocation hiding somewhere between freedom-from-religious-doubt in (1) and freedom-of-choice-in-general in (3), but I can't quite name the slip. I'd rather keep (2) and (5) if I can—there's something noble about the Inquisitor's self-sacrifice that I don't want to throw away. Where does the argument actually go wrong, and what am I paying if I hang on to those premises?
