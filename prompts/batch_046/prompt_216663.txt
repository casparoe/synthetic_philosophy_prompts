I have been thinking on my own, without any formal philosophy training, about whether people who share a country really owe each other more help and loyalty than they owe strangers elsewhere. The usual examples—disaster aid, immigration rules, tax burdens—feel too familiar to me, so I want this treated with a specific twist: the dispute should partly turn on whether growing up inside the same predictive information environment—the same news cycles, school curricula, legal assumptions, and shared expectations about how institutions behave—can by itself ground special moral claims, separate from shared blood, culture, or citizenship papers.

Please draft, in American English and in a formal, polished tone, the joint statement that a committed nationalist camp and a committed cosmopolitan camp could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration.

Structure it as follows:

First, a numbered section of claims both sides genuinely accept. Cover what it actually means to share a nation-state (not just a flag or passport); what moral equality requires regardless of borders; the real limits of what any state can deliver to people outside it; and the specific point about shared predictive environments—state precisely what both sides agree these shared environments do and do not establish, without letting either side smuggle in its conclusion.

Second, a disagreements section—formatted either as a table or as clearly separated items—stating exactly where the camps still part ways. Each position should be written in wording that its own supporters would accept as fair. One side should defend that compatriots have special claims rooted in co-authorship of institutions; the other should deny that borders carry intrinsic moral weight. Both sides must address the predictive-environment framing explicitly.

Third, identify the cruxes: the precise sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one camp. At least one crux should concern whether shared predictive environments generate genuine obligations or only practical coordination problems.

Fourth, for each crux, present one concrete case or line of reasoning that both camps agree is relevant, and record in advance what each camp predicts about it. You may invent a scenario—for instance, two people who share an identical information environment but live on opposite sides of a border, or citizens deciding whether to share a domestically developed climate-adaptation resource that works only inside their territory.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke highly obscure theories by name. Keep everything accessible to someone reasoning from first principles, but make the residual disagreement exact rather than vague.
