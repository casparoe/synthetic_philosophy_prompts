Two philosophers, Sam and Jordan, argued over whether I can literally exist in other possible worlds.

Sam defended a Lewis-style counterpart view: I exist only in the actual world; elsewhere there are only counterparts—individuals similar to me but not identical. So "I could have been a musician" is false of me and true only of a counterpart. Jordan defended a Kripke-style transworld-identity view: I am the same individual across worlds, with world-relative properties. Just as I can be tired here and alert elsewhere, I can be a musician in one world and a physicist in another, with no contradiction.

Sam pressed a fission scenario: imagine a world in which I split into two people, both retaining my full psychology and memories. Counterpart theory handles this cleanly—there are two counterparts, and since I am not literally present in that world, there is no paradox about "which one is me." Jordan replied that fission is indeed hard, but it is a general problem for diachronic identity, not special to modality. Worse, Sam's similarity metric is not transitive: my closest counterpart's closest counterpart may differ sharply from me, so modal properties shift unexpectedly across worlds. Jordan also argued that counterpart theory makes first-personal modal imagination systematically misleading—when I picture myself as a musician, I picture me, not a duplicate—whereas transworld identity respects that intuition.

Sam countered that imagination tracks psychological resemblance, not literal presence, and that non-transitivity is a feature: it captures how small changes accumulate. He added that transworld identity is metaphysically extravagant, scattering one entity across infinitely many worlds, and that without a principled similarity threshold, "counterpart" risks being ad hoc.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and say what both sides miss. In particular, does the fission case genuinely favor counterpart theory, or does it merely relocate the problem? And can counterpart theory preserve the first-personal force of modal self-talk without implicitly smuggling in transworld identity?
