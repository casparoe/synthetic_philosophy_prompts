Draft a 90-minute final-exam essay question for an upper-level seminar on nationalism and historical ontology that forces students beyond summarizing Hobsbawm's concept of invented tradition and Anderson's concept of imagined communities. Present a specific constructed case: after independence in 1960, the state of Marisania promotes an official origin myth linking its citizens to an ancient river-civilization that archaeology confirms never occupied the present territory, yet within two generations ordinary people report a deep, horizontal solidarity with anonymous fellow-citizens they will never meet, sustained mainly by vernacular newspapers, standardized school calendars, and shared ritual observances rather than by state propaganda. The question should require students to locate the precise conceptual tension between Hobsbawm's elite-instrumentalist account and Anderson's model of popular, modular imagination; to argue which framework better explains the persistence of national feeling in Marisania; and, most importantly, to identify the specific point at which the two accounts become mutually undermining—namely, whether national consciousness can be both deliberately fabricated and spontaneously imagined without collapsing into contradiction. Provide a model answer of approximately 600 words and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points totaling 20 marks, each with its point value and a one-line note on the characteristic failure mode.
