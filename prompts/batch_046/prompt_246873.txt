Write a villanelle — nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a quatrain, with two refrains and two rhymes — that takes Jain anekāntavāda (the doctrine of many-sided reality) and its logical expression syādvāda (qualified, partial predication introduced by syāt, “in some respect”) as its conceptual architecture. Choose one concrete, ordinary object — for instance a lamp flame, a silk thread, or a river at dusk — and let the two refrains be flat, unqualified contradictions about it, such as “The flame is steady” and “The flame is never the same.” Do not resolve the contradiction within the poem; instead, let each of the five tercets supply a different qualifying standpoint — substance, temporal appearance, relational context, linguistic framing, and practical use — so that in one respect one refrain holds and in another the other holds. The form itself should perform the philosophy: the refrains must take turns through the tercets, and in the closing quatrain they should stand side by side without synthesis, leaving the tension open exactly as syādvāda leaves contradictory predicates co-valid. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with Jain epistemology recognizes the refusal of absolute predication rather than mere vocabulary, and it should work as verse for a reader with no background in the tradition. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or explanatory prose outside the poem.
