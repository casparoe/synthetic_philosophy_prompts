I have been trying to work through T.M. Scanlon’s contractualism on my own—the idea that an action is wrong if it is justified by principles that someone with reasonable grounds could reject—and I keep getting stuck on what people call the aggregation problem. As I understand it, the difficulty is that if we are not allowed to add up small benefits or harms across many people, then contractualism seems to block saving a thousand people from serious discomfort by imposing a heavy burden on one person, which feels wrong; but if we do allow aggregation, we seem to lose the very protection of the individual that makes the view attractive. I want to see whether this tension actually breaks the theory when it meets a real decision, not just a thought experiment.

Consider the global COVID-19 vaccine allocation choices made between late 2020 and 2022: wealthy countries and manufacturers prioritized domestic booster and third-dose campaigns while vaccination rates in many lower-income countries remained in the single digits. Do not recount the epidemiology; treat the situation as an ethical allocation problem with clear parties. Apply Scanlon’s framework precisely: identify the affected parties (citizens receiving boosters, citizens denied first doses, the governments and firms making the allocation, and the healthcare workers in under-vaccinated regions), specify what each party could reasonably reject about the principle governing distribution, and ask whether the aggregated lives saved or protected in wealthy countries can outweigh a reasonable rejection from those left unprotected. Then go further: contractualism is usually framed as a dyadic relation between specific people, but here the harm is mediated by supply chains, patent structures, and national budgets in which no single agent is the direct cause. Does this structural mediation mean contractualism is silent, or does it still yield a verdict about which principles of allocation are rejectable?

Finally, reach an actual conclusion. Does the aggregation problem in this case show that contractualism needs some quantitative threshold rule—say, that aggregation is permitted only when the per-person burden in the aggregated pool falls below a certain proportion of the burden imposed on the protected individual—or does it show that contractualism was never designed to handle distributive questions at a global structural scale? Be direct about where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what would change your mind: would evidence that booster doses in wealthy countries saved more lives per dollar than first doses elsewhere overturn your conclusion? Or would a demonstration that the “reasonable rejection” standard collapses into simple utilitarian counting once numbers get large? I want an honest account of where the theory maps cleanly onto the case and where it either breaks or needs an amendment it never originally contained.
