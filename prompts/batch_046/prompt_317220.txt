I'm trying to map out what actually needs solving in the indirect normativity literature, specifically around idealized deliberation. Most discussions I see either defend Railton-style indirect strategies (where you don't aim at the good directly) or attack them via Korsgaard-style objections about the authority of reflective endorsement. But I'm wondering about the upstream questions that decide whether this is even a live debate, not just a recycling of the same objections with new examples.

Specifically, I'm considering three open lines: (1) whether idealized deliberation should be modeled as counterfactual reasoning about what you'd choose if you knew everything, or instead as a normative constraint on how you actually reason now with your current limitations; (2) whether indirect norms can be justified without collapsing into a form of rule-utilitarianism that looses the agent-centeredness the view was supposed to preserve; and (3) how to handle cases where the idealized agent and the actual agent have divergent temporal identities — like, is the 'you' in the idealization the same practical identity that bears the costs of following the indirect rule?

If you had to advise someone with maybe two years of focused research time (not a full career, just a tight project) on which of these — or maybe something I'm missing entirely — is most worth pursuing first, how would you rank them? I want the reasoning to be explicit about dependency: does resolving one unlock answers to the others, does it have hidden implications for practical rationality outside ethics, and is it tractable without needing empirical psychology or neuroscience. Don't just list them; defend the ordering, say what evidence or argument would change your mind, and flag which ones are probably dead ends that only look open because of sloppy framing in the literature.
