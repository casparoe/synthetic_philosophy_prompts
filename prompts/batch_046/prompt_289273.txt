I was listening to two colleagues argue over coffee about why academic histories of late imperial Rome so often describe its fifth-century collapse as “overdetermined” — economic debasement, frontier pressure, dynastic instability, climatic stress, and institutional sclerosis all listed as concurrent causes. The first colleague said this is mostly hindsight bias in action: because we know the Western Empire dissolved around 476 CE, we retrospectively gather every preceding difficulty and treat it as a contributing cause, even though contemporaries weathered comparable crises without expecting total collapse. The second colleague objected that the real problem is the narrative fallacy — historians need an intelligible plot with rising tension and a resolution, so they selectively thread discrete events into an arc of irreversible decline and quietly discard the recoveries, reversals, and background noise that would break the story. The first replied that this misses the causal priority: the narrative fallacy is just the mechanism by which hindsight bias operates in historiography, since knowing the ending is what motivates the selection of a coherent plot leading to it. The second pushed back that the two biases actually pull in opposite directions — hindsight bias should make an outcome seem inevitable and therefore over-explained by a single sufficient factor, whereas the narrative fallacy tends to produce complex “perfect storm” explanations in which many independent threads must converge to preserve dramatic structure. So if historians invoke a dozen simultaneous causes, she argued, that is narrative complexity doing the work, not probabilistic overestimation after the fact. The first then claimed they form a hybrid error: the “perfect storm” framing lets historians claim inevitability through accumulation (hindsight) while retaining dramatic tension through multiplicity (narrative). The second insisted that unless you can specify which cognitive error is actually driving a given explanatory move, invoking both is just name-calling, and that in any case hindsight bias concerns subjective probability revision whereas narrative fallacy concerns structural selection of evidence — they are not the same failure mode.

Can you go through their exchange point by point and say who is right about each specific claim — whether hindsight bias and narrative fallacy are distinct, whether one explains the other, whether “overdetermined” collapse explanations reflect inevitability or dramatic convergence, and whether the “perfect storm” framing genuinely combines both errors? Also say what, if anything, both of them are missing about how these biases interact in actual historical explanation, and whether there is a cleaner way to separate their effects when evaluating a narrative of imperial decline.
