As a retired physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of mind and computation, I have been trying to understand whether symbolic representations ever genuinely influence their physical substrate, or whether they are merely descriptive overlays on causally closed physical dynamics. To focus the question, I will use two notions that have recently been introduced in the discussions I have been reading. A careenium is the purely formal, syntactic layer of a representational system: the network of symbol-types and transformation rules defined without reference to the physical properties of their tokens. A simmball is the particular physical state—whether a neural firing pattern, a voltage configuration, or an activated weight matrix—that realizes a given symbol-token at a particular time.

Consider the following argument.

(1) By definition, the dynamics of a careenium depend only on formal relations among symbols; they make no reference to the physical properties of simmballs except as arbitrary vehicles.
(2) If symbols genuinely push the substrate around, then altering the syntactic structure of a careenium must cause changes in simmballs that are not already fixed by the substrate's own physical laws.
(3) But every symbol-token is realized by a simmball, and every change in a symbol-token corresponds exactly to a change in its realizing simmball; the causal work is done entirely by substrate-level forces.
(4) Therefore symbols do not push the substrate around; substrate dynamics push substrate dynamics, and the careenium is causally inert.
(5) Human mental representations are careenial structures implemented in neural simmballs.
(6) So the content of my belief that a bridge is unsafe never causes my retreat; only neural dynamics do.
(7) By parallel reasoning, an LLM's symbolic processing never causes its outputs; only weight adjustments and activations do.
(8) Hence there is no causal or explanatory distinction between a philosopher reasoning and a calculator computing: in both cases, the careenium is epiphenomenal.
(9) But this erases the distinction between reason-guided action and mere physical reaction, which is implausible.
(10) So something in (1) through (4) must fail.

The argument seems valid, and premise (2) in particular captures what causal efficacy should require. Yet I find conclusion (8) unacceptable. Which premise or step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically, does abandoning (2) commit me to downward causation or to violating physical closure? How do the standard positions—reductive physicalism, non-reductive emergentism, and the symbol-grounding view—each pay for rejecting the problematic step?
