hey, my study group has been arguing about living wills and whether the person who wrote one still "owns" the decision once they're deep in dementia. we tried to formilize it and ended up with this chain that feels like it proves way too much, but none of us can spot the exact slip. can you tell me which step to drop?

1. an advance directive is binding only if there's diachronic soverignty—basically the earlier self has legitimate authority over the later one.
2. that authority can't rest on bare biological continuity, otherwise my 8-year-old self could bind my 40-year-old self just by writing a note.
3. so it has to rest on psychological/narrative continuity: the same values, life-plan, and concern for one's own future.
4. in advanced dementia or PVS that continuity is shattered; the patient has different (or no) values and can't recognize the life-plan.
5. therefore the directive loses its authority—it's not "me" anymore, it's a stranger with my social security number.
6. but wait: if authority requires that kind of rich continuity, then no long-term precommitment works at all. my 25-year-old self can't bind me now with a contract, a promise not to smoke, or a power of attorney, because the psychological gap is massive.
7. yet we clearly think those things do bind.
8. so the continuity condition in (3) must be wrong; authority just needs legal personhood or bodily continuity.
9. but then the directive binds even when the patient is psychologically alien—meaning we must treat the body according to old instructions even if the present person is suffering or begging to live.
10. that proves too much: it turns every demented patient into a kind of biological storage unit for past preferences, and makes present suffering or contentment morally irrelevant.

we want to keep both (a) that directives aren't totally pointless, and (b) that present suffering/contentment matters. which premise do we reject? explain why it looked okay, and then lay out what the standard views—maybe the continuity view, the present-autonomy view, the substituted-judgement view—have to pay to reject it. also, is there a hidden equivocation between "authority over treatment" and "authority over identity"?
