Please argue that the standard triadic periodization—'Middle Ages,' 'Renaissance,' and 'Scientific Revolution'—is best understood not as a chronological taxonomy of European intellectual history but as a 19th-century narrative technology that imposes a false teleology of awakening and progress. Your argument should proceed in three steps. First, show that both 'Middle Ages' and 'Renaissance' fail a specific diagnostic: they do not correspond to discontinuities in the epistemic infrastructure—the institutional mechanisms for generating, testing, and discarding knowledge claims—of the communities they purport to describe, and therefore function primarily as moral-geographical labels (backwardness versus rebirth) rather than as period concepts. Second, concede that the 'Scientific Revolution' also fails as a sudden rupture narrative, but defend the claim that it can be salvaged if reconceived not as the birth of modern science but as a localized transformation in the social organization of error-correction within natural philosophy, roughly between the institutionalization of the Royal Society and the consolidation of peer-reviewed contestation, a transformation visible in the practices of claim-evaluation rather than in the content of theories. Third, explain why retaining this stripped-down 'Scientific Revolution' as an event-structure, while rejecting the other two terms as period-blocks, actually improves our ability to describe long-duration continuities in medieval and early modern knowledge practices without collapsing them into an undifferentiated 'pre-modern' darkness. In developing the argument, rely on conceptual distinctions—between taxonomy and teleology, between epistemic infrastructure and doctrinal content, and between event-structures and chronological blocks—rather than on exhaustive empirical catalogues of discoveries or personalities. Do not provide a bibliography or list of sources; build the case from conceptual analysis and the internal logic of historiographical categories.
