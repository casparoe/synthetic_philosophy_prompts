I was discussing the meta-problem of consciousness with two friends from a local rationalist reading group — Maya, who works in cognitive science, and Leo, a philosopher of mind — and they reached an impasse that I found genuinely difficult to resolve. The disagreement turned on whether solving the meta-problem, that is explaining why intelligent systems report having qualitative experiences, dissolves the hard problem or merely relocates it. To keep things concrete, Maya proposed a somewhat idiosyncratic framing: she suggested we think of consciousness-attribution not as tracking an intrinsic phenomenal property, but as a kind of normative status assignment within a social coordination game, structurally analogous to how a blockchain ledger assigns ownership to a token without there being any physical ownership substance embedded in the metal or code. Leo found this provocative but ultimately insufficient, and they exchanged six moves, each of which seemed to me to contain a real insight and a real blind spot.

(1) Maya opened by arguing that the meta-problem is actually the only genuine problem. Once we explain the distribution of consciousness-reports through predictive processing of interoceptive signals and social-signalling dynamics, she said, there is no remaining explanatory gap. The sense of mystery, on her view, is merely an error-management heuristic: in evolutionary terms it is cheaper to over-attribute consciousness and avoid social blunders than to under-attribute and risk offending a sentient being. (2) Leo replied that explaining reports explains behaviour and disposition, not first-person ontology. He introduced a distinction between explanatory access and phenomenal access: even if every report is fully accounted for by interoceptive prediction, the subject still enjoys direct phenomenal access that the functional explanation never touches, so the meta-problem solution leaves the hard problem entirely untouched. (3) Maya countered that phenomenal access is just the content of the report itself, illicitly reified. She appealed to the transparency of introspection: when we introspect we do not observe an internal movie but rather the world-as-modelled. Consequently, there is no extra phenomenal-access object over and above the representational content that the meta-problem explains; the intuition of an extra layer is a misinterpretation of representational transparency. (4) Leo granted transparency for sensory states but denied it applies to the what-it-is-likeness dimension. He argued that even if introspection is transparent, there remains a structural feature of consciousness — a kind of subjectivity or for-me-ness — that is not identical to representational content. Maya, he claimed, was conflating the vehicle-content distinction at the wrong level: representational content explains what is represented, but not the mode of presentation being experienced rather than merely registered. (5) Maya then pressed her normative-framing analogy further. She proposed that consciousness-reports function like status-assignments in a shared coordination framework — comparable to ledger entries — and that the hard problem arises only when we treat this functional status as if it referred to an intrinsic property, like treating the economic value assigned to a coin as a physical feature of its metal. Once we see reports as normative attributions within a coordination game, the urge to find an intrinsic phenomenal substrate dissolves entirely. (6) Leo responded that normative-functional explanations can account for why we talk about consciousness, but they do not account for why there is something it is like to be the system making those attributions. He offered a thought experiment of a silent reporter — an artificial system with identical algorithms for assigning consciousness-status to others and itself, including a global workspace for integration, but lacking any internal phenomenological binding — and argued that we would not attribute phenomenal consciousness to it despite perfect functional reports. This, he said, shows that functional explanation of reports is insufficient for phenomenal consciousness, and that Maya’s meta-problem solution overreaches by treating a necessary condition for report-generation as sufficient for consciousness itself.
