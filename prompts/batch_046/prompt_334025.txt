I am currently enrolled in an introductory moral philosophy course, and after our recent unit on distributive justice I found myself defending a modified version of prioritarianism against the leveling-down objection that my classmates kept raising. The standard view, associated with Derek Parfit, holds that benefiting someone matters more the worse off they are, but only because well-being itself carries absolute weight, not because equality is intrinsically valuable. The familiar objection is that this allows, or even requires, reducing the welfare of the better-off when no one benefits. To avoid this, I have been working with a position I call threshold-sensitive prioritarianism: the moral weight assigned to a benefit is non-linear, increasing sharply as a person's well-being approaches a critical threshold of basic functioning, while harms inflicted on those well above the threshold carry no compensatory moral weight unless they produce corresponding gains for those near or below it. Consequently, leveling down is not merely wrong but structurally excluded by the weighting function, since harming the well-off without lifting anyone near the threshold violates the very conditions that activate the moral weight.

Play this position straight: what would have to be true for it to fail, and how exposed is it? I want an honest assessment rather than a defense. Is there a formal result in welfare economics or social choice theory demonstrating that any non-linear, threshold-dependent weighting scheme either collapses into strict egalitarianism or reduces to standard utilitarianism once standard consistency conditions—such as independence of irrelevant alternatives or continuity—are imposed? Are there intuitive moral cases, perhaps involving intergenerational climate obligations or catastrophic-risk distributions, in which the threshold mechanism produces recommendations that even proponents of the view would themselves concede are unacceptable? Would empirical findings in moral psychology—showing, for instance, that subjects consistently endorse pure leveling-down in carefully framed scenarios—count as decisive refutation, or merely as descriptive data that normative defenders could dismiss without inconsistency? And if the location of the basic-functioning threshold turns out to be genuinely arbitrary, does that arbitrariness undermine the view's insulation against the original objection, or does it only shift the burden to an independent defense of the threshold? Please distinguish evidence that would be fatal from evidence that would merely be awkward, and say whether you think the position is well insulated or whether its qualifications have made it unfalsifiable.
