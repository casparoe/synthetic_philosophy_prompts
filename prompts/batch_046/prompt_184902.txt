I am writing a dissertation chapter in political philosophy on the moral equality of combatants, and after years of reading the standard literature I have become convinced that the Walzer-McMahan stalemate is not a straightforward empirical or normative disagreement but a structural misalignment that the field has failed to name. The debate is usually presented as a direct clash over whether unjust combatants forfeit their right not to be killed—Walzer defending a reciprocity-based exemption that applies ex ante regardless of just cause, and McMahan denying it on the grounds of individual liability for unjust aggression. My working hypothesis, however, is that the two sides are answering different questions under one label: Walzer is articulating a distributive principle about pooled moral risk within a collective agent, whereas McMahan is tracking ex post culpability for wrongful harm. Consequently, equality functions ambiguously—as deontic shielding, as parity of permission, or as fairness in exposure to risk—and neither framework offers a non-circular criterion for when collective membership should override individual liability. The disagreement has therefore persisted not because of missing data but because the shared vocabulary conceals incompatible baselines about whether moral risk can be socialized across a state.

I want an honest structural diagnosis rather than a survey or adjudication: is this a verbal disagreement masked by common terms, a substantive conflict about the metaphysics of collective agency, or an irreducible clash of intuitions? Could a precise conceptual distinction—say, between deontic shielding and distributive risk-sharing—actually dissolve the impasse, or is the dispute essentially hardwired?
