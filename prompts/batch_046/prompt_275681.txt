Write an essay addressing the following controversy: Bernard Williams's integrity objection holds that utilitarian moral reasoning can require agents to violate their deepest commitments—ground projects—by demanding that they treat their own attachments as having no special weight. He illustrates this with the familiar charge that, in a rescue scenario, a husband who saves his wife only after confirming that morality permits it has engaged in reasoning that involves one thought too many. Most treatments of this objection treat it as a problem for individual practical reasoning. The question is whether the objection can be extended coherently to collective, democratic deliberation—say, to a legislature that must justify distributing scarce medical resources among citizens whose ground projects are incommensurable. If Williams is right that certain justifications alienate agents from their own values, does it follow that democratic bodies must practice a kind of institutional moral silence, refusing to articulate the comparative weights of citizens' deepest commitments? Or does the objection lose its force once reasoning moves from the first-person singular to the institutional?

Your essay should reach and justify a definite conclusion—either that the integrity objection applies with equal or greater force at the institutional level, or that it collapses there, or some intermediate position. You should not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; work entirely from conceptual analysis and the framework Williams provides. Do not invoke highly specialized empirical studies or obscure secondary literature.

Please organize your response as follows:
- Introduction: Frame the individual-level objection and state the institutional question precisely. (~300 words)
- Reconstructing Williams's Integrity Objection: Explain ground projects, alienation, and the charge involving one thought too many without relying on direct quotation from the original texts. (~350 words)
- The Institutional Analogue: Develop the scenario of democratic resource allocation and the problem of comparative justification among incommensurable ground projects. (~350 words)
- The Case for Extending the Objection: Argue that institutional justification reproduces, and perhaps amplifies, the alienation Williams identifies. (~300 words)
- The Case Against / Limits: Argue that collective reasoning has structural features—impersonality, procedural legitimacy, or representativeness—that insulate it from the integrity charge. (~300 words)
- Verdict and Justification: State your conclusion clearly and defend it against the strongest counter-position. (~300 words)
