A common way to motivate resistance to Dennett's multiple drafts model is to compare conscious experience to a private film screening: peripheral processes supply raw footage, a central editing suite splices it into a single coherent narrative, and an inner spectator watches the finished print in real time. Proponents use this to argue that consciousness requires a unified locus—what Dennett calls the Cartesian theater—where all drafts are projected for a single audience, and that without such a locus, the multiple drafts model cannot account for the seamless unity of subjective experience. Audit this analogy in detail. First, provide the correspondence table: what plays the role of the raw footage, the editing suite, the finished print, the projection booth, and the spectator? Second, identify where the mapping collapses, especially with respect to Dennett's claim that there is no "final version" of any narrative and no privileged temporal instant at which consciousness occurs. Third, determine whether the analogy quietly imports assumptions—about temporal sequence, about the need for a single consumer of representations, or about the transparency of the editing process—that the multiple drafts model explicitly denies. Fourth, assess whether the conclusion—that multiple drafts is incompatible with the unity of consciousness—survives once those imported features are removed. Finally, if the screening-room picture fails, is there a better analogy—perhaps a distributed newsroom in which no managing editor ever assembles a master copy—that captures the work Dennett needs done without reinstating the theater?
