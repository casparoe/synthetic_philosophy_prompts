You are a professional philosopher with expertise in ethics, philosophy of action, and the conceptual analysis of autonomy. Before answering, please take a moment to brainstorm: list the main dimensions along which consent can fail, distinguish what makes a failure internal to the consenter from what makes it relational or structural, and consider whether a single principle can accommodate all of them without ad hoc adjustments.

I am trying to reach reflective equilibrium about what makes consent valid, and I want your help testing whether my intuitions hang together. Here are five concrete cases with my verdicts.

Case A — The jargon form. A surgeon asks for consent to a procedure using only Latin anatomical terms and statistical risk phrases. The patient is fluent in the language but has no medical training; she signs after the surgeon says, “This is routine.” She later explains, “I trusted him; I knew he meant well.” I think her consent is invalid: she lacked the domain-relative comprehension required for autonomous authorization, even though she was not deceived about material risks and faced no pressure.

Case B — The buried clause. A digital platform’s terms of service include a clause permitting resale of biometric data, placed in paragraph 47 of a 12,000-word document presented behind a single “Agree” button. The user has used similar services for years and never reads terms. I think this is not valid consent to that specific clause. The failure is not individual ignorance but institutional framing: the architecture of choice makes meaningful comprehension practically impossible, which functions as a structural form of deception by omission.

Case C — The migrant worker. A migrant worker accepts dangerous construction work with inadequate safety equipment. The employer does not threaten him; immigration law makes deportation the consequence of unemployment, and his family depends on remittances. I think this is still valid consent. The pressure comes from background conditions and legal structures, not from a wrongfully coercive offer made by the employer. He retains moral agency in choosing the lesser evil.

Case D — The delirious patient. A patient with high-fever delirium consents to an elective cosmetic procedure that is not medically necessary. The surgeon notices confusion but proceeds because the form is signed. I think this is clearly invalid: temporary incompetence voids consent regardless of the patient’s subjective certainty at the moment.

Case E — The reshaped member. Over two years, a community leader gradually isolates a member, restricts sleep, and uses repeated affirmation until the member “freely” requests to sign over property and sever outside ties. At no point is there an explicit threat, and the member reports feeling more authentic than ever. I think this should be treated as invalid consent, analogous to Case D: autonomy has been structurally undermined, except here the mechanism is social and psychological rather than biochemical, and the subject’s own narrative of freedom is part of the damage.

My difficulty is reconciling Case C with Case E. Both involve powerful external forces that severely constrain and shape the choice. In C the force is economic and legal; in E it is social and psychological. Yet I want to say C is valid and E is not. Meanwhile, Cases A and B both involve failures of understanding—one individual and linguistic, the other systemic and architectural—and I want both to be invalid, but I am unsure whether they should be invalid for the same reason.

She is fully informed, faces no economic pressure, and has no hidden deception about the flight’s risks, but her therapist—who is secretly paid by the space company—gently encourages her that “taking risks is part of healing.” She consents enthusiastically. Give your verdict and explain whether it falls closer to C, E, or elsewhere. Third, invent one further case of your own and show what your principle predicts. Finally, be honest: state exactly which of my five verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether saving them all requires a more complex account or whether one verdict should be overturned.
