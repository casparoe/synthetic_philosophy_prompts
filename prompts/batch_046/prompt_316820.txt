Exam question: Expected-value maximization with a linear utility function over welfare appears to license "fanaticism"—accepting gambles with astronomically low probabilities of cosmically large payoffs and near-certain failure otherwise. One proposed resolution is a "reflective stability" constraint: an agent should pursue a gamble only if they would still endorse having pursued it upon learning that the high-payoff outcome did not occur. Analyze whether this constraint dissolves the fanaticism problem without collapsing into ordinary risk aversion, and whether it is rationally mandatory or merely psychologically prudent. Apply your analysis to the comparison between Project Safe (a certain welfare gain of 10^6 utils distributed among existing moral patients) and Project Moonshot (a 0.01% probability of generating 10^40 utils through a highly speculative causal chain, and a 99.99% probability of zero gain).

Student answer:

Reflective stability clearly blocks the Moonshot gamble. If the agent takes Moonshot and it fails—which will happen in 99.99% of worlds—then ex post they have traded a certain 10^6 utils for nothing. Because they would not endorse that trade upon learning of the failure, the constraint rules out Moonshot. This differs from standard risk aversion, which works by applying a concave transformation to the utility function over outcomes; reflective stability instead operates on the agent's diachronic endorsement conditions, leaving the utility scale linear.

That said, the constraint is not rationally mandatory; it is only psychologically prudent. Rationality requires maximizing expected value, and importing an ex post endorsement filter is essentially smuggling loss aversion through the back door. In fact, this framework is essentially the minimax-regret approach developed in statistical decision theory, which treats ex post evaluation as a legitimate rational criterion rather than a cognitive bias. Once we recognize this, we see that reflective stability is just regret minimization applied to moral choice, and there is no principled reason to privilege it over EV maximization.

There is also a consistency problem. If we apply reflective stability without qualification, we must reject any long-horizon investment with a non-trivial failure probability—retirement savings, climate mitigation programs, and even routine vaccination campaigns all carry some risk of being wasted ex post. Since these are clearly rational to pursue, the constraint proves too much and should be rejected.

Finally, the constraint mislocates the real source of fanaticism. The problem is not that agents will regret failed gambles, but that linear utility over unbounded domains violates the Archimedean property in a way that makes interpersonal comparison impossible. Once we fix the measurement scale so that utility differences are bounded and comparable, the Moonshot problem disappears without needing any stability filter. Therefore reflective stability is both unnecessary and unstable.
