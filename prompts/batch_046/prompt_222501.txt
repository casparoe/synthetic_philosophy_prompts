I've been working through Sartre's early existentialism with a small reading group that has spent the semester on decision-theoretic rationality, and we've reached an impasse that prose seems unable to resolve. The issue is not whether Sartre's radical freedom is compatible with determinism in the causal sense, but whether his analysis of bad faith illuminates a specific failure mode in forecasting and belief-formation: the refusal to own the gap between one's facticity (the fixed circumstances and past commitments one finds oneself in) and one's transcendence (the perpetual project of being what one is not yet). One of our members, who works on prediction markets, described this as treating one's own credences as world-states rather than as sustained commitments — an ontological refusal of anguish rather than a mere cognitive error. I want to close our session with a poem that renders this hybrid idea precisely, without reducing it to a sermon about choice or a shrug about determinism.

Please write a villanelle — nineteen lines, the two refrains alternating and meeting only in the final quatrain — that stages Sartre's structure of bad faith formally. One refrain should speak from the position of facticity, something along the lines of being fixed in what one already is; the other should speak from transcendence, something along the lines of never being identical with what one is. Between them, the tercets should trace the specific moves of self-deception: perhaps the waiter playing at being a waiter, perhaps the forecaster who hides behind probabilities, perhaps the refusal to acknowledge that freedom is precisely the anguish of holding facticity and transcendence together without synthesis. The form's repetition should mirror the circularity of bad faith, and the final couplet should place the two refrains side by side without resolving them, since that unresolved tension is exactly where Sartre locates authentic freedom. It should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the ontology recognizes the structure rather than just the vocabulary, and it should avoid both the easy optimism that freedom is unburdened and the deterministic excuse that we are merely our conditions. No citations or bibliographic apparatus — just the concept rendered with philosophical precision.
