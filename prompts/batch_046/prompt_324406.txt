In discussions of hard choices between genuinely incommensurable values—say, deciding whether to honor deep loyalty to one's local community or to pursue a cosmopolitan intellectual vocation that requires leaving it—people often reach for an analogy with tuning a musical instrument: just as a piano cannot be simultaneously tuned to both just intonation and equal temperament without sacrificing the purity of specific intervals, so too a life, institution, or policy cannot fully realize two values whose internal demands pull in structurally different directions without some distortion or loss in each. I have been discussing this with a colleague who works on institutional design, and we began to wonder whether the analogy actually carries the philosophical weight its users assign to it, or whether it quietly imports assumptions that undermine the very idea of incommensurability it is meant to illuminate.

Can you audit the analogy carefully? First, provide an explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the tuner, the instrument being tuned, the competing temperaments, the listener who judges the result harmonious, and the physical substrate that must bear the adjustment—then identify precisely where the mapping breaks down. Does value conflict genuinely involve a single substrate being adjusted along one dimension, or are we dealing with plural goods that do not compete for space within a unified structure? Does the analogy smuggle in the idea that there exists a correct overall "sound" or optimal state that both values approximate, thereby treating them as commensurable after all? And if the analogy collapses under this pressure, does the underlying reality of hard choice disappear with it, or can the phenomenon of rationally irreconcilable values survive without the musical scaffolding? Finally, consider whether a modified analogy—perhaps comparing values to orthogonal dimensions in a decision space rather than to competing tunings—would survive these same objections, and whether such a replacement strengthens or weakens the case that rational choice remains possible even when comparison is impossible. I am especially interested in whether defenders of incommensurability would themselves concede that the tuning image misleads, and what they might put in its place.
