I run an undergrad philosophy/econ seminar, about 16 people, 90 minuts. I want an activity that makes them feel why Keynes thought solving the economic problem wouldn't actually feel like solving it — specifically the slide from absolute needs into relative ones that keep moving the finish line. Don't just lecture about the 15-hour week dream; build it so they construct the trap themselves.

Design a four-round paper simulation, about 10 minuts per round, where small groups act as households deciding how to spend freed-up hours after each round's "productivity jump." Give them small stakes — maybe candy or extra-credit tokens tied to "life satisfaction" scores they assign themselves at the end of each round. The hidden twist: each group gets a secret "referance group" card that silently raises thier comparison baseline (neighbors now take art classes, now they travel, now they work flexible consulting gigs) so the same leisure choice feels like falling behind. They shouldn't know the cards exist until round three.

Include roles: one "productivity reporter" who announces the material gains, one "social auditor" who must justify any non-work time to the table in one sentence, and one "needs accountant" who tracks whether spending went to absolute comfort or status competition. The rules should reward groups for "using" freed hours productively, but let them define "productive" — so peer pressure does the work.

Then a debrief that maps each round onto Keynes's distinction: where did abundance feel real, where did comparison eat it, what did the auditor's role reveal about the social shame around idleness? Be honest about what this can't reproduce — Keynes also believed we could slowly cultivate the arts of life through education and culture, and a 90-min classroom game can't mimic that generational shift. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time, probably that one sharp student guesses the card trick in round two and breaks the illusion for everyone, and how to hide it better.

Keep it runnable with just paper, pens, and index cards. No tech, no required readings with page numbers.
