Consider the current philosophical landscape on causation by omission, particularly where metaphysical accounts intersect with institutional and technological responsibility. The dominant divide separates counterfactual-dependence accounts—on which an omission causes an outcome only if the omitted act would have prevented it—from normative-expectation accounts, which require that the agent possessed both capacity and duty to act. Within this landscape, I want to treat a further, less settled distinction as live: between omissions of initiation, where an agent fails to begin a causal chain that would have averted harm, and omissions of maintenance, where an agent allows an existing preventive structure to decay. Applied debates in climate inaction, algorithmic neglect in healthcare triage, and platform liability often presuppose answers to these distinctions without defending them.

If you had to direct a single graduate student or a small interdisciplinary group—limited time, no large team—toward the line of work that would most reshape how institutions assign responsibility for inaction, how should they prioritize?

Specifically, rank the following open questions by the order in which they deserve serious attention, and defend the ranking with explicit criteria (importance for institutional practice, tractability with current conceptual tools, neglectedness relative to other debates, and how many downstream questions depend on this one):

First, is the initiation/maintenance distinction metaphysically robust, or does it collapse once we model causation as continuous rather than discrete?

Second, can normative-expectation accounts accommodate collective omissions where no individual agent meets the threshold for duty, but the institutional architecture itself does?

Third, how should counterfactual-dependence be evaluated in overdetermined omission cases, where multiple parties each fail to act and any single one of them could have prevented the harm?

Fourth, should limited resources go primarily to refining these metaphysical distinctions, or to translating whichever distinction proves stable into criteria for institutional design and legal liability?

In your answer, provide a dependency-aware prioritization: identify which questions are upstream of which, which are likely intractable with present methods and should be deprioritized, which risk being largely semantic, and what practical consequence—if any—would follow from settling each. Do not provide a survey of the literature or a reading list; give a defended agenda for where effort should go and why.
