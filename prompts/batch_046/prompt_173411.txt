I am writing a dissertation chapter on rational choice under conditions of strict value equivalence, and I want to clarify a conceptual distinction that standard discussions of Buridan's ass tend to collapse. The classic formulation treats the ass's paralysis as arising from perfect equilibrium: two options with identical evaluative properties—nutritive value, accessibility, cost—such that there is no reason to prefer one over the other. But I am trying to determine whether the problem is metaphysical (the options genuinely share all relevant properties, so there is no fact of the matter about superiority) or epistemic (the agent lacks access to differential properties that in fact exist).

Consider the following modified case. An agent must choose between two options that are provably isomorphic in every first-order evaluative dimension—utility, risk, temporal cost—but differ in causal provenance. One option was generated by a deterministic process that the agent observed directly; the other was generated by an observationally identical stochastic process concealed behind an opaque interface. The agent knows the two are equivalent in value, but does not know whether the hidden causal structure introduces a second-order reason—such as predictive reliability, procedural authenticity, or instrumental stability—that breaks the symmetry.

Is this genuinely an instance of Buridan's problem? More specifically: does the agent suffer from rational indifference, or from a second-order epistemic limitation about whether there exists an unobserved asymmetry? Can one consistently defend arbitrary selection—say, by coin toss—without first resolving whether causal provenance constitutes a relevant reason? Explain the conceptual structure, particularly how the distinction between first-order value equivalence and second-order uncertainty about reason-relevance bears on the normative justification of breaking ties arbitrarily.
