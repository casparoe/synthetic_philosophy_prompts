I run a postgraduate seminar in philosophy of mind with twelve students for a single ninety-minute session. I want you to design a structured classroom activity that makes the group feel the force of the distinction between Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions of conscious experience, taking the colour phi phenomenon and the temporal location of experience as the central case. The exercise should begin without any lecture: participants must first undergo a brief, precisely staged perceptual sequence—either through timed visual slides or a tightly scripted verbal simulation—that induces an illusion analogous to colour phi, in which they seem to experience a colour change at an intermediate point in space or time that was never physically presented. Each student should record a first-person report in real time, noting exactly when and where the change seemed to occur, before anyone reveals the objective stimulus sequence, which lacks any intermediate presentation. Once the objective sequence is disclosed, divide the seminar into two groups of six, assigning one the Orwellian commitment that the original conscious experience itself was retroactively revised, and the other the Stalinesque commitment that only the subsequent narrative record or verbal report was altered, leaving the original experience intact but mischaracterised. Give me explicit rules for the perceptual staging, the reporting protocol, the debate structure, and a collective incentive—perhaps a shared evaluation condition or a consensus prize—so that participants have reason to defend their assigned positions rigorously rather than collapsing into easy agreement. The debrief should map each moment of the activity onto the conceptual architecture: the temporal boundary of the specious present, the authority of immediate report, and whether consciousness can be rewritten after the fact without violating first-person privilege. Be explicit about what will predictably fail on first running—most likely that students conflate the perceptual illusion with the metaphysical claim, or that they treat memory error as equivalent to experiential revision—and tell me how to correct for it. Also be frank about which feature of the philosophical debate the activity cannot reproduce: for example, that in actual psychophysical experiments the relevant neural dynamics unfold over very brief intervals that are impossible to replicate in a seminar room, whereas here the revision is mediated by linguistic deliberation and group dynamics, so the facilitator must supply the neuroscientific framing separately. To prevent the exercise from being a mere recapitulation of the standard textbook distinction, inject a niche conceptual twist: include a variant condition in which the second dot appears in exactly the same location as the first, producing a stationary colour change with no motion trajectory, and ask the groups to argue whether this absence of spatial movement undermines the Orwellian claim that there is a coherent intermediate experience to be revised at all. Write the response as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a formal letter. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not ask for any.
