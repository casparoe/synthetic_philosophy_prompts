I've been running this idea past my grad studnets — the hedonistic imperative, basically the claim that we have an urgent moral duty to use germline editing and neurotech to wipe out suffering in all sentient beings, wild animals included, and that this overides concerns about 'naturalness' or whether engineered happiness counts as authentic experience. I think the evolutionary-psychology objections are weaker than they look, but I want to stress-test the view properly rather than just defend it.

So here's what I'm asking: what would actually count against this position? I don't want the usual 'playing god' hand-waving that defenders brush off. I want the real defeaters — arguments, empirical possibilities, or formal tensions — ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Specifically, I'm worried about a hidden dependency: does suffering-abolition presuppose a specific theory of consciousness (say, integrated information or global workspace) to know what we're editing out? And if that theory is wrong, does the imperative collapse? There's also the identity-continuity worry: if we engineer dopaminergic baselines across generations, are we reducing suffering for the same moral subjects, or just replacing them with happier ones? Does that distinction matter? And has the view been insulated into unfalsifiablity — e.g., by treating all resistance as evolutionary bias — and if so, is that insulation intellectually dishonest or just appropriate confidence?

Please also tell me what the view predicts that rival bioethical positions don't — maybe about the scale of wild-animal suffering — and whether that prediction puts it genuinely at risk. Finally, be honest: is this well-insulated in a bad way, or genuinely exposed? Don't lay out options and leave me hanging — take a definite position on whether these defeaters should make me significantly less committed, and say which ones you think the defenders themselves would have to concede are damaging.
