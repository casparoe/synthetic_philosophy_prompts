Been reading Bernard Williams on opera and that whole anti-moralist line — the idea that opera shows human situations in ways that outstrip moral judgement, and that trying to extract a 'message' from Tosca or Don Giovanni is already a kind of mistake. I've hit on a pattern in how people actually deploy this idea in arguement, and I can't tell if it's got a name.

The pattern is this. In discussion, someone raises a moral objection to an opera — say, that the music makes a villain's violence sound glorious, or that a heroine's suffering is aestheticised. The Williams-influenced reply is that the music doesn't endorse the action; it complicates it, creates ambivalence, shows the situation in its full human density. But then, with a different opera where the music is completely straightforward — no irony, no complication, just pure celebration of something morally dubious — the reply shifts: now it's not about ambivalence at all, it's about opera being allowed to be amoral, to present things without moral framing, and that's exactly what Williams wanted. So the 'complexity of form' is used as a shield in case one, but in case two the absence of that complexity is praised as artistic freedom. The underlying principle — that opera escapes moralism — is constant, but the mechanism invoked to protect it flips.

I've seen this with Don Giovanni, with Carmen, with a few others. Is there an established term for this specifc argumentative toggle, where an aesthetic feature (musical ambivalence) is treated as essential when it deflects criticism, but as optional — even undesirable — when it isn't present? It feels like a misreading of Williams rather than Williams's own view, but I'm after the concept for the misreading itself, if there is one.

If not, I'd like a proper carving: what makes it different from a simple double standard, where exactly the boundary is between legitimate anti-moralism and this slide, and whether it applies only when the aesthetic form is doing the work or also to other art forms where form and content can come apart. Please answer in British English — I'm writing this up for a seminar and want the spelling consistent. (Sorry for any typos — writing this on a train; there might be a couple I missed.)
