After a long argument with a friend last night about whether justice requires compensating people only for misfortunes that are genuinely no fault of their own, I find myself needing to turn our disagreement into an administratively usable tool. I sit on a municipal discretionary-support panel that meets monthly to allocate limited emergency and adaptation funds. Each case receives roughly twenty minutes of structured review; the decision must be defensible in writing; a senior officer must sign off; and I have no formal training in philosophy or law. I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in luck egalitarianism but stripped to what is practically operable, that lets the panel classify an applicant’s disadvantage as arising from brute luck, option luck, or a compound of both, and then assign a compensation tier—full, proportional, or none—with explicit stopping rules and explicit flags for where human judgement cannot be eliminated.

The procedure should take the form of an ordered sequence of tests or questions that a non-specialist can run in sequence, each with a criterion for a sufficient answer, and it should include: (1) a clear three-way taxonomy with decision boundaries that survive simplification; (2) a counterfactual isolation step—holding constant the background conditions of brute luck (birth, genetics, unchosen social position) and asking whether the disadvantage would still have occurred through the applicant’s own pathway—so that we do not confuse inherited resources or social capital with the choices made with them; (3) a foreseeability filter that distinguishes reckless or uninformed choice from choice made under reasonable uncertainty or constrained information; (4) explicit markers for cases where mental capacity, coercion, addiction, or severe information asymmetry render the option-luck classification unreliable, at which point the procedure must stop and hand the decision to the panel chair; (5) an honest statement of what the procedure cannot settle, including budget trade-offs, intergenerational effects, and cases where brute and option luck are so entangled that isolation fails; and (6) a brief note on where it is likely to misfire in practice.

Then apply the procedure to three cases. First, an applicant born with a progressive genetic condition that now requires extensive home modifications. Second, an applicant severely injured while pursuing a high-risk recreational sport they had engaged in for years with full awareness of the dangers and adequate safety equipment. Third, an applicant who inherited substantial capital, invested it in a speculative venture through a series of poorly researched strategic decisions, lost everything, and now applies for emergency housing support. For this third case, the procedure should produce a result I will probably dislike—specifically, that the bankruptcy is classified as option luck and therefore discretionary compensation for the investment loss is denied, even though the initial capital was itself a product of brute luck, leaving only minimal emergency shelter provision. Finally, state clearly what the procedure is unable to do when confronted with compound-luck scenarios in which background deprivation shaped the very preferences and risk-calculus that led to the choice. Write in formal British English, avoid bibliographies or citation requests, and do not ask me to look anything up.
