You are delivering the opening address at a public symposium held in the town hall of a region whose last major manufacturing plant has just completed its transition to full automation. The audience is divided: former assembly-line workers, the engineers who designed the automated systems, union representatives, local policymakers, and family members who have no background in philosophy. The address should run about ten minutes. Its central task is not to report on economic trends but to perform a conceptual clarification that determines what can honestly be said in this specific room.

Use Hannah Arendt’s distinction between labor (the cyclical effort required to maintain biological life), work (the fabrication of durable objects that constitute a shared world), and action (speech and deed among plural people). Do not treat these as decorative labels. Apply them to the automation paradox at this plant: the machinery eliminated not only repetitive labor but also the skilled craft—tactile calibration, troubleshooting, judgment under material resistance—that once made the line a site of world-building. What remains for humans is either high-level systems design (which is not labor but thinking) or residual maintenance tasks that machines have not yet economically justified replacing: a category of leftover labor that is neither meaningful craft nor necessary survival work, leaving a hollowed middle in human contribution.

The speech should ask whether honoring the displaced workers requires pretending that all their lost jobs were world-building, and whether praising the engineers requires ignoring that they have systematically removed the conditions for that kind of work. It must avoid two traps: false consolation about reskilling and the future of work, and romantic nostalgia for factory labor as inherently dignified. The framework should do structural work—shaping which forms of comfort, acknowledgment, and praise are conceptually available and which are dishonest—while remaining fully intelligible to someone who has never read Arendt. Do not reduce the idea to slogans, and do not ask the audience to admire abstraction for its own sake. Write the speech.
