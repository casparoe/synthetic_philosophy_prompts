I am writing a paper for my epistemology seminar on the structural features that distinguish conspiracy theories from legitimate historical inquiry, and I have built an argument that now seems to collapse the distinction entirely. (1) A belief system is epistemically vicious if every potential counterexample can be neutralized by an auxiliary hypothesis that is not independently testable. (2) Conspiracy theories are vicious in exactly this way: they deploy a distributed network of cover-up hypotheses—attributing contradictory testimony to disinformation campaigns, destroyed records, or coerced witnesses—so that no anomaly can ever reach the core claim. (3) Yet mainstream historiography about complex, partially classified events relies on structurally identical auxiliaries—archival gaps attributed to institutional secrecy, contradictory accounts explained by compartmentalization, missing physical traces explained by cleanup operations—and these auxiliaries are often equally insulated from direct testing because the relevant sources remain sealed or classified. (4) Moreover, the usual rescue—distinguishing “ad hoc” from “independently motivated” auxiliaries—fails here, since conspiracy theorists can cite well-documented histories of state deception and intelligence operations to motivate their cover-up hypotheses with precisely the same kind of background theory that historians use to motivate archival-loss hypotheses. (5) Therefore either mainstream historiography shares the same epistemic vice, or the presence of neutralizing auxiliaries is not sufficient to mark a belief system as a conspiracy theory. (6) I want to preserve the intuition that conspiracy theories are genuinely defective in a way standard historical belief is not, but I cannot see where the argument slips. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost—must I import a normative social-epistemic criterion such as peer review and transparent source access, or can the structural difference be recovered another way?
