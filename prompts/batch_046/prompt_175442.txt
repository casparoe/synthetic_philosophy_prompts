Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with two refrains (A1 and A2) and the rhyme scheme A1bA2 / abA1 / abA2 / abA1 / abA2 / abA1A2—centered on Bernard Williams’s integrity objection and the scenario in which a husband saving his wife from drowning is said to have one thought too many. The first refrain (A1) should carry an unmediated ground-project reason—e.g., “I pull her up because she is my wife”—and the second refrain (A2) should carry the mediating moral permission—e.g., “The calculus permits me to save her.” Let the repetition of these refrains across the tercets enact the recurring intrusion of impartial moral demand into personal attachment, and let the rhyme scheme’s circularity mirror the way that demand keeps returning rather than resolving.

In the first four tercets, build Williams’s picture with philosophical precision: ground projects as sources of meaning, the alienation that occurs when moral reasoning displaces the direct reason, and the specific charge that a theory requiring the agent to think “it is permitted” introduces one thought too many. Make the content accurate enough that a specialist in ethics recognizes the structure of the objection—its focus on authenticity, the critique of systematizing moral theory, and the distinction between what morality requires and what gives life point—rather than merely seeing the name attached.

In the fourth tercet, place the volta: introduce a less-discussed tension that Williams’s objection presupposes a kind of phenomenological transparency about motives—that we can cleanly separate an unmediated reason from layered ones—while psychological realism suggests motives are usually mixed. Add that stripping away the moral thought might not restore purity but could expose the ground project to contingency and moral luck, precisely the fragility that makes the attachment matter. Do not resolve this complication; let it distort both refrains so that by the quatrain they appear together unreconciled.

Conclude with the final quatrain bringing both refrains into proximity (A1 A2) without synthesis. The poem should leave open whether the moral thought corrupts the attachment or whether its absence reveals the attachment’s vulnerability. Avoid both a sermon about moral intervention and a shrug about inevitability; the form’s enforced repetition should do the work of showing how integrity and moral demand remain in perpetual, unresolved pressure. Keep the language formal but not archaic, and ensure the philosophical moves are rendered precisely enough to be recognized by someone familiar with the literature on integrity and moral psychology.
