I curate a small community seed archive. A long-time contributor delivered heirloom squash seeds that match, in appearance and handling, the variety I have catalogued before; her record is unblemished, and the packaging notes align with what I expect. Yet upon opening the packet I experienced a fleeting, difficult-to-articulate impression that something was wrong—only afterwards identifying a faint chemical sharpness in the smell. There is no visible mould, the texture seems normal, and a preliminary germination test has produced ambiguous results. I must decide whether to register these seeds as authentic for distribution.

My inclination is to weigh the contributor's reliability and the resemblance to prior samples heavily, but I am uncertain whether that initial, pre-verbal awareness—before I could specify what was amiss—ought to override or at least modify my judgment. Is there a principled way to resolve conflicts between immediate sensory awareness, testimony from a trusted source, comparison with known instances, and inference from the absence of expected signs of contamination? Please advise me on how to reason through this situation, distinguishing what is well settled in the relevant philosophical tradition from what is genuinely contested among its scholars, and present your answer in American English.
