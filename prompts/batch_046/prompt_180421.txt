I hold that in decision problems like the Pasadena paradox — where an option's expected utility is undefined not because it diverges to infinity but because its payoff series oscillates without convergence — the correct response is not to reject the option or declare the problem malformed, but to apply a truncation-robust rule: evaluate the sequence of expectations under successively larger bounded truncations of the payoff space, take the limit inferior, and prefer the option with the highest such liminf. This treats undefined expectation as a failure of pointwise convergence rather than a reason to suspend rational choice, and it allows comparison among options that all have undefined expectations. What would have to be true for this position to be false? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: which formal results about convergence or representation theorems would undermine it, which intuitions about rational risk-taking it violates, and whether it collapses into a disguised form of maximin or bounded-utility theory. In particular, which of these objections would a committed defender concede as genuinely damaging — perhaps the charge that liminf comparison is not invariant under equivalent reformulations of the payoff structure — and which would they dismiss as importing an unjustified demand for pointwise expectation? Does this view risk anything in adjacent areas, say about persistence conditions in decision theory or about how undefined probabilities should be treated more generally? I would personally find it most damaging if the position turned out to be extensionally equivalent to a simple bounded-utility fix, since that would strip it of its claimed distinctiveness.
