I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of aesthetics working on emotional responses to fictional narratives, and I am constructing a diagnostic instrument for the paradox of fiction. The standard literature organizes responses around canonical labels—Radford's irrationalism, Walton's make-believe, Currie's simulation, Carroll and Lamarque's deflationary accounts—but I want a taxonomy built from underlying commitments rather than from names, since readers often agree on the cases while disagreeing about which theory they "hold."

Please design a branching decision tree (or equivalent taxonomy) with exactly four binary diagnostic nodes. Each node must be phrased as a concrete judgment about a specific case or claim, so that a respondent can navigate it without knowing any philosophical labels. The four nodes must cover the following distinctions in this order:

1. Phenomenal parity: whether an emotional response to a fictional scenario (e.g., fear during a horror film, grief over a novel's protagonist) is phenomenologically identical in kind to its real-world counterpart, or whether it is a distinct affective mode (such as "quasi-emotion," "aesthetic fear," or "pretend-grief").

2. The coordination thesis: whether belief that the emotional object actually exists and is present is a necessary condition for the emotional response to be rationally appropriate, or whether such belief is unnecessary or only contingently related.

3. Source-monitoring asymmetry: whether the appropriateness of an emotional response depends on the subject correctly attributing its trigger to a fictional rather than real source—relevant to cases like weeping over Anna Karenina while fully aware she is not real.

4. Normative appraisal: whether emotional responses to fiction must be subject to the same norms of justification and practical action-guidance that govern real-world analogues (for instance, whether pity for a suffering fictional character is rationally appropriate in exactly the same way as pity for an actual acquaintance).

For each of the sixteen logical combinations, indicate whether it is occupied or unoccupied. Map at least the following positions into the tree: Radford's view that emotional responses to fiction are genuinely irrational; Walton's quasi-emotion / make-believe account; Currie's empathic simulation view; and the "no-paradox" deflationary position associated with Carroll and Lamarque (on which there is no genuine emotion, only thought about emotion). Note any empty combinations and briefly diagnose them: are they incoherent (for example, accepting phenomenal parity and full coordination while denying any source-monitoring asymmetry?), unattractive, or merely unexplored?

Additionally, flag any pair of positions that the tree fails to separate—particularly cases where two standard accounts share answers on all four nodes and diverge only on explanatory mechanism rather than on the diagnostic commitments themselves. The tree should be usable by someone with no prior knowledge of the debate: no node may ask "Do you accept Walton's view?" and the instrument must not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Keep the structure self-contained and the cases concrete.
