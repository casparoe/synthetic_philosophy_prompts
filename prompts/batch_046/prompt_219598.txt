Question: In their 2010 Behavioral and Brain Sciences article, Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan argue that WEIRD populations are psychological outliers, including in domains of moral cognition. Drawing on moral foundations theory (Graham, Haidt, et al.), explain whether cross-cultural variation in the weighting of care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and sanctity undermines moral objectivity. Your answer should (i) distinguish descriptive moral disagreement from the denial of culture-transcendent moral facts, and (ii) assess whether WEIRD data establish that moral judgments are causally determined by cultural ecology in a way that precludes tracking independent moral truths.

Student answer:
Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan (2010) show that WEIRD populations are outliers not only in analytic reasoning but in moral cognition: they weight care and fairness heavily while treating loyalty, authority, and sanctity as cognitive errors rather than genuine moral concerns. Graham and Haidt’s moral foundations framework confirms that in many non-WEIRD societies these binding foundations are weighted equally. Because the two populations reach contradictory verdicts—for instance, on whether disrespect to elders is a moral failure—there can be no culture-transcendent moral fact of the matter. The differences are driven by ecological variables such as market integration and kinship intensity, which causally determine moral intuition from the bottom up. So moral judgments do not track mind-independent truths; they are locally adaptive coordination devices. The objection that disagreement might reflect only partial access to shared norms fails, since there are no shared norms underlying the disagreement.
