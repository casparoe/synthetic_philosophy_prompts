I am preparing materials for a seminar on early modern philosophy of mathematics, and in discussion with colleagues I have become uncertain whether Hobbes’s constructivist geometry genuinely diverges from axiomatic geometry in concrete cases or merely redescribes the same figures with a vocabulary of motion. To settle this in my own thinking, I want an engineered scenario rather than a historical example. Please construct a realistic thought experiment—set in an ordinary workshop or precision-engineering context, confined to one paragraph, with no exotic physics or perfect-measurement devices—in which a continuous-motion mechanism traces a curved path around a fixed center at constant radial distance, yet due to an unavoidable physical constraint the path’s terminus fails to coincide with its origin by an amount smaller than any resolution the mechanism can detect, leaving no observable gap but also no true closure. The case should be built so that three accounts deliver three distinct verdicts: first, Hobbes’s generative account, according to which a circle is constituted by the continuous motion of a point equidistant from a center and returning exactly to its origin, so that an incomplete return breaks the geometric identity even when the defect is imperceptible; second, a strict Euclidean axiomatic account that defines a circle by a closed boundary of points and therefore denies that the traced path is a circle because the endpoint set is missing one member; and third, a modern formalist account that treats the mathematical model as describing a perfect circle but denies that the physical trace instantiates that model exactly. After presenting the scenario, walk through the reasoning that produces each verdict, identify the precise structural feature—the real but sub-resolution non-coincidence of origin and terminus—that forces the three accounts apart, and explain whether removing that feature collapses the case back into trivial agreement. Finally, include an honest assessment of how a defender of Hobbes’s view might attack the Euclidean verdict by appealing to Hobbes’s distinction between geometric description (the actual trace produced by motion) and abstract definition (a static boundary conceived independently of generation), and say whether that defense succeeds on its own terms or merely shifts the dispute to a different question about what geometry is for.
