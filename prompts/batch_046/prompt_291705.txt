Does the following pattern, which I keep running into when thinking about whether mental content is fixed by internal states or by external causal and social links, have an established name? The standard scenario is well known: two individuals share identical psychological profiles and use the same term, but one is causally connected to substance A in her environment while the other is connected to observationally indistinguishable substance B in his, and externalists claim their thoughts differ in reference. The configuration I have in mind is a variant. Picture not two isolated communities, but one linguistic community distributed across both environments — through migration, remote coordination, or shared institutional practices — so that the social chain that fixes the term's reference runs through both substances. Some members of the community are anchored to A, others to B, and a given speaker is linked to both subgroups with no internal psychological marker distinguishing the connections. Here the familiar division of linguistic labour appears to break down: there is no single environment or expert group that anchors the term for everyone, and the reference-fixing chain is bifurcated rather than unified. I want to know whether philosophers of language or mind treat this as a distinct problem with its own label — perhaps a distributed twin-environment case or a split reference chain — or whether it is subsumed under broader notions such as reference indeterminacy, ambiguity, or division-of-labour failure. Specifically, how does it differ from ordinary cases of vague or ambiguous reference? Where are its boundaries: does it require actual cross-environment interaction, or can it arise from purely counterfactual links? And is there any established argument that externalism either avoids or necessarily generates this bifurcation? I am not asking for a bare name; I want the carving — what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and which existing concepts come closest.
