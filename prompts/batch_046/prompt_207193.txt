I am a doctoral student in political philosophy working on global distributive justice, and I am trying to reach reflective equilibrium on what wealthy states actually owe to poor ones—treating my case judgments as data rather than consulting the literature. Here are six verdicts I hold with some confidence, though I worry they do not all cohere.

Case A: State X colonized State Y, extracted resources now valued at roughly 15% of Y’s current GDP, and now sends unconditional aid equal to that present value, with no policy strings attached. I judge that X has done precisely what justice demands—no surplus obligation remains, though further aid might be laudable.

Case B: State Z is affluent and has never harmed State W, which is impoverished due chiefly to internal corruption and misrule. Z sends large unconditional transfers. I judge that Z acts well, but does not discharge a strict duty of justice; the reason is weighty but closer to beneficence than to repayment of a debt.

Case C: State V’s historical emissions have disproportionately damaged poor State U. V provides climate-adaptation funding, but publicly frames it as charitable generosity and demands that U liberalize markets benefiting V’s exporters. I judge the transfer wrong in kind: the framing mischaracterizes the obligation as gratuitous, and the conditions corrupt the moral status of what should be compensatory.

Case D: State S sends aid to poor State T calculated explicitly to prevent migration from T to S. The aid is efficiently delivered, T’s citizens benefit materially, and migration falls. I judge that S has satisfied no genuine moral obligation to T; the self-interested motive does not merely diminish virtue but prevents the transfer from counting as justice done, even though T is better off.

Case E: States A and B both caused comparable climate harm to poor State C. A gives twice B’s contribution, but both exceed the compensatory threshold. I think A has done nothing more morally required than B; beyond the threshold, extra aid is supererogatory for both.

Case F: State R owes compensation of a determinate sum, but delivers it through an intermediary that retains 70% for administrative costs, so only a fraction reaches C’s population. I think R has not discharged its obligation, because justice requires that the beneficiaries receive the remedy, not merely that the donor expend the correct nominal amount.

My question is whether there is a single principle—perhaps combining causal responsibility, capacity, and a condition of respect for agency—that accommodates all six, or whether I must abandon one. I am especially torn on Case D: if the aid genuinely improves conditions in T, does a corrupt motive merely make S less virtuous, or does it genuinely nullify the claim that justice has been served? And on Case F, is the efficiency condition part of justice itself or only a separate prudential requirement?

Please state the best principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, apply it to a situation I have not listed—specifically, a case in which the owed compensation should go to a tyrannical government likely to steal it, and the donor can instead deliver aid through non-state channels but at higher risk of misallocation—and then say honestly which of my six verdicts the principle cannot save and why.
