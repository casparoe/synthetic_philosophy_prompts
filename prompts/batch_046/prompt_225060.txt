I'm a graduate student in a medical ethics seminar, and I want to turn the philosophical problem of diachronic authority—whether and under what conditions an earlier competent self can bind a later, differently situated self through an advance directive—into an operational decision procedure. The practitioner I have in mind is a hospital clinical ethics consultant who must make a documented recommendation within roughly forty-five minutes, using only the chart, a brief capacity screen, and one structured interview with an available proxy or family member, whenever a patient's currently expressed preferences conflict with a valid advance directive.

Please construct the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions or tests, written in plain language that a consultant without formal philosophical training can run. For each step specify: (i) what counts as a sufficient answer to pass, (ii) what counts as failure, and (iii) whether failure stops the procedure with a recommendation to honor the directive, to honor current preferences, or to escalate explicitly to a values-based deliberation that the procedure cannot settle. The procedure must distinguish three specific failure modes of current preferences: transient capacity fluctuation; adaptive preference formation shaped by the very condition the directive was designed to address; and genuine diachronic value revision. It must also specify what documentation is required at each node so that legal review can reconstruct the reasoning without interpreting clinical notes.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases, including one in which running it correctly produces a recommendation I personally find hard to accept:

1. A patient with moderate Alzheimer's dementia, whose directive refuses artificial nutrition and hydration, now reaches for food, smiles when spoon-fed, and shows no distress, but cannot articulate reasons or recall the directive.
2. A patient with treatment-resistant depression who executed a psychiatric advance directive categorically refusing future electroconvulsive therapy; she is now catatonic, minimally responsive, and the treating psychiatrist and family agree that ECT is the only life-preserving intervention.
3. A patient with severe anorexia nervosa whose directive refuses forced nasogastric feeding; she is now critically malnourished, repeatedly states that she prefers death to being fed, and her family is divided over whether this statement expresses her enduring values or is spoken by the disorder itself.

Finally, state precisely what the procedure cannot do: which metaphysical questions about personal identity or the "true self" it deliberately leaves open; where it is structurally likely to misfire; and what kinds of decisions—such as resource-allocation triage or purely symbolic disputes—it should never be used for.
