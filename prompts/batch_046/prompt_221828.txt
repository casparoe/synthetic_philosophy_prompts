Write an essay assessing whether Darwin’s evolutionary account of adaptive design and Turing’s formal model of computation together establish a single, coherent thesis of “competence without comprehension,” or whether they are structurally disanalogous inversions that should not be unified. Consider the view that natural selection generates functional complexity without foresight or understanding, while universal computation generates intelligent outputs without semantic grasp; then weigh the objection that evolutionary processes lack the intentional and representational architecture that computation, even in its barest syntactic form, implicitly presupposes. Examine whether accepting one inversion genuinely commits you to accepting the other, and whether both show that comprehension is inessential to competence, or merely relocate it to the environment, the selector, or the system designer. Reach and defend a specific conclusion: either that Darwin and Turing jointly support a general anti-comprehensionist thesis about design and intelligence, or that the analogy collapses at a precise point—such as the presence or absence of teleosemantic content, or the distinction between causal selection and rule-following—so that one inversion survives and the other does not.
