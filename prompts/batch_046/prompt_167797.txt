Consider the federal direct-democracy architecture in Switzerland, specifically the institutional asymmetry between constitutional and statutory lawmaking. At the federal level, citizens may launch a popular initiative—collecting 100,000 signatures—to propose a partial or total revision of the constitution, placing the amendment directly before the people and the cantons. By contrast, citizens possess no analogous power to initiate an ordinary federal statute: statutes originate in parliament, and citizens may only respond through an optional referendum, triggered by 50,000 signatures within 100 days, to challenge a law already enacted. Meanwhile, constitutional amendments proposed by parliament face a mandatory referendum, and both pathways require not merely a popular majority but also a majority of cantons. The result is a two-tier design in which the people are treated as direct co-authors of the fundamental law but merely as veto-players over ordinary legislation.

What philosophical commitments about democratic legitimacy, popular sovereignty, and the division of political labor does this architecture presuppose? Does it encode a distinction between constituent power and constituted power—implying that citizens hold an inalienable authority to constitute the basic rules but ought to delegate the crafting of ordinary policy to representatives? Or does it rest on an epistemic judgment that constitutional questions are broadly value-laden and accessible to lay judgment, while statutory questions are technically complex and best handled by legislators unless a substantial minority demands a popular check? Further, the practice applies a simple-majority threshold for rejecting statutes but demands both popular and cantonal majorities for constitutional change: what conception of equality—between individual citizens and between territorial units—does that differential weighting assume?

Reconstruct the implicit theory of democratic authority that best explains these combined features, and say whether the practice is philosophically coherent or whether its parts pull in opposing directions—for example, if citizens are deemed competent enough to draft constitutional provisions by initiative, why are they judged incompetent to draft statutes? Then describe what an alternative system built on the opposite premise—making statutory law more directly open to popular initiative than constitutional law—would have to change in its safeguards and thresholds.
