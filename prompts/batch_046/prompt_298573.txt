I chair the Joint Governance Committee of the Alpine-Adriatic Confederation, an intergovernmental body whose structure draws on Swiss cantonal autonomy, German Länder competence, and EU multi-level governance, though the Confederation itself is a hypothetical framework we use for case-study deliberation. We must render a formal opinion by Friday on an appeal filed by the federal Ministry of Territorial Integration against the canton of Obervinschgau. The canton has enacted an ordinance granting legal personality to the glacial watersheds within its borders, empowering a trustee board to block any federal infrastructure project—specifically an expanded transit corridor—that would alter watershed dynamics. The Ministry argues that this creates what our staff report calls a subsidiarity trap: local authority is being exercised over a resource system whose ecological and hydraulic effects are irreducibly cross-border, so that local jurisdictional closure makes cooperative governance structurally impossible rather than merely politically difficult. The canton replies that proximity is not only instrumentally efficient but democratically expressive—that recognizing the watershed's standing is precisely the kind of value-laden protection that the smallest competent authority should provide, and that federal preemption would reduce subsidiarity to a hollow administrative convenience.

Please draft the Committee's decision memo recommending one of three courses: (a) uphold the ordinance under a strict reading of subsidiarity; (b) invoke the Confederation's rarely used override clause on the ground that watershed governance exhibits recursive externalities defeating local jurisdictional closure; or (c) adopt a differentiated integration model in which the watershed retains legal personality but its veto is suspended for projects meeting a federally defined cross-jurisdictional necessity standard, with disputes resolved by a mixed arbitration council. The memo should present a clear, actionable recommendation with an implementation time frame; it should treat the canton's democratic-recognition argument seriously rather than reducing it to efficiency; and it must honestly address the strongest consideration for the losing option—namely, that option (c) risks instituting a subsidiarity of second order, in which every local autonomy claim becomes subject to a meta-test that effectively recentralizes authority beneath the language of cooperation.
