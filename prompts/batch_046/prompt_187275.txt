Write a deadpan parody of an end-user license agreement (approximately 500–600 words, in the voice of a Silicon Valley legal and technical document) for a fictional smart-home thermostat branded the "Dennett 3000." The agreement must require users, upon installation, to select one of Daniel Dennett's three stances—physical, design, or intentional—under which all device behavior will be interpreted for the duration of the warranty and support contract. The satire should exaggerate the real philosophical claim that real patterns are stance-relative rather than stance-independent, extending this logic to absurd bureaucratic consequences: physical-stance users must waive all claims that invoke the thermostat's "goals" or "beliefs" and may only report mechanical failures described in terms of heat-transfer physics; intentional-stance users must formally acknowledge that the device "desires" a specific ambient temperature and therefore forfeit coverage for any malfunction that can be redescribed as the thermostat successfully acting on that desire; design-stance users occupy a restricted middle tier with predictive rights but no ontological privileges. Introduce a fictional, niche "fourth stance"—the "market stance"—under which the thermostat's behavior is interpreted solely through supply-and-demand dynamics and price signaling; users selecting this option receive a rebate but surrender both predictive accuracy and any moral consideration in the event of malfunction. The humor should come from treating Dennett's actual argument about predictive strategy and ontological parsimony as though it were a legally enforceable metaphysical commitment, so that a philosopher of mind recognizes the piece as an internal exaggeration rather than a misunderstanding. Maintain sustained irony without winking; the document should read as if its drafters genuinely believe that stance selection binds the user to a particular ontology. Do not include citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for further sources.
