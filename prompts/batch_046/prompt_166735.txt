I'm writing a seminar paper on nineteenth-century poor-law administration, and I have become stuck on a pattern I see in the municipal archive that I cannot name. The papers—intake ledgers, disciplinary logs, and occasional correspondence—fill thousands of pages, yet almost nothing in them was authored by the inmates themselves. More puzzling is what happens when an inmate's own words do survive. A woman writes a letter protesting her separation from her children; it is preserved not in any file marked family petitions but clipped to a disciplinary report, catalogued under agitation and non-compliance. Another inmate dictates testimony about inadequate food to a visiting magistrate; the transcript is filed as inspection notes—irregularities, with the inmate's name listed only as an informant in the margin. The physical documents are present, but the archive's finding aids and folder titles render them legible only as evidence of institutional disorder, never as autonomous accounts of suffering or resistance.

I have begun calling this refractive presence in my notes, but I do not know whether archival theory or the history of record-keeping offers an established concept for exactly this phenomenon: the preservation of subordinate speech in a form that structurally prevents it from being read as speech from the subject's own position. It is not simple archival silence, since the papers are physically there; it is not ventriloquism, because no one is speaking for them; and it differs from the familiar methodological call to read against the grain, since the problem is not interpretation but the prior categorical framing that makes certain readings institutionally unavailable. I want to be precise about where the edges lie.

Is there a recognized term for this specific mode of inclusion-through-categorization? If there is, how does it differ from the nearby concepts I have mentioned? If there is not, can you propose a name together with a definition and boundary conditions—what would clearly count as an instance, what would be a nearby but different case, and whether my two examples above are central or marginal? I am not asking for a bibliography; I need the carving, not the citation.
