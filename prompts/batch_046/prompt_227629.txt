In our school's environmental ethics club, two members—Maya and Jordan—debated whether the district should halt neonicotinoid spraying on the athletic fields. Maya opened by arguing that even if an individual insect has only a one-in-a-thousand chance of being sentient, the global insect population—she cited an estimate running into the quintillions of living individuals—makes the aggregate expected suffering vastly greater than the harm we typically condemn in factory farming. Jordan replied that multiplying an arbitrary prior by a large cardinality is arithmetic, not reasoning; without an empirically calibrated base rate for insect consciousness, the expected-value figure is just a number we invent. Maya proposed a reference-class remedy: take the conservative lower bound of sentience prevalence among animals with centralized nervous systems, apply it uniformly, and the resulting aggregate still dominates familiar moral problems. Jordan then introduced what he called a unity condition: morally significant suffering requires a persisting subject that can experience harm over time, whereas insect nervous architecture—decentralized ganglia lacking sustained integration—produces only discrete nociceptive events, so treating each insect as an independent patient commits a category error. Maya challenged that the unity condition seems tailor-made to exclude insects post hoc, observing that rigorous versions might also exclude severely demented humans or newborns without justifying a proportional discount of their interests. Jordan conceded the consistency risk but raised a separate problem he termed modal aggregation: if we weight suffering across all insects by epistemic probabilities, consistency demands we do so across all possible worlds as well, which quickly yields undefined or divergent totals and thus renders the scale argument useless for practical decision-making rather than merely conservative. They asked me to adjudicate since neither would yield. Please examine each of the six moves in order—Maya's initial scale argument, Jordan's arbitrariness objection, Maya's reference-class defense, Jordan's unity-condition counter, Maya's consistency challenge, and Jordan's modal-aggregation closing argument—rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and giving brief justification for each rating. Then state who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing, particularly whether there exists a principled way to bound aggregation without abandoning probabilistic moral reasoning, or whether the unity condition can be refined so that it does not risk excluding marginal human cases.
