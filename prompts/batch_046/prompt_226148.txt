Consider the following argument about symbol grounding in cognitive systems that employ recursive synthetic anchoring — a framework in which every symbol receives its content by being linked to other symbols in a structured network, and that network is anchored at the periphery only by a small set of sensorimotor tags that are never themselves interpreted. (1) In such a system, any symbol that is not itself one of the peripheral sensorimotor tags has its content determined entirely by its relational position within the network. (2) If the anchoring tags are finite and fixed, then any symbol lying outside the causal reach of those tags — for instance, an abstract term such as justice or transfinite ordinal — receives its content solely from relations to other similarly unanchored symbols. (3) But relations among unanchored symbols are purely formal; they do not connect the system to the world in a way that distinguishes correct use from consistent misapplication. (4) Therefore, no symbol outside the peripheral set is genuinely about anything in the world; it is merely a node in an ungrounded formal game. (5) Human cognition, however, clearly employs such abstract symbols with genuine world-directed content. (6) So either human cognition is not a recursive synthetic system, or — if it is — it cannot possess the meaningful abstract concepts it manifestly does. (7) Hence, any account of mind that treats symbol grounding as purely relational and peripherally anchored must deny that humans have meaningful thoughts about abstract domains, which is absurd. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Identify the weakest step, explain why it initially looks compelling, and then price out the alternatives: what must one accept if one rejects that step? In particular, address whether there is a principled distinction between direct causal grounding and derivatively inherited grounding, and whether circularity in the anchoring loop is vicious or benign.
