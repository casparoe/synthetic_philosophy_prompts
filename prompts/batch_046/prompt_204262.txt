Consider the cluster of electoral practices adopted in various contemporary democracies: compulsory voting applied to citizens (as in Australia and Belgium), the lowering of the voting age to sixteen for certain local or regional elections, and the extension of local franchise to long-term non-citizen residents (as in several Nordic municipalities and some school-board elections). Taken together, what implicit theory of democratic membership and obligation do they presuppose? Do they treat voting as a republican duty tied to nationality, as a liberal entitlement grounded in affected interests, or as a capacity-based privilege that matures differentially across domains of governance? Identify the internal tensions: if universal citizen participation is compulsory because legitimacy requires it, why is that obligation withheld from permanent residents who are subject to the same local taxes and regulations? And if cognitive maturity is sufficient at sixteen for municipal decision-making, does reserving a higher threshold for national elections presuppose that political competence scales with jurisdictional complexity, or does it instead reveal citizenship functioning as an unexamined proxy for judgment? Distinguish what this cluster strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to accommodate, and determine whether any single coherent philosophical account—stakeholder, republican, or liberal—can justify all three practices without introducing ad hoc qualifications.
