I'm currently taking a course in philosophy of mind, and I've become preoccupied with a recurring argumentative maneuver I keep encountering in discussions about whether self-deception is genuinely possible. The pattern runs like this: a defender begins by describing self-deception as an internal division, where one mental subsystem withholds information from another so that the subject as a whole holds a belief while a constituent part knows its negation. When critics object that this collapses into two distinct agents within one body, or ask how a single subject can simultaneously occupy the roles of deceiver and deceived, the defender shifts ground and reconstrues the case in temporal terms: the self at one stage hides evidence from its own future stage, or constructs a narrative that a later temporal slice accepts without adequate scrutiny. Once that pressure subsides, however, the defender typically reverts to speaking in the language of parts, compartments, and simultaneous knowledge, as if the partition model had never been abandoned. I have observed this oscillation in seminar debates, in journal articles, and in my own attempts to formulate a coherent view. Is there an established concept or literature that captures this precise movement between simultaneous-partition and diachronic-fragmentation models? If there is not, can it be named and defined in a way that distinguishes it clearly from mere theoretical inconsistency, from straightforward equivocation, and from the defensive retreat characteristic of a motte-and-bailey strategy? I am less interested in receiving a bare label than in having the phenomenon precisely carved out: what it is, what it is not, and where its boundaries lie, especially with respect to theorists who genuinely integrate both models rather than simply switching between them for rhetorical shelter.
