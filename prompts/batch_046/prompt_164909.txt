I sit on the Oversight Committee for Strategic Forecasting Methodologies at the National Intelligence Assessment Board, and we face a concrete vote at Thursday's meeting. A proposal called the Two-Track Conditional Revision Protocol has been submitted by the regional forecasting division; it governs how analysts update their credences when they receive embedded conditional reports—structured as predictions that if adversary posture A obtains, then strategic outcome B is probable—and then independently observe either A or B through separate intelligence streams. The philosophical core is the well-known Judy Benjamin asymmetry: just as Benjamin was asked whether observing a revolution should increase her belief that her division was in enemy territory despite having no direct evidence on location, our analysts face pressure to revise beliefs about adversary posture when they observe the predicted outcome alone. The protocol's architects want to block this reverse update for all "strategic-tier" forecasts, claiming that treating conditionals as epistemic partitions rather than material implications prevents cross-domain belief contamination. But our senior epistemic-risk methodologist argues that this block is unstable, because the protocol offers no principled criterion distinguishing strategic from tactical conditionals, meaning the restriction either collapses into arbitrary gatekeeping or, if applied consistently, paralyzes legitimate forecasting altogether.

Please draft the committee's formal written opinion—addressed to the Board chair and suitable for circulation before Thursday—recommending whether to approve the protocol outright, reject it, or approve it with specific conditions, together with a concrete implementation timeline or sunset clause if it moves forward. Your memo should explain whether the Judy Benjamin-style reasoning actually supports suppressing reverse updates in an operational setting, or whether it reveals that such suppression requires a distinction the protocol fails to provide. You must treat the methodologist's objection about the missing principled line as the strongest consideration against adoption, engaging it honestly rather than dismissing it, and your bottom line should be clear enough that committee members can vote on it without further interpretation. Write in polished American English, avoid bibliographies or citations, and keep the reasoning accessible to board members without specialist training in epistemology but without reducing the philosophical stakes to a generic risk-management formula.
