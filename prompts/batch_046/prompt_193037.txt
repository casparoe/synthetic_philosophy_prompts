I serve as the philosophical advisor to the clinical ethics panel of a regional reproductive-medicine consortium, and we have reached an impasse that I think requires turning theory into machinery rather than more commentary. Our panel — a clinician, a patient advocate, and a rotating ethicist — must review roughly two embryo-selection proposals per month, now increasingly involving polygenic risk scores for non-pathological traits such as cognitive endurance, emotional resilience, and temperamental profiles. The framework we are attempting to operationalize combines the principle of procreative beneficence, which directs parents toward the child with the best expected life among available embryos, with the constraints of liberal eugenics: state neutrality, respect for plural conceptions of the good life, and an absolute prohibition on coercion. To prevent the procedure from collapsing into a single-axis optimization algorithm, I have proposed injecting what I call a pluralistic beneficence audit: well-being is treated as a vector of basic capabilities rather than a scalar, and every selection for a non-medical trait must pass an intergenerational openness test — namely, that the selected profile does not foreclose the future child's capacity to reject the value framework that motivated the selection.

Please construct for the panel a reusable decision procedure that can be executed in approximately thirty minutes per case without requiring live literature access. It should take the form of an ordered sequence of questions with clear stopping rules: specify what counts as a sufficient answer to move forward, where the procedure issues a firm negative recommendation, and where it must suspend judgment and hand the decision back to the panel because the conflict is one of values rather than applied ethics. The procedure must survive three hard cases: selection against a severe monogenic disorder; selection for the highest available polygenic score for educational attainment, where I suspect your procedure may approve the request despite my own intuition that cognitive enhancement selection should be blocked; and selection for a predicted high-agreeableness, low-novelty-seeking temperament profile chosen primarily to match the parents' preference for a tranquil domestic environment. In each case, show how the procedure runs and where it misfires or reaches its limits. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot do — for instance, whether it can adjudicate between parental autonomy and concerns about social stratification when both conditions are technically satisfied — and flag the steps that are judgment calls disguised as formal checks.

Finally, I ask that your response include a brief reflexive note identifying the points at which your reasoning is most vulnerable: which distinctions — for example, between disease and enhancement, or between legitimate parental preference and impermissible design — are doing the heavy lifting, and what kind of empirical finding or conceptual objection, such as the incoherence of the openness-to-revision criterion or the collapse of the scalar-versus-vector distinction in actual welfare economics, would lead you to revise your endorsement of the procedure's validity. Do not provide bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for further reading; the panel needs a tool, not a syllabus.
