I am applying for a six-month, part-time independent research grant to develop a philosophical framework for the ontology of generational cohorts, motivated by a recent personal change: my child was born last year, and I found myself unable to answer whether assigning her a “generation” describes anything real or merely applies a shifting statistical label. The precise question is whether cohort terms—Millennial, Gen Z, and so on—denote social kinds with modal and explanatory stability, or whether they collapse into age-plus-context descriptions with no independent ontological weight. Existing sociology treats them either as demographic artifacts or as identity-forming communities, but neither approach asks whether generational membership supports counterfactual generalizations—whether, for instance, being Gen Z explains dispositions that persist across nearby possible histories, or whether the explanation dissolves once we control for macroeconomic and technological conditions. I propose to adapt debates from the philosophy of social kinds—on whether race or gender function as “real kinds”—to cohorts defined by birth-year ranges anchored to contingent historical events. The method involves three conceptual test cases over six months: first, whether generational explanations survive counterfactual intervention on the defining technology or crisis; second, whether cohort boundaries are sharp or vague in ways analogous to natural-kind boundaries; and third, whether these kinds are “indexical,” meaning their membership conditions depend on the speaker’s present reference point rather than on intrinsic properties of members. Months one through two will frame the ontology and select comparative cases, including historical cohorts like the Lost Generation and life-phase sorts like adolescence. Months three through four will analyze counterfactual stability and modal robustness. Months five through six will draft the framework and specify failure conditions: the project fails if every generational claim can be translated without loss into non-generational terms, leaving no distinctive explanatory or modal work for the cohort concept. The deliverable will be a conceptual map distinguishing historical cohorts, phase sorts, and indexical demographic bins, with direct implications for how policymakers should use—or avoid—generational language in education and welfare design. The scope is deliberately philosophical rather than empirical; no survey work or citation-heavy literature review is required, and I will supply references myself.
