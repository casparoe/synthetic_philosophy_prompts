I have been working through an analogy between personal Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment that a colleague presented in a workshop on intertemporal democracy, and I find myself unable to discharge the conclusion without damaging something I want to preserve. Here is the reconstructed argument, with each step marked. (1) A rational agent may bind her future self through a Ulysses contract when she predicts that the future self will act against her enduring interests—say, by succumbing to temptation—provided that the present and future selves are parts of one continuing agent with legitimate authority across time. (2) By strict analogy, a democratic people may entrench constitutional provisions—such as rights against emergency suspension—when it predicts that future majorities will act against the people's enduring interests, provided that present and future majorities are parts of one continuing democratic agent with legitimate authority across time. (3) The condition in (2) is satisfied: present and future majorities constitute the same people extended diachronically, so we may call the relation diachronic popular sovereignty, and it licenses present binding of future democratic wills. (4) But if diachronic popular sovereignty licenses present authority over future temporal parts of the people, then consistency requires that it also license present majority authority over present dissenting minorities, since both are compositional or temporal parts of the same sovereign whole. (5) Therefore, constitutional precommitment justified on Ulysses-contract grounds does not merely protect rights against future backsliding; it also authorizes the present majority to suppress present dissent in the name of the people's true enduring will. (6) Yet this result is intolerable: it transforms precommitment from a safeguard for minorities into an instrument of present majoritarian domination, which is precisely what constitutional entrenchment is meant to prevent. (7) Hence either the analogy in (2) collapses, or constitutional precommitment carries authoritarian implications that undermine its democratic justification. I am inclined to reject (4), but I worry that doing so severs the analogy at its root and leaves constitutional precommitment without any intertemporal authority. Which step should be given up, and what does that surrender cost for the theory of democratic precommitment?
