I'm a doctoral student in moral philosophy, and at a recent workshop on distributive ethics I observed an extended exchange between two postdocs—Alex and Jordan—over whether the classic separateness of persons objection survives once we reject a substantialist metaphysics of the self. I am trying to work out the dialectic for my own research and would like a careful adjudication.

(1) Alex opened by arguing that the objection tacitly presupposes a metaphysically robust boundary between persons. If personal identity consists in psychological continuity or four-dimensional perdurance, there is no non-arbitrary line that blocks aggregation across conventionally distinct persons without also blocking aggregation across different temporal stages of one life. So if utilitarians are wrong to aggregate interpersonally, consistency demands they reject intrapersonal aggregation—yet we rationally trade present comfort for future welfare. The only coherent position is to permit both aggregations or reject both.

(2) Jordan replied that this misidentifies the objection's ground. The separateness constraint is normative, not metaphysical: it derives from the authority of first-personal practical reasons. Within one life, a single practical reasoner can rationally authorize present burdens for future benefits. Between persons, the sacrificed party cannot authorize benefits accruing to others. The relevant boundary tracks the scope of rational authorization, not an ontological substance.

(3) Alex countered that Jordan has now abandoned the classic formulation—treating persons as fungible containers of utility—and substituted a claim about practical authority. But if practical authority does all the work, the objection is not specifically about the separateness of persons but about the conditions for rational consent. A contractualist could accept Jordan's point without endorsing any ontologically interesting separation between persons. Thus the objection either collapses into contractualism or remains unsupported.

(4) Jordan conceded the reformulation but insisted it preserves the core anti-utilitarian insight. Even on this normative reading, interpersonal sacrifice lacks the authorizing relation that renders intrapersonal trade-offs rational. The utilitarian treats the sacrificed individual's reasons as lacking special weight, which is precisely what the separateness objection targets—regardless of whether the vocabulary is metaphysical.

(5) Alex then pressed with split-brain and severe dissociative cases: if unity of practical reasoning is the criterion, then where one brain supports two largely non-communicating centers of reasoning, sacrificing one center's interests for the other should be as impermissible as interpersonal sacrifice. Yet our intuitions here are muddy, and if Jordan's criterion fractures in these cases, it shows that "separateness" tracks functional integration rather than moral authority. This suggests the utilitarian might reply that aggregation is permissible precisely where functional integration is absent—reversing Jordan's conclusion.

(6) Jordan responded that split-brain cases are pathological limits, not counterexamples. The criterion is not perfect functional integration but the presence of a unified practical standpoint capable of endorsing trade-offs. Where that standpoint is genuinely divided, we may indeed have two persons, and aggregation between them is wrong for the same reason. The edge case therefore confirms rather than undermines the framework.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—particularly whether there exists a principled distinction between intrapersonal and interpersonal aggregation that depends neither on a contested metaphysics of personal identity nor on an unexplained normative primitive such as "first-personal authority." Be explicit about where your own adjudication relies on premises that a reasonable philosopher could reject.
