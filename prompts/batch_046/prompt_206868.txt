J. used it to write a long refusal of further ventilation, but the family is arguing the interface only has derived intentionality, like Dennet's wandering vending-machine example, so the output isn't J.'s original mental state and shouldn't count as informed refusal. The clinical team says that's wrong because J.'s brain is still the source, just mediated like a pen or a voice box. Our policy says we honor "authentic autonomous refusal" but doesn't say wether mediated expression counts.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate before thursday's meeting. Recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame — approve honoring the refusal, reject it, or approve with conditions — and say exactly what evidence or safeguards we'd need. Treat the family's worry seriously: don't just restate that the brain is biological so it's original. Deal with the Searle-style objection that any computer mediation introduces derived intentionality that might corrupt the source, and also with the Dennett-style point that all intentionality is interpreted anyway so mediation doesn't change the status. The memo should say where the line is between acceptable mediation (like writing with a pen) and unacceptable derivation (like a chatbot composing the refusal for you). Include an honest treatment of the strongest argument against whatever you recommend, and flag any point where the committee is really making a value call rather than applying a fact. Keep it practical enough that the clinicians and family can actually respond.
