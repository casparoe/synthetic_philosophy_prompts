hey — working on a piece about ancient death-philosophy sneaking into modern grief-talk, and i keep hitting this Zhuangzi argument that feels like it collapses into wildness. here's the chain i built:

(1) Zhuangzi frames death as qi transforming, like ice melting into water or a log burning to ash — no real loss, just rearrangement.
(2) So grieving a death is like crying because your ice cube melted: you're clinging to a temporary shape, not the substance.
(3) Therefore the rational response is total equanimity toward dying.
(4) But transformation never stops — cells, moods, relationships are all shifting every second, so life itself is continuous death-and-rebirth.
(5) If we shouldn't grieve the big transformation, we shouldn't be attatched to any of the small ones either.
(6) That means no joy in friendship, no pride in craft, no reason to stay alive — basically nillism with a daoist hat.
(7) Yet Zhuangzi clearly celebrates the butcher dancing with the knife, or friends making music together.
(8) So either there's a hidden distinction between 'natural' and 'forced' transformation he never justifies, or the jump from (3) to (5) is a scope slip that proves too much.

where does it actually break? i want to keep (1) and (3) — they feel humane in a strange way — but i don't want to pay the price in (6). is there a hidden premise about identity being tied to form rather than pattern? or is the error somewhere in how i applied the transformation idea from dead bodies to living experience?

would love your take — especially if there's a standard move i'm missing (though i can't check right now since i'm writing this on a plane without wifi).
