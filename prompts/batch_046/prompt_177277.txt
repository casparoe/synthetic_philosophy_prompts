I have been working on the boundary problem in democratic theory—specifically, how to justify a stable demos without collapsing into either universal inclusion or arbitrary exclusion. I want to press what I call the problem of recursive constituency: if democratic legitimacy requires that every decision affecting interests be made by those affected, then the decision about who belongs to the demos is itself such a decision. Consequently, the constituency entitled to draw the boundary must include everyone affected by that boundary-drawing act, which presupposes a prior boundary, generating an infinite regress. It seems to me that unless we can identify a non-arbitrary stopping point, democratic self-rule is either globally diluted—every local decision requiring global input—or reduced to pure conventionalism. Given this, should we not conclude that the boundary problem is not merely practical but conceptually fatal to the normative coherence of democracy? I would be persuaded otherwise if there were a principled distinction—perhaps between constitutive and derivative affectedness—that could halt the recursion without smuggling in an arbitrary cutoff, though I suspect any such distinction merely relocates the conventionalism. Please address this directly, and indicate what would change your assessment and where your reasoning is most vulnerable to failure.
