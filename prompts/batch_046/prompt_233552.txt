The dispute over Newton's bucket—whether the concavity of water in a rotating vessel demonstrates absolute space or can be fully accounted for by relations to distant bodies—has persisted from Newton through Mach and into the philosophy of general relativity without resolution. Substantivalists treat spacetime as an independent entity with intrinsic geometric properties; relationists insist that all spacetime structure reduces to relations among physical fields and bodies. Today both sides accommodate the hole argument and the dynamical equations of GR, yet neither concedes ground. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not empirical but conceptual: the sides disagree about whether rotational inertia must be explained by reference to an unobservable substrate, or whether such a substrate is an explanatory redundancy that violates ontological parsimony, and they may be forcing two distinct questions—one about the ontology of geometry, one about the criteria for adequate physical explanation—under a single heading. Diagnose whether this is a verbal disagreement about the term 'space', a substantive metaphysical conflict about existence, or an ill-posed problem generated by treating inertia as derivable rather than primitive. Given your diagnosis, say what would actually count as progress: a formal result showing that relational dynamics necessarily reproduce a specific rotational feature, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the need for absolute structure, or an argument that rotational inertia is a bedrock disposition making the debate moot?
