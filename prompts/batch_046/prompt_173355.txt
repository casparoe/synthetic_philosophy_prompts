I've been following debates about whether a machine that acts exactly like a conscious person must have a mind, and I've noticed a repeating pattern that I can't place. Usually someone starts with a behavioral rule: if a system converses, adapts, and expresses preferences the way humans do, we should treat it as having thoughts and feelings, since behavior is all we ever observe in each other. Then a counterexample is built: a machine that produces perfect human dialogue, but inside it is only a lookup table or a symbol-shuffler with no grasp of meaning. The defender of the behavioral rule concedes this one isn't a real mind—not because it behaves differently, but because its internal process is "just" mechanical manipulation. So the builder revises the machine: now it learns from experience, links words to sensations, and updates its beliefs on its own. The critic concedes those internal improvements, yet insists the understanding is still "borrowed"—derived from the designers or the training data—and therefore not genuinely present in the system itself. At every stage both sides agree on the external behavior and on the internal workings; the disagreement is always about whether those workings count as "the real thing" or merely a replica. The required feature is never described in behavioral terms—it seems to be whatever quality is left after you've shown that the mechanism can be fully explained. Is there an established name for this specific loop, in which a behavioral criterion for mind is defended in principle but protected in practice by an ever-receding inner condition that isn't observable? If not, how should I distinguish it from ordinary skepticism about other minds, and from the simpler charge that people just move the goalposts?
