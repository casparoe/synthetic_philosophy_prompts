A friend built this argument about Nietzsche's diet-and-health stuff and I can't tell if it's actually broken or just uncomfortable. Here's the chain:

(1) In Ecce Homo and his letters, Nietzsche basically says every philosophical system is a confession of the author's body—especially digestion, climate, and what they eat. You can't understand Kant or Schopenhauer without knowing thier diet and whether they were constipated.

(2) Today we know the gut-brain axis is real: microbiome composition affects cognition, mood, and even decision-making. So Nietzsche's claim isn't just metaphor—it's empirically backed. Philosophy really is partly a digestive symptom.

(3) If that's right, then the validity of any argument depends on the arguer's physiological state at the time. Two people with opposite diets could reach opposite conclusions about the exact same logical problem, and neither is wrong—they just have different guts.

(4) But then there's no trans-personal standard of rationality. Modus ponens doesn't work everywhere; it works for people whose microbiomes happen to favor it. Logic becomes a digestive preference writ large.

(5) Yet Nietzsche clearly thinks some arguments are genuinely better—he spends hundreds of pages tearing down Christian morality and pushing the will to power and eternal recurrence. He doesn't treat these as mere taste reports.

(6) So he must believe his own digestive state tracks truth, while Kant's tracks error. But he never gives a non-circular test for which digestion is truth-tracking.

(7) The problem is that health for Nietzsche is partly defined by the very outlook he's defending—noble, life-affirming, strong. So the criterion is circular: healthy digestion produces the will to power, and the will to power proves the digestion was healthy.

(8) Therefore either his physiological reductionism applies to himself, which makes his own philosophy just another symptom with no special authority, or he secretly relies on a non-physiological standard of truth he claims doesn't exist.

I feel like step (3) or (6) might be where it slips, but I'm not sure. Which premise do you reject, and what does giving it up cost? If I want to keep (1) and (2) intact, what's the least painful way out?
