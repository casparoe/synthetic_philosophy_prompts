In a recent discussion of political legitimacy, an interlocutor summarized Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor as holding that human beings will invariably surrender freedom in exchange for bread, miracle, and authority, and that freedom is therefore too heavy a burden for ordinary people to bear. I want to understand precisely what philosophical work this summary can do. Could you lay out the distinct, precise claims it might be expressing—psychological, normative, metaphysical, and conceptual—paying particular attention to whether it targets negative liberty (the absence of external interference), positive liberty (the capacity for rational self-direction), or both? For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and say whether it actually supports the conclusion that democratic autonomy overestimates human capacity. In addition, identify the hidden presuppositions about happiness—hedonic, eudaimonic, or otherwise—that the argument requires to move from "people suffer under freedom" to "they ought to be relieved of it." I am not seeking references or a reading list; I want a menu of disambiguated interpretations with verdicts on which ones the slogan's typical deployment actually requires.
