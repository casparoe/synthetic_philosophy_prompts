I'm attempting to reach reflective equilibrium on the ethics of communicative indirectness, white lies, and culturally coded bluntness, using my own verdicts on concrete cases as the data. Case judgments: when a Dutch interlocutor states plainly that my argument is confused and needs restructuring, I judge this not wrong—indeed, respectful; when a Japanese interlocutor offers the phrase "That is a very interesting direction" in a context where both parties understand this to signal serious reservations, I likewise judge it not deceptive; telling an elderly relative that I cherish an unwearable sweater she made feels permissible, though barely; telling a hiring committee that I resigned voluntarily when I was dismissed feels clearly wrong; telling a dying patient, in response to direct inquiry, that an estranged child telephoned when no such call occurred feels wrong despite the comfort it might provide; telling a close friend that their evidently failing venture is sound feels wrong; writing "payment has been sent" when it has not feels wrong, though less gravely wrong than the hiring deception. The pair that resists reconciliation: in Dutch interactional norms, refusing an invitation with an unadorned "No, I will not attend" is regarded as polite, whereas in Japanese norms, uttering "I will think about it" with mutual recognition that refusal is intended avoids explicit negation without seeming deceptive—yet telling a child that a deceased pet went to live on a farm, which similarly spares direct emotional harm and operates within a shared narrative frame, strikes me as wrong. Is there a unified principle—perhaps distinguishing belief-targeting deception from affect-targeting misalignment, or weighting epistemic authority against emotional vulnerability—that accommodates these verdicts? If so, state it precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, render verdicts on cases I have not considered (e.g., a physician who describes a terminal prognosis using deliberately ambiguous statistical language to avoid explicit acknowledgment of death, or a supervisor who uses calibrated vagueness—"Your contribution is adequate"—knowing an insecure subordinate will interpret this as high praise, where the intent is purely protective and not strategic), and be explicit about which of my judgments must be abandoned if simplicity demands it. In particular, address whether the farm-dog case and the Japanese refusal can be brought under the same principle without collapsing into either pure cultural relativism or an implausible universalism.
