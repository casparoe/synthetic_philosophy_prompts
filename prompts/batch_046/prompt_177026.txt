Clinical psychology and addiction counseling routinely diagnose and treat “denial”: a patient is said to genuinely believe that they are not addicted, or that a devastating loss has not occurred, despite possessing evidence that convinces observers, and the therapeutic task is to break through this self-deception so the patient can face reality and take responsibility for change. The practice thus treats denial as both genuinely belief-like and motivated, yet it also treats the patient as a single rational agent who, once the denial lifts, can recognize the truth and be held accountable for acting on it. Meanwhile philosophers have long argued that self-deception is conceptually incoherent—one mind cannot simultaneously know P and intentionally conceal P from itself—and have offered divided-self, non-intentionalist, or deflationary reconstructions to save the appearances. What theory of self-deception does the therapeutic practice of diagnosing and treating denial strictly presuppose? Must it commit to a partitioned mind in the Freudian style, or can it be sustained by a non-doxastic or revisionist account? Is the practice internally consistent with its own commitment to unified rational agency, and what would clinical intervention have to become if we replaced the underlying model with one that denies self-deception is ever genuinely possible?
