I spent thirty years in condensed-matter physics before turning to philosophy of social science, and I have become convinced of a particular reading of Weber: that the Protestant Ethic thesis is best understood not as a simple correlation but as a claim that a specific value-orientation—inner-worldly ascetic rationalization—exerted downward causal efficacy on economic behavior, analogous in logical form (though not in mechanism) to how boundary conditions constrain physical systems without being reducible to the constituent particles. I want to know what would genuinely count against this position. Not merely historical counterexamples, but conceptual defeaters: does the thesis become unfalsifiable if every economic outcome can be narrated as an elective affinity? Can values be causally efficacious without being epiphenomenal to material interests? And if we reject downward causation in the natural sciences, are we compelled to reject it here, or does the social domain carry a special exemption? Rank the defeaters by how decisive they should be for someone holding this view, distinguish those a committed defender would concede from those they would dismiss, and indicate whether insulating the thesis against all of them is a theoretical virtue or a vice.
