everyone in my philosophy of politics circle keeps comparing a nation across generations to a corporation with perpetual legal personality -- the idea being that just as a corporation survives the death of its shareholders and can still be sued for old contracts, a nation survives its citizens and can still bear guilt for old crimes. people use this to defend the idea that modern Germans can bear some kind of guilt for the Holocaust, or that Americans inherit guilt for slavery. but i'm skeptical. can you audit this analogy? map out the correspondance table -- what plays the role of the shareholders, the board, the contract, the lawsuit, the bankruptcy -- then show where it breaks down, especially once you bring in Jaspers's distinction between criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt. does the corporation model smuggle in the idea that guilt is just a transferable legal liability? and does the conclusion that collective guilt survives across generations actually hold up, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument?
