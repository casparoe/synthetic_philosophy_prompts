I am attending a graduate seminar on moral responsibility and witnessed a sustained disagreement between two fellow participants—let us call them Leo and Mara—concerning what they termed "professional outcome luck." They were examining a pair of parallel scenarios: in both, a civil engineer approves a bridge design that contains an identical, non-obvious miscalculation in load distribution. In Scenario One, the bridge is constructed in a region that later experiences an unprecedented seismic event; the flaw interacts catastrophically with the tremor, and several people are killed. In Scenario Two, the bridge is built in a geologically stable area, stands for decades without incident, and is eventually decommissioned, with the error discovered only during demolition and no harm caused. Leo and Mara agreed that the engineer's mental states, diligence, and actions were identical across both cases, yet they disagreed sharply about the proper moral evaluation.

Leo argued that the engineer in Scenario One is properly subject to graver moral blame—not merely legal liability—because moral assessment tracks the actual causal footprint of one's conduct in the world. He claimed that to insist upon identical blame is to treat moral evaluation like an internal scorecard, ignoring that being a responsible agent means being answerable for what one's actions actually bring about, including unforeseeable interactions with the environment. Mara countered that this collapses the distinction between moral blame and causal responsibility; she said that if the seismic event was genuinely unforeseeable, then the harmful outcome lay outside the agent's control, and the control principle demands that blame vary only with what was within the agent's power—namely, the diligence exercised and the error made. She added that Leo's view makes moral luck inescapable in a manner that undermines the very point of holding people accountable for their choices rather than for cosmic accidents.

Leo replied that Mara was treating blame as purely retributive and internal, whereas in professional contexts it also serves communicative and restorative functions: expressing graver concern in Scenario One honours the victims and signals to the profession that outcomes matter, even when they are partly unlucky. Mara responded that this makes blame a kind of theatrical gesture calibrated to audience needs rather than to the agent's desert; she asked whether, on Leo's view, we should also praise the engineer in Scenario Two more highly had the bridge accidentally saved lives during an unforeseen flood, and if not, why blame should be asymmetrically outcome-sensitive. Leo conceded the asymmetry point but maintained that negative outcomes carry special moral weight because they involve harm to others that demands acknowledgment, whereas fortunate outcomes do not generate parallel obligations. Mara then said that Leo had smuggled in a consequentialist justification for blame—its social effects—while claiming to defend a non-consequentialist intuition about answerability, and that this tension undermines his position.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move. For each contribution by Leo and by Mara, indicate whether the move is clearly mistaken, largely correct, partly right but incomplete, or otherwise, and briefly justify your rating. Then say which of them is more right overall, and identify at least one important consideration that both have overlooked—particularly regarding how institutional or professional roles might reshape the control conditions they are both assuming apply uniformly. Please write your response in British English, and maintain an analytical rather than conversational tone throughout.
