I'm designing a diagnostic taxonomy for an advanced philosophy-of-science seminar on Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference. I want a decision tree — not a catalog of named schools — that sorts a respondent into a position by how they answer concrete diagnostic cases, with each branch labeled by the specific claim that splits it from its siblings.

Build the tree from the logical space first: begin with whether the paradox demonstrates that continuous probabilities are impossible, that indifference requires an invariant symmetry constraint, that every parameterization is an equally legitimate prior, or that the difficulty is purely epistemic (a fact about our ignorance rather than about the world). Only after fixing the branching structure should you place named positions in the leaves.

Use these three cases as the core sorting questions, phrased so someone can answer without knowing the labels:
1. The chord problem: is there a single correct probability that a randomly drawn chord in a circle is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle?
2. The inscribed triangle: if three points are chosen independently and uniformly on the circumference, is there one well-defined expected area for the triangle they form?
3. The square-point case: if a point is chosen uniformly within a square, is the probability that it lies closer to the center than to any corner well-defined independently of the coordinate system or metric used to express "uniform"?

For every leaf, provide:
- the position's name (or "unnamed" if the combination is unoccupied);
- one sentence stating its most awkward or counterintuitive commitment.

The tree must include exactly these named leaves:
- a Jaynes-style invariance / symmetry-selection view;
- a view that rejects continuous applications of indifference altogether (in a von Mises-style spirit);
- a Bayesian parameterization-subjectivist view that treats each parameterization as a legitimate prior;
- and one "unnamed" leaf for a meta-indifference position that applies the principle of indifference not to outcomes within a parameterization, but to the space of parameterizations itself.

Additional structural requirements:
- Identify at least one unoccupied combination of answers, and briefly diagnose whether the gap is best explained as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
- Flag explicitly any pair of named positions that your branching questions cannot separate, and explain why they collapse onto identical answer patterns.
- Keep the tree to about six to eight branching nodes and no more than nine leaves total.
- The intended audience is philosophically literate but not assumed to have memorized the literature's taxonomy; answers should depend on conceptual commitments, not on recognizing technical labels.
- Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, requests for citations, or prompts for further sources.
- Do not rely on obscure historical details, precise publication dates, or niche formalisms that might not be reliably recalled; ground the distinctions in commitments visible in standard philosophical treatments of the paradox.

Present the taxonomy with the splitting claims clearly labeled at each node, the case-questions embedded where they do diagnostic work, and a brief scoring key that maps a pattern of answers to the corresponding leaf.
