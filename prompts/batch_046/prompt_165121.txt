I'm preparing a handout for an independent philosophy study group at my high school, and I want a decision tree—no more than six branch nodes, ending in named or unnamed positions—that sorts views on institutional reform guided by Burkean conservatism and Chesterton's fence. Rather than repeating the standard examples of property boundaries or ancient monuments, I want the tree applied to what I'm calling latent infrastructure: social practices or design patterns whose original purpose has been forgotten yet still coordinate behavior in ways not visible to the people using them, such as the spacing of trees along old postal roads, formatting conventions in legal briefs, or the architecture of grading rubrics that quietly shape classroom dynamics. The tree should begin from one concrete commitment: you discover that a widely used institutional feature no longer serves its original purpose, but removing it causes subtle coordination failures you did not anticipate. Each branch should split on a claim that genuinely divides positions—whether invisible coordination counts as a reason to preserve, whether the reversibility of reform matters, whether the institution protects a moral boundary or merely an efficient one, and whether epistemic humility about unintended consequences is by itself a decisive reason for inaction. Every leaf should carry a position name (or unnamed if no thinker defends it) and one sentence describing its most awkward commitment, for instance a view that must defend preserving an obviously harmful coordination mechanism, or a view that must defend tearing down something whose full function it admits it cannot map. Please flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate, and identify one combination of answers that occupies no named position, with a brief diagnosis of whether that vacancy is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the tree should be built from the logical space of answers rather than from a roster of famous authors. Keep the language precise enough that a student could answer each node without already knowing which interpretation it leads to.
