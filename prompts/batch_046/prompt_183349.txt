Write a dialogue for an advanced seminar in philosophy of mind and value. The interlocutors are Dr. Elena Marchetti, who defends a qualitative-identity thesis about pleasure—holding that every pleasant experience contains an identical, irreducible phenomenal quality she terms “positive affective tonality”—and Dr. Marcus Liu, who advances a functional-heterogeneity thesis, treating “pleasure” as a family-resemblance concept that groups together disparate neural and behavioral states lacking any shared introspective core. Rather than rehearsing familiar examples, have them engage a specific, under-examined tension: Marchetti argues that even in ostensibly “cool” cognitive satisfactions—such as recognizing the flaw in a long-held argument—there persists a subtle but genuine affective warmth accessible to refined phenomenological attention; Liu counters by distinguishing “reward registration” (a subpersonal computation tracking utility) from “hedonic experience” (a first-person qualitative state), and contends that Marchetti systematically mistakes the former for the latter whenever she reports detecting “warmth” in intellectual achievement. The exchange must contain real back-and-forth: Marchetti should challenge Liu to account for the cross-contextual reliability with which subjects identify diverse states as pleasant unless they are tracking a common phenomenal feature, and she should ask why his functional taxonomy does not collapse into an eliminativism about felt quality altogether. Liu should press Marchetti on whether her “tonality” remains detectable in pathological anhedonia, where patients pursue rewards and register success without reporting any qualitative feel, or whether it risks becoming an unfalsifiable theoretical projection; he should also demand an explanation for masochistic pleasures that seem phenomenologically aversive yet are pursued as goods. Each speaker should conclude by identifying the precise empirical, conceptual, or phenomenological finding that would compel them to concede. Avoid attributing specific published arguments to named scholars outside the dialogue; develop the positions through original reasoning, and do not include bibliographic references or reading lists.
