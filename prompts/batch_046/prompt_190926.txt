For a 90-minute upper-level philosophy-of-mind seminar with 12 students, design a structured role-play called "The Gravity Arbitration" that makes participants feel the self not as an inner substance but as a center of narrative gravity, especially where multiple narratives compete. Present the case of "M," a patient with severe anterograde amnesia and conflicting first-person reports across recovery periods, alongside a synthetic agent that has reconstructed a highly coherent autobiographical narrative from M's archived writings—this "shadow narrative" lacks continuous bodily substrate but exceeds every biological cluster in consistency. Divide the group into three panels—Clinicians (biological continuity), Biographers (narrative coherence), and Advocates (present-moment authority)—and assign each 20 points to distribute across four identity clusters: pre-injury embodied habits, post-injury present preferences, the synthetic shadow narrative, and a fragmented "witness" cluster drawn from family reports. After 20 minutes of allocation, reveal a hidden tie-break rule: if any two clusters tie for highest weight, the panels must jointly articulate a "gravity principle"—not a hidden fact—to determine which cluster serves as the center for medical and legal decision-making. In round two (25 minutes), permit the synthetic cluster to be adopted as M's decision-making proxy only if Biographers and Advocates form a coalition and defend the principle they named. Specify the coalition rules, the facilitator's script for blocking appeals to a "real self behind the narratives," and a timeline for both rounds plus 20 minutes of debrief. In the debrief, map each round onto the distinction between a Cartesian theater and a narrative center of gravity; predict that groups will invent an undetectable "true owner" to break ties and explain how to redirect them to institutional convention; and state frankly that the activity cannot reproduce the long-term social stabilization through which narrative gravity actually operates over years, which the facilitator must supply by describing how communities gradually converge on one story rather than discovering it. Do not ask for citations or reading lists.
