i just had this long, kind of heated argument with my friend about whether the self who signs an advance directive is basically a different person from the self lying in the ICU, and now i have to actually make this useful at work. i’m the clinical ethics consultant at a mid-size hospital, and every few weeks i get paged because a family wants to override a living will or an attending is unsure whether to honor a refusal of treatment when the patient can’t speak. i need a reusable decision procedure—not a one-off opinion, but something like a flowchart in words—that translates whatever philosophy actually knows about the authority of the earlier self into steps i can run in about fifteen minutes. it has to work for someone without a philosphy background, include a clear sign-off point for the attending and the family liaison, and be honest about which steps are real tests and which are just judgment calls dressed up as boxes on a checklist.

the procedure should handle the hard cases we actually see: ambiguous wording ("no heroics"—does that cover antibiotics?), the patient who wrote the directive at thirty but now at eighty with dementia seems genuinely content despite earlier instructions, and family members who claim the patient "changed their mind" without documentation. apply it to three worked cases, including one where the checklist gives an answer i personally won’t like—maybe honoring a very old refusal that now looks like it’s causing avoidable suffering. also tell me exactly where the procedure will misfire or stay silent, and what it can’t settle on its own. no bibliographies or reading lists, please; i just need the operational version of the idea so i can use it monday morning. oh, and flag the points where i still have to make a call that can’t be eliminated by the steps, especially around personal-identity judgments and changed-values claims.
