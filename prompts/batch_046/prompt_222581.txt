Consider this reconstruction of Hume's position in "Of the Standard of Taste" that seems to drive him into contradiction.

(1) Beauty is not a quality in objects but a sentiment in the mind of the perceiver.
(2) Still, there is a genuine standard of taste: the joint verdict of true judges—those with delicate sentiment, practice, comparison, and freedom from prejudice.
(3) We identify true judges not by prior knowledge of which objects are beautiful, but by observing which subjects agree with one another.
(4) Now imagine a structurally identical "inverted" community: they possess the same perceptual capacities, the same habits of comparison, and the same freedom from prejudice, yet their sentiments are systematically reversed—what we call beautiful they consistently call ugly, with perfect internal coherence.
(5) By the structural criteria in (2), they qualify as true judges.
(6) So Hume must either grant that their inverted judgments are equally correct (making beauty relative to a community) or deny that they are true judges.
(7) To deny (6), he must claim that true judges necessarily feel pleasure in response to specific objects; but then the standard depends on a pre-existing correlation between objects and sentiments, which is exactly the kind of object-dependent fact (1) rules out.
(8) Hence Hume's view either collapses into community-relative relativism or implicitly treats beauty as objective.
(9) Both outcomes violate his explicit commitments.

The conclusion feels unavoidable, yet Hume's essay is not self-refuting in this obvious way. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would prefer to keep (1) and (3), but I want to know whether saving them forces me into (8) or whether there is a hidden slide—perhaps between the structural criteria for being a judge and the content of the sentiments they share, or between intra-group agreement and cross-group correctness.
