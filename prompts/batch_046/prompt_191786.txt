I have recently been appointed to a university committee tasked with reviewing an endowed chair established in 1923 with capital derived from a manufacturing concern that employed coerced labor. The chair remains active, its benefactor's name displayed in university publications, and the original endowment agreement contains no provision for historical review. The committee must decide whether to recommend renaming, issuing an institutional statement of responsibility, or creating a reparative scholarship fund. This practical obligation has led me to revisit Karl Jaspers's well-known postwar framework distinguishing criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, which I had previously treated as a matter of abstract philosophy. I now find that applying these categories to an institutional body that persists across generations but lacks the attributes of a state or a person produces significant conceptual strain.

Below are notes for a short essay I intend to draft. Rather than summarizing Jaspers, I want to argue that institutional continuity creates a form of responsibility that his four categories fail to capture—something I am tentatively calling archival guilt. The core claim is that when an organization's records document its origins in harm, and when current members maintain, cite, and benefit from those records, including the material and symbolic capital they generate, they assume a custodial obligation that is neither criminal, since no individual act is at issue, nor purely political, since the institution is not a sovereign, nor metaphysical in Jaspers's sense, since it does not concern solidarity with all human suffering. Instead, it operates through the institution's narrative identity: to continue using the benefactor's name without annotation is to perform an implicit endorsement that is sustained by the very archives the institution controls.

My outline runs as follows. First, I examine why Jaspers's criminal and political categories require a subject capable of legal or civic standing, which a university endowment does not possess. Second, I consider whether moral guilt—guilt felt by individuals for benefiting—can be aggregated to the institutional level, and I argue that aggregation fails because institutions lack the consciousness required for moral self-reproach. Third, I introduce archival guilt as an obligation tied not to consciousness but to custodianship: the institution holds the documentary evidence, and its continued silence or commemorative practice constitutes a kind of performative contradiction. Fourth, I address an objection that this is merely metaphorical guilt, borrowing the emotional weight of personal culpability to license symbolic gestures like renaming. I respond that archival guilt is better understood as a structural feature of institutional speech-acts—similar to how a corporation's annual report can implicitly reaffirm a founding narrative—rather than as an emotional state. Finally, I ask whether this framework supports the practical conclusion that renaming is obligatory, or whether it only requires transparent historical annotation, leaving the material benefits untouched.

Before I develop this into a full essay, I would value critical comments on the following points. First, am I interpreting Jaspers's distinction between political and moral guilt correctly in treating political guilt as essentially tied to citizenship rather than to membership in any enduring collective? Second, does my proposed archival guilt actually do independent work, or is it reducible to a combination of political responsibility and moral complicity distributed across individual members? Third, I am aware that my personal position on this committee may bias my framing: does the essay's reliance on a specific, recent institutional case weaken its philosophical generality, or does the concreteness help expose gaps that abstract treatments miss? Fourth, I have deliberately avoided asking for bibliographic recommendations or citation lists, since I want the argument to stand on its conceptual coherence; nevertheless, I worry that I may be reinventing distinctions that already exist in the literature on institutional responsibility or transitional justice. Without asking for a reading list, can you identify whether the mapping between custodianship, narrative identity, and institutional obligation sounds like an established position I am rediscovering, or whether it has a genuinely novel structure?

Please be direct about weaknesses in reasoning, particularly where the analogy between state-level collective guilt and institutional-level archival responsibility might break down.
