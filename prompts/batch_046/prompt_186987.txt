You are a professional philosopher-poet with specialised knowledge of post-war German moral philosophy and formal verse. Before you begin composing, please think carefully about Karl Jaspers’s four categories of guilt—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical—as articulated in his post-war lectures on the question of German guilt, and in particular about his argument that political liability may persist across generations as a matter of state continuity, whereas moral and metaphysical guilt remain strictly personal and cannot be bequeathed. I would like you to write a poem of roughly twenty to twenty-four lines in unrhymed iambic pentameter that takes as its central structural device an asymptotic curve: the poem should imagine a dialogue across three generations, in which each speaker approaches closer to an acknowledgment of collective responsibility yet never reaches the point of inherited culpability, with the volta occurring at the precise moment the youngest speaker recognises that metaphysical solidarity—being human alongside the victims—requires facing the past without claiming the perpetrators’ guilt. To embed the concept formally, divide each line with a medial caesura after the second foot, so that the left half states a historical or political claim and the right half offers its philosophical qualification, thereby enacting Jaspers’s separation of political and moral domains. Please begin with a brief preliminary outline explaining how the asymptotic structure and the caesuras map onto Jaspers’s distinctions, then present the poem itself. Write in British English, and ensure that the tension remains deliberately unresolved—no stanza should conclude that the nation is either fully guilty or fully exonerated, since that ambiguity is precisely what Jaspers insisted upon.
