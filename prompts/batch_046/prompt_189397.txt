As a novelist observing the institutions that preserve my craft, I am struck by a quiet asymmetry. Museums and collectors will pay fortunes to acquire an author's original handwritten manuscript, yet they treat a perfect forged manuscript—indistinguishable in ink, paper, and calligraphy—as not merely inauthentic but as something to be destroyed or suppressed, even when every word is identical to an authorized draft. At the same time, the literary marketplace freely sells high-quality facsimiles of first editions and distributes the identical digital text to millions, with no suggestion that these copies lack aesthetic value. What theory of artistic or literary value does this cluster of practices presuppose? Does it commit institutions to the view that causal contact with the author's hand is constitutive of the work's identity, or can it be explained by a distinction between aesthetic value and historical or provenance value? Reconstruct the implicit ontology—whether a novel is a type with many tokens, while a manuscript is a singular artifact—and ask whether the practice is philosophically consistent, or whether it smuggles in a commitment to authenticity that undermines the autonomy of the text itself. What would have to change in archival and market practice if we replaced that commitment with a strictly pluralist or purely aesthetic account?
