In our interdisciplinary philosophy-of-mind reading group, a long-running disagreement about Daniel Dennett's notion of real patterns and the three stances—physical, design, and intentional—has stalled in a way that feels structural rather than empirical. One camp holds that if a predictive pattern is genuinely projectible and counterfactual-supporting, then attributing intentionality to a system is not merely a pragmatic heuristic but the recognition of an objective, stance-independent feature of the world. The opposing camp maintains that the three stances are irreducibly interpretive frameworks applied to the same underlying physical reality: because a single system—say, a large language model—can be correctly and completely described at the physical, algorithmic-design, and intentional levels without contradiction, none of these descriptions tracks mind-independent ontological structure; rather, they reflect our explanatory interests. Both sides accept Dennett's anti-eliminativist claim that intentional patterns are real in the sense of being indispensable for prediction. Both also agree that contemporary neural networks instantiate robust design-level patterns. Yet for over a year neither side has revised its view of whether those patterns license genuine intentional-stance attribution. Is this impasse best understood as a hidden empirical question about whether current architectures actually support counterfactual-rich intentional predictions; as a conceptual disagreement about whether real pattern means the same thing across the physical and intentional stances; as a clash of bedrock intuitions about whether all legitimate explanation must bottom out in physical causation; or as a case where both sides are answering different questions under the single label real patterns?
