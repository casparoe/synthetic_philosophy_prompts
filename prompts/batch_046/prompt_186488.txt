I have noticed a recurring shape in explanations of why societies develop particular beliefs, laws, or artistic forms. The pattern usually begins with someone pointing to how goods are produced and distributed—large-scale industry versus small-scale farming, for instance—and claiming that this economic arrangement explains the society's broader culture. Yet I can often find two societies with very similar economic arrangements that produce quite different legal codes, religious practices, or styles of art. Instead of revising the economic claim, the explainer adds a separate layer: the economic conditions establish a range of possibilities or a general pressure, while local history, geography, or institutional accident determines the specific outcome within that range. I have seen this structure applied to religious reform movements, to the development of family law, and to shifts in artistic style—each time with a new local-factor story, and each time with the economic claim left intact, now functioning more as a background frame than as an active cause of the particular result.

Is there an established concept or literature for this precise explanatory pattern—where a broad economic cause is protected by being turned into a necessary-but-not-sufficient condition, while individually tailored non-economic conditions accumulate to cover every deviation? If such a concept exists, I want to know how it is distinguished from ordinary multicausal analysis and whether it carries recognized criticisms about unfalsifiability or explanatory emptiness. If there is no precise term, please coin one with a clear definition, specify its boundary cases—particularly what separates it from a straightforward interaction between economic and cultural factors—and indicate where the accumulation of local conditions becomes problematic rather than merely descriptive.
