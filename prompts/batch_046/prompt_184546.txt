I'm writing the take-home final for my graduate epistemology seminar, and I'm tired of answers that simply map foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism onto the standard Agrippan trilemma without confronting where each view actually fractures under pressure. I'd like one essay question—answerable in roughly 90 minutes, targeting about 1,200 words—that forces students to work through the regress problem via a deliberately constructed structural edge case rather than through textbook labels.

Specifically, the question should center on what we might call a "justificatory loop with asymmetric transparency": imagine a subject who holds belief B₁, justified only by B₂, which is justified only by B₃, and so on, except that at every even-numbered step the subject is fully aware of the justification relation, while at every odd-numbered step she takes the belief to be basic or self-evident and is entirely unaware that she is relying on a prior belief. There is no actual termination—no genuinely basic belief—just an infinite alternating chain of transparent reliance and opaque self-attribution. The exam prompt should ask students to diagnose exactly what goes wrong epistemically in this structure, and then require them to say precisely how foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism would each handle it, with special attention to whether any position can accommodate the transparency/opacity asymmetry without collapsing into one of the others. They should not be able to answer well merely by naming the positions; they must identify the specific point at which each view succeeds or breaks, particularly regarding whether an opaque node can ever count as justified within that framework.

Please provide: (1) the exam question itself, clearly framed for a 90-minute graduate-level response; (2) a model answer of about 600–800 words that demonstrates the crux moves—especially showing why the asymmetry undermines a naive foundationalist termination, why a coherentist circularity objection misfires if coherence is indexed only to transparent nodes, and why an infinitist acceptance of the infinite chain still faces a problem about opaque nodes never being genuinely justified; and (3) a marking scheme out of 20 points, broken into five must-hit points (with point values totaling 20), each accompanied by a one-line note on how candidates typically miss it—for instance, treating the asymmetry as merely psychological rather than structural, or assuming infinitism automatically resolves the opacity problem. I do not want bibliographies, reading lists, or citation prompts; the answer should stand on conceptual analysis alone.
