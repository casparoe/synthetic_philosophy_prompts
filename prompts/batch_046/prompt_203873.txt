I'm defending a paper at a joint philosophy-of-mind and cognitive-neuroscience workshop next Thursday. The thesis is that in active-inference architectures, Hofstadter's principle of an inviolate level—where a strange loop terminates in a base that does not loop back—fails not accidentally but structurally: the precision-weighting mechanisms that regulate lower-level prediction errors are themselves optimized by the same error-minimization dynamics they control, producing a tangled hierarchy with no genuinely unlooped base. I treat Escher's Drawing Hands not as a metaphor for self-reference but as a literal structural analog, arguing that the drawing function is distributed across cortical levels rather than anchored at a top, which lets the system avoid infinite regress without invoking a skyhook level.

The audience is about fifteen people: predictive-coding researchers who think clean hierarchical separation is what makes the framework computationally tractable; philosophers familiar with Hofstadter's strange loops who regard them as logical puzzles with no empirical brain analog; and systems neuroscientists who are skeptical of any model that collapses sensory and meta-cognitive boundaries. They care about empirical tractability, not philosophical elegance, and they will not ask for citations or reading lists.

Prepare my Q&A sheet. Order the objections by how likely they are and how much damage they would do if I stumble. Phrase each objection the way that specific audience member would actually say it—predictive-coding researchers first, then the Hofstadterians, then the neuroscientists. For each: a two-minute reply, what I should concede up front, what I must hold, and where the reply is soft. Then identify the one question I am hoping nobody asks—likely the deepest weakness in my own position—and draft the reply I should have ready, with an explicit note on why it is the weakest point and what I should not pretend. Make sure the objections target the actual argument rather than a strawman, and include at least one that attacks the Escher analogy directly.
