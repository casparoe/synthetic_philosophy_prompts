Following a prolonged and rather tense discussion last night with a friend in our advocacy division—who insists that our preliminary reports apply the genocide framework far too cautiously—I need to formalize how my team actually makes these classification calls. I serve as a senior documentation analyst for an independent atrocity-monitoring consortium; every few weeks we must run rapid preliminary assessments, typically within forty-eight hours and with incomplete field verification, on whether an emerging pattern of violence and systematic repression should be analytically framed as genocide, and separately whether it should be publicly designated as such in reports that must be signed off by both our legal counsel and our advocacy director. The conceptual tension between us is precise: she works almost exclusively with the narrower intent-based standard of the international Convention adopted in 1948, which centers on physical and biological destruction coupled with specific intent to destroy a protected group, whereas I have been trying to keep alive Lemkin's broader original conception, in which genocide encompasses the coordinated dismantling of a group's political institutions, social structures, cultural practices, economic life, religious organizations, and moral cohesion—not merely mass killing. Rather than debating individual crises ad hoc, I want a reusable decision procedure that preserves this conceptual distinction but makes it operational for analysts without legal or philosophical training.

Please construct an ordered procedural checklist—explicit questions in sequence, with defined stopping conditions, sufficient-answer criteria, and clear decision nodes—that translates this dual framework into practice. The procedure should open with an evidentiary screening for coordinated, group-targeted harm across the multiple dimensions of collective life that Lemkin's framework identifies; then apply a separate intent filter that asks whether available evidence supports an inference that the harm is directed at destroying the group as such; and only then reach an explicit politics-of-naming checkpoint, where the question is not factual but strategic and institutional: given our mandate, risk exposure, partner obligations, and the legal and diplomatic consequences that follow from designation, should the preliminary report invoke the term? At each stage, specify what counts as sufficient evidence to proceed, where the procedure terminates with a negative or indeterminate finding and hands the matter back for further investigation, and where it necessarily defers to human judgment because the issue involves values, institutional risk, or moral urgency rather than determinable facts. The procedure must also contain an explicit misfire analysis and be applied to two constructed cases: first, a state campaign that systematically suppresses a minority language, replaces religious leadership, restricts economic participation, and dismantles social institutions without direct mass killing; and second, a counter-insurgency in which civilian casualties are heavily concentrated in one ethnic community but command-level intent to destroy the group remains genuinely ambiguous. For both, show exactly where the procedure lands, including the case in which it recommends against public designation despite severe group-targeted destruction across multiple life dimensions, and state plainly what the procedure cannot resolve—especially whether moral urgency should ever override institutional risk, and whether a pattern satisfying Lemkin's multi-dimensional standard but failing the Convention's intent filter should be named in preliminary reports at all. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; this is meant for practitioners working without library access, and it should conclude by identifying the precise points at which the procedure will misfire or produce recommendations that conflict with intuitive moral judgment.
