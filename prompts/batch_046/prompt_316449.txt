I'm serving on the heritage board of a small river town whose civic identity is built around a founding story we have taught for generations: that in the 1880s, families recently freed from slavery and members of a local indigenous community jointly designed and dug our shared canal system, and that this cooperation established the water-sharing agreements and town-meeting rituals we still practice. The narrative is in our school curricula, inscribed on our central monument, and recited at our annual harvest gathering.

Over the past year, digitized county probate files and labor contracts—preserved almost entirely by the descendants of a single outside financier—have come to light. They indicate that he alone directed and legally owned the canal project, with the other groups working as paid laborers or tenants. The paper trail is largely silent on joint governance. At the same time, elder families maintain detailed oral accounts of collective decision-making about the canal's routing and gate placements, and architectural historians have noted that the masonry and interlocking gate mechanisms include structural features associated with cooperative building traditions rather than with standard commercial construction of that period. Some board members want the schools to adopt the archival version, the monument to be revised, and the civic rituals reinterpreted. Others argue that the cooperative story is not an error to correct but a living tradition that sustains present-day solidarity, and that privileging documents created by parties with every reason to erase cooperation—especially when material culture and oral testimony point the other way—would do more harm than historical accuracy could repair.

We must submit a concrete recommendation within the month: should the curriculum change, should the monument be altered, and should our current collective land-stewardship practices—which are explicitly justified by reference to that cooperative origin—be reconceived if the origin story is false?

Take a clear, committed position rather than balancing options. If the community's functioning identity and its documentary past genuinely conflict, which should govern what we teach, commemorate, and build policy upon? Be explicit about whether you think the conflict is irreconcilable here, whether there is a principled way to honor both without collapse, and where your reasoning depends on contested assumptions about epistemic authority, group identity, or the moral weight of origins.
