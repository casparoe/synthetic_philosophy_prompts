I sit on the data-ethics and classification review panel for a municipal archive digitizing twentieth-century labor records. We face a concrete recommendation due before next Thursday's meeting: how should we catalog the payroll and membership rolls of a cooperative factory that operated from 1923 to 1961 using its own internal currency—redeemable only within the factory's supply network and functioning as a parallel medium of exchange—and that classified workers through a hybrid scheme combining craft skill, marital status, and a peer-endorsed "credit-trust tier" rather than through modern racial or gender categories?

The philosophical problem is whether these factory-specific kinds—the internal currency and the trust-tier ranking—are socially constructed in the same manner and to the same depth as race, gender, or state-issued money, or whether they represent thinner, more conventionally institutional constructions. Our decision will determine whether the archive applies modern demographic tags retroactively for accessibility and bias auditing, preserves the factory's native taxonomy entirely, or adopts some intermediate protocol.

Please draft the panel's written opinion recommending a specific course of action with an explicit time frame—such as a phased dual-tagging system, a moratorium on retroactive demographic labeling for certain fields, or a pilot comparison study—together with the reasoning that supports it. The opinion must treat the strongest argument for the opposing position seriously: specifically, the claim that imposing modern categories is not anachronistic violence but a necessary corrective, since all social kinds—including the factory's own—are equally constructed and none possesses privileged ontological standing. At the same time, engage the counter-claim that money and gender, though both socially constructed, differ in their modes of entrenchment—money through collective intentionality and institutional backing, gender through embodied practice and structural repetition—such that analogizing the factory's scrip and trust tiers to them risks either over-ontologizing conventional instruments or under-ontologizing deep social kinds. Conclude with the concrete recommendation, the conditions under which the panel should revisit it, and an honest acknowledgment of what evidence would weaken your proposed course.
