The comparative debate over church taxes—Germany’s Kirchensteuer, France’s strict laïcité, England’s established church without direct taxation, and the American voluntary model—has cycled through the same institutional comparisons for decades without convergence. Proponents of the German system argue that state collection is administratively neutral: citizens direct a portion of income tax to recognized bodies, anyone may opt out, and churches hold no special legislative status, so the arrangement is pluralist facilitation rather than endorsement. Critics reply that requiring citizens to declare religious affiliation (or its absence) to tax authorities, granting churches a public-law corporate status unavailable to secular associations, and locking in historical denominational shares through administrative inertia amounts to a fiscal establishment that violates substantive neutrality, regardless of formal opt-out rights. Defenders of England’s model sometimes claim that symbolic establishment avoids these pathologies by separating ecclesiastical status from fiscal machinery, yet they rarely address whether symbolic privilege is less coercive or merely less visible. Meanwhile, the American case is usually treated as a baseline of separation, though its reliance on tax exemptions rather than direct collection raises parallel questions about indirect subsidy. My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is largely conceptual: one side treats secularism as procedural neutrality among religions, the other as substantive neutrality between religious and non-religious life, and neither side can adjudicate this without begging the question about whether religious membership is a private preference or a public identity. But I also suspect a hidden formal disagreement is doing work—specifically, whether a state can remain neutral while performing a revenue-collection function that only religious bodies can legally receive, which turns on contested definitions of religious association. I want a diagnosis rather than a survey: are the sides genuinely answering different questions under one name; is the dispute verbal, empirical, or rooted in unarguable intuitions about equality; and is there any distinction—say, between fiscal establishment and administrative delegation—or any empirical finding about opt-out behavior and denominational mobility that could actually resolve it? Or is the impasse permanent because the underlying commitments about religion’s place in public life are incommensurable?
