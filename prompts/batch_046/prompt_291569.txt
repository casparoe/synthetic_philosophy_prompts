I recently encountered a summary of an argument—presented, I believe, in an essay on practical rationality—defending the claim that honoring sunk costs is not invariably irrational. The author frames it as follows: "The standard economic critique of the sunk-cost fallacy presupposes time-slice rationality: at each decision point, a rational agent maximizes expected utility going forward, treating past expenditures as causally inert. But human agency is diachronically extended. Once resources have been invested in a project, they become embedded in the narrative structure of one's practical identity. Abandoning the project does not merely forfeit future benefits; it severs the coherence between one's present and past commitments, rendering the prior self's actions unintelligible. Consequently, bearing further costs to continue the project can be rational—not as a means to recover losses, but as a constitutive act of maintaining the unity of agency over time. The fallacy literature therefore errs by treating all retrospective costs as instrumentally irrelevant, when some carry constitutive weight in sustaining diachronic personhood."

I am inclined to think this reasoning overreaches, but I want a precise audit rather than a blanket dismissal. Could you evaluate the fairness of this summary? Specifically: which claims—the characterization of economic rationality as strictly time-slice neutral, the idea that past investments acquire narrative-embedding, the distinction between instrumental and constitutive costs—are accurately reflective of positions in the philosophical literature on diachronic rationality and practical identity, and which are subtle conflations or overstatements? Does the conclusion that continuing a project can be rationally required actually follow from premises about narrative coherence, or does it depend on an unstated and questionable premise about the value of diachronic unity? Finally, is there a more charitable reconstruction of the underlying intuition—perhaps distinguishing path-dependent commitment from mere loss aversion—that avoids the misrepresentations present in this account?
