I've been thinking about sphexishness—the mechanical rigidity observed in Sphex wasps and used in rationalist circles as a model for unthinking behavior—in relation to free will. I've come to hold a somewhat specific view: that sphexishness is not best characterized as behavioral determinism or lack of flexibility, but as a particular kind of counterfactual insulation, where an agent's decision procedure is structured so that local perturbations to its inputs (including its own reflective states) cannot propagate to alter its outputs, not because the procedure is deterministic, but because its architecture actively suppresses such propagation. On this view, free will is not the opposite of determinism, but the presence of a decision architecture with low counterfactual insulation—one where perturbations can cascade through evaluation layers. I want to know what would actually count against this framing. Which empirical results about animal cognition or human decision-making would undermine the claim that counterfactual insulation is the right axis? Are there formal results in decision theory or computational psychology that show flexibility and insulation are not separable in the way I assume? What about the possibility that human reflective reasoning is itself sphexish at a higher order—would that be fatal, or could my view absorb it by distinguishing orders of insulation? I'd like the defeaters ranked: which would force abandonment of the counterfactual-insulation framework, which would merely require qualification, and which are things I should already concede as awkward but not damaging. In particular, I'm interested in whether the view risks collapsing into a trivial claim about complexity, and if so, whether that collapse is a genuine vulnerability or just a feature.
