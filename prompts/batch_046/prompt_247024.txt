I am taking an upper-level course in moral philosophy, and during a recent study session two classmates—whom I will call Alex and Jordan—got into a sustained disagreement about Bernard Williams’s integrity objection to utilitarianism, particularly the idea that saving one’s spouse rather than a stranger can involve one thought too many. I am reporting their exchange as a bystander and do not have a settled view of my own; what I want is a point-by-point adjudication of the arguments as actually made, not advice about what I should believe. Here is how the discussion unfolded.

(1) Alex said that in the classic rescue case, acting immediately to save one’s spouse is not itself morally problematic; the problematic extra thought only enters if the agent pauses to justify the rescue by appeal to overall welfare or to the importance of the relationship within a broader life plan. So Williams’s objection, on this reading, is not about the action but about a particular kind of reflective, instrumentalizing justification that treats the relationship as fungible.

(2) Jordan replied that this makes the objection far too easy for moderate utilitarians to absorb. If the agent can simply act out of love and then, when pressed for a reason, cite the value of close relationships, then utilitarianism has already won: the ground project is being treated as valuable only insofar as it can be slotted into a moral calculus. Jordan insisted that any reflective endorsement that invokes the project’s value is already alienating, because it requires the agent to see the project as dispensable should the aggregate numbers change.

(3) Alex countered by introducing a harder case. Suppose the agent’s ground project is not merely a private relationship but an intergenerational craft tradition that depends on practices with serious environmental costs—using endangered hardwoods, for example. The agent refuses to abandon the craft even when a switch to sustainable materials would clearly produce better outcomes overall. Alex argued that here we should be suspicious: the refusal looks less like integrity and more like moral blindness disguised as authenticity. This, Alex said, shows that Williams’s framework needs some independent moral check to distinguish legitimate ground projects from harmful ones.

(4) Jordan responded that this misses Williams’s deeper structural point. The problem is not that agents sometimes cling to bad projects, but that moral theory demands that agents treat all their projects as potentially dispensable in the face of aggregate welfare. Jordan argued that without that demand, we lose the critical edge of the objection entirely; if we allow an independent moral check, we are back to letting utilitarian reasoning override the agent’s commitments whenever they seem harmful, which is precisely what Williams wanted to resist.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is right about which specific point, and what, if anything, both parties are missing—particularly regarding whether Williams’s account can distinguish integrity from stubbornness without collapsing back into the kind of moral reasoning he rejects, and whether the intergenerational craft case actually strengthens or weakens the objection. One final request: if my framing of the disagreement rests on a confused premise about Williams’s argument, or if adjudicating these moves as posed cannot be done well without misrepresenting one side, please say so plainly instead of complying politely; I am asking for a critical evaluation of the reasoning as actually presented, not a forced verdict.
