I have been teaching a graduate seminar on biography as a vehicle for moral learning, and I keep noticing a recurring structural pattern in hagiographic and biographical narratives that I do not have a precise term for. Take Plutarch’s treatment of Alexander: he does not present Alexander’s insatiable ambition as a regrettable accident separable from his magnanimity, nor as a vice that could have been excised without loss, but as the very same disposition that produced his strategic vision and generosity, merely viewed under a different aspect. The reader is invited to admire the virtue and fear the vice at once, without being given any practical principle for separating them. I see the same configuration in modern hagiography, where a reformer’s destructive arrogance is narrated as the unavoidable reverse face of his moral courage, or where a saint’s relentless self-neglect is shown as inseparable from her charity. The pedagogical force depends precisely on this inseparability: if the vice were merely contingent, the biography would collapse into a simple warning, and if it were fully excused, it would collapse into pure celebration. Instead, the narrative holds admiration and warning in an unresolved tension that is itself meant to instruct.

What I am trying to isolate is the specific rhetorical-epistemic structure in which a biographer presents a subject’s gravest moral failure not as an exception to an otherwise virtuous character, but as the necessary shadow or structural correlate of the very virtue being praised, so that the reader must learn from both without being able to extract a clean rule—cultivate the virtue, but avoid the vice—because the text insists they share a common root. Is there an established concept for this exact configuration in the literature on moral exemplars, narrative ethics, or the philosophy of biography? I am familiar with the general terrain—exemplarist theory, the problem of flawed heroes, the Aristotelian idea that virtues become vices through excess, and the hagiographic tendency to subordinate flaws to an overarching holiness—but I want to know whether this particular pattern, where moral learning requires holding admiration and warning in a tension that the narrative refuses to resolve, has been named and analyzed with clear boundaries. If there is no precise term, I want you to coin one, together with a definition that distinguishes it from mere moral ambiguity, from the doctrine of the mean taken to extremes, and from the biographical move of excusing vice by appealing to the subject’s ultimate sanctity or greatness.

Please take a definite, committed position rather than surveying options: either identify the nearest established concept and explain exactly where the fit breaks down, or propose a new term and defend its boundaries against the neighboring distinctions I have mentioned. I am not asking for a bibliography or a literature review; I want a judgment about what this pattern is, what it is not, and why the distinction matters for how we read lives as moral texts.
