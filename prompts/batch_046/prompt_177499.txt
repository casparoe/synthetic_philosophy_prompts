You are a professional philosopher working at the intersection of aesthetics and moral philosophy. I want you to read the 1984 film Amadeus through the philosophical problem of what obligations, if any, we have to real dead people when we dramatize their lives—specifically, whether fictionalizing a deceased person's actions can wrong them in ways that do not depend on causing them psychological harm. To avoid a generic historical-accuracy debate, begin by briefly brainstorming two competing frameworks: (1) a posthumous-autonomy view, on which the dead retain an interest in the integrity of their life-narrative, and (2) a purely instrumental view, on which obligations run only to living audiences and survivors, so that dramatization is permissible provided it does not defame the living or spread harmful falsehoods about them. Next, apply these frameworks to Amadeus's central invention: the framing narrative in which an aged Salieri confesses to poisoning Mozart, despite the absence of historical evidence for either the rivalry or the deathbed accusation. Analyze specific narrative choices—Salieri's asylum confession, the juxtaposition of his mediocrity with Mozart's genius, and the film's use of Mozart's actual music as ironic commentary—asking whether they violate posthumous autonomy, commit a form of hermeneutical injustice by rendering Salieri's real achievements unintelligible to viewers, or instead serve an artistic purpose that outweighs any wrong to the dead. Be precise about where the philosophical idea illuminates a scene and where the film resists being reduced to an illustration. Conclude by identifying what this particular dramatization reveals about the ethics of biography that a purely conceptual argument misses—particularly regarding how visual and musical form can make a false narrative feel emotionally true. Think step by step, and do not cite specific scholarly sources; work from the philosophical concepts themselves.
