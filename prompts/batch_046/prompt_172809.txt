Two colleagues in my study group argued about whether the agricultural revolution was a mistake. (1) The first said yes: skeletal records show stature declined and dental pathologies increased after the transition, labor intensified, and burial goods reveal new hierarchy. He invoked Marshall Sahlins's idea of original affluence — satisfying wants with minimal labor — to describe what was lost, and added that storage technologies and prestige economies actively pulled societies into farming; it was not purely passive population pressure. (2) The second said this applies modern welfare metrics anachronistically. The right counterfactual is not "stayed forager" but "foraging at higher density," which was ecologically unstable. She also argued that affluence is want-relative: agricultural societies developed ritual monumentality, craft specialization, and larger networks — goods foraging could not supply — so measuring well-being by leisure assumes the wrong scale. (3) The first replied that this rationalizes ex post: the first generations experienced the health costs directly; they did not choose monumentality over ease. Calling it not a mistake because later societies built temples is like praising a harmful policy because distant descendants eventually prospered. (4) The second countered that "mistake" requires a decision node with viable alternatives agents could understand. If the transition was structurally overdetermined — density and climate shifts creating a basin of attraction — there was no choice to err in, only path-dependent lock-in. Without identifying a principled choice point, she said, the "mistake" framing collapses into "history I dislike."

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Also say what, if anything, both parties are missing about how to evaluate a structural transition like this.
