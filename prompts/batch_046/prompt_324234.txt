I've been trying to understand a long-running disagreement between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso about punishment and whether anyone truly deserves it. Caruso argues that since we lack the kind of free will that would make us ultimate authors of our actions, retributive punishment—making people suffer because they have done wrong—is unjustified; instead he proposes treating dangerous individuals like carriers of disease, isolating them for public safety but never assigning blame, in a framework he describes as a public-health quarantine model. Dennett responds that even in a deterministic universe people possess the practical, evolved capacities for choice and self-control that ground real moral responsibility, and that discarding the notion of just deserts would undermine the cooperative social practices that depend on holding one another accountable; he charges Caruso with letting a scientific picture of causation erase the normative reality of human agency. For years they have repeated the same moves—Caruso says Dennett is hiding metaphysical assumptions about guilt, Dennett says Caruso is ignoring the evolutionary and functional role of responsibility—without either conceding ground. I think part of the stalemate might be that they use just deserts to mean different things: one side seems to picture a cosmic moral ledger in which suffering cancels out wrongdoing, while the other treats it as a stable social convention for distributing burdens fairly. But I am not sure whether that is the real blockage, or whether they are actually addressing two separate questions—one about the metaphysics of blame, the other about institutional design—under a single heading. Is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or a case of parallel monologues? Diagnose it by referring to what each thinker actually says, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a sharper conceptual distinction, an empirical finding, a formal model, or an argument that the question itself is ill-posed.
