I'm preparing the closing session for our high school philosophy seminar on just war theory, and I want to end with an original villanelle that takes up G.E.M. Anscombe's argument in Mr. Truman's Degree that the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki constituted murder according to the doctrine of double effect. The poem should use two refrains—one articulating the intended end of hastening surrender and saving lives, the other articulating the forbidden means of deliberately killing innocent civilians as part of the adopted plan. These refrains should take turns through the stanzas and finally stand side by side in the closing couplet, leaving the conflict open rather than resolved.

To avoid a mere name-drop of double effect, please let the poem's structure reflect a less-discussed dimension of Anscombe's thought: her claim in works on the philosophy of action that whether an event counts as an intentional killing depends not on a hidden mental state but on the description under which the agent performs it. Let the volta occur at the point where the poem shifts from describing the physical event—the aircraft, the fall, the blast—to describing the action as intended—the killing adopted as a means. A change in meter or in the rhyme's register at that turn should make the qualitative distinction felt, so that the same bodily movement is shown to be, under one description, a military strike and, under another, murder. The tone should remain contemplative rather than polemical, accurate enough that a reader familiar with Anscombe's ethics and her philosophy of intention would recognize the point, and poetic enough to work as verse for an audience that knows the history but not necessarily the philosophical detail.
