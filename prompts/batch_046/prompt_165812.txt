I am preparing an interview for a philosophy-of-science podcast with a historian who defends a specific, non-standard reading of Darwin's moral and religious thought, which she labels the functional-dualism interpretation. According to this view, Darwin's account of moral sentiment in his 1871 study of human origins employs a two-level explanatory structure drawn from his botanical work: immediate social-physiological instincts function at one causal level, while long-range group-adaptive outcomes function at another. The defender claims that Darwin never resolves whether this framework genuinely eliminates teleological design-language or merely relocates it into functional biology, and that this ambiguity shapes Darwin's religious position—not as passive agnosticism, but as an active stance she calls functional naturalism, in which the existence of an evolved moral sense undermines the argument from benevolent design without offering any theistic consolation, particularly in light of the problem of suffering evident in nature.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that press well beyond a restatement of this thesis. I want at least one question that examines the botanical-parallel claim by asking precisely how Darwin's analysis of plant irritability and adaptive movement is supposed to map onto human conscience without collapsing into anthropomorphism, together with a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that the parallel is structural rather than analogical. I want at least one question that targets the design-language relocation problem by asking which specific feature of group-level selection could perform the explanatory work previously assigned to a designer, paired with a follow-up for her probable answer that selection is blind and therefore non-teleological. I want at least one question that probes the interaction between this moral framework and Darwin's religious stance—specifically, whether granting moral instincts this dual-level causal efficacy intensifies the problem of suffering or dissolves it, with a follow-up anticipating her claim that suffering is only a problem for theism, not for naturalism. The remaining questions should anticipate objections from historians of Victorian science and philosophers of biology: for example, whether this reading overstates Darwin's commitment to group selection relative to individual-level mechanisms, whether functional naturalism is substantively different from the agnosticism Darwin explicitly avowed, and whether the interpretation risks projecting contemporary evolutionary psychology onto nineteenth-century texts. Order the questions so that the botanical and design-language questions come early, the religious-interaction question comes in the middle, and the historiographical objections come later. Do not include generic biographical questions, and do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
