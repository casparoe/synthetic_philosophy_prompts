In a graduate seminar on modal epistemology, I have been developing a qualified conceivability-possibility principle that tries to split the difference between rationalist and empiricist intuitions. The core claim is this: if a scenario is genuinely conceivable—meaning one can form a coherent, contradiction-free mental representation of it—then it is metaphysically possible, but only under the condition that the scenario is what I call structurally transparent. By structural transparency I mean that every feature of the conceived situation is specified in terms of its relational, causal, or functional role, leaving no phenomenal residue—that is, no property that is accessible only from a first-person perspective and that can vary without altering the underlying structural description. On this view, standard zombie-style arguments seem convincing precisely because they appear structurally transparent (the physical structure is held constant while consciousness is absent), whereas inverted-spectrum or disembodied-consciousness scenarios are unreliable guides to possibility because they trade on phenomenal residue that the structural specification fails to fix. In particular, are there structurally transparent scenarios that are nevertheless metaphysically impossible—perhaps due to hidden necessary connections between consciousness and physical structure, or to mathematical constraints on physical possibility that outstrip relational specification? Conversely, are there cases involving phenomenal residue where conceivability nevertheless tracks possibility, undermining the restriction? Please distinguish evidence that would be decisive—say, a demonstration that structural descriptions necessarily underdetermine phenomenal properties in a way that makes the transparency condition collapse or become arbitrary—from evidence that would merely be awkward but absorbable by refining the notion of transparency. Say also what a defender of the view would themselves concede as genuinely counting against it, not only what critics typically allege, and assess whether the position risks collapsing into a more familiar rival, such as a pure rationalist modal epistemology on one side or a physicalist denial of phenomenal residue on the other.
