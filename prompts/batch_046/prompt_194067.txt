I was sitting with my study group when two friends got stuck in an argument about Bernard Williams's integrity objection and that one-thought-too-many idea. I mostly listened, but now I want an outside take since neither of them would budge.

Friend A started with the classic drowning case: a husband saves his wife instead of two strangers. Williams says thats okay, she argued, but only if he doesnt run the numbers first. If he thinks well its one life versus two but shes my wife so Ill save her anyway, thats one thought too many — hes let impartial morality into the room and then tried to bribe it with loyalty. Friend B pushed back and said that sounds like psychological fiction. Any conscious agent notices theres a choice, and noticing isnt weighing. The husband can see the strangers and still reach for his wife without doing utilitarian math. So Friend B said Williams is attacking a strawman reasoning process nobody actually uses.

Then Friend B introduced a twist she made up. Theres a climate researcher whose ground project is pure scientific transparency — she lives for honest data. She discovers her partner engineered minor fraud in a study they co-authored, and exposing it destroys both their marriage and her reputation for collaboration, killing her career. If she stays silent to protect the relationship and her work, shes acting on ground projects. But if she thinks exposing fraud saves science but ruins my life so I wont, thats weighing, right? Friend A said exactly — which proves integrity just collapses into follow your ground projects, and the warning is useless because we cant tell from the outside whether shes weighing or just being loyal. Friend B said wait, no: transparency IS her ground project, so suppressing fraud violates it regardless. The only way silence is okay is if loyalty to partner is a genuinely conflicting ground project. Friend A then said okay, but what if she resolves that conflict by thinking the harm of exposure is greater than the harm of this one fraud? Is that weighing? Friend B said yes, and thats precisely where Williams should step in. Friend A said no, thats just practical reasoning within her projects, not impartial morality.

So who is right about what? Is Friend B correct that Williams only works if we can cleanly separate ground-project reasoning from weighing, and that hard cases make the distinction collapse? Or is Friend A right that noticing alternatives isnt weighing, and that the researcher case actually supports Williams? Also, what are they both missing about how integrity works when ground projects conflict?

Please be honest with me — if this framing is confused, or if Im asking you to adjudicate something that cant be done well because it misrepresents Williams's actual view, say that plainly instead of forcing a neat verdict. I genuinely want pushback if the premise is off.
