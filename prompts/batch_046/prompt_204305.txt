I sit on the university's Deep Storage Ethics Board — we decide whether to keep funding defunct research archives. There's a proposal to decommission the Silent Vault, an encrypted server holding raw hydrophone data from the 2014 Marianas trench survey. The decryption keys were lost in a lab fire, so literally no human or machine has ever rendered these files into perceptible form. It costs about $180k a year to keep the drives spinning.

One board member (a philosphy of science guy) filed a brief arguing that these files are just unperceived ideas, and per Berkeley's master argument they don't genuinely exist — trying to conceive of them "out there" in the server still involves perceiving the idea in your own mind. He calls them "latent perceptibles" and says funding their storage is like paying rent on ghost furniture. He wants us to approve immediate shutdown and reallocate the budget.

The other half say this is a category error: physical bits on a drive aren't mental ideas, and federal environmental-data retention rules apply regardless of phenomenological access. They want a flat rejection.

Draft the board's decision memo — approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions — with a clear bottom line the researchers and funding office could actually respond to. Take the Berkeleyan objection seriously in the reasoning, include an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, and don't bother with citations, bibliographies, or reading lists. Just the argument and the recommendation. Make it sound like a real institutional document, not a chatbot essay — a typo or two is definatly okay.
