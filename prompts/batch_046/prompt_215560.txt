You are a professional philosopher specializing in moral psychology, collective action, and the logic of practical reason. Before drafting the proposal, take a moment to think carefully and brainstorm: what exactly is the boundary of the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, where does its force come from—psychological motivation, structural fit between reason and action-type, or both—and where might a genuinely collective-level analog hide that is not just an aggregation of individual cases?

Write a short research proposal (approximately two pages, suitable for a one-semester dissertation fellowship or workshop pitch) on the following question: can the distinction between right-kind and wrong-kind reasons, standardly applied to individual moral motivation, be extended coherently to collective or institutional agents? Specifically, can an institution act for the wrong kind of reason—say, self-preservation, electoral legitimacy, or organizational survival—even when every individual constituent within it acts for the right kind—say, justice, welfare, or respect for rights—and does recognizing such a case reveal a structural gap in existing accounts rather than merely a sum of individual failures?

State the question so that it has a determinate answer: either there is a coherent extension with distinctive conditions and explanatory power, or the extension collapses into individual-level aggregation and teaches us nothing new about institutions. Explain why the standard literature—focusing on individual agents and psychological motivation—leaves this open: it either assumes methodological individualism about reasons, treats collective action as reducible to shared intentions without examining whether the reason-structure of the collective can diverge from that of its members, or conflates the wrongness of a collective outcome with the wrongness of the reasons that produced it.

Set out the approach step by step. First, reconstruct the core logical structure of the right/wrong reason distinction in a way that does not depend essentially on first-person psychological states, isolating whether it requires a specific relation between the content of the reason and the type of action it supports. Second, apply this reconstructed structure to institutional decision-procedures, using concrete but hypothetical cases: for example, a legislative body that maintains a substantively just policy primarily to avoid public backlash rather than because justice demands it; a corporation that adopts an environmentally sound practice mainly for brand protection; or a court that issues a rights-protecting ruling chiefly to preserve institutional authority. Third, determine whether such cases are best analyzed as aggregated individual wrong reasons, as emergent collective wrong reasons that depend on organizational roles and decision-rules, or as category errors that show the distinction cannot apply above the individual level.

Say what the possible conclusions are and which considerations would decide between them. If the structural reconstruction succeeds and the collective analog holds, then theories of institutional legitimacy and democratic accountability need revision to account for institutional-level reason-structure. If it fails—if every apparent collective case dissolves upon analysis into individual motivations or into a different problem altogether—then we learn that the wrong-kind problem is essentially psychological and individual, and attempts to use it in critiques of institutional hypocrisy are conceptually misplaced.

The project is tractable if the case analysis is limited to two or three institutional types and the conceptual reconstruction avoids building a full general theory of collective intentionality from scratch. Identify precisely what could make the project too big: attempting to resolve the metaphysics of group agency, engaging with empirical organizational psychology or sociology, or trying to cover historical variations in institutional design across many periods.
