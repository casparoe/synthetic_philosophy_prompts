I'm drafting a novel in which a narrator gradually loses autobiographical memory from chapter to chapter, and at a writers' retreat two fellow authors argued over whether this structure genuinely illuminates Buddhist anatta or merely imports Western metaphysics into it. The essayist claimed that anatta denies any enduring substrate, so personal identity is nothing more than a narrative fiction—like my protagonist—meaning there is literally no one who persists from page one to page three hundred; continuity is only causal, comparable to one flame lighting another candle with no shared essence. The poet countered that if there is no conventional self robust enough to own actions, then karma and moral responsibility dissolve entirely, because nothing can serve as the bearer of merit or blame across moments, let alone across lives. The essayist conceded that Buddhism accepts a conventional self, but insisted it carries only pragmatic, not metaphysical, weight—no more a "person" than the word "chariot" denotes something over and above its assembled parts. The poet then pressed further: if causal dependence alone is sufficient to explain karma without personal identity, then personal identity itself must be reducible to that same causal stream, which makes the denial of self redundant; conversely, if identity requires something stronger than mere causation, then karma requires that same stronger thing, which Buddhism explicitly rejects. Neither would yield.

Please adjudicate point by point: who is right about whether the conventional self can sustain responsibility, whether causal continuity actually undermines rather than supports identity, and whether the narrative-fiction analogy holds water? What crucial point are both missing about the relationship between anatta and the distinction between ultimate and conventional truth? And in what specific way does their shared framework—appealing to substance, identity conditions, and bearer-properties—distort the Buddhist position rather than map onto it?
