Restate David Benatar's asymmetry argument—the claim that non-existence involves no harm in the absence of pleasure but a genuine good in the absence of pain, yielding an asymmetry that counts against bringing people into existence—using only the vocabulary of modal decision theory: treat potential persons as indices over accessible possible worlds, assign utility values to world-index pairs, and represent the decision not to create someone as selecting a world in which that index is unoccupied rather than as an act that affects an agent. For each of Benatar's three core moves—the asymmetry of harm and benefit for actual persons, the evaluation of pleasures and pains that never occur, and the normative conclusion about procreative duties—indicate whether it translates without distortion into this framework, whether it can be captured only by distorting a key concept (for instance, by treating an unoccupied index as carrying zero utility or by smuggling in a deontic constraint on world-selection), and whether some element has no expression at all (such as the intuition that failing to confer a benefit is not wrong while conferring a harm is). Conclude by assessing whether the untranslatable residue points to a limitation in modal decision theory's handling of non-actual entities, or whether it reveals that Benatar's argument depends on a pre-formal intuition about absence that dissolves once the vocabulary is made precise.
