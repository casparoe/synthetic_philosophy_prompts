In my epistemology seminar I have been trying to work through the reductionism versus anti-reductionism debate about testimony, and I have constructed the following argument, which now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion—that reductionism makes justified testimonial belief impossible—seems to prove far more than I intended. Here are the steps as I laid them out:

(1) Reductionism holds that testimonial justification is never basic: when a speaker S tells me that p, my reason for believing p must always be reducible to my own non-testimonial grounds for taking S to be reliable about p.

(2) Those non-testimonial grounds are typically background beliefs about S’s competence, sincerity, and domain—beliefs that I rarely form through direct personal observation, and that I usually acquire through further testimony, such as being told that physicians are experts, or reading institutional credentials in directories and manuals that I have not independently verified.

(3) So if every testimonial belief requires an independently justified background belief about reliability, and those background beliefs are themselves testimonial in origin, then justifying any single belief B launches a vicious infinite regress: B requires R₁, R₁ requires R₂, and so on without termination.

(4) Because an infinite regress of justification provides no actual ground, reductionism entails that no testimonial belief can ever be fully justified.

(5) But we plainly do possess justified testimonial beliefs—historical knowledge, scientific consensus, geographical facts we have never observed firsthand—so (4) is false.

(6) Therefore reductionism is false, and anti-reductionism is correct: testimony can be a basic source of justification that does not depend on an independently justified chain of reliability assessments.

What makes me uneasy is that the same regress seems to threaten perception and memory whenever they rely on background calibration, for example trusting that my eyes function properly because an optometrist told me so, or trusting an instrument reading because I followed manual instructions I did not author. So if the argument works, it might undermine not merely reductionism about testimony but any epistemic view that requires independent grounding for every background condition. I would prefer to keep (1), since it captures what reductionists actually claim, but I am willing to concede (2) if that is the weak link. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, is there a way for the reductionist to allow that some reliability beliefs are basic or directly observed, without collapsing into full-blown anti-reductionism about testimony itself?
