I serve on the research-ethics review board of a university-affiliated mental-health research consortium, and we must issue a binding opinion before Thursday’s vote on Protocol 2024-MH-117. The proposal is a two-year school-based study of adolescents that uses a newly developed dimensional instrument—the Adaptive Regulation Profile (ARP)—to assign each participant a continuous “emotional-flexibility score” rather than a categorical diagnosis. Researchers will share the score with the student, match them to tailored cognitive-behavioral modules, and re-administer the ARP quarterly. They argue that dimensional labeling avoids the stigma and looping risks associated with categorical psychiatric kinds, since there is no binary “kind-making” boundary that invites self-reclassification.

A minority of board members objects that dimensional scores are not immune to looping effects—indeed, they may produce a more insidious variant. Because students know their profile and can calibrate emotional expression to match or resist it, the very behavioral markers the ARP measures may shift in response to measurement and feedback. If data are pooled across sites, these shifts could update the algorithm’s normative benchmarks, meaning the study would not merely describe but actively shape the regulatory capacities it tracks, particularly during identity formation. They urge rejection or strict conditions.

Please draft the board’s decision memo. Open with your bottom-line recommendation—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—in a single sentence. Then present reasoning that takes seriously the strongest counter-argument: specifically, the claim that dimensional instruments escape Hacking-style looping because they lack the categorical “kind-making” force that reshapes self-conception. Propose concrete safeguards, a concrete rejection rationale, or a time-bound conditional approval, and address what should happen if the looping concern is borne out during the first year. Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or references to legal precedent alone. Keep the entire memo to one page, approximately 450–550 words.
