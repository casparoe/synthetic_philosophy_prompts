I have recently become the primary decision-maker for my elderly father's care, and this has forced me to examine which considerations actually move me to act versus which ones I merely acknowledge. I want to know whether there is a coherent principle behind the following verdicts about internal and external reasons.

Case A: I rearrange my work schedule to visit him because I genuinely want his company and feel distressed when I miss it. Verdict: this is a fully internal reason.

Case B: I complete a mandatory risk-assessment form for the care home because regulations require it, and I do so only to avoid legal liability. Verdict: this is not my reason; it is an external constraint I comply with.

Case C: I administer his medication at specific times because a digital pillbox sends alerts to my phone; over months, the rhythm has become my own, and I now feel uneasy if I miss the window even when the alert fails. Verdict: ambiguous — it originated in external scaffolding but now operates as an internal reason.

Case D: I decline an expensive experimental treatment because I believe, on moral grounds, that extending his suffering violates a duty not to harm. Verdict: this counts as my reason, though it appeals to a principle that does not depend on my desires.

Case E: I decline the same treatment after computing that the risk-weighted expected utility is negative, using probabilities I read in a clinical study. Verdict: this is a reason, but of a different kind — instrumental rather than moral.

Case F: I stay in a draining job near his care home because this is what a responsible child does — a phrase repeated in my family culture. I do not feel this phrase expresses my own judgment, yet I cannot imagine leaving. Verdict: not a genuine reason, though it motivates me.

Case G: I stay in the same job because I have constructed a first-person narrative in which I am someone who does not abandon commitments when they become costly. Verdict: this is my reason, though its content concerns my self-conception rather than my immediate desires.

The tension: Cases C and F both involve externally originated structures that now govern behaviour, yet I judge C as eventually internal and F as still external. Meanwhile, Cases D and E both justify the same action but feel categorically different. Is there one principle that distinguishes internal from external reasons and delivers all seven verdicts? If not, which verdict should be overturned? Please also state what your principle predicts for the following unlisted case: I begin exercising not because I enjoy it or value health, but because my wearable device gamifies steps and I have come to experience the missed target as a personal failure, even though I never endorsed the goal.
