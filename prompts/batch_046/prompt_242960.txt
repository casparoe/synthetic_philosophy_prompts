I'm in a course on classical ethics and we are working through Peabody's 1884 translation of Cicero's De Senectute. I am struggling to parse Cato's argument in Section II: he says nature is the surest guide, that she could not have slighted the last act of life like an unskilled poet, and that old age should be borne patiently just as berries and fruits, once fully ripe, are ready to wither and fall.

My specific confusion is whether Cato is advancing a genuinely teleological claim—that old age is intrinsically good as the natural completion of a life—or whether he is deploying Stoic resources, perhaps treating bodily health and long life as preferred indifferents, to argue only that rational acceptance is virtuous regardless of the biological process. Furthermore, when Cato insists that complaints about old age belong to deficient character rather than to age itself, is he making a conceptual point about misattribution, or is he implicitly relying on adaptive preference formation, where elderly people adjust their desires downward to match declining capacities? I do not want modern gerontological citations; I want to see whether the argument holds together on Cicero's own terms, and whether translating it into the vocabulary of preference adaptation reveals a gap in his account or in ours.
