Consider the view that the legal category of genocide—rooted in Lemkin's original formulation but narrowed by the 1948 Convention's requirement of specific intent—is structurally incapable of recognizing large-scale group destruction that operates through distributed, systemic mechanisms such as forced cultural assimilation, ecological dispossession, or economic strangulation. Because proving "intent to destroy" demands a prosecutorial reconstruction of discrete mental states, bureaucratic or structural violence rarely qualifies, and attempts to expand the category to include "structural" or "slow" genocide risk collapsing the distinction between genocide and crimes against humanity, thereby sacrificing the moral specificity that makes the label politically powerful. What would have to be true for this position to fail? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: conceptual arguments about whether intent can be genuinely distributed across institutions; empirical findings on how international tribunals actually infer specific intent; normative arguments about whether moral urgency requires a legally distinct category; and observations about whether the position has insulated itself by defining any successful prosecution as proof that hidden intent was present all along. For each, say whether a committed defender of the structural-incapacity thesis would concede it as damaging or dismiss it as confusing legal form with moral substance, and give an honest assessment of whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a concept with such heavy political weight.
