I am a doctoral student in political philosophy and ethics serving on my university's Civic Memory Oversight Committee. During our preliminary session last week—where the discussion kept circling back to whether remembrance is always a virtue or can become a form of institutional cruelty—we received a formal proposal to accession a donated archive of approximately 400 hours of unedited audio testimony and personal correspondence from mid-level administrative officials who served during the transition away from the authoritarian period now referred to in the records as the Consolidation Era. The materials were donated by the officials' adult children, who have submitted a joint request for what they term "structured forgetting": complete destruction of identifying metadata, permanent restriction of all access to aggregated statistical summaries only, and an absolute prohibition on direct quotation in any future publication. They argue, drawing on a Rieffian line of critique, that perpetual institutional preservation of these records has become part of a therapeutic culture that prevents genuine mourning, trapping descendants in an inherited guilt that can never be discharged; without such forgetting, they claim, the archive functions not as historical accountability but as an intergenerational mechanism of trauma transmission. Opposing them, representatives from the university's human rights documentation center and several faculty historians invoke Margalit's framework of ethical remembering, insisting that bureaucratic complicity is precisely the kind of wrong that must be preserved in concrete particularity rather than dissolved into anonymized abstraction, and that a society's duty to remember operates through the acknowledgment of specific human actions rather than through collective symbolic ritual alone.

The committee must circulate a written opinion before the vote scheduled for the 18th of next month, recommending either full open accession, accession with a concrete tiered-access and anonymization protocol bound to a specific time frame, or refusal to accept the donation, together with explicit conditions for future review and a clear implementation schedule. Please draft the memo I will circulate. It should take a definite position—approve, reject, or approve with binding conditions—not an ambiguous compromise, and it must include an honest, substantive treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than a dismissive summary. Lead with the bottom-line recommendation in the very first sentence, then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the committee's charge. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation suggestions, or references to specific texts that members would need to verify externally, since the committee has no access to outside databases.
