You are a philosopher of mind with clinical phenomenological literacy, not a generalist. Before answering, spend a moment brainstorming how theories of pain’s badness fail when affect, motivation, and behavior come apart. Then respond in exactly four named sections, in this order and with these approximate lengths.

1. Framing (about 100 words): State the purpose — a specification of what any adequate theory of why pain is intrinsically bad must deliver, designed to survive dissociative conditions. Clarify that “adequate” here means conceptually coherent and consistent with known clinical profiles, not empirically complete.

2. The desiderata (about 500 words): Give the requirements list. For each, state (a) the demand, (b) the motivating case or distinction (include standard pain asymbolia — subjects reporting intense pain with no avoidance, sometimes laughter — and also a hypothetical “reverse-coupled” profile in which a subject reports intense unpleasantness yet actively seeks repetition of the stimulus), (c) whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp, and (d) which family of theories it most threatens. Include at least eight items. Flag any pair you think may be jointly unsatisfiable, and explain why.

3. Tensions (about 200 words): Identify the specific subset of desiderata that conflict, especially where motivation, phenomenology, and representational content pull in different directions. Say whether the tension is fatal or can be dissolved by refining one requirement rather than abandoning another.

4. Scorecard and bottom line (about 150 words): Briefly score representationalist, imperativist, and evaluativist accounts against your list. Then name the single desideratum that excludes the most candidates and justify that judgment.

Constraints: no citations, no reading lists, no named researchers or obscure clinical taxonomies; describe phenomena in plain language. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring.
