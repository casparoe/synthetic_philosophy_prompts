In a philosophy of science seminar, a colleague kept invoking Fermi’s old lunchtime question—usually rendered as “Where is everybody?”—to shut down discussion of resolutions that depend on civilizations being hidden, silent, or self-destructive. The move seemed to pack several steps into one rhetorical gesture: if technologically mature species had arisen anywhere in our observable past, we should already see some trace—radio leakage, stellar engineering, or self-replicating probes—so the fact that we do not see them just is the fact that they are not there. I want to understand exactly what logical and empirical machinery is compressed into that sentence.

Can you disambiguate “Where is everybody?” into its distinct precise formulations? For each reading, specify: (i) the scope of “everybody”—biological life, communicative civilizations, or entities capable of large-scale astro-engineering; (ii) the spatial and temporal boundaries of “here”—the Milky Way, our past light cone, or the region accessible to current surveys; (iii) the inference pattern—whether it expresses probabilistic surprise given a Drake-style prior, an ontological identification of observational absence with non-existence, or an epistemic principle that one should not posit entities that are in principle undetectable from our vantage. Indicate which formulations are tautologies, which depend on contingent assumptions about technological trajectories or detection thresholds, and which smuggle in a particular philosophy of science about absence of evidence. Then tell me: if the slogan is being used specifically to dismiss the zoo hypothesis and self-destruction filters, which of these precisifications actually do that work, and which ones leave those hypotheses entirely untouched?
