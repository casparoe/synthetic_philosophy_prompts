I've been working through David Lewis's modal realism for a dissertation chapter on cross-world identity, and after a long discussion with my reading group I find myself caught in what seems like an internal tension—though I strongly suspect I'm importing an assumption that Lewis himself would reject outright. The standard presentation is clear enough: since objects are world-bound, when we say that I could have been a musician, we are not claiming that I literally exist in another world with different properties; rather, one of my counterparts in a nearby possible world is the musician, and that counterpart resembles me in the relevant respects. But here is the confusion. If counterpart relations are genuinely determined by qualitative resemblance—perhaps weighted by context—then for any given world W there might well be several individuals that resemble me equally well: one who is a musician, one who is a philosopher, perhaps others with different origins. Without some further metaphysical constraint, there seems to be no unique fact of the matter about which of them counts as "my" counterpart. Yet Lewis writes as though counterpart relations are determinate: he speaks of "my" counterparts, not merely of individuals that happen to be similar to me. This determinacy appears to require something like a primitive cross-world tracking mechanism, or perhaps a hidden essentialist commitment to my origin or haecceity, that selects the right representatives across worlds. If that is correct, then hasn't Lewis smuggled transworld identity back in—not as direct identity of the object, but as identity of the relational role that picks out the representatives? Or is there a defense of determinacy—say, via contextual salience or weighted similarity—that avoids any such hidden linkage? I am particularly interested in whether this problem reveals that counterpart theory is ultimately a form of "disguised transworld identity," and what that implication would mean for the claim that modal properties are reducible to properties of this-worldly objects and their world-bound counterparts. Could you help me see where the false step is in this line of reasoning, and whether the determinacy of counterpart relations can be secured without appealing to the very transworld commitments the theory was designed to eliminate?
