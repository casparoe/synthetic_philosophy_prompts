I serve on the ethics and compliance board of an international health-access consortium that funds vaccine and basic-care distribution in regions where formal state infrastructure is weak and social coordination runs through long-standing reciprocal networks. Our local partners allocate scarce doses partly through kinship and patron-client obligations, and community coordinators routinely accept small ritual gifts—livestock, labor, or ceremonial goods—that our Western auditors classify as bribery and clientelism violating universal anti-corruption standards. The auditors recommend immediate grant suspension, arguing that any departure from impersonal, first-come-first-served procedural neutrality corrupts program integrity. Our field staff reply that excising these relational obligations would collapse local trust, deny access entirely, and that labeling reciprocal gift-giving as corruption applies an ethnocentric norm that mistakes Western bureaucratic form for moral substance.

The board must issue a formal decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—addressed to the directorate, with a clear bottom-line recommendation up front, followed by institutional reasoning and an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. I want you to take a committed, defensible position rather than listing options neutrally: specifically, argue that corruption in this context should be defined functionally—by whether a practice systematically diverts resources away from the program’s stated equitable-access goals and erodes public accountability—rather than by deviation from Western procedural neutrality alone. On that basis, recommend that funding continue only under a restructured transparency protocol that documents relational allocations without banning the reciprocal obligations that make local delivery possible. Defend this functional, context-sensitive framework, apply it directly to the case, and then confront the weightiest objection—namely, that any contextual relaxation risks sliding into unaccountable patronage and undermines the universal moral claim that public goods must be distributed impersonally. Write in a formal institutional voice, with explicit reasoning for why your chosen view should prevail despite that objection. I am asking for a committed judgment: do not present all three outcomes as equally viable, but defend the one you choose and explain why the opposing universalist view, though serious, should not govern this decision.
