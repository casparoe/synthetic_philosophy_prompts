I have been thinking about the tragedy of the commons and public goods, but in a way that does not quite match the usual examples about overfished waters or overgrazed pastures. I have been calling it a "maintenance commons": a shared resource that is not destroyed by taking from it, but by neglect, because keeping it alive requires continuous, unglamorous labor that benefits everyone equally whether they contributed or not. A neighborhood watch or a shared pump might fit, but I am more interested in how it plays out in small groups where the labor is invisible and the reward is distant. I want to apply this to Lord of the Flies, which I know well. The signal fire seems like a maintenance commons: it only works if someone tends it constantly, yet everyone benefits from rescue, and the boys who keep it alive gain no special status. Meanwhile, the conch and the rules around it seem like a different kind of public good—perhaps an institutional one that requires acknowledgment rather than labor. Read the novel through this lens. Where does the maintenance-commons framework illuminate a specific scene—the shift from Ralph's fire to Jack's hunting, or Piggy's glasses being used to start fires? Where does the novel resist being reduced to a collective-action problem—do the boys fail because of rational free-riding, or because something about power, fear, and visibility breaks the logic of cooperation altogether? And does Golding's story add something to the philosophical debate that economic models miss—something about how recognition and the visibility of labor shape whether people will maintain a commons at all? I am preparing this for a discussion group, and I want a reading that takes both the novel and the idea seriously, without reducing either to an illustration of the other.
