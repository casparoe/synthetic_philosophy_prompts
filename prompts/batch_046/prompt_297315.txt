I am preparing materials for a graduate seminar on metaphysics of mind and causal exclusion, and I want you to design a diagnostic taxonomy — a decision tree with clearly labeled branches — for classifying a philosopher's actual position on the paradox of phenomenal judgment, assuming strict epiphenomenalism. You should adopt the role of an expert philosopher of mind with special competence in consciousness studies and philosophy of psychology. Before you begin constructing the instrument, take a moment for careful brainstorming: map out the independent dimensions along which responses to the paradox genuinely split, then organize them into a tree built from logical space rather than from a list of familiar labels.

The tree must begin from one shared commitment: phenomenal properties are entirely causally inert with respect to all physical events, including the neural processes that constitute judgment. From there, each branch should present a concrete, answerable question — not a label check — so that someone can navigate the tree without knowing in advance which view they hold. The questions should cover at least the following dimensions: (i) whether the brain states that generate a given phenomenal experience also generate the corresponding phenomenal judgment (the co-occurrence question); (ii) whether the reliability of that judgment can be secured without any causal link to the experience itself, for instance through non-causal correlation or functional isomorphism; (iii) whether the paradox applies symmetrically to negative judgments, such as the judgment that one is not in pain; and (iv) whether one permits any indirect or "shadow" efficacy — for example, through structural coupling or supervenience relations that do not violate physical causal closure.

Crucially, you must inject a niche distinction not standard in most textbook summaries: distinguish between token-mirroring, in which a judgment's content is fixed by the specific phenomenal token that is present, and type-redundancy, in which the judgment tracks only the general neural type that typically accompanies consciousness, with no token-specific connection. Make this a branching node, and show where each option leads.

The tree should terminate in named positions (e.g., Correlationist, Self-Defeat Dissolutionist, Illusionist Dissolutionist, Shadow-Efficacy Theorist, and any others appropriate), but you should first lay out the logical combinations of answers and then place the positions within them. For every combination that remains unoccupied, include an empty leaf with a one-sentence diagnosis: is it incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored? You should also explicitly flag any pair of terminal positions that this tree cannot genuinely separate — that is, positions that agree on every diagnostic question but differ only in rhetoric or framing.

For each leaf — both occupied and unoccupied — provide one sentence describing its most awkward commitment, the price it pays for holding that combination of answers. Present the tree with clear branch labels phrased as questions, and with leaf labels.

After presenting the tree, please write a brief meta-note stating how confident you are in the overall classification, and specifically identify the point — whether the placement of a particular view, the coherence diagnosis of an empty branch, or a claimed inseparability — about which you are least sure.

Please write in a formal, polished register suitable for distribution to philosophy graduate students. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; the instrument should be usable by someone without access to external sources. Think step by step, and begin with your brainstorming outline before presenting the final tree.
