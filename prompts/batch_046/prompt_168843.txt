Since retiring from experimental physics, I have taken up philosophy with the same seriousness I once reserved for laboratory work, and I have been trying to understand the recent debate over metaphysical grounding and fundamentality. At an interdisciplinary colloquium, a colleague offered me the following summary, which I reproduce from my notes: metaphysical grounding is the non-causal, determinative relation that holds when one fact obtains in virtue of another—analogous to, yet distinct from, the way the liquidity of water depends on the structure of H₂O molecules, since grounding concerns ontological dependence rather than causal mechanism. Defenders of the view, notably Jonathan Schaffer, maintain that grounding is irreflexive, asymmetric, transitive, and well-founded, and they deploy it to construct a hierarchical ontology in which the entities described by fundamental physics are absolutely basic, while tables, mental states, and normative facts are merely derivative. Their opponents, however, are said to argue that grounding collapses into ordinary scientific reduction on the one hand, or into an unnecessary metaphysical fiction that flouts Occam’s razor on the other; because empirical science already explains how higher-level phenomena arise from lower-level ones, there is no explanatory work left for a sui generis ‘in-virtue-of’ relation. My colleague therefore concluded that grounding is redundant with physics and represents a relapse into pre-scientific essentialism.

I suspect this account is largely correct but contains subtle distortions—perhaps conflating metaphysical explanation with conceptual analysis, or treating a contested formal property as uncontroversial, or overstating the uniformity of the opposition. Without asking for a bibliography or reading list, I would like you to audit this characterization: which parts faithfully represent the mainstream positions, which are misrepresentations or overstatements, and does the charge of redundancy survive once the errors are corrected? I ask this not as an examination exercise, but as a reader trying to determine whether this gloss can serve as a reliable map of the terrain.
