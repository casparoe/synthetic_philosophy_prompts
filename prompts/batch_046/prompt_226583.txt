Me and my coworkers were debating whether it's ever okay to lie to users to stop some bigger disaster -- like hiding a security flaw to prevent mass panic. One engineer was real adamant about bright-line rules: he said some actions are just wrong, full stop, no matter the outcome. But then he started explaining how in practice you'd build the system so the lie gets told automatically by a protocol, not by a person, and suddenly that was fine with him. I keep seeing this pattern in ethics discussions: people defend an absolute ban on doing X directly, but they're comfortable with designing a machine, process, or organizational layer that produces X automatically, and they don't think they've broken their own rule. It's not plain hypocrisy, I think, because they genuinely don't feel they've done X -- the system did it. And it's not motte-and-bailey, since they're not retreating to a weaker claim; they're holding the strong claim but carving out a structural loophole.

Is there an established name for this specific configuration, where a deontological prohibition stays symbolically intact but gets hollowed out funcitonally through delegation or automation? I know there's the paradox of deontology, but this feels more specific -- like the rule survives at the individual level while being bypassed at the architectural level. If there is a term, how exactly does it differ from moral licensing or from just being a rule-utilitaran in disguise? If not, can you coin one with a clear definition and boundary cases? I don't care about a bare label; I want to know what the thing is, what it isn't, and where its edges sit -- for example, does it apply when the delegation is accidental rather than designed?
