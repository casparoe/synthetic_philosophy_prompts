Hey, so I've been reading some Marx for a side project and I think I walked myself into a bad argument. Not sure where the leak is.

(1) Marx ties exploitation to capital grabbing surplus value, and alienation is the worker being split off from the product, the process, their species-being, and other people. Those two go together because the split is caused by capital owning the means of production.

(2) But he also splits concrete labor (the actual doing) from abstract labor (the value-making social average). Alienation is really your concrete work being swallowed by that abstract social machine you don't dirrect.

(3) Now take me: I'm a software engineer. Even if I write an open-source library on weekends, own the copyright, and don't earn my employer a dime, my code still runs on Linux kernels I didn't write, in languages designed by commitees, served on AWS that I don't own. My concrete labor is totally mediated by this abstract social stack.

(4) Same deal for a subsistence farmer using inherited tools and soil she doesn't own in any cosmic sense — her concrete labor is mediated by prior social labor and nature.

(5) So if alienation just means "your concrete work is embedded in an abstract social division you don't control," then every human labor ever is alienated. Even in Marx's communist utopia people would share tools and cooperate.

(6) That makes the idea useless for criticizing captialism specifically — it applies to everything, including the thing Marx wants to save.

(7) But I want to keep that writing open-source on my own time isn't alienated in the Marxist sense, since I'm dirrecting the process and keeping the product. So maybe (2) is wrong and alienation really requires a captialist extracting surplus value, not just social mediation?

Where does this go wrong? Am I smuggling in a bad definition of abstract labor, or is Marx's framwork actually that broad? I'd rather not throw out (1) or (5) if I can avoid it, but I feel like I'm missing a distinction between being mediated and being actually alienated.
