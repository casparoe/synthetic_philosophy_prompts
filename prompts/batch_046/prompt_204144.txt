For our graduate seminar on punishment and justice, I am writing down my philosophical profile so that the group can test its coherence. Here is where I have landed:

(1) Retributivism is the correct justification for criminal punishment: punishment is legitimate only when it is proportionate to the wrong actually done by the offender, and only backward-looking desert can supply that proportion.
(2) Capital punishment is permissible in principle, since there are crimes for which death is not disproportionate.
(3) Nevertheless, because the institutional processes that determine guilt in capital cases necessarily operate with a non-zero, structurally irreducible risk of false conviction, the state never possesses the categorical epistemic warrant that retributivism demands to justify an irreversible execution. On my view, this risk functions not as a utilitarian cost to be weighed against deterrence, but as a moral defeater that blocks the permission granted by (1) and (2).
(4) Deterrence-based justifications for the death penalty fail independently of (3), since they treat the condemned as a means to social ends, violating the constraint that punishment must respect the offender's rational agency even in denying it.
(5) Moral luck should not affect culpability: two agents with identical wrongful intentions and actions deserve identical punishment regardless of whether their acts actually resulted in death.
(6) I also maintain that when guilt is genuinely established, the state has a positive duty—not merely a permission—to carry out the deserved sentence; failure to punish the guilty is itself an injustice to the victim and to the moral order.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are in genuine tension, and which only appear to be? For each genuine tension, rank it by severity, identify the cheapest revision that restores consistency, and say what that revision costs me elsewhere in the profile. In particular, I want to know whether (3) can be sustained alongside (1) and (6) without collapsing into a skeptical abolitionism that I do not want, and whether (5) creates a hidden conflict with (3) that I have missed—perhaps by importing an outcome-sensitive reasoning that my rejection of moral luck should forbid. Also tell me which single revision—giving up one of (1) through (6), or qualifying one—would clean up the most tension with the least damage to the overall view.

One further request: if my framing depends on a confused premise—for instance, if retributivism does not in fact require categorical epistemic certainty but only reasonable moral certainty, or if the task of auditing these commitments cannot be done well without resolving empirical controversies about capital conviction error rates that I have left unspecified—then say so plainly and do not force a reconciliation merely to be accommodating. I want direct pushback if the profile is incoherent in a way I have not seen.
