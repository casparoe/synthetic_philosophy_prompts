Last month a new resident pointed out that this pattern happens to place all the long-term residents in the easier spring and autumn slots, while newer residents always draw the harder summer and winter turns. She proposes we switch to a random draw each year. The original residents object that the current pattern isn't an accident of injustice—it's the arrangement that actually solved the problem of who waters the tomatoes when everyone is traveling, and that we've all kept it going by acting as if it were fixed. They say changing it now would break an unspoken understanding. I'm genuinely unsure whether my sympathy for the new resident is overriding something real: the pattern did emerge on its own, it does keep the garden alive, and no one ever promised anything different. At the same time, the fact that it correlates with seniority feels like it shouldn't be protected just because it's old. Is there a philosophical distinction between a practical arrangement that earns protection because it solves a shared problem, versus one that should be open to revision precisely because its structure was never chosen and happens to distribute burdens unevenly? I'd particularly like the answer to address whether the absence of an explicit agreement makes the practice more fragile or more legitimate, and whether the possibility that we could have adopted a different rotation from the start undermines the current one's claim on us.
