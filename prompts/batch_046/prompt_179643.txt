I've been thinking about natural kinds - the idea that science carves nature at its joints. There's a defensive move I keep noticing in textbooks that I dont have a name for. A category gets defined by some deep feature, like metallic bonding explaining conductivity. Then counterexamples show up - a ceramic that conducts, a metal that stops in some weird state. Instead of dropping the kind, people add hidden qualifiers: 'true metals,' 'in canonical conditions,' 'ignoring surface contamination.' Each fix is reasonable alone, but together they seem to make the claim unfalsifiable, yet everyone still talks like they found a real joint.

Is there an established term for this slow build-up of retoractive 'except when' rules that protect a natural-kind claim? I know Popper's stuff on auxiliary hypotheses, but this feels different - the rules arent about bad measurements, they're about quietly rewriting who belongs to the kind after the fact. It's not quite essentialism with explicit ceteris paribis clauses either, since those are usually stated up front. Here they're patches. Does this have a specific label in philosophy of science, and if not, what's the right way to draw its edges - how is it different from ordinary theoretical refinement? No need for citations or reading lists, I'm just after the carving.
