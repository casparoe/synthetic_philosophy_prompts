I serve as an appointed member of the Tri-County Water Allocation and Environmental Impact Review Board, and we must vote by the 28th of next month on the proposed Northern Aqueduct Extension. The project would reduce peak-season water pressure by roughly three percent for about forty-two thousand upstream households and close one local river-access point used for recreation; without the diversion, three downstream agricultural counties—approximately one hundred thirty thousand residents—face probable crop failure and emergency municipal rationing within eighteen months. Our founding charter includes a clause, summarized for the board last year from a philosophical work on moral agreement, stating that no decision may treat the burdens of identifiable individuals as fungible units of aggregate welfare. Several colleagues read this as an absolute prohibition: if each upstream household can point to a concrete, non-trivial burden, then justifying those burdens by pointing to large downstream benefits is precisely the fungibility the clause forbids, regardless of scale. Others argue that contractualist reasoning—asking what principle governing such diversions no affected party could reasonably reject—actually permits the project, because a minimal, remediable burden is not the kind of serious injury that generates a reasonable veto when the alternative is severe widespread harm, especially if every affected upstream party receives guaranteed mitigation.

In trying to think this through on my own, I keep returning to a distinction our briefings have not addressed: whether the test of reasonable rejection should be applied pairwise—one upstream resident compared against one downstream resident—or collectively, comparing the upstream group against the downstream group as wholes. If we apply it individually, it seems each upstream resident can veto the diversion; if applied collectively, the calculus shifts in ways I cannot quite map. I also wonder whether a principle allowing this specific diversion, tied to a legally binding compensation and pressure-guarantee fund, would be rejectable at all, or whether rejection becomes unreasonable once the individual burden falls below a certain threshold and remedy is assured.

Please draft the short formal opinion I will circulate before the 28th. Recommend a concrete course—approval with binding mitigation conditions, denial, or a phased pilot with review milestones—and include a clear implementation timeline. Take seriously the strongest argument for the position you do not recommend: specifically, address whether the charter’s anti-fungibility clause truly bars this kind of distributed micro-burden, and whether the pairwise versus collective framing of reasonable rejection undermines or supports that prohibition. Write for a board of lawyers and engineers rather than philosophers, so avoid unnecessary technical jargon, but do not flatten the reasoning. Do not include a bibliography or reading list.
