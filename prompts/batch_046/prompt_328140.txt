I am drafting the final take-home exam for an upper-level philosophy of religion seminar, and I want one essay question that pushes students well past the standard textbook summary of A.J. Ayer’s verificationist rejection of life after death. A colleague suggested I simply ask them to recite the verification principle and declare survival meaningless, but I think that misses the actual philosophical work. Instead, please center the question on a conceptually sharp but rarely discussed scenario: imagine a form of post-mortem existence in which a subject’s consciousness continues with complete phenomenological continuity—retaining memories, personality, and first-person experience—yet remains entirely causally isolated from the physical world, making it in principle unverifiable by any living observer. The essay prompt should ask students to determine precisely where Ayer’s verification principle lands on this case—whether it is literally meaningless, empirically false, or merely an unfalsifiable pseudo-hypothesis—and, crucially, whether he can apply this verdict consistently without also undermining his acceptance of other minds or of the self’s temporal continuity. They should also address whether Ayer’s distinction between strong and weak verification offers a coherent, non-ad-hoc rescue. Please provide the exam question itself, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme totaling 20 marks built around five specific analytical moves a top answer must contain, with a one-line note for each on the characteristic error that should cost marks. The difficulty should come from navigating the crux of verificationism’s scope, not from recalling labels. Finally, please state in your response how confident you are in the reconstruction of Ayer’s strong versus weak verification distinction, and identify explicitly the single point about which you are least certain—whether that concerns the boundary between the two forms of verification or the consistency of his parallel argument about other minds.
