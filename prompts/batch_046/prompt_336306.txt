hey, for my phil of mind seminar i'm supposed to write up my philosophical profile on dreams so the others can poke holes in it. here it is, somewhat messy and probably with a typo or two:

1. i think dreams are experiences in a thin, minimal sense — there's something it's like to be dreaming — but they aren't live-streamed consciousness like waking perception; they're more like an offline simluation that only gets narrated into a story when you wake up.

2. i'm sympathetic to Malcolm's worry that we have no independent criterion for dream occurrence except waking report, so any theory of dream experience has to take that epistemic gap seriously.

3. i think phenominal consciousness requires some kind of global workspace integration or availability for reasoning/report; if a mental state is totally encapsulated and can't feed into cognition, it's not phenomenally conscious.

4. lucid dreams are real counterexamples to the idea that dreams always lack meta-cognitive structure — in lucidity you have monitoring and voluntary control, which shows the sleeping brain can support workspace-like dynamics.

5. i think there's nothing it's like to be in deep NREM sleep, but if someone reports a vivid dream after REM, we should treat that report as mostly veridical about some mental event that happened during sleep, not just a made-up story.

6. i think the cassette-theory idea — that dreams are literally inserted like a tape at the moment of awakening — is implausible because REM physiology correlates too tightly with dream reports; there has to be something going on during sleep.

7. i've also been flirting with this niche view that dream generation isn't memory retrieval or sensory processing but more like predictive-error resolution on random brainstem noise: the cortex gets noisy ascending signals during REM and spins a narrative to explain them, and that narrative is what we report. the "experience" is just the report-generation process, not a prior stream.

8. i think the hard problem applies differently to dreams: in waking life the gap is between world and mind, but in dreams there's no world, so the gap collapses to a gap between brain dynamics and post-hoc narrative — which is actually easier, not harder.
