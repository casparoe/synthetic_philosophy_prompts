I am currently drafting a novel in which a widow's grief gradually transforms into a kind of distributed consciousness—her late husband's mannerisms, judgments, and memories begin to animate not only her own thoughts but those of their adult children and closest friends, until it becomes ambiguous whether he survives as a singular ghost or as a dispersed pattern across several living minds. In the course of this research I have been reading Douglas Hofstadter's reflections on the persistence of the self in others, and at a recent literary gathering a philosopher friend offered me the following summary of his position: Hofstadter holds that the self is not a physical substance but a strange loop, an abstract pattern of symbol-manipulation in the brain that can, in principle, be copied or transmitted like software; consequently, when a person dies, the mental representations of them held by loved ones are not mere approximations but literal fragments of the deceased's actual consciousness. From this premise my friend derived a further claim: that personal identity is therefore fully distributed across social networks, so that asking where a self resides is like asking where a university resides, and that in principle one could reconstruct an entire consciousness from the collective mental models of a community. I find this account aesthetically compelling—indeed it shapes the architecture of my novel—but I worry it may conflate Hofstadter's actual thesis with more radical transhumanist fantasies about mind-uploading. Could you audit this summary with precision? Specifically, does Hofstadter truly regard mental representations in others as literal fragments of the original self rather than analogues? Does he claim that identity is fully distributed, or only that parts of one pattern may overlap with others while a core remains localized? And does the analogy to institutional location genuinely license the conclusion that a consciousness could be reconstructed from collective memory, or is that an overreach? I am less interested in a defense or critique of the view itself than in knowing exactly where the summary is faithful, where it subtly overstates, and which conclusions survive once the misrepresentations are corrected.
