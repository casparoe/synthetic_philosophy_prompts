In my philosophy of science discussion group we have been stuck for months on a question that sounds simple but refuses to settle: what is intelligence, in the sense that makes it the same thing whether we are talking about a human, an animal, or an artificial system? On one side are those who say intelligence is essentially a predictive construct — the measurable tendency of a system to learn quickly across domains — and that any deeper mechanism is just the substrate that realizes this tendency. On the other side are those who say intelligence is an explanatory kind, defined by specific information-processing architectures (for instance, hierarchical predictive coding or symbolic abstraction) that generate the behavior we call smart; without identifying the architecture, they argue, we are only describing correlations, not explaining intelligence.

What makes the stall frustrating is that both camps now accept the same empirical landscape: large-scale psychometric studies show a general factor, yet modular skills dissociate in brain injury; large language models display broad competence without clear architectural analogues to human reasoning; and evolutionary biologists document domain-specific adaptations that nevertheless correlate with one another. Despite this shared data, neither side has revised its position. So I want to ask: what kind of disagreement is this, actually?

Is it a hidden factual dispute about whether there is a single neural or computational substrate underlying all intelligent behavior — something an experiment could settle? Is it a difference in what counts as an explanation, where one side demands mechanistic decomposability and the other accepts functional prediction? Or is it a bedrock disagreement about whether intelligence is a natural kind at all, as opposed to a useful statistical fiction — an intuition neither side can argue for from shared premises?

My own hypothesis is that part of the impasse comes from both sides using the word intelligence to answer two different questions: one asking what we should measure when we want to rank systems by capability, and the other asking what internal property makes a system capable of open-ended adaptation. If that diagnosis is right, then clarifying which question is being asked might dissolve part of the dispute. But is that correct? And if the core disagreement is indeed about natural kinds versus practical kinds, is there any result — formal, empirical, or conceptual — that could actually move either camp, or should we expect permanent stalemate?

Please diagnose the structure of this disagreement rather than simply adjudicating it: specify whether it is verbal, substantive, or mixed; identify the cruxes; and say what kind of progress, if any, your diagnosis permits.
