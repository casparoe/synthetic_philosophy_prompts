I am a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying analytic metaphysics. In discussions of material constitution I repeatedly hear a reductionist one-liner: a statue is nothing over and above its clay arranged in a statue-like form. At a recent colloquium, a speaker invoked this claim to argue that the destruction of a statue is metaphysically indistinguishable from the reshaping of its clay, since there is only one material reality and the statue is merely a convenient description of a particular spatial arrangement.

I am trying to understand precisely what philosophical work this slogan could be doing. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses it might express, arranged as a menu rather than a single interpretation? For each reading, please indicate whether it amounts to a trivial tautology, a substantive but defensible metaphysical claim, or something that is actually false. I am particularly concerned with which of these readings—if any—would genuinely support the speaker's dismissal of the statue as an independently existing object, and which would merely restate that dismissal in different vocabulary.

As someone trained in physics, I am also struck by an apparent tension: in physical science we do not treat two distinct systems as occupying exactly the same state-space region without interaction terms, yet metaphysicians often treat coincident objects—such as a statue and its clay—as unproblematic. Does any defensible interpretation of the slogan resolve that tension, or does it simply bracket it? I would like to see the readings separated clearly enough that I could take them to a philosophy seminar and ask which one the speaker actually intended.
