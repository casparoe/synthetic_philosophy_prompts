I've been working through Dennett's distinction between cranes and skyhooks in a philosophy of mind seminar, and a classmate and I built an argument about phenomenal consciousness and moral attribution that now seems to collapse into something absurd. We called the intermediate position "scaffolded dissolution"—the idea that phenomenal properties are genuinely real but depend on representational architectures in a way that makes them vanish from ontology once the architecture is fully described, without being declared illusory. Here is the chain as we laid it out.

(1) A crane explanation of consciousness explains subjective experience bottom-up using only mechanistic processes; a skyhook explains it by invoking irreducible top-down causal powers. (2) Greedy reductionism is the error of treating a successful crane explanation as having explained away its target, so that once neural computation is fully mapped, consciousness is dismissed as an illusion rather than accounted for. (3) The alternative to greedy reductionism need not be a skyhook; it can be scaffolded dissolution, where phenomenal properties depend on descriptive scaffolding in a way that dissolves upon full explanation without being declared unreal. (4) If scaffolded dissolution is correct, then attributing moral status on the basis of phenomenal experience requires grounding rights in a property that lacks stable ontological footing independent of our explanatory framework. (5) Therefore, no computation—whether biological or synthetic—can genuinely possess phenomenal consciousness in a manner that supports moral consideration, because the property being appealed to dissolves upon complete explanation. (6) But this applies symmetrically: human consciousness dissolves just as thoroughly as artificial consciousness under the same regime. (7) So either we deny that humans have moral status based on consciousness, or we smuggle in a skyhook specifically for biological systems to preserve an asymmetry we have no independent reason to accept. (8) Hence, avoiding greedy reductionism without accepting skyhooks collapses into either universal moral nihilism about consciousness or biologically parochial special pleading.

The slide I feel most suspicious about is between (4) and (5): an ontological claim about explanation seems to be doing normative work about moral attribution. I want to keep Dennett's point that greedy reductionism is a mistake—explaining the parts can genuinely explain the whole without explaining it away—but I also want to avoid committing myself to skyhooks or to the claim that consciousness is always illusory. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Does giving up (3) force me back into greedy reductionism? Does giving up (5) require treating phenomenal consciousness as a robust, skyhook-like property after all? And if I retain (6), am I forced to say that moral consideration for consciousness is either always illusory or arbitrarily restricted to biology?
