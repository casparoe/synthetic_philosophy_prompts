I've been thinking on my own about living wills and whether the person who signs one still holds authority after dementia changes who they are. I'm in a small reading group with no formal philosophy background, and I want to build a take-home exam for us on this problem that can't be answered just by repeating the standard arguments about personal identity and autonomy. To keep it from being generic, I want the essay built around a specific case I came up with: a woman writes an advance directive refusing life-extending care only if she "fails to recognize her closest family members," then develops a progressive disorder that destroys her narrative memory and language but leaves emotional responsiveness intact. By stage three she recognizes her daughter clearly on Tuesdays, shows unmistakable joy during those visits, and asks for her by name, but on Thursdays she is frightened, does not respond to her daughter's voice, and seems to have no grasp of who is present. The prompt should ask students to decide whether the earlier self's authority applies on Tuesday, Thursday, both, or neither, and whether that authority can be partitioned rather than treated as all-or-nothing. They should have to explain what it costs to preserve the directive—does that require dismissing her Tuesday emotional well-being as irrelevant?—and what it costs to abandon it—does that mean any later self-state can veto a past commitment? Please write the exam question with instructions suitable for about 90 minutes and roughly 1,000 words, then provide a model answer of about 600 words that takes a clear position but weighs both costs, and include a marking scheme out of 20 points that lists the specific conceptual moves an A answer must contain—such as distinguishing narrative identity from emotional continuity, addressing the intermittent-recognition problem directly, and explaining whether authority can be time-sliced—along with a one-line note on the typical mistake that separates a B answer from an A.
