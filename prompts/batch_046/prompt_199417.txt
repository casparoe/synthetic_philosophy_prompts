I'm writing a feature on how planning law quietly encodes philosophical assumptions. One institutional rule puzzles me: in several UK and Commonwealth jurisdictions, an owner who wishes to demolish any pre-1950 boundary wall, gatepost, or disused footbridge must submit a "heritage justification" that explains the structure's original purpose and confirms that the applicant understands it, even when the owner holds unencumbered freehold title and no formal preservation order applies. The rule is commonly defended by analogy to Chesterton's fence—one ought not remove a barrier until one knows why it was put there—yet it applies with equal force when the builder left no record and the purpose is genuinely irrecoverable.

What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it treat tradition as a repository of distributed practical reason in the Burkean sense, or only as a risk-management heuristic? Must it assume that present individual reasoning is epistemically deficient without intergenerational scaffolding, or is it fully compatible with a purely instrumental account in which the documentation requirement is merely a transaction-cost filter? Distinguish strictly what the practice requires from what merely accompanies it. Is there an internal tension when the fence's purpose is lost? And if the underlying view were replaced by a straightforwardly consequentialist or autonomy-based framework—where a clear-title owner may alter property provided external harms are priced—what specific procedural elements would have to disappear, and which might survive?
