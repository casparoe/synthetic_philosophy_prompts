After a lengthy argument with a friend who insisted that any democratic procedure with more than two options must either be manipulable or authoritarian, I have decided to design a rigorous exam for an upper-level seminar on social choice theory and democratic design. I want a single essay question that cannot be answered by merely stating the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem. Instead, it should present a concrete institutional mechanism—specifically, a three-stage university dean-selection procedure in which faculty submit complete preference rankings over three candidates, a veto threshold eliminates the lowest-ranked option, and a final pairwise approval vote selects the winner—and ask students to diagnose whether this mechanism is strategy-proof. The question must require them to identify the precise stage at which strategic misrepresentation of preferences becomes rational, to explain why restricting the domain to single-peaked preferences does or does not resolve the vulnerability in this particular design, and to distinguish strategy-proofness from Pareto efficiency and monotonicity in their analysis. Please provide the exam question itself, a model answer of approximately 700 words, and a detailed marking scheme that separates an excellent response—one that applies the theorem correctly to the institutional details and pinpoints the manipulation opportunity—from a competent but superficial response that knows the theorem's abstract conditions but misapplies them to the mechanism. The scheme should specify the essential analytical moves, assign point values totaling 25 marks, and describe the typical misconceptions that should reduce the score.
