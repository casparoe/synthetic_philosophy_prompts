I have recently entered a research collaboration with a philosopher whose technical competence I deeply respect, yet we now find ourselves in a sharp disagreement over a methodological question; this has made the conciliationism versus steadfastness debate personally pressing. I wish to examine an argument that, if sound, would show conciliationism to be self-defeating. (1) Define epistemic peerhood with respect to a proposition P as holding only when two agents share equal evidential access, reasoning competence, and mutual acceptance of a shared calibration protocol for revising credences. (2) If peers disagree about P, conciliationism requires each to adjust their credence towards the other according to that protocol. (3) But peers can disagree about whether the calibration protocol itself is appropriate for P, or about whether they genuinely share it—call this the protocol-disagreement, R. (4) Applying conciliationism to R requires each to adjust their view of the protocol's suitability, which may include downgrading their belief that the peer relation holds. (5) Once either party assigns non-negligible credence to the failure of peerhood—because the protocol is disputed—the conditions for applying conciliationism to P are undermined. (6) Since any sustained first-order disagreement plausibly generates a second-order disagreement about the appropriate revision protocol or the peer relation itself, conciliationism will systematically dissolve its own applicability conditions before reaching the first-order belief. (7) Thus conciliationism either demands an infinite regress of meta-conciliations with no stable object-level revision, or it tacitly presupposes steadfastness about the peer relation, which is exactly the position it seeks to deny. The conclusion—that rational peer disagreement is impossible under conciliationism—seems absurd. Which step should I reject? I am inclined to preserve (1) and (2), as the idea that recognised equality should motivate some revision strikes me as correct, but I want to understand whether the error lies in treating protocol-agreement as part of peerhood, in assuming conciliation applies to R, or in the inference from (4) to (5). Please identify the weakest link, explain why it initially looks plausible, and assess what each repair strategy—restricting conciliation to object-level beliefs, treating peerhood as a binary status immune to credal revision, or accepting that peer disagreement is necessarily unstable—would commit me to.
