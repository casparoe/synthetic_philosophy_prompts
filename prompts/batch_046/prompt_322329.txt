I work in software engineering and have no formal background in philosophy or quantitative genetics, but I have read enough of the behavioral-genetics literature to notice a recurring argumentative pattern that I have not seen given a precise name. Here is the configuration I keep encountering.

Take two populations—call them Group A and Group B—in which the same polygenic trait is measured (for example, performance on a standardized cognitive task, risk for a particular psychiatric condition, or even a proxy like programming-test scores in different socioeconomic environments). In Group A, environmental conditions are relatively uniform, and the heritability estimate is high, say around 0.75. In Group B, environmental conditions vary widely, and the heritability estimate is low, say around 0.25. People then reason that the trait is “more genetic” in Group A and “more environmental” in Group B, and they draw concrete policy conclusions from that ranking: they argue that environmental interventions should be prioritized in Group B because “the environment is doing the work there,” whereas in Group A “the genes dominate” and intervention will hit a hard ceiling.

What interests me is that the same speakers will usually concede, if pressed, that heritability is a population-level variance ratio rather than a measure of causal strength, and that the underlying gene pool is essentially identical across the two groups. So they understand the statistical definition, yet they still slide into treating the coefficient as a kind of compositional percentage—as if Group A’s trait were 75 percent genetic material and 25 percent environmental material. They are not committing the simple heritability fallacy of saying “this trait is heritable, therefore it is fixed,” because they do allow that altering Group A’s environment could shift the coefficient downward. Rather, they are using the coefficient comparatively across populations to rank where genetic causation is “stronger,” even though they know that the coefficient’s magnitude is driven by the amount of environmental variance present in each sample, not by any change in the size of genetic effects.

I have seen this in debates about education policy, psychiatric genetics, and casual tech-industry discussions about whether programming aptitude is “innate.” Does this specific comparative cross-population form have an established name? If the standard answer is just “the heritability fallacy,” I want to understand exactly how this differs: here the error is not about immutability within a single population, but about ranking populations by causal dominance based on variance ratios. Is there a conceptual framework that distinguishes this comparative form from ordinary statistical illiteracy, from legitimate gene-environment interaction claims, and from issues in population ethics? I am not asking for a bare label or a reading list; I want to know precisely what the phenomenon is, where its conceptual edges lie, which nearby established concepts come closest and why each misses something, and if there is no established term, what definition and boundary conditions would cleanly separate it from those neighbors.
