However, this criticism misses the point: evolutionary psychology makes concrete, falsifiable predictions. For example, it predicts that loss aversion should appear universally across cultures and should emerge early in development without explicit financial instruction. Experimental and cross-cultural data confirm both predictions. Moreover, because loss aversion appears in young children before they receive formal financial education, this demonstrates that the mechanism could not have been shaped by cultural learning and must therefore reflect direct ancestral selection for resource conservation in the Pleistocene.

The central issue is whether current utility proves evolutionary origin. A trait can be useful today—helping people protect resources—without having been selected for that function. It might instead be a spandrel, a byproduct of general cognitive architecture, or a culturally transmitted heuristic. To establish true adaptation, researchers need comparative phylogenetic data, genetic correlations, and evidence that the mechanism is domain-specific rather than domain-general. Evolutionary psychologists sometimes blur this line by treating cross-cultural universality as sufficient proof of ancestral selection.

Thus, the defense succeeds in part but not entirely. The prediction of universal loss aversion is empirically testable and well-supported, which refutes the charge of total unfalsifiability.
