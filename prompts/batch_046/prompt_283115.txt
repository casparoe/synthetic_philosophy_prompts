As a student in an upper-level seminar that crosses classical Islamic philosophy with contemporary philosophy of mind, I would like you to compose a formal dialogue between two equally capable interlocutors on Avicenna's flying man argument. One speaker should defend the classic Avicennian interpretation: she holds that the suspended, sensory-deprived subject's immediate certainty of his own existence proves that the self (nafs) is an immaterial substance whose being does not depend on bodily awareness. The other speaker should grant the phenomenological datum—there is indeed a form of self-certainty that survives total sensory deprivation—but deny the metaphysical inference; he should contend that this certainty is better accounted for by a minimal, pre-reflective self-model that can operate without sensory input, and he should press the defender on whether modern distinctions between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, together with clinical phenomena such as blindsight or dissociative identity disruption, weaken the move from epistemic independence to ontological independence.

The discussion should stay concrete rather than abstract: they should examine what precisely the flying man is aware of—whether it is a bare existential judgment or a temporally extended first-person perspective—and whether Avicenna is entitled to derive the soul's immateriality from that awareness alone. Each speaker must respond directly to the argument just advanced: if the defender invokes the unity and simplicity of consciousness, the critic should address that claim specifically rather than shifting ground; if the critic appeals to predictive processing or embodied cognition, the defender should explain why those frameworks fail to capture the flying man's case. Include at least three substantial exchanges for each side, maintain rigorous philosophical tone throughout, avoid strawman representations, and leave the disagreement genuinely unresolved at the close. Please do not include bibliographies, citations, references to specific editions or page numbers, or requests for further reading; the dialogue should proceed entirely from the arguments themselves.
