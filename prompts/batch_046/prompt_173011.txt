I've been helping my older sister settle our late grandfather's watchmaking workshop, and we've reached a frustrating impasse over a single object: a carbon-steel mainspring he kept for decades in a brass-lined box fitted with a desiccant packet and a rubber suspension cradle. He always called it "the brittle one," and his handwritten notes describe the alloy as prone to sudden fracture under torsion at ordinary room temperature. My sister, who works in materials testing, points out that in over forty years inside that protected environment the spring never cracked, never exhibited micro-fractures under magnification, and even survived a rough cross-country shipment without damage. She argues that this perfect record proves the property was never genuinely present—that the box and cradle did not "mask" some hidden tendency, because there was nothing there to mask.

I keep insisting that the climate control and shock absorption are exactly why the fragility never manifested, not evidence of its absence. She replies that I'm treating an unobservable tendency as metaphysically real just because my grandfather assigned it a name, and that if my reasoning were consistent I would have to claim every sturdy object is secretly brittle inside a padded case. I need an honest assessment: is my intuition tracking something philosophically substantial about properties that can genuinely exist without ever being displayed, or am I simply romanticizing an old craftsman's caution? Is there a principled distinction between a true disposition that has been systematically prevented from showing itself and a fictional property we've projected onto an ordinary spring?
