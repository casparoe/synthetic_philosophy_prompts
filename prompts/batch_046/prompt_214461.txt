I am serving as a teaching assistant for a graduate seminar on philosophical logic, and I am working through final essays that compare accounts of vagueness. The exam prompt introduced a framing drawn from our syllabus—what we call the adjacent-case opacity problem—which asks whether a speaker's inability to discriminate between neighboring instances of a vague predicate generates a distinctive objection for epistemicism that supervaluationism genuinely evades, or whether both theories must absorb that opacity in structurally different ways. One student whose previous work has been strong submitted the following response.

Question: Explain the adjacent-case opacity problem and assess whether it damages epistemicism more severely than supervaluationism. In your answer, distinguish the epistemicist claim that vagueness is ignorance of a sharp boundary from the supervaluationist claim that vagueness is semantic indecision among admissible precisifications, and show how each view handles the fact that adjacent cases are phenomenologically indistinguishable despite falling on opposite sides of some division.

Student answer: Epistemicism maintains that vague predicates possess precise extensions and that our hesitation reflects merely epistemic limitation. Supervaluationism denies that there is one correct extension; instead, a sentence is true if it holds across all admissible sharpenings, false if it fails on all, and otherwise lacks a truth value. The adjacent-case opacity problem notes that if epistemicism is correct, then two individuals with 4,000 and 4,001 hairs might straddle an unknown sharp boundary, making them metaphysically different despite being perceptually identical. This seems to make the boundary arbitrary and therefore implausible. Supervaluationism avoids this difficulty because it rejects the idea that there is a single hidden boundary. On this view, the two individuals may fall on different sides of some admissible precisifications but on the same side of others, so there is no metaphysical fact that distinguishes them in reality—only semantic variation across precisifications.

Nevertheless, supervaluationism does not fully escape the opacity concern. Because each precisification itself contains sharp boundaries, the problem of adjacent indistinguishability is reproduced within every sharpening: on any given precisification, 4,000 and 4,001 hairs still fall on opposite sides, and the speaker cannot know which sharpening is being applied. Thus, supervaluationism multiplies the sharp boundaries rather than eliminating them. Meanwhile, the epistemicist can reply that the boundary is not arbitrary but grounded in the actual distribution of properties, and that our failure to discriminate adjacent cases is a limitation of our cognitive apparatus rather than a metaphysical defect. Consequently, epistemicism withstands the opacity objection, whereas supervaluationism merely relocates it inside its framework of admissible interpretations.
