Since I retired from the biology dept I've been trying to think more clearly about moral categories, and I got stuck on genocide. Here are my gut verdicts, and I need help seeing if there's a pattern or I'm just being sloppy.

The Holocaust and Rwanda in '94 are genocide, full stop.

The Armenian killings in 1915: I think yes, this should be called genocide, even though Turkey disputes central intent and the paper trail is messier than for the Nazis. But then the Holodomor in Ukraine, 1932-33 -- I keep going back and forth. Millions died, Stalin was clearly targeting Ukrainian national identity, but I feel like calling it genocide is wrong because the death was a means of political control, not an end of destroying Ukrainians as such. Though I'm not sure that distinction holds water.

The Canadian residential school system for indigenous children: I think this is not genocide in the legal sense. Lemkin's original concept apparently included cultural destruction, but the UN Convention left that out. Even if the intent was to erase indigenous culture, without mass killing I don't think it should carry the genocide label, awful as it was.

Now here's the case that breaks my intuition: imagine a state uses algorithims and social engineering to gently push one ethnic group to intermarry and dissolve over generations, with zero violence and no explicit coercion. If the explicit intent was to eliminate that group, I think that should count as genocide. But current law wouldn't cover it.

So what's the principle? Is it specifc intent plus some threshold of harm? Then why is Armenia in but Holodomor maybe out? Is it about direct physical destruction? Then why is my algorithm case in? Or am I just inconsistent about Stalin? Please tell me what principle captures the most of these, which verdict I'd have to drop for simplicity, and what it predicts for a case I haven't listed -- maybe a government that forcibly sterilizes every member of an ethnic minority but otherwise lets them live? I'm not looking for citations or a reading list, just the conceptual structure.
