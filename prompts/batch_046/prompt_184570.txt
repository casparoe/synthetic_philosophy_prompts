Consider the conjunction of two theses in practical philosophy: first, that normative force never bears directly on particular actions but only indirectly, through second-order norms that regulate the architecture of an agent's deliberation; second, that the correct architecture is determined not by the agent's actual psychological or social constitution but by a model of idealized deliberation in which all contingent communal attachments and historical particularities are methodically suspended. What are the most important implications of holding both theses together for the design of institutional oversight mechanisms—such as constitutional courts or ethics boards—that are structurally barred from issuing direct operational directives and may only annul or reshape the procedural frameworks within which others act? Address specifically: (i) whether the indirect structure genuinely prevents these bodies from exercising hidden direct control, or whether institutional power causes the distinction to collapse; (ii) whether suspending social embeddedness in the idealization strips the resulting norms of authority over the actual, embedded agents they are meant to govern; and (iii) whether the framework can yield determinate guidance when second-order norms themselves come into conflict, or whether it necessarily defers to a further, unspecified meta-deliberative layer.
