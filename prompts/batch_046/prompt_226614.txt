You are a professional philosopher of language specializing in speech-act theory and the philosophy of technology. Before drafting anything, take a moment to brainstorm the standard objections to extending Austin’s framework beyond face-to-face interaction, especially regarding speaker authority, conventional procedure, and the distinction between misfires and abuses.

I host a philosophy podcast, and next week’s guest is a philosopher who defends a view he calls distributed performativity. On this view, in algorithmically mediated environments an illocutionary act is not completed by an individual speaker alone; it is jointly constituted by the speaker’s utterance and the platform’s automated curation, ranking, and enforcement mechanisms. He argues that Austin’s felicity conditions must be revised so that procedural correctness includes algorithmic uptake, and that the conventional effect of, for example, a public apology or a contractual offer can be nullified not by human refusal but by automated shadow-banning or demonetization.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that go far beyond asking him to restate this view. The questions should turn on the actual content of his position: press where the literature presses, anticipate the standard reply, and include a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give.

In particular, include at least one question on each of the following, with an embedded follow-up:

- Whether algorithmic amplification and suppression should be treated as parts of the illocutionary act itself or as separate perlocutionary effects, and what hangs on that distinction for speaker responsibility. Anticipate his answer that uptake is constitutive; follow up by asking whether this makes every speaker a co-author with an opaque algorithm, and whether that dissolves the normative conditions for apology.
- How his framework handles Austin’s distinction between misfires and abuses when a platform’s terms-of-service enforcement retroactively nullifies a user’s declaration—does this count as a procedural misfire, an abuse of convention, or a collapse of the distinction? Anticipate his answer that retroactive nullification is a novel form of misfire; follow up by pressing whether a misfire requires a failure of speaker procedure or can be caused entirely by a third-party mechanism the speaker never authorized.
- Whether distributed agency dissolves the speaker’s authority to correct or retract an utterance once algorithmic dissemination has taken over, and whether this undermines the normative structure of promise or apology. Anticipate his answer that authority is shared rather than dissolved; follow up by asking what the speaker can meaningfully retract if the algorithm continues to circulate the original utterance regardless of the speaker’s subsequent correction.

Take a committed, definite position in framing these questions: construct them so that they force a choice between preserving Austin’s speaker-centered framework and accepting the distributed revision, rather than neutrally listing options. Write the response in American English.
