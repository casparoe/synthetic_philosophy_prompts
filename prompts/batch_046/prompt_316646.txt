I've recently begun to notice a pattern in my attempts to change my habits that feels less like ordinary laziness and more like a structural feature of how I weigh options across time. In January I committed to a rigorous writing schedule because I genuinely valued finishing a long-form essay by autumn; at that distance, the future reward seemed to outweigh any present inconvenience by a wide and stable margin. Yet every evening, when the choice is between an hour of drafting and an hour of passive entertainment, the immediate option dominates by what feels like an irrationally large factor—much larger than the modest impatience I display toward rewards that are months away. I have since learned that standard rational-choice models assume people discount the future at a constant rate, whereas observed human behavior often follows a curve that plunges steeply near the present and then flattens abruptly. This describes me with unsettling accuracy. The practical question is whether I should treat these recurring failures as lapses that demand stronger self-discipline, or as predictable distortions that should be engineered around—locking away my devices, scheduling mandatory co-working sessions, making the near option physically unavailable. But beneath the practical question is a conceptual one: if my preferences are genuinely different at close range—if the agent deciding at 8 PM sincerely prefers relaxation while the agent planning in January sincerely prefers the completed essay—then in what sense is there a single rational agent being undermined? Should I regard the long-range preference as authoritative simply because it is temporally broader, or is that just an unexamined prejudice in favor of the planner? I am not asking for generic productivity advice; I want to understand how these time-sliced preferences relate to one another, and whether recognizing this specific, non-constant shape of impatience changes what it means to fail by one's own lights.
