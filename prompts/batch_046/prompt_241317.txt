In my aesthetics course we have been working with Nelson Goodman’s distinction between autographic arts—those in which the history of production is constitutive, so that a perfect duplicate remains a forgery—and allographic arts—those defined by notational or structural compliance, so that an accurate reproduction is fully the work itself. I want to apply this framework to Oscar Wilde’s The Picture of Dorian Gray, but I want to push it beyond the obvious point that Basil’s portrait is an autographic object. Here is the twist: Dorian’s physical body seems to operate like an allographic “performance” of his soul—if a different body instantiated the same moral corruption and youthful appearance, it might count as the same “Dorian-state”—yet the narrative treats his original body as uniquely valuable and irreplaceable, precisely the way an autographic original is valued over a copy. At the same time, the portrait ages and decays as an autographic record of his true moral state, while Dorian himself remains visually identical, functioning like a flawless allographic reproduction of his youthful self that hides the underlying “notation” of sin.

Please read the novel through this lens. Does Wilde dramatize a category mistake by treating personal identity as autographic—valuing the original body for its history—when Goodman’s framework suggests identity should be allographic? Or does the novel reveal that personal identity is indeed autographic in a way Goodman’s account of art misses? Take both the philosophical distinction and the plot seriously: show how the reading illuminates specific moments—such as Basil’s refusal to exhibit the portrait because he has invested too much of himself in that particular canvas, or Dorian’s decision to destroy the portrait as if killing the “original” record of his corruption—while also saying where the novel resists the framework and forces the honest critic to concede that the analogy breaks down. I am not looking for a bibliography or citation list; I want a critical reading that treats Wilde’s story and Goodman’s aesthetics as genuine interlocutors, and that ends by saying what the novel adds to the philosophical debate about originals and forgeries that pure argument alone does not capture.
