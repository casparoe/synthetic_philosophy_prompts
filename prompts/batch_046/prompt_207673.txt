I'm working on a novel where one character runs a consciousness lab, and over drinks my mate summed up heterophenomology — he said it's basically applying literary criticism to neuroscience. The idea is you treat a subject's report about seeing red or feeling pain like a first-person narrative rather than a direct transcript of brain events. From that you build what Dennet calls a heterophenomenological world, an imaginary story-space made up entirely of the beliefs the subject holds about their own mind. The scientist doesn't ask whether the redness or pain are real in the head; instead they score the account with a kind of narrative reliability index — if parts of the story clash, those bits get downgraded, but nothing is treated as raw evidence for qualia. My mate reckoned this dissolves the hard problem completely, since consciousness just becomes a well-edited fiction, and he even said Dennet nicked the framing from reader-response theory. It sounds clever but a bit too neat? Is this a fair summary? Can you separate what's accurate about the method, what's a harmless simplification, and what's just wrong — especially the reliability-index thing and the reader-response claim — and tell me whether the conclusion that the hard problem vanishes actually follows?
