In revising a seminar paper on strategic rationality for a graduate course in philosophy of economics, my colleagues and I have been arguing about commitment races—not the canonical pre-commitment models, but a narrower subclass I am calling asymmetric lock-in tournaments, in which one agent can make an irreversible, publicly observable commitment while the other retains a private, revocable option, and payoffs depend on the temporal order of lock-in rather than on simultaneous choice. I need the full specification of what any normative theory of such tournaments must actually achieve to count as adequate: the phenomena it must explain, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the constraints it must respect, each with the motivating case behind it.

Give me exactly five non-negotiable desiderata, numbered 1 through 5, each in one or two sentences with no introduction or conclusion; each should identify a specific requirement—such as preserving the distinction between strategic inevitability and moral coercion, or between first-mover advantage and exploitative threat—together with the case or consideration that makes it mandatory. For each entry, note what argument or finding would overturn it, and within the same list identify which single desideratum is the weakest link, explaining where the reasoning is most likely to fail and what consideration would change your mind about including it.
