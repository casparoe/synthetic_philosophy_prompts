I have been observing an interpretive stalemate in Hume scholarship that I find conceptually revealing, and I would like a structural diagnosis rather than another exegetical survey. The dispute concerns whether Hume's moral philosophy permits suicide when life becomes an unrelieved burden. One faction argues that, on Hume's account, the artificial virtues—including the conventions that stabilize society—are justified solely by their utility, and that when an individual's continued existence produces no further benefit and causes only misery, the reasons that ordinarily sustain the prohibition dissolve. The opposing faction contends that Hume's decision to suppress his essay on the topic, together with his broader commitment to the authority of established social practices, indicates that suicide violates a de facto boundary of the conventional order regardless of private cost-benefit calculations. Both factions grant the same textual evidence and agree that Hume never endorsed religious arguments against self-destruction. The debate has persisted without movement, and I am skeptical that further archival discovery will resolve it. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily about Hume's intentions but about a hidden disagreement over explanatory priority: whether moral reasons derive from the preservation of conventional stability (a structural condition) or from the alleviation of suffering (a substantive outcome). I also wonder whether there is an unacknowledged third factor—specifically, whether Hume's theory of property and possession, which treats the person as the primary holder of conventional rights, generates a special obligation not to destroy the very subject that bears those rights, thereby creating a constraint that operates independently of both utility and social stability. Is this stalemate verbal, substantive, or rooted in incompatible standards for what counts as a moral reason within a sentimentalist framework? And if it is substantive, what conceptual distinction—perhaps between prudential and moral reasoning about the self, or between negative and positive obligations of preservation—would allow progress? If it is not resolvable, I want to know why not, and what that implies about the limits of reflective equilibrium in interpreting historical moral theories.
