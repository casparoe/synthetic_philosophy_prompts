I work as a software engineer and have no formal background in philosophy or archival theory. In recent reading on archives and power—specifically on who gets recorded and who is left out—I have repeatedly encountered an analogy: an archive is like a database schema designed by dominant institutions. The claim is that the categories which exist (tax rolls, land titles, military conscription lists) reflect the interests of the state or ruling class, while anything that falls outside those categories—informal labour, domestic care, oral tradition—is treated as noise and discarded. Proponents use this to argue that historical silences are structural rather than accidental: the absence of a group from the record is not a random gap but proof that the archive was built to recognise only certain kinds of lives.

I want a rigorous audit of how far this analogy holds. Please set out the correspondence explicitly—what maps onto the schema, the database administrator, the missing rows, the query that returns no results, and the storage layer. Then identify where the mapping breaks: does an archive really have a single governing logic comparable to a schema, or is it a dispersed collection of practices with conflicting logics? Can absence always be read as intentional design rather than compound neglect, decay, or never-captured origin? And does the analogy smuggle in assumptions—such as the possibility of version control, redundant backups, or rollback—that archives lack, thereby making silences look more recoverable or more centrally orchestrated than they are?

Finally, tell me whether the conclusion—that missing records demonstrate a unified will to power—survives once these disanalogies are on the table. If the analogy collapses in serious ways, propose a better one for describing how power shapes what survives, distinguishing between silences that matter for the intended argument and those that do not. I am not asking for citations, bibliographies, or source lists; the analysis should stand on its own reasoning.
