I'm helping draft conceptual guidelines for long-duration extraterrestrial settlement governance—frameworks meant to outlive their founders and operate across interplanetary distances where democratic revision is structurally impeded by communication latency. Rather than rehearsing the familiar colonization-versus-exploration framing, our group is working with two interlocking but under-theorized concepts: intergenerational sovereignty under jurisdictional latency, which concerns the binding of non-consenting descendants to institutions they cannot amend in real time; and engineered-ecology lock-in, which refers to the irreversible obligations future populations inherit when first-generation settlers commit to specific biosphere-maintenance regimes under conditions of extreme epistemic uncertainty. Given these framing devices, I want to know which open conceptual questions and lines of philosophical work in the ethics of space settlement should take priority for a small research group with roughly two years of focused effort, and I want the ordering defended rather than surveyed.

Specifically, please rank the importance of resolving three clusters—whether founding authority can ever be legitimate when exit costs are astronomical and consent is intergenerational; how obligations to maintain engineered biospheres should be weighed against reproductive autonomy when ecological stability and population control become technically inseparable; and whether jurisdictional latency transforms legitimate governance into irrevocable trustee mandates or undermines authority altogether—though you should feel free to displace one of these with a different crux if you think the framing misses it. For each highly ranked question, explain why it is upstream of the others in terms of dependency, how tractable it is for philosophers working without empirical settlement data, and what kind of conceptual result over the next couple of years would cause you to reorder the priorities. I am less interested in a literature review than in a defended agenda: which problem, if left unresolved, makes the others either moot or unsolvable, and where limited conceptual labor will actually shift the design of governance frameworks rather than merely decorate them.
