As a software engineer without any formal training in philosophy, I have been trying to understand semantic externalism after encountering it in discussions about AI interpretability. A technically sharp colleague offered me the following summary of Putnam's Twin Earth argument and its implications: on Earth, a speaker's mental representation of water might be identical to that of a Twin Earth speaker whose environment contains XYZ rather than H2O, yet because the actual chemical composition of the liquid in each speaker's environment differs, the word "water" refers to H2O in one case and XYZ in the other. From this, my colleague concluded that meaning is never fixed by internal mental states alone but always requires an external environmental anchor. He then extended this directly to large language models, arguing that a model with identical weights and architecture but trained or deployed in different data environments literally means different things by the same token outputs, because the statistical environment fixes reference in the same way that H2O fixes "water." Consequently, he claims that interpretability techniques focused solely on internal weights are fundamentally incomplete, since they ignore the environmental contribution to semantic content.

I have a nagging suspicion that this analogy overreaches—particularly in treating statistical correlations as mental representations, and in sliding from the fixing of reference to the fixing of full semantic content—but I do not have the philosophical vocabulary to pinpoint exactly where the argument breaks down. Is this a fair characterization of what Putnam's externalism actually claims, and does the conclusion about language-model interpretability genuinely follow from it? Please take a committed position rather than laying out neutral options: identify which parts of my colleague's summary are accurate to the view, which parts subtly misrepresent it or conflate distinct claims, whether the Twin Earth scenario can be legitimately applied to artificial systems at all, and tell me clearly whether I should accept or reject the claim that externalist considerations make purely internalist interpretability methods inadequate.
