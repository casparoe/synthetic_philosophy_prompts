I am a high school student working through the philosophy of moral progress, and I have come to defend a position I call contractive refinement. Rather than accepting Peter Singer’s familiar expanding-circle model—in which moral advancement consists mainly of extending moral consideration outward to animals, strangers, or future generations—I hold that genuine progress occurs primarily through the intensification and internal differentiation of obligations within an already-established circle of moral concern, often accompanied by a tacit acceptance that peripheral beings remain outside or hold only thin, derivative status. I take modern environmental ethics, for instance, not as extending personhood to ecosystems, but as deepening the texture of human stewardship obligations while leaving distant strangers and non-human animals at the margin with essentially unchanged, minimal duties. What evidence or argument would genuinely count against this view? I ask for the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: historical cases in which progress unmistakably required boundary-crossing rather than internal deepening; conceptual arguments showing that intensifying internal obligations without extending membership logically collapses back into the expanding-circle framework; and empirical or sociological patterns suggesting that societies which deepen internal moral textures reliably do so in tandem with broadening external concern, not in place of it. I also want an honest assessment of whether my position risks unfalsifiability—whether any apparent case of circle-expansion can be redescribed as internal refinement—and if that risk materializes, whether it undermines the view’s value or merely reflects its conceptual flexibility. Please do not provide citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; I am reasoning without access to external sources.
