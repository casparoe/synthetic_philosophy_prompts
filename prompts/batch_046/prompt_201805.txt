I'm preparing a paper for my philosophy of language seminar on Wittgenstein's account of language games and family resemblance. Rather than rehearse the standard examples from the literature, I want to design a precise thought experiment that isolates the structural difference between a family-resemblance analysis and a classical essentialist account of concept-application. Please construct a realistic, non-scientific scenario—set in an ordinary workplace or community practice, not a trolley case or fictional physics, and no longer than one paragraph—in which a particular concept applies to a borderline case through criss-crossing similarities, yet every plausible essentialist analysis must exclude that same case because it lacks the single necessary feature the essentialist is compelled to identify as the core. After presenting the scenario, state the concept being analyzed, describe the borderline activity, and show exactly how the family-resemblance verdict is reached by mapping the overlapping traits; then show the essentialist verdict, identifying which putative necessary condition is missing and why that absence is unavoidable rather than superficial. Next, specify the structural feature of the case that does the isolating work—such as the absence of a common thread across all members combined with dense pairwise overlap—and explain whether removing that feature collapses the distinction into a mere definitional dispute. Finally, indicate how a defender of essentialism might attack the case, for instance by arguing that an unnoticed functional core is present after all. The objective is to demonstrate that the family-resemblance inclusion is generated by the internal structure of the practice rather than stipulated in advance.
