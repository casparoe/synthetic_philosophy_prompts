I have been trying to understand Bradley's regress and the problem of the unity of the proposition—not merely as a historical puzzle in metaphysics, but as a live constraint on theories of representation. The core difficulty is familiar: if the connection between a subject and a predicate is itself a separate entity (a relation), then that relation must in turn be related to the terms it connects, generating an infinite regress; and even if the regress is halted, one still needs an account of how the constituents form a single unified proposition rather than a mere list.

I want to know what the most important implications of this problem are for contemporary non-symbolic accounts of mental representation—specifically, architectures that encode concepts as distributed vectors and attempt to bind roles and fillers through mechanisms like tensor-product representations or holographic reduced representations. Please describe these implications in detail, addressing at least three issues: first, whether the regress applies with equal force to implicit binding mechanisms that do not postulate explicit relational symbols; second, whether the demand for propositional unity requires distributed architectures to approximate some irreducible structural relation that they do not literally contain, or whether unity can be reconstructed from patterns of activation alone; and third, whether Bradley's own resolution—treating relations as internal to their relata rather than external—offers a coherent model for distributed representations, or whether adopting it collapses the distinction between a representation and what it represents in ways that undermine representational error. I am not asking for a literature review or citations, but for a direct assessment of which philosophical pressures survive translation into this representational vocabulary and which turn out to be artifacts of assuming classical, symbol-structured propositions.
