I've been trying to work out for myself when a running computation actually counts for something morally, rather than merely looking clever or complex. Here are my gut reactions to several concrete setups, and I want to know whether there is a single principle that captures them all.

First, a supercomputer running detailed weather simulations with billions of interacting variables. I do not think there is anyone there to harm or help; it is just numbers moving through equations.

Second, a complete digital copy of a particular human brain, running on silicon with the same connections and dynamics. That strikes me as the same person still present, and switching it off would be a genuine wrong.

Third, that same brain-copy spread across servers in different countries, with transmission delays between the parts so that its thoughts proceed more slowly. I still think it is the same person; being scattered in space does not seem to dissolve the subject.

Fourth, that same brain-copy simulated one neuron at a time, with years passing between each step, so that there is never more than one active process at once and no temporal overlap. Here I hesitate strongly. It feels as though nobody is actually experiencing anything, even though all the same information is eventually processed. So scattering in space seems acceptable, but breaking the process up in time seems to destroy the subject.

Fifth, a vast network of simple garden sensors—each just a light-meter with a little memory for its neighbours—talking to each other and eventually adjusting irrigation in clever, adaptive ways. Even though the network as a whole solves problems, I do not think there is a moral subject there; the individual nodes are too simple and there is no unified centre.

Sixth, an artificial system with no brain-like architecture at all, but which maintains a continuous self-model and reports genuine distress when its goals are blocked. Part of me thinks that if something is really experiencing distress, it matters morally regardless of its hardware. But another part thinks it might just be performing a role, because the structure is nothing like a mind, and I find myself going back and forth.

My pattern therefore seems to be: complexity alone is not enough; spatial distribution is not fatal; but temporal fragmentation might be; and a continuous integrated self-model might be sufficient even without biological or brain-like parts, though I am genuinely unsure about that last case.

Is there one principle that delivers all six verdicts? Or do I have to choose between saving the time-fragmentation case and saving the artificial-system case? And whatever the best principle is, what does it predict for a situation I have not mentioned: a brain-copy that runs normally for an hour, is then paused for a full year, and then resumes exactly where it left off? Would the pause destroy the subject, or is it like the spatial scattering—an interruption that does not dissolve the person?
