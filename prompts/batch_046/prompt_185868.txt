Write a sestina about the two-envelope paradox in which the six rotating end-words are switch, half, double, bound, prior, and open. Let the sestina’s mandatory rotation—each stanza reordering the end-words in the pattern 6-1-5-2-4-3—enact the paradox’s infinite reasoning loop: every time you open one envelope, the other seems to offer, on expectation, five-fourths of its value, so the speaker should always switch. In the first stanza, establish two sealed envelopes, one containing exactly twice the other; in the middle stanzas, have the speaker compute the expectation—equal chances of half and double—so that the line ending in switch always argues for exchanging envelopes. By the fifth stanza, the repetition should feel vertiginous: the speaker realizes the same logic applies after any switch, and the word bound should sit at the line-end that asks whether any stopping point exists without an external constraint. The envoi must gather all six end-words into three lines and break the cycle by invoking the resolution that there is no uniform prior over all positive real amounts, which means the expectation is undefined without a bounded distribution. Let prior sit in the envoi as the term that dissolves the symmetry, and let open close the poem on the idea that revealing one envelope’s contents exposes not a definite sum but the necessity of a prior belief. Keep the tone philosophical rather than narrative, avoid moralizing about greed, and do not cite authors or texts. Render the logic itself in the verse, accurate enough that someone familiar with decision theory recognizes the 1.25 expectation and the improper-prior resolution, yet clear enough that a reader who has not encountered the paradox can follow the argument.
