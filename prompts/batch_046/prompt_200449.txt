so i've been reading around indirect normativity — the idea that normative claims aren't basic but come out of some idealised deliberation procedue, like what you'd endorse if you had all the facts and no internal contradictions. there's this long stall in the literature, going back decades, where one camp says this just collapses into direct normativity with extra steps, and the other camp says no, the idealisation does real work because it filters out bias and incohearence. everyone's rehearsed the same moves: the collapse objection, the response that cohearence isn't cheap, the counter that it's still just a formal constraint. nobody shifts.

my own hunch, which might be wrong, is that the stall isn't about normativity at all but about two different things being smuggled under the label 'idealisation'. there's the epistemic layer — fixing beliefs under full information — and then there's the practical layer — fixing preferences under coherence conditions. in the standard story these run together, but they can come apart: you could be fully informed and still have incoherent preferences, or perfectly coherent about values built on false beliefs. so maybe the two sides are actually running different two-layer models and talking past each other? one side treats idealisation as mainly epistemic, the other as mainly practical, and they keep arguing about whether 'it' collapses without realising they're describing different procedues.

is that the right diagnosis, or is there a deeper substantive disagreement hiding underneath? are they answering different questions — one about belief-formation, one about choice — under the single heading of indirect normativity? and what would actually move things: a formal result showing the layers can't be separated, a conceptual distinction that forces them apart, or is the whole framework just ill-posed? please answer in british english, and don't worry about giving me a bibliography — i just want the diagnosis and what progress would look like.
