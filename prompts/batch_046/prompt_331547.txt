Two colleagues in a philosophy of logic seminar—call them Mira and Jonas—disagreed about the force of Lewis’s triviality results for indicative conditionals. (1) Mira argued that the derivation is decisive against Stalnaker’s thesis: if P(if A then B) equals P(B|A) and conditional beliefs sit in the same precise probability space as beliefs about A and B, then for any C independent of A and B one gets P(A→B) = P(B), which collapses the conditional into an unconditional. (2) Jonas replied that the proof only works under a “flat” credence structure; he proposed a layered model in which first-order world-beliefs and second-order conditional-link beliefs live on separate tiers, so the independence premises fail across tiers and Stalnaker’s thesis survives at the first-order layer. (3) Mira countered that this layering is ad hoc unless it yields different betting behavior, and she pressed Jonas on why, if the tiers are genuinely separate, learning A should ever change one’s credence in A→B at all. (4) Jonas answered that cross-tier updating still occurs, but via a Jeffrey-style rule rather than standard Bayesian conditioning, which means the premises of Lewis’s proof never apply across the boundary, though he conceded that specifying the exact rule is unfinished work. They turned to me as a bystander and asked me to referee. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move—rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification, say who is right about what, and note what both are missing. State how confident you are in your adjudication and single out the specific point about which you are least certain.
