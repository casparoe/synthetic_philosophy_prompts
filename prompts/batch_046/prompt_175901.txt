At a coffee shop I overheard two grad students arguing about virtue ethics after one mentioned she was writing on Aristotle for a seminar. The first one, I think her name was Maya, said the situationist critique basically kills virtue ethics because if people don't have stable cross-situational character traits then talking about "the virtuous person" is just bad psychology. She brought up Milgram and the good samaritan study and said if your behavior is mostly driven by situation then virtues aren't real dispositions, they're just post-hoc stories we tell. The other student, Leo I think, pushed back and said that's a strawman of virtue ethics — Aristotle never said virtues are context-independent robots, he said they involve practical wisdom which is literally about reading situations correctly. Leo said the situationist data just shows people lack practical wisdom in extreme cases, not that virtue doesn't exist. Maya came back with okay but if there's no statistical consistency in trait expression across contexts then "having the virtue of courage" is empirically empty — you can't predict behavior and that's what a scientific concept needs. Leo replied that prediction isn't the point, virtue is a normative ideal and also that recent replication crises in social psych weaken the empirical attack anyway. Maya said even weak situationism undermines the idea that we should cultivate character rather than just redesign environments, which was her real worry. They kept going but eventually asked me to weigh in since I'm taking the ethics course, but I just said I need to think about it. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall?
