In my graduate seminar on comparative moral failure, we have reached an impasse that mirrors debates in the literature but feels more concrete and less tractable. The question is whether ranking historical evils—the Rwandan genocide, the Atlantic slave trade, the Holodomor, the Belgian Congo atrocities—is conceptually coherent or inherently obscene. One faction holds that comparison is not only permissible but obligatory for reparative justice, provided we construct what they call an index of irreplaceable harm that weights mortality, cultural annihilation, and intergenerational trauma without reducing them to a single currency. The opposing faction insists that each atrocity produces a singular moral residue: a form of wrong that is structurally incomparable because the victim community’s relationship to the harm is non-substitutable. Both sides concede the full historical record, both condemn the events unreservedly, and yet neither has moved in two semesters. My working theory is that this is not a dispute about empirical magnitude or about the moral weight of suffering per se, but a hidden disagreement about whether moral reasoning requires transitivity across qualitatively different categories of harm—essentially, whether comparison presupposes a metric that the second faction regards as conceptually impossible. Is that diagnosis correct? Or is this a verbal dispute about the word ranking, with one side meaning ordinal assessment for institutional policy and the other meaning metaphysical equivalence? Alternatively, are they answering different questions—one about institutional accountability, one about the phenomenology of victimhood—under a single heading? I want a diagnosis grounded in what each position actually commits to, not a survey of the literature. Specifically, tell me whether there is a substantive disagreement here about the logic of moral comparison, whether it turns on an empirical question we could in principle resolve (for instance, about the commensurability of trauma metrics), or whether it rests on bedrock intuitions about uniqueness that neither side can argue for. And given your diagnosis, what would actually move this debate? A formal result about incommensurability? A conceptual distinction between ordinal and cardinal comparison in ethics? Or should we conclude that the comparative framing is ill-posed and abandon it altogether? Be direct about whether progress is possible here.
