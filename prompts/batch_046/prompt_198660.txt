I have been thinking about the familiar contrast between German folk morality — with its emphasis on Ordnung, rule-following, and the integrity of systems — and American folk morality, which tends to privilege individual conscience and situational judgement over codified norms. In discussions of which has the better of it, I keep seeing two analogies do heavy lifting. The German orientation is likened to a layered network-protocol stack: each normative rule (traffic obedience, tax filing, recycling discipline) functions like a layer that assumes the reliability of the layers beneath it, so that breaking a surface rule is not merely a local error but a corruption of the entire transmission pathway. The American orientation, by contrast, is likened to a peer-to-peer mesh network: each moral agent broadcasts its own values and negotiates local agreements, treating formal rules as cached consensus that can be overridden when direct moral signalling — compassion, fairness, personal conviction — demands it. I want a precise audit of both analogies: map the correspondences explicitly, identify where the technical properties of protocol stacks and mesh networks fail to match the social phenomena, expose what each analogy quietly imports about the value of the whole versus the autonomy of the parts, and determine whether either analogy actually supports the normative conclusion that one culture’s approach is superior. In particular, does the stack analogy smuggle in a false assumption that all rules are interdependent in the same way, and does the mesh analogy conceal the costs of coordination failure? If one mapping collapses more severely than the other, does that tell us anything about the underlying moral psychology, or only about our taste for architectural metaphors? Please say what would change your mind: which empirical finding about rule-compliance or moral reasoning, or which disanalogy you regard as fatal, would lead you to reject one of these mappings or to reverse your assessment of which folk morality has the stronger claim.
