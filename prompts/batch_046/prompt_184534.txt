I'm prepping for a seminar next week and need to push back on this anology I keep hearing in my philosophy of tech course: people compare autonomous decision systems to corporate persons. The idea is that since a corporation can be fined for antitrust violations without anyone inside having a guilty mental state, we should be able to hold an AI logistics system responsible for misallocating medical supplies during a crisis without locating a conscious intent inside the code. They use this to argue that responsibility gaps aren't real gaps -- they're just distributed the same way liability is in a firm, so we can fill them by assigning agent-status to the machine.

Can you audit how far this holds? I want an explicit mapping -- what's the corporation's board of directors in the AI case, what's the shareholder interest, what's the legal fiction of personhood, and where does each of those correspond to weights, loss functions, or human operators? Then separate the disanalogies that actually undermine the conclusion from the ones that don't: for instance, corporations can be dissolved or have boards replaced, but an embedded model can't be fired in the same structural sense, and I think that matters, though I'm not totally sure why. Finally, take a definite position: does the corporate-person analogy survive once these breaks are on the table, and if not, is there a better analogy -- maybe from distributed cognition or ecological networks -- that captures shared accountability without importing human-like agency? Please don't just lay out options; I want you to commit to wether the gap gets filled and what analogy should replace it. I'm especially worried about that board-replacement point and wether it actually breaks the comparison or is just a red herring.
