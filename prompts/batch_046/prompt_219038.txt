During a recent graduate seminar on practical rationality, two colleagues—Maya and Jonas—debated whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational. Maya began by insisting that rational decision-making is necessarily forward-looking: resources already expended are causally inert, so continuing a failing endeavor solely because of prior investment is a clear reasoning error, and only anticipated future benefits and costs should matter. Jonas objected that this assumes every commitment is purely instrumental, and he introduced the idea of "constitutive sunk costs"—cases in which past labor has become woven into the very identity of the project, so that abandoning it now would not merely waste earlier effort but dissolve an ongoing commitment whose present value derives partly from its history, comparable to maintaining a long friendship not for future utility but because the shared past is now part of what the relationship is. Maya replied that Jonas was merely relabeling emotional attachment or status-quo bias: if the project's future prospects are genuinely poor, invoking the past as a present reason is precisely the fallacy in disguise, and calling it "constitutive" adds rhetorical cover without logical substance. Jonas then pressed that Maya was smuggling in a contested premise—that all practical reasons must be future-directed—whereas promises and institutional roles generate present obligations precisely in virtue of past commitments; by her logic, keeping a burdensome promise to a friend would also be irrational once its costs outweigh its benefits. Maya granted that promises involve obligations to others, but argued that self-directed projects lack that intersubjective structure, so past investment in one's own work remains a psychological trap unless it serves some identifiable future good; she added that without this boundary we could never justify abandoning failing careers or artistic programs. Jonas concluded that some self-directed endeavors—such as a scholar's lifelong research program—do generate obligations to one's past self, and that without allowing past commitment to supply present reasons, perseverance through difficulty becomes inexplicable, undermining sustained long-term agency itself. They turned to me for adjudication.

Please adjudicate their exchange round by round: evaluate Maya's opening claim, Jonas's reply about constitutive costs, Maya's relabeling objection, Jonas's promise analogy together with Maya's distinction between other-directed and self-directed commitments, and then state who is more right overall and identify what both miss about the conditions under which past investment becomes genuine present value rather than mere bias. Present your answer as exactly five numbered items, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
