I am examining whether "national character" denotes anything coherent, and whether generalizations about entire peoples can ever be epistemically or ethically defensible. In surveying arguments on this question, I have repeatedly encountered a specific rhetorical pattern that I lack a precise name for, and I am trying to determine whether it represents a recognized failure mode. Consider two typical cases. First, a writer claims that members of society N possess a deep-seated cultural reluctance to delegate authority. When challenged with well-documented counterexamples—prominent hierarchical institutions within N, or influential individuals who actively seek centralized power—the writer modifies the claim: not that every member exhibits the reluctance, but that the distribution of attitudes is genuinely skewed in that direction relative to other societies. The writer treats this statistical reformulation as having answered the objection. Yet in a subsequent explanation of a particular political crisis or administrative decision, the same writer invokes the reluctance not as a population-level tendency requiring aggregation to apply, but as a direct causal factor explaining why that specific leader or institution behaved as it did. A second case concerns the assertion that people of society M are unusually oriented toward long-term planning. Under criticism, the claim is softened to a measurable difference in average time-preference across the population. But in historical explanation, it is redeployed to account for why one particular dynasty or corporation made a specific long-range investment, as though the population mean were an operative mechanism in that single instance rather than a comparative statistical property. In both cases, the speaker does not substitute a different, weaker claim for the original strong one; the same conceptual content is defended with a probabilistic or distributional qualifier and then applied with a deterministic or essentialist qualifier. This is not precisely the motte-and-bailey fallacy, since there is no clear separation between a defensible core and an indefensible extension—the statistical and the causal formulations are presented as two faces of one and the same claim. Nor is it straightforward ecological fallacy, because the speaker is not necessarily inferring an individual property from a group average in a purely logical error; rather, they oscillate between treating the group property as statistical and treating it as causally generative for individuals, using whichever framing suits the argumentative context. Is there an established concept—in the philosophy of social science, in the literature on stereotyping, or in informal logic—that captures this exact oscillation? If no established term exists, I would welcome a coined concept together with a precise definition and boundary conditions that distinguish it from the motte-and-bailey, from ecological fallacy, and from ordinary overgeneralization. I am especially interested in whether this pattern is peculiar to debates about national character, or whether it represents a more general structural defect in any group-level attribution.
