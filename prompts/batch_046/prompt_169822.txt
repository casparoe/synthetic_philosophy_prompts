I am designing a graduate seminar module on psychiatric classification and Ian Hacking's looping effects, but I find the usual realism-versus-social-construction binary too coarse for the discussion I want to have. To sharpen things, I have introduced a distinction—borrowed loosely from systems-thinking literature but applied specifically to taxonomy—between first-order looping, in which individuals classified under a diagnostic category alter their self-conception or conduct in response to that label, and second-order looping, in which the classification criteria themselves are revised by committees or clinicians precisely because the population being classified has shifted under the influence of the original criteria. I would like a decision tree, intended for philosophy-of-psychiatry students, that sorts philosophical stances on whether psychiatric taxonomies retain epistemic validity when both orders of looping are simultaneously active.

The tree should open with a concrete diagnostic commitment that does not presuppose philosophical literacy: for example, whether one accepts that the prevalence, symptom profile, and clinical presentation of conditions such as dissociative identity disorder or attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder have changed over successive editions of major nosologies partly as a consequence of how those categories were applied and internalised. From there, branch through three layers of questions. The first layer should concern ontological status: does the respondent treat mental disorders as natural kinds, as practical kinds shaped by human interests, or as interactive kinds that co-evolve with the people they classify? The second layer should address normative evaluation of looping itself: is first-order looping a harmful distortion of the taxonomy, a neutral by-product, or a valuable form of self-knowledge? The third layer should isolate second-order looping specifically—does revising criteria in response to a looped population constitute a virtuous feedback loop that improves clinical utility, or a vicious circularity that severs any link between the category and an independent reality?

Each leaf should correspond to a clearly characterised position—such as a strong medical realist who denies that genuine disorders are susceptible to looping; an interactive-kind realist who accepts first-order looping but rejects second-order revision as epistemically corrupt; a pragmatic nominalist who regards both orders as healthy taxonomy maintenance; and a radical constructivist for whom second-order looping demonstrates that the classification tracks nothing beyond its own history. Phrase every node as a direct question that a student could answer without already knowing which philosophical label it maps onto. Where combinations of answers appear unoccupied or conceptually unstable—perhaps a view that treats disorders as natural kinds yet celebrates second-order looping as refinement—flag them and offer a brief diagnosis of whether the gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect in the literature. Finally, note explicitly any pair of positions your tree cannot separate, such as certain forms of pragmatic realism and sophisticated constructivism that may converge on identical answers despite differing rhetoric, and specify the audience size expectation of roughly six to eight terminal leaves.
