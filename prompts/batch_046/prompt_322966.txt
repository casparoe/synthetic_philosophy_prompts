I am a high school student in an advanced civics and philosophy elective, and during a recent seminar on democratic innovation my classmates and I started debating whether prediction markets could ever replace traditional voting. Rather than asking about the standard version of this idea, I want to examine a specific hybrid institutional design that my instructor mentioned only briefly, and I am trying to figure out what philosophical assumptions it silently relies on.

Consider a municipal governance system—let us call it a modified futarchy—where citizens do not cast direct votes on proposed legislation. Instead, each resident receives a fixed allocation of non-transferable "forecast stakes" that they place on predicted outcomes of a given policy, measured across a composite index that combines near-term economic productivity, long-term ecological resilience, and estimated aggregate subjective well-being across both present and future generations. The policy with the highest predicted net improvement is automatically enacted, unless a randomly selected citizens' jury—compensated not in currency but in publicly recorded reputation capital—issues a veto grounded in a separate deliberative assessment of procedural fairness and distributive justice. The market mechanism is treated as the primary epistemic engine, while the jury functions solely as a moral brake.

What philosophical commitments about democratic legitimacy, collective knowledge, and the nature of value does this specific arrangement presuppose? Does it require a sharp separation between epistemic authority and moral authority, such that predicting outcomes and judging fairness belong to fundamentally different kinds of institutional reasoning? Or does it implicitly depend on a unified theory of value that treats economic, ecological, and psychological welfare as fully commensurable on a single scale? Furthermore, is the practice consistent with the view that democratic authority derives from the affected interests of all citizens—including those not yet born—or does it quietly presuppose a presentist or aggregative-utilitarian framework in which future people appear only as variables in a prediction function? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value and knowledge that makes this design coherent, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely happens to fit it well, and indicate whether the same institutional structure could be sustained under rival philosophical commitments—such as a deontological prohibition on treating persons as prediction targets, or a pluralist view that rejects the trade-off of incommensurable goods.
