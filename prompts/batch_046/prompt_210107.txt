Two members of a translation-studies seminar have been arguing about whether formal preservation in verse translation is a meaningful achievement or an empty exercise, using Clément Marot’s “Ma mignonne” and Douglas Hofstadter’s attempts to render it into English while retaining rhyme, metre, and syllable count.

(1) The first argued that preserving the rhyme scheme and syllabic regularity is essential, not decorative: the poem’s tenderness is carried by rhythmic predictability, and a free translation sacrifices part of the emotional content along with the form.

(2) The second replied that English syllable stress and phonetic material are structurally different; forcing Marot’s regularity into English produces doggerel, and Hofstadter’s versions demonstrate this precisely — they are ingenious puzzles rather than functioning poems, because the preserved form is an empty shell stripped of its original acoustic substance.

(3) The first countered that form is not purely phonetic: it includes syntactic parallelism and stanzaic architecture that reveal semantic patterns invisible in free translation. She added that recent work in constrained poetics suggests formal constraints generate meaning rather than merely containing it, and that mnemonic functions of rhyme in early modern verse make formal preservation cognitively, not just aesthetically, significant.

(4) The second said this redefines form to include semantics, rendering the claim tautological. He insisted that if the constraint can only be met through severe lexical distortion — as Hofstadter’s contorted diction shows — then preservation fails on its own terms, and that appeals to mnemonic rhyme remain speculative without empirical support.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then identify what, if anything, both parties miss about whether formal preservation can be assessed independently of the linguistic material in which it is realised.
