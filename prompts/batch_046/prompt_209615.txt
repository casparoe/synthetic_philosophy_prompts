Give an example of a conceptual issue that emerges in the causal exclusion argument about mental causation when one adopts a non-reductive emergentist framework that permits downward causation, but only if the mental state's causal efficacy is construed relationally rather than intrinsically. The example should illustrate not the familiar overdetermination problem—where mental and physical causes compete for the same effect—but a more subtle circularity: the physical substrate's causal closure conditions depend for their specification on the very mental states they are meant to exclude, so that defending mental causation by appealing to downward influence ends up making the substrate's closure conditions metaphysically parasitic on the mental. In constructing the example, make clear why treating mental causation as causally relevant but not causally productive fails to break the circle without collapsing mental properties into purely descriptive or epiphenomenal roles.
