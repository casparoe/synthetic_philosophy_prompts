Below is a draft essay for a discussion group that has worked through Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot and the broader problem of translation under formal constraint. Rather than rehearsing the familiar observation that rhyme and meter are difficult to preserve, I want to argue that formal constraints are partly constitutive of a poem's identity—so that translating Marot's poem Ma mignonne does not preserve the original object but creates a successor work that stands in family resemblance to it. The translator is therefore less a courier of content than a co-author of a new language-specific artifact, and talk of the same poem in another language may be as confused as talk of the same painting rendered on an entirely different canvas with different pigments and proportions.

I am asking for comments on three points. First, does this reading misrepresent Hofstadter's own aims in the essay—does he treat formal preservation as proof that the poem survives intact, or is he implicitly conceding that every successful version is a replacement rather than a transmission? Second, is the ontological claim defensible—that poems are not abstract structures that can be re-instantiated across languages but are tied to specific acoustic and syntactic configurations—or does it smuggle in an aesthetic theory about performative identity that it never explicitly defends? Third, is the painting analogy apt for what translation produces, or would a different comparison better capture the relation between an original poem and its formally constrained successors? I am especially interested in whether the argument collapses once we distinguish sharply between a poem's semantic content and its performative or acoustic identity, since that distinction might rescue a weaker but more defensible version of preservation without committing to the stronger claim that cross-language identity is impossible.
