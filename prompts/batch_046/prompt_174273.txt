I spent the better part of last evening arguing with a close friend about whether there is a coherent principle behind our intuition that certain exchanges feel morally wrong even when both parties consent and no direct harm is intended. We kept circling back to three cases he insisted belong together: selling kidneys, charging extortionate prices for essential goods during emergencies, and buying votes. Each time I tried to separate them—suggesting that kidney sales save lives, that price gouging is merely unfair pricing rather than a category mistake, and that vote-selling undermines democratic equality in a structurally different way—he would fall back on the line, "There are things money shouldn't buy." I pressed him on what exactly that meant, and he offered a more specific framing I want to test: he said the wrong is not merely about bad outcomes or unfair distribution, but about "constitutive corruption," where introducing a price mechanism does not just allocate a good badly but replaces the internal norms that define what the good is—citizenship for votes, solidarity for emergency aid, perhaps bodily integrity for organs—while other goods, like plasma or labor, survive pricing intact because they lack those defining norms. I am not convinced his framing actually covers all three cases uniformly, and I suspect the slogan is doing far more work than his distinction allows.

Can you take the slogan "There are things money shouldn't buy" and lay out, as a precise menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct claims it could be expressing? For each reading, indicate whether it is a near-platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, a false overgeneralization, or something that collapses into a different moral complaint altogether. In particular, identify which reading corresponds to my friend's "constitutive corruption" account, and assess whether that reading genuinely groups kidney sales, price gouging, and vote-selling together or whether it forces an artificial unity onto cases that invoke different wrongs—distributive injustice, exploitation of vulnerability, and degradation of democratic standing, respectively. I am not looking for a bibliography, a survey of named philosophers, or a reading list; I want a conceptual dissection of the language itself, with verdicts on which precisification could actually do the argumentative work my friend was asking it to do.
