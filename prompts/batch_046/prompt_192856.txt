My advisor's rulings on identity puzzles in our metaphysics seminar have followed a clear but puzzling pattern. She holds that Black's two spheres in an otherwise empty symmetric universe are numerically distinct, thereby rejecting a strong Principle of Identity of Indiscernibles. When two atoms are interchanged in an otherwise qualitatively identical world, she insists they remain the same atoms by virtue of their continuous spatiotemporal trajectories, not merely by relational position. Yet in the quantum case of two fermions in a singlet state with identical intrinsic properties and no definite trajectories, she denies that they are individually identifiable, treating the system as a single two-particle state rather than two individuals. Finally, in a cosmic duplication scenario in which every object and relation in our universe is replaced by a perfect qualitative duplicate arranged in the same structural pattern, she rules that there is no metaphysical fact of the matter about whether the duplicate is the same universe or a different one.

For next week's session I have designed a limiting case: two macroscopic bodies that occupy precisely the same spatiotemporal region and share every qualitative property and every external relation, existing instead in causally decoupled layers of reality that never interact, so that no observation can distinguish them, though each layer apparently contains a fully structured object with its own internal parts. Predict whether she will count this as one sphere or two. Identify the implicit rule—spatiotemporal essentialism, primitive haecceity, or structuralist anti-individuality—best explaining her earlier verdicts, say what each predicts here, state which interpretation you favor and why, and specify the single further case that would adjudicate between your two leading candidates.
