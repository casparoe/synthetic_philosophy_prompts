I've been going back and forth with a study group about this Platonia setup — twenty people, each in a seperate room, can send a telegram reverse-charges, but only one should send it or nobody gets the billion — and that Luring Lottery Scientific American ran back in the 80s, where readers mailed postcards with numbers and the million-dollar prize got split by however many entries there were. Our debate keeps stalling in this frustrating loop: I think both cases show rationality has to involve evidential correlation, since in Platonia you randomize with prob 1/20 even with no causal link to the other guy, and in the Lottery the superrational move is to roll a die with sides matching expected responders and send a '1'. My friend says that's just cooperative preference smuggled in, and points to the actual results — people sent googolplex-sized entries, ruined the prize, nobody actualy rolled the die — so it's descriptive fantasy, not normative rationality. So we circle: is this about evidential vs causal decision theory, or is it that I'm asking what we should do while he's asking what people do?

There's also this weird middle position I ran into on a forum — they call it 'indexical correlation' — that says the analysis depends on whether you treat the other agent as an indexical copy of yourself or as an independant draw from the population. Does that break the stall, or just rename the split? I want a diagnosis, not a winner. Is the disagreement verbal, is there a hidden empirical claim about human reasoning we're not making explicit, or are we answering different questions under the single word 'rationality'? And if your diagnosis is right, what would actualy count as progress — a formal result, an experiment, or just agreeing to talk about two different puzzles?
