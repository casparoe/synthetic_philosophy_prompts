As a journalist covering formal social choice and collective reasoning, I have been tracking the decades-long impasse over judgment aggregation—specifically the discursive dilemma, in which majority judgments on premises and on a conclusion can jointly violate logical consistency. Defenders of premise-based aggregation treat collective rationality as a non-negotiable constraint: a group that reasons inconsistently fails to count as a single belief-forming agent. Defenders of conclusion-based aggregation reply that democratic legitimacy requires honoring the majority’s direct verdict on the outcome, even if the group’s implicit reasoning is incoherent. The formal result is not in dispute; the normative disagreement is. Is this stalemate substantive—perhaps about hidden commitments regarding whether groups genuinely possess beliefs and intentions, as debates in collective intentionality suggest—or is it largely verbal, with each camp using “rationality” and “legitimacy” to name different normative standards? Might the two sides actually be answering distinct questions—one about the epistemic architecture of collective reasoning, the other about the authority of democratic outcomes—under the shared heading of “aggregation rules”? I want a precise diagnosis grounded in what each side actually argues, not a survey of the literature, together with an honest assessment of whether anything—a formal extension of the impossibility result, a conceptual distinction such as that between procedural coherence and outcome accountability, or a reframing of collective agency—could genuinely move the debate, or whether the disagreement rests on bedrock intuitions about group minds that no theorem can adjudicate.
