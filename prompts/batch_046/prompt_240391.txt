For the closing session of my graduate seminar on agency and moral responsibility, I have been working through Galen Strawson's Basic Argument—the regress according to which no agent can be the true originator of her actions, since being responsible for an act requires being responsible for the psychological nature that produced it, and being responsible for that nature requires being responsible for the prior conditions that shaped it, ad infinitum without a terminus. Over coffee yesterday, a colleague remarked that the argument feels less like a linear proof and more like a tightening loop: the harder you pull on the thread of authorship, the more you discover it anchored in something prior that was never yours to choose. Rather than ending with a conventional discussion, I would like to present a poem that renders this regress structurally visible rather than merely describing it. Please write a pantoum—the form of interlocking quatrains in which lines recur and shift position like returning obligations—of exactly four stanzas. The speaker should be attempting to maintain a ledger of origins, tracing each character trait (ambition, moral sensitivity, temper) back to its causal source—heredity, environment, an earlier choice—only to find that every entry records a debt owed to a prior condition for which she is not ultimately responsible. Use the form's mechanical repetition to enact the infinite push-back: each time a line returns, it should carry the speaker one step further back in the causal chain, never reaching an origin that belongs to her alone. The poem should not resolve; let the closing lines leave the loop open, reflecting Strawson's conclusion that true moral responsibility, in the sense of being causa sui, is unavailable to beings constituted as we are. The diction should be precise enough that a philosopher of action recognizes the regress and the distinction between being the cause of one's actions and being the cause of oneself, yet compressed enough that it works as verse for an audience encountering the idea for the first time. I am asking this specifically for a seminar closing, so the tone should be contemplative rather than sermonizing, and please avoid any bibliographic apparatus or citation—the poem must stand entirely on its own.
