I have been observing a particular pattern in how people deploy inference to the best explanation, and I wonder whether the philosophy of science literature assigns it a specific name. The pattern runs as follows. When evaluating two competing explanations for some phenomenon, a reasoner rejects the objectively simpler or more unifying explanation not because it conflicts with the data, but because accepting it would require revising some background commitment—say, a long-held theoretical framework or a methodological principle—that is not itself part of the explanatory target. The reasoner treats that background commitment as a fixed constraint, as if the explanation must be selected from within the space it defines, rather than allowing the commitment to be weighed in the balance alongside the explanation. I am not referring to ordinary theoretical conservatism or to Quine’s web of belief, because in those cases the background beliefs are genuinely part of a holistic assessment; here, the background belief is shielded from evaluation altogether, functioning more like a boundary condition on admissible explanations. Does this shielding have an established label—perhaps a variant of auxiliary-hypothesis protection, or a recognized failure mode of abductive reasoning? If there is no established term, how exactly should one distinguish it from ordinary conservatism and from the Duhem–Quine problem, and what would be an apt name and precise definition for it, including its boundary cases?
