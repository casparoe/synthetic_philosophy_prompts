I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training, and I've observed a recurring asymmetry in how people reason about identity across biological and artificial domains. In conversations about human origins, people often insist that had the particular sperm and egg that formed a given person been different, that very individual would never have come to exist—implying that specific genetic provenance is essential to being that person. Yet the same intuition is not applied to complex artifacts. If I rebuild a container from an identical Dockerfile and dependency tree, but with a different base-layer digest or temporary build artifacts, we regard it as the same software instance; only the "genesis" differs, and no one treats that difference as producing a numerically distinct program. Even with biological clones, people seem less inclined to claim that a clone with identical DNA but a different cellular origin is "not the same person" in the way they claim about gamete substitution.

The pattern I want to name is not the biological claim alone, but its selective application: treating initial biological conditions as identity-constitutive for organisms while treating analogous initial conditions as incidental for functionally equivalent systems. Is there an established philosophical concept that captures this specific origin-essential intuition, particularly its restriction to biological individuals? If the nearest concepts are broader—say, essentialism about origins in general, or biological exceptionalism—please specify how they differ from the selective asymmetry I'm describing. And if no established term fits precisely, please define the phenomenon with clear boundary conditions: what distinguishes it from mere causal continuity, from identity conditions based on functional structure, and from the claim that all entities depend essentially on every part of their causal history?
