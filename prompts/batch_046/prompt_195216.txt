hey so i'm a retired physicist who got bored in retirement and started reading political philosophy, and i stumbeled onto this lottocracy idea -- picking lawmakers by lottery instead of elections. the usual argument is that a random sample is more representitive and avoids campaign coruption. but i think there's a hidden problem that comes straight from how i used to think about noisy dynamical systems.

my position: sortition is actually worse for collective decision-making than election, not because the sample is unrepresentative (the law of large numbers handles mean properties fine), but because legislative deliberaton is a nonlinear, path-dependent process -- like a catalytic reaction where a few agents with the right competence steer the whole trajectory. elections act as a fliter for those catalytic individuals. random selection injects variance that doesn't average away; instead it derails the path and destroys the conditions for good convergence. so the epistemic case for lottocracy collapses once you model deliberation as nonlinear dynamics rather than linear opinion aggregation.

what would actually count against this? i want the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be -- formal results, empirical studies of citizens assemblies, historical cases, maybe intuition pumps about catalysis failing in social settings. also, has anyone already qualified this "catalytic competence" story into unfalsifiability, like saying any failure of sortition is just bad implimentation or poor facilitation? and if it has been insulated that way, is that insulation a real problem for a view of this kind?

personally i'd be most moved by a proof that deliberation is basically additive/linear, or by a concrete case where a lottery-selected body solved a hard coordination problem that an elected body botched. please be honest about whether my view risks anything at all, and don't just send me to chase citations i can't verify without a library.
