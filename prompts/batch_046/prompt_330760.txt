I have been trying to understand why history education works so differently across countries, and I keep coming back to two patterns that seem philosophically strange. In Japan and South Korea, state panels approve public-school history textbooks and regularly require revisions to passages about Japan's colonial rule—particularly regarding forced labor and the comfort women system—so that the final versions match each government's official narrative. Meanwhile, in parts of the United States, school boards have begun requiring American-history courses to present the framing that treats 1619 as the nation's true founding and the framing that treats 1776 as the true founding as parallel, equally authoritative lenses, testing students on both without instructing them to resolve the conflict.

What puzzles me is that both practices seem to treat historical disagreement not as a problem for evidence to solve, but as something to be administratively balanced or officially fixed. What view of historical truth does that institutional design presuppose? Must it be a form of relativism—where past events have no authority independent of a state's decision—or can it be sustained by a realist who simply thinks citizens need exposure to competing narratives for democratic stability? And what does it presuppose about the purpose of public education and about a nation's right to shape its collective memory: that social cohesion takes precedence over accuracy, or that there are multiple legitimate national identities embedded in the same past?

Please reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory behind these textbook practices. Distinguish what the design strictly requires—say, a particular account of historical knowledge, of pedagogical authority, or of intergenerational moral responsibility—from what merely fits it well but could be dropped. Then tell me whether the same institutional structure could be justified by rival theories, such as a pragmatic conflict-avoidance view or a liberal-pluralist view of viewpoint diversity. Finally, imagine the underlying commitment were replaced by a view that treats historical claims as fully answerable to evidence and independent of state interest; describe the concrete changes that would have to follow in textbook approval, exam design, and teacher training.
