A philosophy instructor I work with has delivered consistent verdicts on what Descartes's cogito argument licenses across five formulations. She grants that it establishes bare existence and the occurrence of thought; she grants that it establishes thinking as the essential attribute of the thinker; she denies that it establishes substance dualism or that the thinker is essentially non-physical; she grants that it yields immediate, non-inferential knowledge of one's own existence independent of bodily knowledge; but she denies that it establishes complete transparency of the mind to itself, and she denies that it proves the self is a simple, indivisible soul rather than a functionally composite system. She will now evaluate a modified case: an artificial processing system that outputs a self-referential report of thinking whenever it executes a specific introspection routine, lacks biological substrate, maintains no continuous phenomenal stream, and cannot access its own architecture except through that same routine. Would she judge that this system has achieved cogito-style certainty about its own existence? I am less interested in the bare answer than in identifying the competing principles—perhaps a minimalist existence rule, an essential-nature rule, or an epistemic-access rule—that all accommodate her prior five rulings, predicting which principle governs her verdict on the artificial case, stating what further case—such as a distributed system with no single locus of report, or a human subject under temporary global amnesia—would distinguish the rules, and noting which prior ruling is the closest analog.
