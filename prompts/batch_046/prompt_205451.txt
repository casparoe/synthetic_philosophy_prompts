I'm writing a feature on the conceptual architecture of data protection for a science-and-ideas publication, and I'm trying to reconstruct the implicit theory behind a little-known but influential European tribunal's published rulings on privacy over the past decade. Their verdicts have followed a striking and somewhat puzzling pattern. They permitted a municipal smart-meter program that collected granular household energy-use patterns, on the condition that the data be aggregated at the neighborhood level and never linked back to individual dwellings. They blocked a corporate wellness initiative that required employees to wear biometric monitors during off-duty hours, rejecting it despite explicit written consent from every participant. They permitted a major hospital to share fully anonymized patient records with a pharmaceutical consortium for large-scale drug-efficacy research. They rejected a municipal proposal to install predictive-policing cameras in residential neighborhoods after discovering that the algorithm's training data incorporated socioeconomic proxies likely to reproduce discrimination. And they held that a social-networking platform's default privacy setting—which made users' location history visible to their contacts unless each user actively disabled the feature—violated a substantive right to informational seclusion, even though the raw data never left the company's own servers.

Now the tribunal must decide a proposal for what its designers term an ambient emotional-modeling service: an artificial-intelligence system installed in consenting private homes that listens continuously to domestic conversation, not in order to record, store, or transmit any audio, but to generate a real-time emotional-state vector used solely to adjust lighting, temperature, and ambient music, with all raw acoustic input discarded immediately after processing and the emotional-state profile retained only on a local device never connected to external networks. Every household has given explicit informed consent, and the firm promises that no vector will ever be shared, sold, or used for advertising. If the tribunal wants to remain consistent with its own track record, how should it rule? Which of its earlier decisions is the closest analog?

Please respond in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as you might in a thoughtful letter or email. I want you to identify the two or three plausible interpretive rules that jointly explain their earlier verdicts—perhaps one that treats privacy as a matter of individual control and informed consent, one that treats it as contextual integrity governing what is appropriate within specific spheres of life, and one that treats it as protection against inferential or predictive harm regardless of whether data is actually recorded or shared. For each rule, state clearly what it predicts about this ambient listening proposal. Then offer your best guess at the tribunal's actual verdict, explaining which rule you think best captures their implicit reasoning and why the others fit less well. Finally, describe one further hypothetical case—perhaps involving a non-auditory but behaviorally predictive system operating in a semi-public space such as a university library or a transit station—that would most sharply distinguish between the rules you have identified, and say exactly how each rule would come out on that case.
