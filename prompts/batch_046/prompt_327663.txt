I'm trying to figure out where the wrong-kind-of-reasons literature actually needs fresh work, not just more recylcing of the same Williams-style examples. Three angles feel underexplored to me: first, whether collective agents like corporations or states face a structurally different version of the problem when they adopt moral policies mainly for reputational cover; second, whether there's a mirror-image 'wrong kind of belief' issue in epistemology that gets ignored because people treat belief and action as separate domains; third, how this all interacts with moral encroachment—if stakes can shift what's rational to believe, does that blur the boundary between right and wrong reasons for acting?

Of these, which open lines actually matter for the core problem of moral motivation, and which are just side applications? I'm especially unsure about the second one—it feels like it might collapse WKRP into general rationality theory, but maybe that's wrong. Give me a defended ordering: what resolving each would change downstream, which ones are tractable without just doing more hair-splitting about 'motive' versus 'reason', and what would make you flip the ranking.
