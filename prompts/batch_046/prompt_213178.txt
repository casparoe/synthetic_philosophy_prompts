Please construct a rigorous argument for the following position: Berkeley's master argument—that one cannot conceive of an object existing unperceived—does not fail primarily because of the familiar counterexample in which a thinker imagines an unperceived tree. Rather, it fails because applying the argument consistently to distributed or collective cognition generates a social idealism that conflicts with Berkeley's ontology of simple, individual spirits. To develop this, examine a case in which different members of a scientific community perceive distinct temporal slices or spatial regions of a complex object, with no single person perceiving the whole, yet the community functionally tracks the object's persistence and structure. If the master argument demands that every aspect of the object be present to some perceiving mind for the object to exist, then either the collective counts as one perceiving mind—thereby committing Berkeley to a group-spirit ontology that violates his claim that spirits are essentially simple and indivisible—or the object lacks being despite being empirically tracked, which undermines the reliability of observation that Berkeley seeks to preserve. Your argument should anticipate Berkeley's probable response that only individual spirits are genuine perceivers, and should demonstrate why that response either presupposes the individualism that is at issue or erodes the distinction between direct and indirect perception on which his account of knowledge depends. Do not rely on bibliographic references or appeals to modern secondary literature; ground the reasoning in the internal structure of the master argument and Berkeley's commitments regarding the nature of minds.
