As a philosophy professor serving on the advisory board of a municipal memorial commission, I need a reusable decision procedure that my fellow commissioners—who lack philosophical training—can apply during our quarterly 90-minute adjudication sessions. The commission evaluates proposals for erecting, maintaining, renaming, or removing public monuments and commemorative practices tied to past collective injustices; our recommendations carry legal advisory weight for the city council, which retains veto authority but expects reasoned justification. Ground the procedure in Avishai Margalit's ethics of memory, specifically his distinction between thick moral relations that generate caring remembrance and the thinner politics of collective memory, and in David Rieff's case for strategic forgetting, including his warnings about memorial fixation, the instrumentalization of grievance, and the conditions under which letting the past recede is morally preferable to institutionalizing it. The deliverable should be a stepwise protocol—ordered diagnostic tests or decision nodes—that distinguishes cases where remembrance is ethically mandatory, permissible but not obligatory, or actively harmful, and that explicitly handles three hard cases: monuments that simultaneously commemorate victims and preserve evidence of perpetration; commemorative practices that risk re-traumatizing survivor communities versus those that build cross-group solidarity; and removal demands motivated by present political convenience rather than moral correction. Mark precisely where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment and requires an explicit commission vote with stated reasoning. Apply the protocol to three worked scenarios: one where remembrance is clearly required; one where strategic forgetting is warranted; and one where the procedure produces a nuanced recommendation—such as partial decommissioning with archival preservation—that will be objectionable to commissioners committed to either pure remembrance or pure erasure. Take a definite, committed stance on the central governing criterion—do not merely catalog options—and defend it against the strongest counter-argument drawn from the other theorist.
