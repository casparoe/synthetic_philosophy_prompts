I'm giving the opening remarks this saturday at the reopening of our old courthouse square — they finally tore down that wooden stockade thing where people used to get put on display for shaming in the 1800s, and replaced it with a circle garden they're calling the Repair Circle, where folks do community service and talk about what they broke instead of being seen. The mayor asked me to speak because I teach a bit on moral emotion, but half the crowd will be local historians who know every date about the stockade and half are social workers running the new guilt-and-repair program, so it can't sound like a lecture and it can't just cheer for progress. I want the speech to actually wrestle with whether guilt is genuinely better than shame, and whether a town can just decide to switch its moral emotion like flipping a switch — not just put shame in prettier packaging. My friend Marisol mentioned this idea she calls "chosen shame," where a culture picks which failures stay visible and which get hidden, and I don't fully understand it but maybe you can weave that in as part of the problem. About five minutes, please — and don't say anything false just to comfort people, but also don't leave the historians feeling like we're erasing what the square meant.
