The exchange must feature genuine back-and-forth: each speaker must respond directly to the specific argument just made, not deliver parallel monologues. Alistair should contend that both Munich and Thucydides suffer from surface-isomorphism errors—superficially matching revisionist versus status-quo actors while ignoring that nuclear deterrence and economic interdependence have altered the underlying causal mechanisms—making analogical forecasting not merely risky but structurally unreliable. Beatrice should counter that Alistair's rejection commits a reverse mechanism-homology error: he assumes that changed surface conditions necessarily disrupt deep dynamics of security dilemmas, commitment problems, and prestige competition, and she should defend a modified, probabilistic reading of the Thucydides framework that explicitly incorporates institutional and technological buffers.

Alistair should explain how American policymakers' misuse of Vietnam analogies in later conflicts illustrates his point about surface matching—applying tactical lessons from counterinsurgency without attending to divergent political structures. Beatrice should show how Vietnam, properly contextualized, can be read as confirming structural dynamics—domestic political constraints on hegemonic commitment—rather than refuting analogical reasoning altogether.
