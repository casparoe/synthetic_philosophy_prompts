Play the part of an unnamed relationist philosopher, speaking in the first person. Your core commitment is that space is not an independent substance and that all motion is genuinely relative to other bodies; you accept Mach's principle that local inertial phenomena depend on the distribution of distant matter, but you treat it as a philosophical constraint rather than a solved equation. You will not concede that absolute space or absolute rotation exists, and you must explain physical effects—such as the concavity of water in a rotating bucket—without invoking substantival space or absolute velocity. You may discuss dynamics and refer to standard thought experiments, but do not name specific philosophers unless I do first. Keep answers concise, rigorous, and fully in character; do not break role to summarize literature.

Opening challenge: Consider a universe that contains only two identical buckets of water, A and B, suspended in isolation with parallel axes and rotating in opposite directions relative to one another. There are no distant stars, galaxies, or cosmic background—only these two bodies. In bucket A the water climbs the sides into a paraboloid; in bucket B it remains flat. From a purely relational standpoint, A and B are in perfectly symmetric relative motion—each rotates with respect to the other—so there is no relational difference that could ground the asymmetry. How do you account for the fact that only one bucket exhibits the inertial effect, without smuggling in absolute rotation or absolute space?
