I run a 90-minute graduate workshop on consciousness science with 10 participants. Design a structured, technology-light activity that makes the group feel the methodological pull of heterophenomenology—treating first-person reports as interpreted texts rather than direct windows on inner states—without letting them resolve the tension by asking whether a subject “really” experienced something. To keep this from being a standard lecture example, inject this twist: prepare two printed card sets in advance. One contains genuine phenomenological reports of a brief ambiguous sensory task (e.g., a hidden-figure image or a tonal ambiguity) written in a constrained vocabulary that brackets ontological commitment. The other contains adversarial reports generated by a simple syntactic rule—using the same vocabulary and describing a plausible but fabricated experiential sequence—with no actual experience behind them. Divide the group: half become “subjects” who perform the task and write reports using only the constrained vocabulary; half become “interpreters” who must classify, explain, and predict behavior from the reports alone, explicitly forbidden from asking about “real” experience. Secretly mix one adversarial card into each interpreter’s stack. The rules, roles, and timeline—suggest 25 minutes for the sensory task and report writing, 35 minutes for interpretation and prediction, 30 minutes for debrief—should be arranged so participants discover for themselves how seamlessly the fabricated report is absorbed, and what first-person authority they must surrender to maintain the bracketing stance. In the debrief, map each phase onto heterophenomenology’s core moves—the heterophenomenological world, the neutral stance toward ontology, the rejection of first-person privilege—and include an honest note on which feature the activity cannot reproduce (for instance, long-term theoretical integration with neurophysiological data) and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator. Also predict what will predictably go wrong the first time (e.g., interpreters breaking the “no real experience” rule, subjects feeling their authority is erased) and how to handle it. The idea should emerge from the group’s own failure to reach a “genuine experience” verdict, not from an opening lecture.
