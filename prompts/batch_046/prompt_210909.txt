After a long argument with a friend last night about whether Rawls’s separateness-of-persons objection targets utilitarian aggregation itself or only a hidden metaphysics of the self, I need a speaker’s prep sheet for a seminar defense. You are an expert in moral philosophy and metaphysics of personal identity, familiar with Rawls, Parfit, and contemporary relational or narrative theories of the self. Think carefully and step by step: first brainstorm the objections each kind of listener will actually raise, then order them by how likely and damaging they are, then draft concise replies, and finally flag the hidden weakness in each reply.

My thesis: the separateness objection succeeds only against a “container” metaphysics that treats each person as a sealed vessel of utility. If we adopt a relational or narrative account of personal identity—where boundaries are porous, maintained by continuity of psychological and social relations rather than by metaphysical walls—then the objection dissolves not because aggregation is justified, but because the boundary conditions it requires for “separate lives” are unstable. I am not defending classic aggregate utility; I defend a threshold-sensitive prioritarian view that weights worse-off positions but permits trade-offs across what are, on this view, partially overlapping lives.

Audience and setting: next Thursday I present to our moral philosophy reading group. The room includes two committed Rawlsians who treat the separateness objection as axiomatic, a contractualist who believes any cross-personal comparison violates moral equality, a metaphysician of personal identity who favors four-dimensionalist or narrative-continuity accounts over bodily criteria, and several agnostics who have read Parfit but not deeply. They care about distributive justice, metaphysical coherence, and whether my position collapses into either standard utilitarianism or an implausible holism that erases individual dignity.

Please prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased in the vocabulary each audience member would use—Rawlsian language of fairness and the distinctness of persons, contractualist language of equal moral status and non-instrumentalization, metaphysical language of diachronic identity and boundary conditions. For each objection, tell me: whether it has real force, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, a concise reply suitable for a five-minute exchange, and the weakest point in that reply that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Include the objection I am most hoping nobody asks—the one that, if pressed, exposes the deepest tension in my position. Be explicit, for each line of defense, about what would change your mind: which argument or empirical finding about personal identity or distributive ethics would cause you to drop that reply and concede the point. Finally, note where your reasoning is most likely to fail—whether in the move from metaphysical porosity to normative permissibility, or elsewhere—so that I can see the fault lines clearly rather than present an overconfident front. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need answers that work without web access.
