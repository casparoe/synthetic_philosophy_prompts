I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm launching a recurring feature called "Above the Line" where we profile people who do things that feel way beyond what anyone could actually demand. But my editor and I keep fighting over the line — last week we nearly ran a story on a surgeon who stayed past shift to comfort a grieving family, then realized that's now in her hospital's palliative protocol, so is it heroism or just compliance? I need a hard, reusable procedre my three-person features desk can run in about 20 minutes per nomination. We see maybe two cases a week, I have to sign off personally, and none of us have taken philosphy since college, so don't give me a seminar — give me a flowchart in words with three ordered tests, clear pass/fail gates, and explicit spots where judgment can't be eliminated.

Turn the theory of supererogation into this operational checklist. First test: map the agent's specific role-obligation ceiling — not generic morality, but what their contract, license, or institutional role actually requires. Second: check whether the cost borne is non-transferable, meaning it can't be reimbursed or made whole without destroying the moral point (money usually fails, physical risk or irreversible moral injury usually passes). Third: run an institutional-failure screen — if the act only happened because an organization dropped a ball it should have caught, then it's compensatory, not supererogatory. Flag the exact breakpoints where the desk has to make a call — ambiguous hybrid roles, hidden psychological costs, informal norms that blur formal duties — and be honest about what the framework flat-out can't settle, like cases where the institution is partly responsible but not fully.

Then apply it to three nominations and take a definite stand, don't just lay out options. Case one: a firefighter pulls a dog from a burning building; animal rescue isn't in her formal contract but is in her union's informal norms, and she risks smoke inhalation. Case two: a teacher spends a big chunk of her own salary buying books for students in a district that refuses to fund libraries — it feels heroic but I think your procedure might kill it, and I need you to say so even if it's the answer I don't want. Case three: a software engineer refuses to deploy a buggy feature, gets fired, then publicly warns users — role ambiguity, career damage, and possible institutional quality-control failure all collide. Tell me exactly which come out yes, no, and messy middle, defend the calls with reasons, and include one worked example where the procedure forces a result that feels wrong but you stand by it. Don't pretend it's foolproof — tell me where it will misfire.
