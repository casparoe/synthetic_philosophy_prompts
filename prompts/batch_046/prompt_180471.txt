Draft the joint statement that a uniform social constructionist and a differentiated constructionist about race, gender, and money could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The uniformist holds that race, gender, and money are social kinds in essentially the same structural sense: each is constituted by collective intentionality, interactional or institutional practices, and normative functions, with no ontologically significant distinction among them in mode of construction or relation to material bases. The differentiated constructionist holds that while all three are genuinely socially constructed, they differ in their anchoring conditions, normative architecture, and relation to biological or material properties—money being an institutional kind governed by explicit constitutive rules, gender being an interactional kind sustained by implicit performative norms, and race being a hierarchical kind anchored in phenotypic perception but constituted primarily by systems of domination and subordination rather than by collective acceptance alone.

The document should contain: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims about social kinds in general, about each of the three kinds specifically, and about whether normative evaluation can be separated from descriptive ontology; (2) a disagreements table with precisely formulated disagreements, each phrased so that the uniformist and the differentiated constructionist would each accept the wording of their own position; (3) the cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party—focusing especially on whether institutional explicitness is necessary for kindhood, whether anchoring in biological or material properties undermines pure social constitution, and whether the normative evaluation of a kind can vary independently of its ontological structure; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case or thought experiment both sides agree bears on it, with each side's predicted resolution recorded in advance.

Make the formulations neither vacuous nor slanted. If part of the dispute turns out to be verbal—for instance, about whether the term social kind should be reserved for institutional kinds—identify which part and explain why. The statement should not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and it should treat the disagreement as constructive rather than adjudicated.
