Two friends of mine, Sam and Riley, got into an extended argument after a biology-and-philosophy seminar on whether people have essential origins. Sam opened with the familiar artifact analogy: if a particular table is essentially made from the hunk of wood it actually came from, then analogously you are essentially the product of the particular sperm and egg that actually formed your zygote; had different gametes fused, the result would be a different person with different DNA, just as a table built from different wood is a different table. Riley replied that people are not artifacts and that biological origins are far messier than the analogy allows. She pointed to tetragametic chimerism, in which a single individual’s body can contain cells tracing back to two distinct sperm-egg pairs—either because two embryos fused in utero or because dispermic fertilization contributed genetic material—so that for some actual people there is no single “the sperm and egg” pair that constitutes their origin. Sam countered that chimerism is a developmental aberration and that essential properties can tolerate abnormal cases; he said a table can have a replaced leg and still be that table, so a chimeric person can still essentially derive from one “true” origin even if another genome was absorbed. Riley pushed back that essentialism concerns what is necessary in every case, not what is typical; if there exists even one actual person for whom no singular gamete pair is necessary, then “coming from that specific sperm and egg” cannot be essential to being them. She added that even in ordinary cases “the egg” is not neatly bounded—mitochondrial replacement therapy means the egg’s cytoplasm and mitochondria can come from a donor, so the nuclear genome traces back to one cell but that cell’s full biological origin already involves multiple contributors. Sam conceded the mitochondrial point but insisted that personal identity tracks the nuclear genome, and that genome traces back to one zygote. Riley then noted that identical twins come from exactly the same zygote yet are different people, so a shared origin can yield distinct individuals; conversely, chimerism shows that distinct origins can yield one individual. Therefore, she argued, the link between origin and identity is broken in both directions. Sam responded by relocating “origin” from the gametes to the specific post-split developmental trajectory: twins diverge after the split, so their origins include different paths, which preserves essentialism once “origin” is understood developmentally rather than genetically. Riley said that this abandons the original claim—if “origin” now means “developmental trajectory,” then it no longer concerns sperm and egg at all, and Sam has tacitly given up origin essentialism as initially stated. They asked me to referee. Please go through their exchange round by round, rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification, say who is right about which specific point, and identify what both parties are missing—especially whether there is a coherent middle position that retains origin essentialism but redefines “origin” in a biologically serious way rather than treating it as either a single gamete pair or a vague trajectory. Do not just declare an overall winner; adjudicate the arguments as they actually unfold.
