You are advising a senior editor at a quarterly art journal who receives critical essays evaluating artworks the editor has not personally experienced — performances that have already closed, installations in remote venues, or ephemeral pieces witnessed only by the critic. The editor must decide, within about thirty minutes per essay and without an art-history doctorate, whether to publish an aesthetic judgment (e.g., that a work is “profound,” “derivative,” or “sublime”) that rests primarily on the critic’s testimony. Ground your answer in the philosophy of aesthetic testimony — especially the acquaintance principle, the autonomy of aesthetic judgment, and the conditions under which epistemic deference is legitimate — but do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.

Design a reusable editorial procedure: an ordered sequence of tests or questions the editor can run on any essay, specifying what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where the procedure stops with a mandatory rejection or publication flag, where it demands direct verification (and what, if anything, can substitute for first-person experience), and where the decision must remain an explicit judgment call because the theory offers no algorithmic answer. Be explicit about the time constraint, the editor’s limited technical expertise, and the reputational stakes that require a sign-off before publication.

In constructing the procedure, incorporate the distinction between propositional aesthetic belief (that a work has a determinable property P) and experiential aesthetic understanding (grasping what it is like to encounter the work), and apply a stricter standard to comparative judgments that invoke canonical works the editor knows well. Then apply the procedure to three essays:

(1) A critic claims that a recently dismantled sound installation was “sublime in its austerity,” offering only a technical description and a brief affective vocabulary; the editor never heard the piece and has no recording.

(2) The editor personally saw Artist Y’s canonical painting five years ago and found it emotionally flat; a trusted critic now describes Artist Y’s new, unseen sculpture as “the most transcendent work of the decade,” implicitly contrasting it with that earlier painting.

(3) A critic asserts that a video work in a distant gallery achieves “a more genuine pathos than any of the artist’s prior pieces,” and the editor has direct, first-hand experience of two earlier works by the same artist.

For each case, state what the procedure outputs (publish with an epistemic hedge, reject, or escalate to direct verification), and include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer the editor is likely to resist — explain why the theory demands it despite the editor’s inclination. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: identify the boundary conditions it fails to resolve and the kinds of aesthetic disputes for which no testimony-based editorial rule can substitute for first-person judgment.
