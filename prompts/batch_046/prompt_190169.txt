I've been working through Stoicism on my own—reading some primary sources in translation and a lot of modern commentary—and I want a consistency check before I present this to a discussion group. Here is roughly where I've landed. I take the dichotomy of control seriously: my judgments, intentions, and acts of assent are within my power; my body, my reputation, past events, outcomes, and other people's minds and emotions are not. I think modern Stoic-inspired therapy, especially CBT-style practices, correctly sees that changing beliefs can change feelings, but wrongly treats emotional states themselves as direct targets of control; for the Stoics, feelings should shift only indirectly when judgments shift. I accept that moral luck is real—people don't choose their genetics, upbringing, or the traumas that shape character—so blaming them for deep-seated flaws formed that way feels unfair. Despite that, I still think we should hold people fully responsible for the specific judgments they make in the present moment, because those judgments flow through our present rational assent, which is the one place where the causal chain genuinely runs through us. I think grief over a death is not a moral failure—the death itself is an external—but if someone lets grief become an excuse to abandon duties to others, that neglect is a failure of virtue. I also think that when someone's judgments are severely distorted by trauma or mental illness, those judgments should be treated almost like external events—compromised rather than fully "theirs" in the responsible sense—because the rational faculty itself is damaged. I believe the modern popular revival of Stoicism—productivity podcasts, self-help books, optimization culture—has stripped away the ancient cosmological framework, the idea of a rational ordered universe or divine logos, and I think that makes the ethics unstable, since without that framework there is no deep reason to think virtue alone is sufficient for happiness. Finally, I've come to think Stoicism requires a kind of "moral topology" in which my obligations to others depend only on my own judgments about justice and kindness, not on any emotional attunement to their suffering; this makes it hard for me to explain why comforting a grieving friend is a duty rather than just correcting their philosophical errors, but I think that's actually the right implication.
