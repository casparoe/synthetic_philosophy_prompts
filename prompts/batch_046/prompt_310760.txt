We need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in Kant's three definitive articles for perpetual peace but operationalized for sub-state actors, that a non-philosopher policy advisor could apply in roughly thirty minutes—running this evaluation about twice a month under deadline—to determine whether a proposed agreement is structurally compatible with a Kantian federation. The advisor's recommendations require sign-off by a senior legal counsel who has no philosophy background, so the procedure must be self-contained.

Please construct the procedure as an ordered checklist: for each definitive article—republican constitution, federation of free states, and cosmopolitan right—specify the diagnostic questions in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer versus an insufficient one, where the procedure stops with a definitive rejection (structurally incompatible), where it stops with conditional approval (compatible only if specific amendments are made), and where it must halt and defer to the senior counsel because the question involves values or distributive justice rather than structural facts. The checklist should distinguish features that Kant's framework strictly requires from those it merely permits, and it should flag cases where the absence of full sovereignty makes a standard Kantian test inapplicable or ambiguous.

Then run the procedure step-by-step on three proposals: (a) a cross-border water-sharing pact between two regional municipalities that includes binding arbitration but excludes temporary laborers from its protections; (b) a mutual-defense agreement among three indigenous territories that creates representative councils yet reserves final decision-making authority to hereditary leaders rather than elected representatives; and (c) a "hospitality corridor" permitting free movement of goods and temporary residents between a city-state and an adjacent special economic zone, but employing algorithmic risk-scoring at entry points to deny access. Show the evaluation for each, including at least one outcome that conflicts with the proposing parties' likely preference. Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot adjudicate—especially internal distributive questions within the confederation—and identify the specific point where genuine ambiguity in Kant's own framework (for instance, whether cosmopolitan right demands open borders or only non-hostile reception) renders the checklist indeterminate and forces a judgment call.
