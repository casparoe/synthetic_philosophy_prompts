I am a professor of moral philosophy, and I have been asked to deliver the principal toast at my doctoral supervisor’s retirement banquet next month in the university’s ceremonial dining hall. The audience will be deliberately split: on one side, comparative ethicists and several visiting scholars who have published on Confucian role ethics and will catch any conceptual misstep; on the other, her family, former undergraduates, and administrative staff who expect warmth, personal gratitude, and a familiar narrative arc about a life in scholarship. She spent thirty-five years defending a rigorous version of Confucian role ethics that denies any pre-social, autonomous self—moral identity, on her account, is constituted entirely through the five cardinal relationships and the reciprocal obligations specific to each role. This creates a real structural problem for the speech. A conventional retirement toast celebrates individual achievement, sentimentalizes withdrawal from duty, and frames the retiree as entering a private, self-authored future—precisely the ontology her work rejects. I do not want to decorate a standard tribute with Confucian vocabulary; I want the philosophy to determine what can and cannot be said. Specifically, I want to build the toast around the idea that retirement is not actually a recognized role within this framework—there is no retired teacher, only a transformed mode of the teacher-student relation—and to invoke zhengming, the rectification of names, to suggest gently that calling this ceremony a retirement risks misnaming the relationship we are actually continuing. I need one concrete personal memory that illustrates our role-relationship—perhaps a moment when she corrected not my argument but my posture toward her authority—rather than an anecdote about her personality or private life. The scholars should hear that I understand the stakes of her philosophy; the family should feel included in a ceremony that honors continuity rather than loss. Please write an approximately eight-minute toast that genuinely collapses if this conceptual framing is removed, with the philosophical architecture shaping both what is said and what is deliberately left unsaid.
