I am a doctoral student in political theory working within a distributed research collective that curates a shared qualitative dataset on urban migration patterns. Our governance charter must be revised to determine who holds decision-making authority over amendments to our informed-consent protocol and data-access tiers. The collective comprises three categories of participants: tenured core investigators who designed the original study; rotating postdoctoral researchers who contribute analyses but depart after fixed terms; remote community partners who supply ethnographic material but do not participate in interpretation; and an automated auditing module that continuously flags potential representational biases in our coding framework but has no deliberative capacity. One senior colleague argues that voting rights should extend to anyone whose interests are substantially affected by how the dataset is used, which would include the community partners and arguably the subjects of the research themselves, though not the auditing module. Another colleague insists that democratic authority should be confined to those who bear sustained epistemic responsibility for the collective's methodological commitments, thereby excluding transient contributors and non-interpretive partners. I find both positions intuitively compelling yet incompatible, and I sense that the auditing module's presence introduces a further complication that neither account addresses. Could you help me identify the precise philosophical structure of this disagreement, apply the relevant theoretical distinctions—without relying on bibliographic references—to determine whether there is a principled way to adjudicate membership in our decision-making body, and explain how the role of the non-deliberative auditing system should figure into that adjudication?
