I'm trying to work out my actual view on when killing a tyrant is permissible, testing whether my intuitions line up with anything like the classical Thomistic framework or with a simpler principle of my own. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

First, a usurper seizes power by murdering the legitimate heir, rules with no legal basis, and suppresses religious practice. After the bishops and nobles formally declare him deposed, a soldier carries out the execution. Verdict: permissible, and perhaps even required.

Second, a king who inherited the crown legitimately begins starving the poor through taxation and imprisoning critics. A private merchant, acting alone and with no institutional mandate, poisons him. Verdict: not permissible; resistance should come from constituted authority, not private murder.

Third, that same legitimate king, but now the noble council convenes, formally strips him of authority by citing natural law, and then executes him. Verdict: permissible.

Fourth, a dictator takes power through a coup backed by a foreign empire. He rules brutally, yet the courts and legal code remain intact, and killing him would almost certainly trigger a civil war that the foreign power would win. Verdict: not permissible, despite the usurpation; the foreseeable chaos outweighs the removal.

Fifth, a democratically elected leader slowly erodes democratic institutions until he rules by decree, but he never personally orders violence and a legal, if slow, path of resistance still exists. A dissident assassinates him. Verdict: I keep going back and forth—part of me says yes because he destroyed the constitutional order, part says no because he was not a violent tyrant and private killing remains wrong.

Sixth, the very same elected leader issues an explicit order for the genocide of an ethnic minority. A military officer refuses the order and kills the leader to stop the killings. Verdict: clearly permissible, even though the structural situation is identical to case five.

Is there a coherent principle behind all six? I want a committed answer, not a menu of options. Either defend a single principle—stating precisely which clause handles which case, predicting its verdict on an unlisted scenario (a legitimate but senile ruler being manipulated by advisors to issue destructive decrees, killed by a court official to restore governance), and honestly saying which of my six verdicts it cannot save—or argue that my intuitions are genuinely plural and identify the two or three distinct principles in tension. In either case, take a definite stand on whether case five should be revised in light of case six, and say whether the principle you end up with tracks Aquinas's distinction between usurpation and legitimate authority turned tyrannical, with its emphasis on public rather than private action, or whether it systematically diverges in a way I should notice.
