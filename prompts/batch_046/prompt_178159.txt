You are a professional philosopher with expertise in bioethics, philosophy of personal identity, and practical reason. Please think carefully: begin by briefly mapping the conceptual stakes—especially the tension between diachronic autonomy and synchronic respect for present preferences—before delivering your adjudication.

At a seminar, two colleagues—Jordan and Taylor—debated the authority of an advance directive written by M, who refused artificial nutrition should she develop severe dementia. M now has advanced dementia, lacks long-term memory and narrative self-awareness, but actively seeks food and appears content. Jordan and Taylor disagreed as follows.

(1) Jordan: The directive binds. The pre-illness self was a fully integrated autonomous agent with a stable life plan; the current patient lacks the narrative and reflective capacities required for autonomy, so her requests are not autonomous expressions but momentary appetites. Honoring the directive respects the only autonomous author in M's temporal history.

(2) Taylor: That assumes an implausibly sharp boundary. Psychological continuity is gradual; M still recognizes family, experiences pleasure and pain, and holds preferences. To claim she has no authority over her body because she cannot articulate a philosophical life-plan sets the bar for autonomy too high and risks treating vulnerable people as non-agents.

(3) Jordan: Even on gradualist views, we routinely allow people to bind future states via Ulysses contracts—wills, powers of attorney, do-not-resuscitate orders. The directive is legitimate prior self-governance: the earlier self anticipated contradictory future desires and pre-authorized refusal. Its authority comes from the right to plan one's life, not from strict numerical identity.

(4) Taylor: Ulysses contracts presuppose the later self is still the same agent facing temptation, not a psychologically discontinuous subject. Severe dementia may be closer to a different person inheriting the body. Furthermore, if we allow past directives to override present preferences whenever cognitive impairment is present, we open a precedent of preemptive disenfranchisement that could cover severe depression or temporary psychosis. Jordan needs a principled reason why dementia is special, not just severe.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what important consideration—conceptual or practical—both parties are missing in their exchange.
