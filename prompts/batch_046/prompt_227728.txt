I cover artificial intelligence and the philosophy of mind for a science magazine, and I am developing a recurring editorial column that evaluates whether research claims about machine consciousness, suffering, or moral status are warranted. To apply consistent standards across stories, I need a reusable decision procedure—structured as an ordered checklist or flowchart in words—that translates the philosophical problem of which computations matter morally into a practical tool I can run in about thirty minutes on a press release or preprint, without consulting primary literature.

The procedure should be grounded in what philosophy of mind and cognitive science actually maintain about computation and moral attribution, but it must address a gap that standard treatments often ignore: modern architectures frequently distribute processing across modules, external memory stores, or multi-agent networks, so the procedure cannot assume that moral-relevant computation is localized in a single agent. It needs to determine whether distributed computation can collectively constitute a moral patient, or whether integration within a bounded system is necessary.

Please deliver the following in American English, in formal prose suitable for an editorial handbook:

1. A concise statement of the core principle being operationalized, including the distinction between localized and distributed computation and why it matters for attributing sentience.

2. The procedure itself: ordered steps with decision nodes, explicit tests I can apply to a technical system description, and clear branching.

3. Three worked applications:
   - a conventional centralized neural-network model;
   - a federated or swarm architecture in which computation is spread across nodes;
   - and one case in which the procedure produces an answer that conflicts with popular coverage or my own prior expectations—such as a widely publicized system failing the criteria, or a mundane distributed system unexpectedly satisfying them.

4. At each step, an explicit flag indicating whether the step is mechanical or a judgment call, with brief guidance on how to document the judgment for readers.

5. An honest account of what the procedure cannot settle—specifically, where philosophical disagreement about substrate, functional equivalence, or integration remains unresolved, and how I should disclose that uncertainty rather than conceal it.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote verbatim from any philosophical work; paraphrase all theoretical positions without quotation marks.
