I have been revisiting Isaac Asimov’s Foundation series and want to read it through Peter Turchin’s cliodynamics, specifically the demographic-structural theory of secular cycles. In Turchin’s framework societies move through integrative and disintegrative phases driven by quantifiable variables—elite overproduction, immiseration of commoners, and fiscal stress—producing statistical predictions about state breakdown and recovery rather than deterministic prophecies about particular events. Asimov’s Hari Seldon, by contrast, uses psychohistory to forecast the fall of the Galactic Empire and then pre-programmed “Seldon Crises” that the Foundation must navigate, with individual agency apparently neutralized except for the anomalous Mule. I want a sustained critical reading that takes both the fiction and the macrohistorical model seriously: where does the narrative illuminate the logic of quantitative history—say, in the depiction of imperial bureaucracy decaying across predictable intervals, or in the Foundation’s isolation as a controlled institutional experiment? Does the Mule operate as a genuine refutation of macrohistorical determinism, or as a narrative escape hatch that saves the plot from testing its own premises?
