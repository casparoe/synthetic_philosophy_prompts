I have recently been asked to advise on whether an isolated population qualifies for protected status under national conservation law, and in drafting my brief I have become entangled in what philosophers of biology call the species problem. Rather than requesting a bibliography, I want a disciplined conceptual analysis of a concrete case. Consider the IUCN's decision in the 2020s to list the African forest elephant and the African savanna elephant as two distinct species, both endangered, and examine it through the lens of the species problem—specifically through the tension I will call process pluralism (the view that different biological criteria—reproductive isolation, ecological niche, phylogenetic divergence—serve different legitimate scientific purposes and no single concept governs all taxonomic judgments) versus legal monism (the institutional requirement that conservation statutes apply a binary, yes-or-no species boundary to determine protection eligibility). I am not asking for ecological data, which are well established; I want the philosophical anatomy of the decision. How does the species problem describe what taxonomists and policymakers actually faced? Which features of the episode—the genetic and morphological divergence of the populations, the downstream effects on anti-poaching enforcement and habitat designation, or the rhetorical framing of restoration versus preservation—does the problem illuminate, and which does it obscure or mischaracterize? Does the case support the pluralist claim that species are real but multifarious, or does it reveal that the so-called problem is largely an artifact of forcing graded, multidimensional biological reality into a binary regulatory switch—suggesting that the debate has been less about ontology than about the institutional design that demands a single answer from nature? Please keep the mapping precise: identify the relevant actors, the options available to them, what counts as evidence within each framework, and where the theory remains silent rather than being made to speak.
