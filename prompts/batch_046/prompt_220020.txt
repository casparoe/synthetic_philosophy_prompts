Two of my grad students, Maya and Leo, got stuck in my office arguing about the Grand Inquisitor. (1) Maya said the parable is basically elitist: Dostoevsky thinks the masses are too weak for freedom, so they need authority to be happy, and modern welfare states prove him right since people vote for security over autonomy. (2) Leo pushed back that the Inquisitor isn't describing human nature but a feedback loop where authority manufactures the very needs it claims to satisfy; he brought up the adaptive-preference problem from development ethics and said that's exactly what happens with miracle, mystery, and authority. (3) Maya said if that's true then the Inquisitor is just a cynical manipulator, not a tragic figure who genuinely loves humanity. (4) Leo said no, that's the point: he loves them through domination, which is why Christ's silence is so devastating. (5) Maya then accused Leo of smuggling in Sartrean negative freedom with out admitting it, and said if we drop Dostoevsky's theological anthropology the parable collapses into a generic warning about technocracy -- maybe useful, but not profound. (6) Leo admitted he was dropping the theology but insisted that's where the real disagreement lives: whether moral agency needs a transcendent ground or can survive in purely immanent terms. They went in circles for like twenty mintues and neither would budge. Can you adjudicate move by move? Rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to mostly right, briefly justify each rating, and tell me what both parties are missing about how freedom and happiness actually relate in the text.
