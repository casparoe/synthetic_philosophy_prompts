I am a journalist covering science and ideas for a serious quarterly review, and I am currently drafting a feature on how moral frameworks migrate from academic sociology into the governance of pluralistic institutions. For this piece, I need an original assessment instrument—specifically, an upper-level undergraduate essay examination—that tests whether students can reason structurally about moral culture rather than summarize familiar taxonomies. The examination must address this precise question: Is any of honor culture, dignity culture, or victimhood culture morally superior?

The exam prompt, presented exactly as it would appear to students, should be answerable in approximately seventy-five minutes and should call for a response of about seven hundred words. Crucially, it must not be answerable by reciting standard distinctions—for instance, that honor cultures are collective, dignity cultures universalist, and victimhood cultures grievance-based. To compel genuine conceptual labor, embed the following synthetic scenario: A pluralistic research institute convenes an arbitration board to resolve a conflict in which a senior scholar publicly challenges the methodological rigor of a junior scholar's published findings. The junior scholar declines to defend the methods on substantive grounds and instead frames the public critique as an assault on personal dignity that activates a victim-status claim, demanding institutional recognition and formal redress. The senior scholar rejects mediated procedure, insisting that intellectual honor requires direct, unmediated contestation of ideas in open forum, and warns that dignity-culture neutrality would suppress conflict necessary to institutional health.

Students should be instructed to determine which cultural framework—honor, dignity, or victimhood—offers the morally superior resolution. In constructing their answer, they must analytically separate three dimensions that are commonly collapsed: first, the intrinsic normative coherence of the culture's moral ontology; second, its functional adaptability under conditions of deep pluralism and large-scale institutional life; and third, its vulnerability to what I term "credential inflation"—the tendency for victim-status claims to become epistemically non-falsifiable and therefore strategically irrefutable. They must also address whether dignity culture's procedural neutrality is genuinely value-free or itself encodes a substantive moral anthropology that privileges individual autonomy over collective reputation.

Please provide the following, written in a formal, polished register and without bibliographic citation, named authorities, or appeals to specific texts:

1. The examination prompt exactly as students would receive it.
2. A model answer of roughly seven hundred words that applies the required three-dimensional separation to the arbitration scenario, demonstrates conceptual precision, and does not depend on external citation.
3. A marking scheme totaling twenty marks, organized around five must-hit analytical moves, each worth four marks. For each move, specify what an A-level answer must contain, what a B-level answer typically omits or mishandles, and the specific misconception that should cost marks—particularly the conflation of functional adaptability with moral superiority, and the failure to recognize that dignity-culture neutrality carries its own non-neutral moral commitments.
