I was discussing with a friend over coffee last week whether being able to imagine something clearly means it must be genuinely possible. I do not have any formal training in philosophy, but I have been reading a little about René Descartes, who apparently argued that because he could conceive of his mind existing without his body, the two must be genuinely separable in reality. That struck me as bold, yet it was written long before anyone had to think about artificial minds or hidden-layer computations.

More recently, I came across the idea of adversarial examples in machine vision — images that look entirely normal to a human observer but are classified by a neural network as something completely different, with absolute confidence, after only imperceptible alterations. At the same time, philosophers such as David Chalmers have pressed the conceivability of philosophical zombies — beings physically identical to us but lacking conscious experience — as a challenge to physicalism. Given all this, I would like to know: how do you think Descartes would have responded, had he been confronted with these posthumous developments? Would he have treated the machine's misclassification as a mere failure of sensory apparatus, irrelevant to the metaphysical reliability of clear and distinct human conception? Or might the fact that both human and artificial cognition can generate coherent but physically impossible classifications have led him to restrict the link between conceivability and possibility? Please answer in British English, and I am not looking for a list of references — just your best reasoning about where his argument would land.
