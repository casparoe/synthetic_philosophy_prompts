As a doctoral researcher in philosophy of science, I'm working through a conceptual problem in Popper's anti-historicist framework that strikes me as underexplored in the standard commentary. Popper distinguishes between historicism—the thesis that social science can discover evolutionary laws enabling long-range historical prophecy—and his preferred model of piecemeal social engineering, which proceeds through trial-and-error adjustments to discrete institutions. The received interpretation holds that this distinction cleanly separates illegitimate holistic prediction from legitimate localized intervention. However, I'm trying to formalize what we might call weak extrapolative continuity: the minimal assumption that the causal and institutional structure of a social system remains sufficiently stable between an intervention and its evaluation for the feedback loop of trial and error to yield meaningful information rather than noise. My question is conceptual rather than exegetical. Can Popper consistently reject all forms of historical law—including any conditions that might ground such continuity—without undermining the rational basis of piecemeal engineering itself? More precisely, does the rejection of laws of history entail that every social intervention occurs in a radically open-ended context where no predictive stability is justified, and if so, by what criterion can the engineer distinguish a successful institutional adjustment from a random correlation? Please explain this tension, particularly whether Popper's framework implicitly relies on a restricted, non-historicist form of predictive stability that his explicit arguments against historicist prophecy fail to account for.
