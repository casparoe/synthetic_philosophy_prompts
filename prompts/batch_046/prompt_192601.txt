As someone who writes about science and ideas, I am trying to understand how philosophers distinguish genuine collective agency from merely coordinated individual behavior, especially inside layered institutions. Could you compare Michael Bratman’s account of shared cooperative activity—built on interlocking intentions, mutual responsiveness, and common knowledge of plans—with Margaret Gilbert’s plural-subject theory, which treats joint commitment as an irreducible primitive that binds participants into a collective “we”? I want to apply this comparison specifically to nested institutional structures: picture a clinical-trial team embedded within a pharmaceutical firm that is itself one partner in a multi-site research consortium. How does each framework explain the normativity that binds members of the trial team to one another versus the obligations, if any, that flow upward to the consortium? Does Bratman’s reduction to individual intentions and meshing sub-plans scale to these layered commitments, or does Gilbert’s notion of joint commitment better capture situations in which a researcher is firmly bound to her immediate team yet does not endorse the broader aims of the superordinate group? And where both theories appear silent—particularly on partial membership, where an agent contributes functionally to one joint action without subscribing to the shared purpose of a larger collective—what does that silence reveal about the current limits of collective intentionality as a philosophical framework?
