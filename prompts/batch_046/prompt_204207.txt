I'm an ethics screener for a regional coalition of about a dozen nonprofits, and every few weeks I recieve requests to waive our shared protocols — procurement rules, data-sharing agreements, conflict-of-interest policies — where the person says they broke the rule for genuinely moral reasons, not just convenience. The board signs off on exemptions but they want me to do a quick pre-check and write a recommendation, usually with about 20 minutes and no philosophy background. I keep thinking about Glaucon's challenge in the Republic — the ring of Gyges that makes you invisible, so you can do wrong without getting caught — and it feels like exactly the problem here: how do I tell if someone is being moral because it's right, or just because they want to look good, or because they think they won't get caught? I need this turned into an actual reusable procedure, not an essay.

Specifically, I want a step-by-step decision workflow I can run on any request: what questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a hard no or a mandatory referral to the board, and where it's just a judgment call I have to own. I want it grounded in the actual logic of Glaucon's challenge — like testing whether the reason still holds if the actor were invisible, if the roles were reversed, and if the exception were universalized — but translated so I don't need to cite Plato. Include a small set of test conditions I can apply quickly, flag the cases the procedure can't settle, and be honest about which steps are just dressed-up intuition. Then run it on three proposals: (1) a director hides a data breach to avoid panicking clients, violating our transparency rule and claiming harm-prevention; (2) a partner org refuses to share donor lists with a new coalition, saying they have a moral duty of loyalty to existing supporters; and (3) a staffer skips competitive bidding during a flood to buy supplies from their cousin, arguing that bureaucracy costs lives. I should warn you I usually side with emergency exceptions, so please make sure one of these comes back with an answer I won't like — maybe the procedure rejects the flood case or approves the donor-refusal in a way that feels wrong. Also tell me what the whole framework misses — like whether there are moral reasons that genuinely survive but still fail your tests.
