I am composing a historical novel in which an Edinburgh professor of moral philosophy marks an examination on Adam Smith’s writings concerning empire, and I need the grading scene to carry real scholarly weight rather than generic period color. To calibrate the dialogue, I have drafted both the examination prompt and one student response below. The answer is not wholly wrong—its errors are of the kind an intelligent but hurried pupil might make, especially in importing a modern rights-based framework into Smith’s practical argument about the American colonies, and in failing to distinguish Smith’s fiscal critique of colonial monopoly from his separate, institutionally specific indictment of the East India Company’s territorial sovereignty.

The examination question is this:

“Adam Smith contends that the mercantile monopoly of colonial trade is economically ruinous for the mother country. Examine whether his opposition to empire is best understood as a fiscal argument about defense costs and capital misallocation, or as a political argument about constitutional corruption and the militarization of the state. Further, explain how his specific recommendations regarding the American colonies relate to his broader theory of the natural progress of opulence, and indicate whether he applies an identical analytical framework to the administration of India.”

The student’s answer reads as follows:

Smith clearly believed that maintaining colonies was a bad deal for Britain. In Wealth of Nations he calculates that the military and naval costs of defending the colonies exceed the profits gained from the monopoly trade, so the mother country loses money on balance. He also argues that the monopoly forces capital into colonial commerce that ought instead to flow first into domestic agriculture, then manufactures, and only afterward into foreign trade; this diversion disrupts the natural progress of opulence. Regarding the American colonies, Smith supports their right to independence because he holds that any people taxed without representation possess a natural right to self-government. He believes they should be freed at once, just as he believes the East India Company should be dissolved immediately, since both cases represent the same corrupt imperial overreach that drains revenue and destroys liberty.

Please evaluate this answer with the rigor of a professor who must separate visible competence from subtle misapprehension. Structure your response exactly as follows:

Overall Assessment (approximately 100 words): A concise judgment of the answer’s quality and the appropriate mark.

What the Student Gets Right (approximately 150 words): Identify the accurate claims, particularly on fiscal burden, capital misallocation, and the sequential logic of natural opulence.

Subtle Errors Requiring Correction (approximately 250 words): Diagnose the specific confusions—especially the misattribution of a modern doctrine of self-determination to Smith’s American argument, and the unwarranted assimilation of his American and Indian policies. Explain why each is a misreading rather than a minor inaccuracy.

Suggested Marginalia (approximately 120 words): Provide three or four precise comments, phrased as a professor might write in the margin, that direct the student toward the errors without simply supplying the correct view.

Verdict (approximately 50 words): State the grade assigned and the single reason it is not higher or lower.

Please do not include bibliographic references, citation lists, or suggestions for further reading; the assessment should stand on its own as it might in a classroom without library access.
