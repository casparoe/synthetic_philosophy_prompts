As a doctoral student in philosophy of mind working with a behavioral-health clinic, I am trying to operationalize the distinction between akrasia—acting against one's own all-things-considered judgment—and hyperbolic discounting—systematic preference reversal driven by temporal proximity rather than by a change in underlying values—for frontline intake coordinators who must choose between cognitive-behavioral restructuring protocols and commitment-device architectures, but who have no philosophical training. I need a reusable decision procedure, not a one-off diagnosis, that such a coordinator can run in about fifteen minutes using only a standardized verbal report and a temporal-proximity probe, with clear rules for when a case must be escalated to a senior clinician because the conceptual distinction collapses into value-laden interpretation.

The procedure should proceed in ordered stages. First, establish whether the patient holds a stable all-things-considered judgment at temporal distance, using a counterfactual consistency check. Second, determine whether that judgment reverses when the reward or punishment becomes imminent, and whether the reversal is accompanied by explicit self-condemnation—indicating judgment-contradiction consistent with akrasia—versus a mere shift in felt preference without retrospective repudiation, consistent with hyperbolic discounting. At each stage, specify what counts as sufficient evidence, provide a small set of verbatim test questions the coordinator can administer and score, flag the cases where strategic self-deception (retrospectively manufacturing a judgment that was never actually held) makes the procedure misfire, and identify the exact point at which judgment cannot be eliminated and a values-based decision must be signed off by a supervisor. The deliverable should also state explicitly what the procedure cannot do—particularly when behavioral data are absent and verbal reports are contradictory across pre- and post-lapse interviews.

Please apply this procedure to three intake vignettes. The first should be a clear case of akrasia: a patient who reports planning to quit gambling by Friday, then deciding to stay "just one more hour" at the casino, followed by intense self-directed condemnation. The second should be a clear case of hyperbolic discounting: a patient who genuinely values exercise when scheduling it for next month, but on the morning of genuinely prefers sleeping in, feels disappointed rather than guilty, and does not claim to have violated any prior judgment. The third should be a borderline case in which the patient gives contradictory reports depending on whether they are interviewed before or after a lapse, such that the procedure must flag the classification as unresolvable without external behavioral tracking, and you should explain why this limitation is conceptually significant—rooted in the epistemic opacity of first-person judgment reports—rather than merely a practical constraint of limited interview time. For each application, show the step-by-step scoring, note any judgment calls, and indicate where the coordinator should stop and escalate.
