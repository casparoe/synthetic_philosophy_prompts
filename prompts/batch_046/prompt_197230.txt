I've been helping out with paperwork at a manufacturing consortium where two plants want to share a safety certification for their turbine-bearing monitors. Plant One uses a deep-learning system trained on vibration spectra; it learns its own feature representations from raw sensor data. Plant Two achieves the same detection rate using only hand-built physics equations and Kalman filters maintained by technicians. The regulations allow one plant to inherit another's certified mechanism without full re-testing, but nobody can agree whether these two systems share one.

I've been trying to understand the disagreement myself. One side seems to say that a mechanism is whatever causal chain turns sensor readings into an alarm—if the input-output behavior and internal information flow match closely enough, the mechanism is the same regardless of how it was built. The other side seems to say that the mechanism includes how the rules came to exist—whether they were extracted by gradient descent or written down from first principles—so the two plants use fundamentally different mechanisms, even if they flag the same failures.

Please write a dialogue between two auditors, both technically competent, who defend these two positions. Have them respond directly to one another rather than talking past each other. They should work through a specific twist: what should the certification body say if Plant Two later replaces its manual equations with a neural network that approximates those same equations for speed, or if Plant One's learned weights are reverse-engineered into readable formulas? Each auditor should clearly state whether they would grant the exemption in the original case and in the twist case, but neither should be made to look foolish.
