I've been working through G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica with a reading group, and we keep circling back to a tension in his treatment of intrinsic goods that none of the standard commentaries seem to address directly. Moore famously pairs friendship and the appreciation of beautiful objects as the two greatest intrinsic goods, and he insists that friendship exhibits organic unity—the value of the whole relation exceeds the sum of the values of the individual pleasures or virtues it contains. At the same time, he treats beauty as fundamentally a property of objects or scenes that can be appreciated in isolation, and he is sharply critical of any account that makes the value of one thing depend instrumentally on another. The puzzle is this: if friendship is genuinely relational and reciprocal, its value must depend on properties of both friends; but if those properties include the very qualities—wit, grace, moral nobility—that Moore also treats as objects of aesthetic appreciation, then it looks as though the value of friendship partly derives from appreciating the friend as one appreciates a beautiful painting or a noble landscape. That would seem to make friendship either instrumentally valuable, as a vehicle for aesthetic pleasure, or reducible to a complex form of aesthetic enjoyment, either of which threatens Moore's claim that friendship is a distinct, non-derivative intrinsic good.

Write an essay that examines whether Moore can maintain a sharp distinction between friendship and aesthetic appreciation without abandoning his doctrine of organic unities. You should weigh the possibility that Moore implicitly distinguishes two modes of regard—what we might call appreciative regard for a person's qualities versus the reciprocal emotional commitment that constitutes friendship proper—and ask whether this distinction can do the work he needs. Reach a definite conclusion: either defend Moore by showing exactly which structural feature of friendship, whether reciprocity, mutual awareness, shared history, or something else, blocks the reduction to aesthetics, or argue that the reduction succeeds and that Moore's pairing of the two goods was not accidental but symptomatic. In either case, identify the feature that carries the argumentative weight, and say briefly what a committed defender of Moore's position would reply to your central objection. I want a committed answer that takes a clear stand and gives its reasons rather than balancing the options indefinitely.
