I am preparing a discussion paper for an advanced seminar that pairs ancient ethics with formal models of cooperation, and I have become preoccupied with what my advisor terms the "monitoring-cost interpretation" of Glaucon's ring of Gyges in Republic II. On the standard reading, the ring asks whether justice is valued intrinsically: if an agent could become perfectly invisible—escaping every mechanism of reputation, punishment, and social feedback—would she still have reason to act justly? Lately, however, I have been studying evolutionary models of indirect reciprocity that operate under conditions of noisy observation and probabilistic belief-updating, where cooperative equilibria persist even when individual actions are not directly observable because agents maintain stochastic reputation tags through second-order signals. This suggests to me that the ring's hypothetical—total invisibility with zero monitoring costs—might not actually collapse the social structures that sustain moral behavior, since reputation dynamics can survive in hidden-information regimes without direct surveillance. Given that justice might therefore remain evolutionarily stable even inside the ring's conditions, I am inclined to think that Glaucon's challenge has been quietly dissolved: the ring no longer proves that justice is purely instrumental, because modern work shows cooperation does not depend on visibility in the first place. Is this reasoning sound, or does it commit a category mistake—perhaps conflating the descriptive stability of cooperative strategies under imperfect monitoring with the normative question of whether a rational agent, knowing with certainty that she alone bears the costs of justice and receives none of its social returns, still has an internal reason to be just? I want to be sure I am not letting a formal result about evolutionary persistence do philosophical work it was never designed to do.
