I spent much of last evening arguing with a close friend about whether democratic backsliding is still a coherent analytical concept or has collapsed into a partisan label, and I need a structured outside view before I chair tomorrow’s funding-panel session. I serve on the review committee for the Transatlantic Civic Studies Consortium. Before us is a proposal titled Institutional Friction Mapping: A Procedural-Only Metric for Regime Erosion in Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States. The investigators—three comparative political scientists and one constitutional lawyer—propose to operationalize backsliding exclusively through observable procedural fractures: legislative-executive conflict-escalation scores, judicial-appointment interference indices, and media-ownership concentration gradients. They deliberately exclude attitudinal or normative indicators—elite commitments to democratic forbearance, public-trust dynamics, or what they term democratic-culture thermometers—arguing that such measures are normatively loaded and invite partisan selection bias. My friend insists this procedural purity is exactly what saves the concept from becoming a left-right cudgel; I worry it risks false negatives, allowing regimes to hollow out pluralism while retaining technically intact elections. The request is for $480,000 over three years, and the inclusion of a U.S. comparative module is already generating political sensitivity among our funders. The panel must deliver a clear recommendation—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—together with reasoning the applicants could actually respond to.

Please adopt the role of a senior scholar of comparative politics with training in philosophy of social science. Think carefully about the conceptual stakes before writing, and begin with a brief internal brainstorming of the tension between procedural and normative conceptions of democratic decay.
