I am a novelist preparing an evening workshop for three fellow fiction writers, all intellectually curious but without any formal background in physics or philosophy. I want them to understand Kurt Gödel's rotating-universe solution to Einstein's field equations, and especially the metaphysical claim he drew from it: that a globally rotating cosmos permits closed timelike curves through pure geometry alone, without faster-than-light travel or mechanical devices, and that this undermines the objectivity of temporal becoming—past, present, and future succeeding one another—reducing it to something like a subjective ordering rather than a feature of the world itself.

Please do not explain the idea to me directly. Instead, design the teaching plan for how I should present it. Lay out the precise sequence in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced, with the dependencies explicit: what must be firmly in place before the notion of circular time permitted by geometry can be heard correctly rather than as science-fiction gadgetry. Identify the single carrying example or analogy—drawn from narrative structure rather than from physics laboratories—that should anchor the entire session; specify what properties it must have to genuinely illuminate the cosmological point, and what properties it must avoid so that it does not collapse into mere metaphor. Then map the misconceptions these writers will almost certainly form: the confusion between global rotational symmetry and local spinning motion; the assumption that returning to one's own past requires violating the speed of light; and, most subtly, the importation of a merely psychological or emotional notion of subjective time that misses Gödel's ontological argument entirely. For each misconception, state exactly where in the sequence it should be intercepted and defused. Propose one diagnostic question I can ask at the end to determine whether they have grasped the philosophical thesis about the non-objectivity of temporal passage, as distinct from having retained only the cosmological image. Finally, state clearly what I must refuse to present or derive at this level—the tensor formalism, the explicit metric, and the technical argument of Gödel's philosophical essay on idealism and relativity—and indicate how I should frame that omission so that it reinforces rather than undermines the coherence of what remains.
