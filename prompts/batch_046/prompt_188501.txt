I'm writing a piece on whether brain-scans can really "talk" for patients who can't move or speak. Take the classic Owen study — a patient diagnosed as vegetative, asked to imagine playing tennis, and her motor cortex lights up in the scanner. The team treats that pattern as a yes/no report. Run this through Dennett's heterophenomenology: he says we should treat first-person reports as texts needing interpetation, not direct windows into private experience. But in Owen's case there's no first-person text — just neural data decoded by researchers into an answer. So here's the question: does heterophenomenology still apply? Definately take a stand — either this is exactly the kind of mediated "heterophenomenological text" the method was built for, which means the report carries no special authority and might just be researcher projection; or the method collapses without an actual subject authoring the narrative. Don't just list pros and cons — argue for one, and say what it shows about consciousness science. Does the episode prove that the "neutral stance" toward reports was never really neutral, because once you need a scanner and a statistician to generate the "word," you've already injected interpretation? And what does that do to heterophenomenology itself — does it extend naturally to these machine-mediated cases, or was it never meant to cover them?
