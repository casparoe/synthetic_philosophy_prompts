I'm organizing the Q&A for a graduate workshop on the ontology of historical periods, and the invited speaker is a philosopher-historian defending a position she terms "bureaucratic discontinuism." Her central claim is that "Renaissance," "Scientific Revolution," and even "Middle Ages" are not merely retrospective labels but actively harmful teleological frames that collapse two distinct temporal logics—synchronic craft accumulation versus diachronic administrative rupture—into a single narrative of progressive enlightenment. Specifically, she argues that the genuinely transformative break in early modern Europe occurred not with Copernicus, Galileo, or Newton, but with the state-sponsored standardization of weights and measures in the late seventeenth century, which produced what she calls "calibrated fact": an epistemic object defined not by its truth-conditions but by its reproducibility across bureaucratic nodes, and one that contemporaries would not have recognized as belonging to "scientia" at all. She is notoriously effective at deflecting objections about anachronism with the refrain that she is not denying contemporaries had concepts, only that those concepts possessed a structurally different grammar.

I need a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that progressively undermine her ability to deploy that deflection. At minimum, include one question on how she distinguishes her view from standard continuist critiques (which deny a single revolution but keep the periodization intact), one on whether her reliance on bureaucratic archives introduces a class bias that erases artisanal knowledge practices, and one on what empirical finding could falsify her claim that "calibrated fact" is a novel epistemic kind rather than an old idea in new administrative clothing. For each, draft a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to offer—respectively, that continuists confuse temporal structure with narrative absence; that artisanal records actually display the absence of calibration protocols; and that falsification would require demonstrating pre-1670 state measurement regimes that treated instrument error as intrinsic rather than user-derived. Finally, if my framing of her view contains a conceptual incoherence—say, if her claim about temporal logic and her empirical claim about metrology actually pull in opposite directions—or if "bureaucratic discontinuism" misrepresents the actual historiographical terrain, tell me so plainly rather than refining questions around a confused premise.
