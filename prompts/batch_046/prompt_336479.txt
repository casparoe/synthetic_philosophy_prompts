Since my sister recently underwent a corpus callosotomy for severe epilepsy, I have become acutely aware of a tension in her care. The clinical team and legal framework treat her as one continuous person with a single will, yet post-operative assessments reveal that her left and right hemispheres can express contradictory preferences—one reaching for an object the other rejects. I have also been reading Hofstadter's claim that an I is not confined to a single brain but is a distributed symbolic pattern, a strange loop that can in principle persist across different minds.

What philosophical commitments about personal identity does the institutional practice of unitary consent and singular guardianship for split-brain patients presuppose? Does it tacitly assume a Cartesian ego, or can it be made consistent with a narrative-continuity view that allows for internal division? Furthermore, if one adopted a Hofstadter-style distributed-self view—where fragments of selfhood might reside in separated hemispheres or even in close others—what changes would be required in clinical ethics? Would separate advocates be needed for each hemisphere, or does the current practice already contain implicit resources for accommodating divided agency without abandoning unitary treatment?
