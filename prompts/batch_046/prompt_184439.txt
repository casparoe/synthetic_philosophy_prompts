As a novelist, I am struck by a comparison I keep encountering in discussions of illusionism about phenomenal consciousness: the claim that phenomenal properties are like fictional entities — that asserting there is a reddish qualitative feel operates structurally like asserting Sherlock Holmes resides at 221B Baker Street, true within a representational framework but ontologically weightless. Proponents use this to suggest we can deny phenomenal ontology without denying experience itself, much as we deny Holmes's existence without denying the text. Audit this analogy: lay out the correspondence table — author, text, reader, fictional world, suspension of disbelief — mapped onto the conscious subject and its qualitative states; then identify where the mapping fractures, especially whether it smuggles in an audience for consciousness that illusionism cannot accommodate. Finally, say whether the conclusion — that phenomenal properties are fictions we can dismiss without contradiction — survives once the disanalogies are exposed, or whether the fiction-comparison was carrying the argument all along.
