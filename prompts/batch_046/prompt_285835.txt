I am organizing a 90-minute workshop for fourteen philosophy graduate students—seven in ethics, seven in philosophy of language—and the normative force of promises has become acutely personal since I broke a professional commitment two months ago to accept an unexpected opportunity, leaving me uncertain whether my wrong was merely a failure of reliability or a breach of a distinct, second-personal relation. I want the participants to feel, before anyone names the concept, that breaking a promise differs in kind from revising a plan or disappointing an expectation. Design a structured, in-person activity that requires no outside reading and no lecture up front, runnable with only printed cards and a score sheet, in which triads rotate through three experimental conditions: (1) a ritualized verbal commitment made with explicit mutual acknowledgment; (2) an identical burden of action accepted without any ritual formula; and (3) a ritual formula spoken without the listener's acceptance. In each round, a randomly drawn crisis card then shifts the payoffs so that fulfillment becomes costly in reputation points—determining, concretely, who in the group must buy the next round of coffee—while defection becomes individually advantageous. The rules should assign the roles of promisor, promisee, and auditor; require any breach to be announced publicly within the triad; and calibrate the reputation penalties differently across the three conditions so that participants experience the differential normative weight through their own choices rather than through explanation. Include a debrief that maps specific moments of tension onto the distinction between promissory obligation (binding even when reliance interests are minimal) and expectation-based reliance, predicts the predictable first-run failure—likely that participants conflate condition 1 with mere forecasting fortified by social pressure—and frankly notes which structural feature, whether the promisee's standing to hold the promisor accountable or the internal normative transformation of the promisor's will, the activity cannot reproduce and you must supply in the closing discussion. Specify the timeline, the card texts or rules that generate the crisis, and exactly what the participants should come away seeing.
