During a recent seminar in democratic theory, a visiting lecturer repeatedly invoked the maxim "the vote is a confession, not a calculation" to reject any defense of strategic or lesser-evil voting. She applied it most sharply when a student argued that supporting a deeply flawed but electable candidate over a worse opponent is rational given institutional constraints; she replied that treating one's ballot as an instrument for outcomes reduces citizenship to engineering and betrays a duty to express one's true moral commitments regardless of consequence. I am trying to understand exactly what precise theses this slogan could be advancing, since its rhetorical force seems to depend on smuggling together several distinct claims about expression, duty, and complicity.

Could you lay out the possible readings as a menu—ranging from the trivial to the radical—and for each indicate whether it is defensible, false, or probably not what she meant? I am especially interested in which reading would genuinely make strategic voting a moral failure rather than merely an imprudent or ineffective choice, and whether that reading can survive the objection that democratic systems are explicitly designed to aggregate preferences through instrumental choice rather than to function as rituals of personal virtue certification. In particular, distinguish the claim that voting expresses identity from the claim that voting creates moral complicity, and tell me which of those, if either, actually supports dismissing the student's institutional argument rather than just changing the subject.
