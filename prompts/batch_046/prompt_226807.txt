I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I recently joined an informal reading group of colleagues who are trying to apply ethical frameworks to algorithmic design. During our discussion of suffering-focused ethics—specifically the view that reducing severe harm should take lexical or near-lexical priority over promoting moderate benefits—two members, Maya and Leo, became locked in a multi-round disagreement that I could not adjudicate as a bystander, and I am hoping for a disciplined, point-by-point analysis of the arguments they actually made.

Maya opened by arguing that negative utilitarianism implies what she called a non-linear moral gradient: a unit of extreme suffering does not merely subtract linearly from overall utility but generates a compounding moral obligation that equivalent amounts of happiness cannot offset. She applied this to technical risk, saying that in AI development we should treat the possibility of catastrophic failure causing profound distress as categorically more urgent than optimizing for average user satisfaction, even when the expected number of beneficiaries is very large. Leo replied that this smuggles in an unjustified asymmetry; if suffering truly dominates in that steep way, then creating any complex system with a non-zero risk of extreme failure—including beneficial ones—becomes morally reckless, pushing the view toward an implicit extinctionist stance against innovation. Maya countered that she is not claiming suffering carries infinite weight, only that it is steeply graded, and she insisted on a distinction between creating suffering and risking it: we may proceed with development if we rigorously minimize risk, but our design priorities must reorder around harm reduction first, not treat it as one factor among many. Leo then pressed her on thresholds, arguing that without a principled cutoff for acceptable risk, she is simply doing standard probabilistic risk management with a philosophical label, and that she still needs an implicit utilitarian calculus to decide when risk is low enough to proceed. He also challenged her comparison of psychological harm from algorithmic discrimination against convenience gains for thousands of users, claiming these goods are incommensurable and that her framework pretends to a precision in measuring suffering that engineering ethics does not possess. Maya responded that demanding perfect measurability before acting on moral urgency is a form of practical paralysis; in software we constantly manage probabilistic risk under uncertainty, and the point of suffering-focused ethics is not to compute exact utility values but to reverse the default order—harm reduction before optimization. Leo conceded that reversing priorities is prudent engineering judgment but insisted that this is not distinctive to negative utilitarianism, and that Maya has defended a normative orientation rather than the core axiological claim that suffering is asymmetrically more important than happiness.

As someone observing without philosophical training, I want to understand who is right about which specific point. Is Maya correct that suffering-focused ethics demands a steep, non-linear weighting rather than simple linear subtraction? Is Leo right that without a threshold mechanism the view collapses into either standard risk management or an implausible prohibition on all risky creation? What does each party miss—for instance, does Maya overlook that her gradient still requires some metric or decision rule, or does Leo miss that negative utilitarianism might be intended precisely as a reordering principle rather than a computational procedure? And does their exchange reveal a genuine tension in applying suffering-focused ethics to technical design, or is it largely a confusion about levels of analysis?
