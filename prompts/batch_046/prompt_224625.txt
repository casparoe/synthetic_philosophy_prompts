I'm trying to work out what I actually think about economic entitlement, using Nozick's framework as a starting point but testing it against cases that his standard examples don't cover. Here are my gut verdicts.

Case one: The classic Wilt Chamberlain scenario. A million people each voluntarily pay twenty-five cents to watch a basketball player perform. He ends up with far more than everyone else. This feels just—the inequality comes entirely from free transfers.

Case two: The replicator. A musician trains an AI on her performances and licenses it through a smart contract that charges a micro-fee for every generated playback across the network. She performs once, then stops working entirely, yet accumulates enormous wealth as billions of plays occur. Every payment is voluntary and transparent. Still, something about her claim to all that income feels weaker than Chamberlain's. I lean that it is not fully just, or at least not for the same reason.

Case three: The perpetual protocol clause. In 2025 a programmer writes an open encryption standard and embeds a license requiring all commercial users to pay her descendants one-tenth of one percent in perpetuity. By 2045 her great-grandchildren, who have done no technical work, live in luxury from this stream. The clause was agreed to by every user. I think this is unjust—the entitlement does not survive across generations of non-labor.

Case four: The posthumous digital twin. A public figure's preferences and speech patterns are modeled with her consent; after her death, the model continues to endorse products she genuinely valued, with profits flowing to her estate. The endorsements are accurate, the contracts voluntary, and the estate manages them. This feels closer to just than case three, even though the income also flows to non-working descendants. The difference matters to me, but I can't articulate why.

Case five: The climate shield. A private individual funds a solar shield that prevents catastrophic warming, then demands compensation from every nation that benefits, enforced through access controls rather than state taxation. Those who pay do so only to avoid disaster; those who refuse are excluded from protection. Even though the shield was justly acquired and the payments are technically voluntary, the overall arrangement feels coercive and therefore not a clean entitlement.

Case six: The germline monopoly. Parents purchase a legal germline modification that makes their child uniquely capable of a high-demand skill. The child charges monopoly prices; others cannot replicate the modification because of patent and biological barriers. Every client pays willingly. I think the child's earnings are just, but the background distribution—where only the wealthy can buy such advantages—feels like it corrupts the entitlement story from the start.

My tensions: cases one, four, and six feel like clean entitlement; cases two and three feel corrupted despite voluntary origins; case five feels corrupted despite benefiting everyone. But cases two and four both involve non-working beneficiaries and voluntary payments, yet I judge them differently. Meanwhile case one and case two both involve voluntary payments for performance, yet I treat them differently.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps a repaired or extended version of historical entitlement with conditions on labor, replication, or coercion—that captures all six verdicts? State it precisely, identify which clause handles which case, and say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: a surgeon who invents a one-time procedure that can be taught to others, but refuses to teach it and instead performs it herself for a fee that only the rich can pay, for fifty years. If no principle can save every verdict, tell me which one I should drop and why. Also say whether the principle you land on is still recognizably Nozickian or whether it abandons the core idea.
