I'm a journalist covering the philosophy of science and mathematics, and I've agreed to present a view—rather than merely moderate—at an interdisciplinary seminar next month on whether completed infinite totalities carry genuine ontological weight. The room will be split between analytic philosophers of mathematics for whom Cantorian cardinal arithmetic is the unavoidable neutral framework, philosophers of physics wondering whether cosmological models genuinely require actual infinities or only very large finite approximations, a mathematical finitist who accepts arithmetic but rejects any completed infinite collection, and a structuralist who regards talk of "the ontology of sets" as already a category mistake. I want to defend the claim that Hilbert's hotel should be read not as an intuitive vindication of actual infinity, but as a reductio of applying cardinal reasoning to metaphysics: specifically, that the hotel scenario smuggles a procedural, step-by-step intuition—potential infinity—into what it claims is a completed actual state, thereby generating a determinacy gap about the occupancy status of an arbitrary room that only makes sense if the infinity is never fully given. Please prepare me for the objections in the order they'll likely come, phrased as each of those subgroups would phrase them, noting for each what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest. Include especially the objection I am hoping nobody raises—that my argument still relies on the very bijection logic and set-theoretic notation I claim to be undermining, leaving me using Cantor's tools to deny Cantor's ontology—and tell me how to defend that weak point in the thirty seconds I'll have rather than hoping the room misses it.
