I am preparing the opening address for an interdisciplinary workshop at our university, entitled 'Dramatising the Dead: Ethics, Aesthetics, and Posthumous Representation'. The audience comprises filmmakers, historians, moral philosophers, and legal scholars; the remarks must therefore be philosophically substantive yet accessible to non-specialists. I should like the speech to be structured around a conceptual tension I have been developing: that the deceased, lacking any conscious experience, cannot be harmed in the ordinary psychological sense, yet they are peculiarly susceptible to what I term 'narrative dispossession'—the irreversible loss of authority over one's own life-story, which dramatic representation can either respect or usurp. Rather than resolving this tension, I want the address to deploy it as a framing device for three guiding questions that any ethical evaluation of biopic or historical fiction ought to pose: not simply whether the portrayal is factually accurate, but whether it treats the subject's life as raw material for our present moral or aesthetic projects; whether it assumes a narrative authority that died with the person; and whether our obligations, if they exist at all, run to the dead themselves in some non-experiential form, or rather to the living, who require truthful rather than flattering engagement with the past. The tone should be formal, appropriate to an academic occasion, written in British English, and should avoid both sentimental consolation and any call for bibliographic citation. Please write the remarks so that the philosophical idea does genuine structural work—determining what can and cannot be said—rather than serving as mere ornament.
