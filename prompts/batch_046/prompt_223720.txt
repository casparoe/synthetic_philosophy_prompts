I am designing a handout for a graduate seminar on the self-stultification problem facing epiphenomenalism: if phenomenal properties exert no physical effects, then judgments about them—being physical events—appear causally disconnected from their subject matter, yet we treat such judgments as authoritative. Please construct a decision-tree taxonomy that classifies responses by three successive commitments. First split: do phenomenal judgments require a causal pathway from phenomenal properties to the brain states that token them? Second split (for those who deny this): can reference to phenomenal properties be sustained by a non-causal relation such as direct acquaintance or phenomenal transparency? Third split: does that relation, or its absence, leave judgments rationally justified, true only by brute correlation, or systematically erroneous? Label each leaf with a standard position name where one exists—e.g., interactionist dualism, direct-acquaintance epiphenomenalism, correlational error theory—mark unoccupied combinations as vacant with a brief diagnosis of why they are unoccupied or incoherent, and for each occupied leaf state its most awkward commitment, particularly commitments visible only when the judgment mechanism is treated as fully embedded in a causally closed physical system. Include at least one deliberately underexplored leaf: a view that accepts bare correlation between neural state and phenomenal property, denies that this undermines justification, and treats phenomenal reports as reliably true but non-rationally grounded. If standard positions collapse onto the same branch, flag them as indistinguishable within this framework rather than inventing a spurious split. Present the tree so that each branch is labeled by the concrete claim that divides it, allowing a reader to place herself without prior knowledge of the philosophical labels.
