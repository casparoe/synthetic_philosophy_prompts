In discussions of material constitution, the relation between a statue and the lump of clay that makes it up is frequently compared to that between a persistent digital document and the rotating set of physical storage devices on which it is distributed at any moment: in both cases, a higher-level entity seems to survive the replacement of its constituent parts, and both invite the question whether the higher-level item is strictly identical to its current material base. Proponents of this comparison use it to argue that constitution is an asymmetric dependence relation—analogous to the document's functional dependence on hardware—rather than identity, and that the constituted object therefore possesses persistence conditions (it can survive complete material turnover) that its base lacks. Audit the analogy systematically: lay out the point-by-point correspondence between the statue/clay pair and the document/storage pair; identify which features specific to digital artifacts—intentional design, causal discontinuity between substrates, functional equivalence across different hardware—are quietly imported into the physical case; determine whether the analogy's central conclusion, that non-identity and distinct modal profiles necessarily follow, survives once those imported features are stripped away; and, if the comparison collapses under that pressure, propose a more apt analogy for capturing the metaphysical relation between an artifact and the matter that constitutes it.
