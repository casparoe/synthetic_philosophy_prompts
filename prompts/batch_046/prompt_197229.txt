I'm trying to figure out what I actually believe about well-being, going by my gut on cases. A buddhist monk who's wiped out all craving and suffering but also feels zero pleasure or interest in music is in an excellent state. A patient in a permanent coma with literally no mental life, so also no craving or suffering, is in an equally excellent state — maybe even better since there's no risk of relapse. But a guy who spends every day white-knuckling his urges, never letting them become full-blown cravings or suffering, but feeling constant low-grade tension, is NOT in a good state, even though technically he never hits either pole. And an AI that runs with no reward signals or error-pain analogues, just processing data, seems morally neutral — neither good nor bad for it, not peak well-being. Is there one principle that gets all four, or am I smuggling in something extra? If the principle is literally just "absence of craving and suffering is all that matters," then I think it forces me to rank the coma patient and maybe even the AI as top-tier, which I'm weird about for the AI. What's it say about a synthetic mind that has no suffering, no craving, but ocassionally generates false positives in its error loop that don't feel like anything? And which of my four verdicts should I drop if I want the cleanest version of the view?
