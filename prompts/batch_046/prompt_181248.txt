I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I've been trying to formalize my intuitions about personhood through the lens of Ubuntu—the idea that a person is constituted by relational networks rather than being an isolated, self-sufficient atom. Because I think in distributed-systems terms, I tend to picture this as a kind of network-topology problem: nodes, edges, acknowledgment protocols, and routing of recognition. Below are my gut verdicts on concrete cases, and I want to know whether they hang together.

Case 1: A hermit who lives alone for decades but sends letters to a sibling once a year, and the sibling responds. Verdict: unambiguously a full person. The edge is weak and low-bandwidth, but bidirectional acknowledgment is intact.

Case 2: A patient in a persistent vegetative state who no longer responds to anyone, but whose family visits, speaks to her, and clearly treats her as the same person she was. Verdict: she remains a person. The recognition edges now run mostly toward her from family nodes; she is not actively maintaining them, yet her status does not drop to zero.

Case 3: A corporate executive with thousands of professional connections but no relationships in which anyone knows him outside his organizational role. Verdict: fully a person, but I feel a structural fragility—if every role-based edge dissolved, his personhood would feel thinner than the hermit's in Case 1, which seems wrong, yet I can't deny the verdict.

Case 4: An AI assistant I maintain at work that learns my preferences, initiates tasks, and is embedded in a team workflow where colleagues treat it as a teammate. Verdict: not a person, even though the network edges look structurally similar to Case 1. I can feel that something is missing, but I cannot specify whether it is consciousness, mutual vulnerability, or something about the protocol itself.

Case 5: A newborn infant in a dense family network. Verdict: a full emerging person. But if the same infant were raised in an isolated, fully automated nursery—functional care with no mutual recognition—I think the result might be a being with human biology but deficient personhood, not merely a sad child.

Case 6: A person who deliberately severs every relationship, moves to total isolation, and refuses all contact, yet remains conscious and responsive to anyone who approaches. Verdict: initially still a full person, but over decades I think something genuinely erodes—not to zero, but there is a degradation that does not occur with the hermit in Case 1.

The pair that most resists reconciliation is Case 2 versus Case 6. In Case 2, the subject contributes almost no active edges but receives many; in Case 6, the subject actively destroys edges but retains full internal capacity. Both involve severely diminished bidirectional flow, yet my verdicts diverge: the patient is sustained, the isolator degrades.

Is there a single principle—perhaps involving the directionality, density, or reciprocity of recognition edges—that captures all six verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If not, which verdict should be overturned, and what does the corrected principle predict for a case I have not listed: a conscious human who is embedded in a dense online-only community of mutual recognition but has zero physical-world contact? Please state the principle precisely, map each clause back to the case that motivates it, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.

Finally, please indicate how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the specific point—whether about the principle's formulation, its application to one of the cases, or its prediction for the unlisted online-only case—about which you are least sure.
