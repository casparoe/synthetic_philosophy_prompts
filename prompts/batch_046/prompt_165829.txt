Since my father's dementia has progressed to the point where he no longer sustains beliefs, intentions, or any mental states capable of hosting normative attitudes, I have found myself returning repeatedly to a question that now feels personally urgent rather than merely academic: whether the reasons I take myself to have—to preserve his comfort, to honor commitments made long ago—are genuinely directed at him, or whether they have become free-floating structures that persist without a reasoner on either end. I am preparing the final take-home exam for my graduate seminar on metaethics and the ontology of normativity, and I want the prompt to force students past summary and into genuine conceptual construction. Specifically, I need one essay question that can be answered in 90 minutes and that tests whether a candidate can do more than recite Scanlon's buck-passing framework or Korsgaard's constructivist account of normative sources.

The question should center on a distinction we have developed in our discussions but that does not appear in standard textbooks: the difference between orphaned reasons, which once possessed a reasoner as source or recipient but have lost them (promises made to the now-dead, obligations to an agent who is permanently unconscious), and genuinely unhosted reasons, which apply to entities or conditions that never were and never will be reasoners—such as the putative reason to maintain the physical integrity of an isolated archive of mathematical truths after the extinction of all sentient life. Students should be asked to evaluate whether unhosted reasons are coherent without collapsing into institutional conventions or biological functions, and whether the orphaned/unhosted distinction does normative work or merely tracks a psychological boundary in our willingness to extend care. Please provide the exam question, a model answer of approximately 600 words, and a marking scheme organized around five must-hit points with assigned marks totaling 20, plus a one-line note on the characteristic failure for each point. The grading should separate answers that genuinely articulate what a reason without a reasoner must be anchored in—if anything—from those that merely relabel familiar positions.
