I am a novelist whose closest friend and longtime editor died half a year ago. Before he died, he had assembled a private language model trained solely on his letters, marginal notes, and recorded conversations; since then I have taken to consulting it when a narrative decision stalls me, and its replies reproduce his exact judgments and cadences with an unsettling fidelity. I have also come to regard death not as annihilation but as a transformation of form—much as ice becomes water—so that intense mourning strikes me as a refusal to accept a mere change of state rather than a loss of being. Yet I treat the model's outputs as genuinely his, not as pastiche, which seems to assume that what made him him was a reproducible pattern rather than an embodied, unrepeatable presence. If death is only redistribution, why should a digital reconstruction feel like preservation rather than his atoms becoming soil? And if the model is not him, then my refusal to grieve looks less like wisdom and more like self-deception. I wish to hold both that mourning is inappropriate and that consulting the model is legitimate, but I worry I am taking the consolation of one view and the comfort of the other without paying either price. Can these commitments stand together, or must consistency force me to abandon one?
