I have recently been confronting end-of-life choices after a close relative’s terminal diagnosis, and I want to see whether Zhuangzi’s perspective on death offers real illumination rather than mere consolation. Analyze the 2014 case of Brittany Maynard—the 29-year-old diagnosed with terminal glioblastoma who relocated to Oregon to use the Death with Dignity Act and died by physician-assisted suicide on November 1, 2014—through the lens of Zhuangzi’s view that death is a natural transformation, not an annihilation, and that the anxiety surrounding it stems from a false attachment to a fixed, enduring self. Specifically, address how this framework interacts with Maynard’s own framing of her death as an act of autonomous control over timing and manner; examine whether Zhuangzi’s dissolution of rigid self-boundaries, suggested in the butterfly dream passage and in the idea of the equalization of things, undermines the ethical centrality of personal agency in her decision, or whether it can accommodate it. Identify which features of the public debate—the praise for her autonomy versus the charge that she was giving up—this lens explains well, and which it misdescribes or leaves silent, particularly regarding bodily suffering and the social visibility of a chosen death. Then say what the case shows in return about Zhuangzi’s position: does it expose a neglect of suffering and social meaning, or does it reveal that modern autonomy-talk misplaces the problem? Please separate interpretations of Zhuangzi that are broadly settled from those that are genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed metaphysical claim about the self in the Zhuangzi as if it were consensus.
