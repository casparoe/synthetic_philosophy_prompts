I was arguing with my roommate last night (she's in poltical theory, I'm in phil of action) about whether the inefficacy objection actually applies to climate-related meat consumption, and we got stuck on this weird middle step that I don't think I've seen in the literature exactly like this. She started with the standard causal story: my buying one burger doesn't cause any extra cow death because supply is set by agregate demand, not marginal purchases, so my individual act is causally inert. I agreed that's the causal version, but then I brought up what I called "normative inefficacy" -- even if my burger doesn't cause harm, I'm still participating in a practice that expresses approval of factory farming, and the wrongness might live in the expressive content rather than the causal chain. She said that's just smuggling in a different objection, and that if the causal chain is broken, there's no harm to be complicit in, so the expressive point collapses unless you can show my specific expression contributes to maintaining the practice.

I then said maybe it does contribute, but only in a non-linear way: if enough people stop there's a tipping point, so my act is part of a potential coalition, not an isolated input. She countered that this just makes it a coordination problem, not an individual moral failure, and blaming individuals for not coordinating is like blaming someone for not solving traffic by not driving -- structurally misplaced. We kept going in circles. Can you go through our exhange move by move and say who's right at each step? Especially I want to know whether the expressive argument survives the causal inefficacy point on its own terms, and whether my tipping-point reply actually answers her coordination objection or just changes the subject. Also am I missing something about how collective harm works that makes both of us wrong?
