Two colleagues in my philosophy of mind seminar—Dr. Reeves and Dr. Okonkwo—have been arguing about whether dreams are experiences in the same sense as waking perceptual states, taking Malcolm’s verificationist constraint and Dennett’s narrative-construction model as reference points. Here is their exchange.

Reeves: If we take Malcolm’s point seriously, the criterion for having had an experience during sleep is the waking report. There is no first-person observational position inside sleep, so saying “I was flying” upon waking is not the recall of an occurrence but a reconstruction assembled at retrieval. Even REM correlates are only correlates of later reports, not independent access to experiential content during the state itself. Therefore dreams are not experiences that happen in sleep; they are thoughts or narratives that take shape upon awakening.

Okonkwo: That collapses ontology into verification conditions. We do not require an internal observer during a waking event to count it as experienced; we infer experience from neural and behavioral markers. REM involves cortical activation patterns associated with visual and emotional experience, and the waking narrative is our best available evidence for the content of that activation. To deny experience during REM because no one is “there” to observe it is like denying digestion because no one watches the stomach.

Reeves: But the coherence of the report far exceeds the fragmentation of the underlying neural activity. This is precisely why a cassette-style loading at awakening is more plausible: the brain constructs a unified story from scattered traces, and we mistake the integrated product for a replay of lived experience. Confabulation explains the mismatch between report and physiology; recall does not.

Okonkwo: Confabulation explains integration, not absence. The visual system also integrates fragmented retinal input into a coherent field, yet no one treats vision as confabulated. The narrative form of the report may be added upon awakening, but the experiential fragments—fear, motion, color—were present during sleep. You are treating the final edited version as proof that nothing was filmed.

Reeves: Consider a limiting case. A patient emerges from propofol-induced unconsciousness—with suppressed REM, minimal cortical integration, and no narrative-construction mechanisms engaged—and reports a single continuous auditory sequence that precisely matches tones delivered during surgery. If you call this an experience, you have severed experience from any integrated neural substrate and made it a ghost that can inhabit isolated auditory processing. If you deny it, you need a principled reason why REM fragments count but this does not, other than “REM is where dreams usually happen.”

Okonkwo: This case actually undermines your verificationist boundary. If there is specific content-matching without REM or integration, then either experience can occur outside the usual conditions—which means Malcolm’s report-based criterion is insufficient to establish presence—or the report is pure subliminal-driven confabulation. But if you choose confabulation, you must explain why REM reports are not also confabulated. You cannot use report coherence to deny REM experience while using report presence to grant it in the standard case. The case forces you to specify what neural or functional conditions, if any, are genuinely necessary.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: where Reeves is right about verification and reconstruction, where Okonkwo is right about inference and integration, and where each overreaches. In particular, address the propofol case: does it break Malcolm’s criterion, Dennett’s model, or both? Finally, identify what both are missing—whether there is a third option for the status of dream content that neither reconstructionism nor fragment-integration captures.
