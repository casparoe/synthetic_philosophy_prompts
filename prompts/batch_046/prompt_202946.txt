I’ve been trying to stabilize my position on whether death is bad for the one who dies, and I want a consistency audit before I commit to it in writing. Here is the package I am currently holding together:

(1) The core Epicurean argument is sound: once a person is dead there is no remaining subject to experience anything, so death as a state is literally not bad for them.

(2) Nevertheless, dying prematurely is a genuine comparative harm: the dead person is worse off than they counterfactually would have been, and the ground for this comparison is the actual network of forward-looking projects, practical commitments, and attachments they possessed at the time of death.

(3) I accept a presentist theory of well-being: only present mental states, satisfactions, and realized goods contribute to well-being at a given time; there is no “whole-life” well-being that aggregates value across non-existent future temporal slices.

(4) Harm requires a bearer at the time the harm obtains, so any harm tied to premature death must be located in the living person—perhaps as a present frustration of interests that extend forward—rather than in a post-mortem subject.

(5) I reject Lucretian symmetry: it is rational to fear my future non-existence but not to regret my past non-existence, because present interests carry actual normative weight that merely possible, never-actualized interests lack.

(6) I want to maintain that harm is an intrinsic property of a subject at a time, not a relational property holding between distinct temporal stages of a person.

My question is whether these six hang together. Specifically: is there a hidden conflict between my presentism (3) and my comparative deprivation claim (2), since deprivation seems to evaluate a life as a whole? Does the combination of (4) and (6) undercut my ability to say that premature death harms the living person in the relevant comparative way, given that the comparison appears relational across time? Is the tension between (1) and (5) merely apparent—because the rationality of fear tracks the harm of deprivation rather than the badness of the dead state—or is it a deeper inconsistency? Please rank the tensions by severity, identify for each what would have to give and what that revision costs, and flag any pair that only looks incompatible. I am not signaling which commitment I would drop; treat all six as live.
