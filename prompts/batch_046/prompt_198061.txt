I'm leading a ninety-minute graduate seminar for students who specialize in ethics of care and have little background in philosophy of personal identity. I want them to grasp why advance directives pose a problem of authority rather than mere prediction—specifically, whether the "earlier self" can legitimately bind the "current self" when severe cognitive decline has altered values and preferences. To avoid the standard textbook framing, I'm using a distinction I'm calling "normative continuity": an advance directive retains authority not through psychological continuity (memories, traits) but through the persistence of practical commitments that the current patient either still bears or has explicitly repudiated. The carrying case should be constructed, not drawn from clinical literature: a former philosophy professor with advanced dementia who no longer recognizes family, shows no distress, and clearly enjoys sensory pleasures, yet consistently pushes caregivers away with the phrase "I'm done"—which her family reads as a residual expression of her lifelong commitment to intellectual autonomy and not being a burden. I need a teaching plan, not the explanation itself. Specify the exact order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so that normative continuity doesn't collapse into psychological continuity or authenticity; design the carrying case—what it must include and what it must exclude—to make both pure precedent-autonomy and pure present-autonomy look inadequate; map the misconceptions these students will predictably form—I expect they will conflate authority with emotional authenticity, and they will assume that present contentment automatically nullifies an earlier directive—and identify precisely where in the sequence each misconception should be headed off; provide one check-for-understanding question that reveals whether they have grasped the authority problem or merely adopted a sentimental preference for the present moment; and state explicitly what should be left out at this level and openly refused. Do not ask for readings, citations, or bibliographies.
