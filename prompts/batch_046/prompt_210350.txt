After a long argument last night with a close friend about whether an advance directive should bind a now-content dementia patient who retains no memory of having signed it, I want to pressure-test the analogy she kept reaching for. She claimed that drafting such a directive is like being a constitutional architect who embeds a non-amendable clause: the earlier competent self is the framer, the body in its successive stages is the polity that must live under the charter, and family members who wish to override the document are like citizens attempting to veto a constitutional provision they happen to dislike. From this she defended an absolutist position—once the clause is ratified, neither the present “citizen-self” nor its representatives have legitimate standing to amend or suspend it, because authority belongs to the framing moment alone.

Audit this analogy with precision. First, provide the correspondence table: what in the medical case plays the role of the architect, the ratification event, the non-amendable clause, the citizenry of successive stages, the family seeking veto, and the enforcement mechanism (the clinical staff?). Then identify the disanalogies that actually matter for her conclusion: is there anything in constitutional practice that corresponds to revocation or amendment procedures available to the framer before incapacity sets in? Does the analogy smuggle in a false metaphysical unity—treating the self across dementia as one continuous state rather than a succession of distinct experiential subjects? What features of constitutional law (for instance, that a polity survives its founders and can be interpreted by later courts) does the mapping quietly import that the biological case does not share? Once these breaks are on the table, does her absolutist conclusion survive, or was the analogy carrying it all along? If the mapping collapses, propose a better analogy for thinking about the authority of the earlier self—one that does not presuppose the very answer it is meant to defend.
