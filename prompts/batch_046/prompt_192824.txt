I've been thinking on my own about those choices where two good things seem to pull in directions that don't share a common ruler—say, staying rooted in a small community with deep obligations versus leaving for a life of broader but thinner connection, or keeping an old craft intact versus adopting a faster method that changes its character. I don't want names of philosophers or a bibliography; I want to know what any serious account of these hard choices actually owes me. Write the full list of desiderata: the concrete cases it must get right (including ones where the options are both genuinely valuable and neither is clearly wrong), the distinctions it must preserve—especially between being genuinely unable to rank two values and merely being unsure which ranks higher, and between values that conflict and values that are in some stronger sense incomparable—the practical results about decision and regret it must accommodate without contradiction, and the puzzles it must dissolve rather than dismiss, with the specific reasoning or scenario behind each requirement. Flag which desiderata are non-negotiable and which are camp preferences—such as the demand that every rational choice must have a hidden correct answer, or the opposite insistence that comparison is always possible with enough information. If some subset of your requirements is jointly unsatisfiable—particularly the tension between taking hard choice seriously and still allowing rational resolution—say so explicitly, since that may be the most important result. Then briefly outline how three broad approaches fare: the view that values are genuinely incommensurable, the view that they are merely difficult to weigh but ultimately comparable, and the view that all values can be translated into a single common measure. Keep the weight on the criteria themselves; the brief scoring is only to show whether the list is doing real work.
