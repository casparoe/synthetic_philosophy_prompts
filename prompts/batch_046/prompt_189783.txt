I'm advising a doctoral student who wishes to work at the intersection of evolutionary game theory and normative social philosophy, specifically on the conceptual foundations of cooperation. She is dissatisfied with the standard pedagogical framing—Axelrod's tournaments, the basic distinction between one-shot and iterated prisoner's dilemmas, and the summary claim that "the shadow of the future explains cooperation"—because it leaves untouched the philosophical question of whether evolutionary stability can ever amount to normative justification. Given roughly three years and a background in ethics and philosophical logic rather than empirical modeling, she needs a defended agenda that tells her which open conceptual questions deserve her primary energy, which are secondary, and why the ordering is not merely a matter of taste.

Specifically, I want you to identify, rank, and justify a prioritization among three interrelated lines of inquiry. First, the mapping problem: whether the prudential reason for cooperation generated by iteration—the shadow of the future—can be translated into a genuinely normative reason without committing a category error, and what this implies for the philosophical weight we should assign to evolutionary explanations of cooperative behavior. Second, the noise-and-forgiveness problem: how mechanisms of indirect reciprocity and reputation tracking (image scoring, partner selection) behave under conditions of moral error—false accusations, signal decay, strategic reputation manipulation—and whether evolutionary fragility in precisely these conditions undermines the claim that evolutionary dynamics track the conditions of morally justified cooperation rather than merely its strategic persistence. Third, the boundary problem: whether genuinely one-shot cooperation can be rationalized without covertly importing pre-commitment structures, repeated-interaction framing, or identity-based continuity conditions, which would suggest that the one-shot versus iterated distinction is conceptually unstable rather than empirically discrete.

Please defend your ranking with explicit criteria—such as upstream conceptual dependence, tractability for a philosopher using conceptual and logical tools rather than computational simulation, potential to reshape debates in ethics or political philosophy, and the degree to which resolving one question would dissolve or reframe the others. For each line of work, explain what kind of result would genuinely advance the field—perhaps an argument that normative commitment is irreducible to evolutionary stability, or a demonstration that evolutionary fragility correlates with moral salience in ways predictive models miss—and indicate what evidence or argument over the next few years might cause you to reorder them. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I want a strategic, defended agenda that directs my student's limited research time toward the questions that matter most, with clear reasoning about the trade-offs involved in leaving the other questions for later.
