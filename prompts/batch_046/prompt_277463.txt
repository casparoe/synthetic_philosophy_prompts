I am currently working through some readings for a course on the ethics of wild animal suffering and the boundaries of moral patienthood, and I keep returning to the scale argument regarding insects. The basic idea, as I understand it, is that with something on the order of ten quintillion individual insects alive at any moment, even an extremely low prior that a given ant or beetle possesses some form of sentience generates an enormous aggregate expectation of conscious suffering—one that might swamp the moral importance of all vertebrate lives combined. What I find puzzling is how this argument interacts with the widespread view in comparative cognition that consciousness, if it exists in invertebrates at all, is likely graded rather than all-or-nothing: perhaps a simple nociceptive state carries only a fraction of the moral weight of mammalian phenomenal consciousness.

If that gradualist picture is right, then it seems to me we should not assign each insect full moral status multiplied by population size, but rather assign each one a proportionally smaller share—say, one-thousandth of a full patient—before aggregating. But then multiplying that diminished share by 10^19 should yield only a moderate total, not the overwhelming figure the scale argument advertises. So doesn't the scale argument actually depend on smuggling in a binary threshold conception of sentience, where an organism either counts fully or not at all? Or is there a mistake in my reasoning about how linear aggregation works across graded moral weights? I want to understand whether accepting gradualism about consciousness genuinely undermines the scale argument, or whether I am misunderstanding the arithmetic of expected-value reasoning in this context.
