As a journalist who writes about science and the philosophy of mind, I am preparing a long-form feature on whether inanimate artifacts could ever be conscious, and I have found myself returning repeatedly to the dialogue in Douglas Hofstadter and Daniel Dennett's The Mind's I in which Einstein's brain is replaced, in a thought experiment, by a book containing an exhaustive neuron-by-neuron description of his mental life. My editor and I have been discussing the standard philosophical reply—that the book is merely syntax, lacking the causal and semantic integration of living tissue—but I am convinced that there is a deeper conceptual knot that this reply does not fully untangle. Specifically, I want to understand whether consciousness might depend not only on correct functional organization but on what I will call a "living topology": a continuous, real-time updating of a self-model in response to environmental perturbation, such that the system maintains an indexical "now" causally anchored to its present interaction with the world. Even if the book encoded a perfect functional isomorphism of Einstein's brain at every discrete instant, it remains an atemporal, static object; yet a digital simulation running in real time might possess the dynamics without the physical substrate. Please explain the conceptual issue here. Is the book's failure primarily a failure of diachronic integration—the absence of temporal continuity in its self-representation—or does it point to a more fundamental gap between representing a mind and realizing one, a gap that persists even for dynamic simulations? I am not asking for a summary of the dialogue, which I already know, and I do not want a bibliography or references to specific academic papers—my readers will not have access to them, and I want to see whether the argument can be sustained on purely conceptual grounds. Make your explanation precise enough for a technically literate audience, but avoid relying on obscure jargon that has not entered broader philosophical discussion.
