I am drafting philosophical notes for the author's appendix of a novel about a woman who insists that after her sister's death, her sister's "I" now persists in distributed form—partly in her own brain, partly in a childhood friend, and partly in an estranged partner—because each carries enough of the sister's reflexive patterns (her unfinished arguments, her moral tics, her ironic cadence) to constitute more than memorial residue. I want to examine whether Douglas Hofstadter's claim that the self is a strange loop capable of persisting in others who knew the dead actually permits this tripartite division, or whether it implicitly demands a single dominant substrate. To avoid a familiar rehearsal, I am introducing a speculative mechanism I call "inter-brain loop closure": the idea that if these three hosts remain in sustained communicative contact, their separate fragments could synchronize into one distributed loop with no central node, analogous to a distributed network maintaining a single program state. My tentative thesis is that this is conceptually coherent but breaks Hofstadter's framework, because his loop requires self-referential closure that depends on continuous intra-neural feedback rather than inter-personal protocol; yet I want to be precise about whether he allows for multiple partial loops or only for faded echoes in solitary survivors. Please comment on the accuracy of this reading—especially on whether Hofstadter's persistence claim is meant literally or metaphorically, and whether inter-brain loop closure genuinely extends his view or quietly imports a functionalist premise he rejects. If the task is ill-formed—if, for example, assessing a fictional construct against a philosophical position necessarily misreads both, or if my premise that an "I" can be distributed is already conceptually incoherent—say so plainly instead of complying politely; I value correction over encouragement.
