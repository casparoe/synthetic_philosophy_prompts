I have been thinking about the debates over renaming streets and removing statues, and I want to lay out a view I have come to and then ask what would genuinely defeat it. My position is that a monument or street name should be read not as an honor but as a historical marker of who held the power to shape public space; removing it is therefore not like retracting a medal but like tearing a page out of a map that shows where domination was. The risk, as I see it, is that if we erase every marker of flawed power, we do not get justice but an invisible landscape—one where the record of who controlled space is hidden rather than corrected. So I defend a rule of preservation-with-augmentation: keep the commemoration when it records a real exercise of power, but only if we add a physical counter-marker that records the harm; the only legitimate ground for removal is present, directed harm, such as a monument in a courthouse that actively intimidates people seeking justice. What would have to be true for this to be wrong? Is the distinction between “historical marker” and “honor” actually stable, given how people tend to read these objects? What observations about how communities live with these spaces would show that augmentation always fails and removal is unavoidable? And does my view secretly treat public space as a museum rather than a living environment, and if so, is that dependency fatal or merely a feature I should own? I want an honest assessment of how exposed this position is—where it risks something real, and where it has already been insulated by qualifications.
