I have just come away from a long argument with a friend about whether genuinely incommensurable values can ever be rationally adjudicated, or whether every hard choice conceals a hidden common measure—perhaps not utility, but something like life-plan coherence or social meaning. Please write a dialogue between two equally sharp interlocutors to help me see where I might be wrong.

The first should defend structural incommensurability: the view that values such as the integrity of a cultural landscape and the distributive justice of climate-adapted housing lack not merely a shared unit but a shared conceptual framework, so comparing them requires a kind of conceptual violence. The second should defend deep commensurability through practical reasoning: the view that a sufficiently rich, context-sensitive practical reasoning can render all values comparable without reducing them to a single scale, yet still yield determinate answers.

Set the exchange in a concrete situation: both are walking through a city square where a nineteenth-century market hall faces demolition for affordable housing or preservation as heritage. Each must respond directly to the other's actual points—neither should be a strawman. They should discuss whether incommensurability belongs to the values themselves or only to our vocabulary, whether rational choice requires comparability, and whether framing preservation 'as heritage' already smuggles in comparison by translating cultural loss into economic terms.

Write this in polished British English, with both voices equally competent, and do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to specific texts by name—let the arguments stand on their own.
