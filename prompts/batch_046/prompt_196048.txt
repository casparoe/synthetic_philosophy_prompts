I've come to think that Camus's opposition to the death penalty in his guillotine essay isn't mainly the usual humanitarian argument about cruelty or irreversability. Instead, I think his core objection is that execution destroys the condemned's remaining capacity for rebellion — that absurd heroism he talks about elsewhere — by preempting any future act of defiance or meaning-making in the face of death. So his penal stance is structurally tied to his absurdism: state killing is wrong partly because it robs someone of the chance to live meaningfully right up to the end. What would count against this reading?
