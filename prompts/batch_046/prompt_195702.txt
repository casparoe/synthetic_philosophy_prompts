You are a philosopher of film with expertise in the metaphysics of death, and you are drafting a critical essay for an advanced philosophy-of-cinema seminar. Before you begin, take five minutes to map the major scenes of Ingmar Bergman’s The Seventh Seal—the knight Antonius Block playing chess with Death on the plague-ridden beach, the squire Jons’s skeptical commentary on religion and mortality, Block’s decision to distract Death so the actor family Jof and Mia can escape, the silent vision of the final dance of death, and Block’s own acceptance of the checkmate—and identify exactly where the Epicurean claim that death is not an evil to the dead might illuminate or clash with each moment.

Read The Seventh Seal through the lens of the Epicurean argument and its most serious modern critics, but do so with a specific conceptual framing that goes beyond the standard classroom version. Distinguish sharply between death as experiential annihilation—the core Epicurean idea that being dead involves no suffering, no deprivation, and no subject who can be harmed—and death as narrative truncation, the view that a life can be wronged by being cut short before its arc reaches a resolution that gives prior suffering and striving their point. Block is terrified not of physical decay but of dying before he has performed one genuinely meaningful act; Jons, by contrast, treats death with pragmatic indifference, as if it were merely the end of a journey with no further stakes.

In your reading, address the following. Does the chess match dramatize a confusion between fearing the process of dying—the violence of the plague, the bodily failure—and fearing the state of being dead, precisely the confusion Epicurus urges us to dissolve? Where does Jons’s acceptance align with Epicurean wisdom, and where does it miss the force of the narrative-truncation worry because Jons has no quest he believes must be completed? Does Block’s salvation of the family resolve his fear by giving him the completed narrative he sought, thereby suggesting that death is harmful mainly when it prevents such closure, or does the film ultimately concede that once Block stops resisting, death is indeed nothing to him? Be faithful in both directions: do not reduce Bergman’s religious imagery—the silence of God, the apocalyptic landscape, the dance of death—to a mere illustration of philosophical positions, and do not distort the symmetry argument or the structure of deprivation-based objections to force them onto the plot. Identify at least one scene where the film actively resists this philosophical reading and an honest critic must admit the mismatch rather than explain it away. Conclude by saying what the film contributes to the debate—perhaps about whether the harm of death requires an ongoing subject who is deprived, or about whether meaning can be secured only by keeping death at a distance—that a purely argumentative essay could not convey with the same force. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; work only from the film’s common-knowledge plot and from the philosophical ideas themselves.
