I have no formal philosophical training; I have been working through these ideas on my own following a recent bereavement. I have arrived at a position I call the subject-nullity view: being dead cannot be bad for the person who dies, because harm requires a subject who can bear it, and death removes the subject entirely rather than placing them in an unpleasant state. I think of it like asking whether darkness is bad for a lamp that has been switched off — once the entity is gone, there is nothing left to suffer the absence. I also hold that this applies symmetrically: just as my non-existence before birth was not a misfortune for me, my non-existence after death will not be one either.

That said, I want to know what would genuinely count against this view, not merely what feels unsettling. Could the position collapse if it depends on a particular account of personal identity? Is there a coherent form of harm — perhaps what I have started calling retroactive interest-frustration, where life-projects with built-in temporal structures are permanently cut off — that applies even when the subject is absent? Or does the familiar asymmetry between our fear of death and our indifference to pre-birth non-existence expose a logical flaw rather than a psychological quirk?

Please sort your response into decisive defeaters, serious but survivable difficulties, and merely awkward observations. For each, indicate whether a committed defender of subject-nullity ought to concede it or could reasonably dismiss it. Give me an honest assessment of how exposed the position really is.
