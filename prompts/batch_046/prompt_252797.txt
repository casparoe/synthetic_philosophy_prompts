I've noticed a recurring dialectical structure in the continuum-hypothesis literature that I don't have a clean term for. The pattern is this: a mathematician holds that CH has a definite answer—say, false, with 2^ℵ0 = ℵ2—while fully accepting that ZFC cannot settle it. Instead of treating independence as an epistemic endpoint, they propose a normative filter for selecting among models: large-cardinal strength, determinacy, or the existence of a canonical inner model like L[U]. When that filter is shown compatible with both CH and its negation, or is undercut by new forcing, they don't abandon the original cardinal preference; they refine the filter—adding Ω-completeness, generic absoluteness, or a specific formulation of the Ultimate L program. Each refinement is individually well-motivated, but collectively it functions as a movable shield: the core preference for a particular cardinal arithmetic is never directly at risk, because any counter-model can be excluded by tightening the criteria for "admissible" universes. Meanwhile, independence is invoked not as agnosticism but as a methodological license to keep searching for the right filter.

Is there an established concept for this exact pairing—an unfalsifiable preference for a specific value of the continuum, protected by an iteratively refined normative filter on set-theoretic universes, with independence results used as boundaries that license further refinement rather than as closures? If there is no name, I want a definite characterization with boundaries: how it differs from ordinary auxiliary-hypothesis defense in empirical science, from a genuine multiverse view that denies there is a fact of the matter, and from straightforward axiomatic pluralism. Don't lay out the options neutrally—tell me whether this phenomenon is real, what its edges are, and what we should call it.
