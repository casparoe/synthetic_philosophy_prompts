Draft a term-paper proposal (approximately 6000 words, one semester in philosophy of history) that asks whether the boundary between pseudohistory and legitimate revisionist historiography is grounded in a structural feature of theories—such as predictive-risk exposure or the pattern of auxiliary-hypothesis adjustment—or whether it reduces to sociological gatekeeping. Anchor the study in a comparative analysis of Fomenko's chronological revisionism and mainstream adjustments to ancient timelines (e.g., shifts in Egyptian dynastic dating driven by radiocarbon recalibration): both reject prior consensus, both selectively reinterpret primary sources, and both propose large-scale chronological displacement, yet only one is treated as pseudohistory. Use this pair to test whether familiar philosophy-of-science distinctions—progressive versus degenerating problem-shifts, evidential openness, vulnerability to specific falsifiers—can do non-circular demarcation work. Contrast them with Atlantis narratives and ancient-astronaut accounts, which generally avoid precise predictive commitments altogether. State the precise question, justify the comparative method over single-case study, outline the two leading conclusions (methodological structure versus pure social authority), identify the considerations that would decide between them, and assess honestly what could make the project unmanageable in one semester—particularly the risk of oversimplifying either the technical historiographical debates or the philosophical framework.
