Analyze the March 2020 ICU triage crisis in Lombardy, Italy, through the lens of Garrett Hardin’s lifeboat ethics. In early March 2020, Italian critical-care societies recommended that patients over 80 or with severe clinical frailty be excluded from mechanical ventilation once ICU beds were full, while younger, healthier patients were admitted; field hospitals and staff redeployments eventually expanded capacity, but only after the exclusion criteria had already been applied and withdrawal of ventilation had occurred in some cases. Treat this as the episode: the actors are the triage committees, the regional state, and the pool of critically ill patients; the options are universal admission, age-and-frailty rationing, or rapid but delayed capacity expansion; the stakes include survival, distributive fairness, and the legitimacy of state-backed exclusion.

Using Hardin’s framework—the lifeboat with a fixed carrying capacity, the swimmers in the water who must not be pulled aboard if it means capsizing, the claim that complete justice yields complete catastrophe, and the requirement that any coercion be mutual and agreed upon—map the episode with discipline. Who is inside the boat, who is in the water, and is medical capacity genuinely fixed, or is it expandable through field hospitals, staff redeployment, and supply chains? Does Hardin’s model explain why the committees adopted age-based exclusion rather than lottery or first-come-first-served? Where does the analogy fracture: for instance, does Hardin’s silence on internal distributive justice—who receives the best resources once inside—become fatal when Italian doctors had to decide not merely admission but also withdrawal of ventilation from existing patients? And what does the Italian decision to eventually enlarge the “boat” rather than maintain permanent exclusion reveal about Hardin’s implicit assumption that carrying capacity is static? Is the episode a confirmation of lifeboat logic in medicine, an anomaly because exclusion proved temporary and reversible, or evidence that the framework was never designed for crises where the water is cold but not necessarily fatal?

Be explicit where the theory is silent: Hardin offers no criterion for selecting among those inside the boat, no account of whether age is a morally relevant proxy for life-years saved, and no guidance for cases where capacity can be expanded at cost. State whether these silences are deliberate boundaries or deficiencies, and say whether the Lombardy case ultimately supports, undermines, or remains outside the scope of lifeboat ethics. Do not force the categories where they do not fit.
