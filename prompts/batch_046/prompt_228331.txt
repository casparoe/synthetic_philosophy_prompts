I sit on the clinical-ethics review panel of a university hospital. By Thursday we must circulate a formal recommendation in the case of a 29-year-old patient with complete callosal transection whose hemispheres communicate contradictory preferences regarding life-sustaining treatment: the language-dominant left hemisphere requests withdrawal, while the right hemisphere, communicating via assistive board, requests continuation. The treating neurologists propose recognizing each hemisphere as an independent consent-source and acting on the left-hemisphere refusal within 72 hours; the patient's family, drawing on Erwin Schrödinger's Vedanta-influenced argument for the fundamental unity and non-plurality of consciousness, contends that a single indivisible subject remains and that consent should be determined through an integrative, holistic evaluation rather than hemispheric partition. Our institutional policy offers no guidance for split-brain decision-making. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate: it should recommend a concrete course of action with an explicit time frame—whether to honor the left-hemisphere refusal and withdraw support by a specified date, to continue treatment pending further integrative assessment with a defined review point, or an alternative structured plan. The opinion should engage the family's philosophical position seriously rather than treating it as mere religious objection; it should also assess whether Schrödinger's view that consciousness is singular and non-local carries any practical weight for clinical consent when neurological architecture is divided. Conclude by stating what specific finding or argument would cause you to reverse your recommendation, and identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
