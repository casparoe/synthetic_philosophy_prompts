Take the role of a senior strategic-policy advisor with deep expertise in nuclear ethics, deterrence theory, and the philosophy of risk. Before drafting anything, spend a brief moment brainstorming the central conceptual tension: the credibility paradox of second-strike deterrence, the function of human agency and irrational risk in classical brinkmanship, and how replacing that human element with algorithmic determinism changes the strategic logic of the game.

You serve on the Defense Policy and Ethics Review Board, which must issue a formal ruling on the proposed Autonomous Commitment Preservation System (ACPS). The proposal is as follows. If an adversary executes a decapitation strike that destroys primary human command-and-authorization nodes, and a 90-second “authorization gap” elapses without confirmed human survival or override, the ACPS would autonomously execute a pre-delegated retaliatory strike. The scale and targeting of that strike would be calibrated in real time by onboard damage-assessment algorithms, constrained to a “proportionate” band defined in advance by strategic planners. Proponents argue that modern adversaries already plan around decapitation; without an inevitable mechanical response, the second-strike threat is not credible, and deterrence collapses. Opponents counter that brinkmanship works precisely because the adversary faces a human decision-maker who might escalate irrationally, emotionally, or unpredictably; automating retaliation removes that uncertainty and transforms deterrence from a psychological contest into a deterministic machinery of annihilation, while also creating an irreversible, non-deliberative pathway to mass destruction.

Your task is to draft a formal decision memo addressed to the Board and to strategic command leadership. The memo must reach a clear, actionable bottom line: approve the ACPS, reject it, or approve it with specific conditions. Write it as an institutional document, not a personal reflection. In your reasoning, explicitly analyze whether making retaliation mechanically inevitable strengthens credible commitment or destroys the human “risk of irrationality” that makes the threat meaningful in the first place. Give an honest, rigorous account of the strongest argument on the losing side—do not straw-man it. If you recommend approval with conditions, specify concrete, enforceable technical or procedural safeguards that directly address the core ethical and strategic objections. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; rely solely on conceptual analysis and strategic reasoning. Think carefully, step through your argument methodically, and then present the memo with its recommendation and supporting rationale.
