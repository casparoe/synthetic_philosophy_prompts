I have been preparing the syllabus for my graduate seminar on moral psychology and recently found myself in a sustained discussion with a colleague about Bernard Williams’s so-called integrity objection and the related idea of one thought too many. She proposed a summary that I want to test against a more careful reading, but I am not entirely sure where the simplifications end and the misreadings begin. According to her account, Williams’s central claim is that utilitarian moral reasoning demands an impossible psychological detachment: the agent must treat her own deepest commitments—what Williams calls ground projects—as merely one source of utility among others, weighing them against the comparable projects of strangers. She illustrates this with the familiar case of the husband who saves his drowning wife, noting that Williams thinks it is not merely permissible but morally required that he save her without running a comparative calculation about overall welfare. However, she then extends this to argue that Williams holds any reflective deliberation about one’s obligations to loved ones to be corrupting; that the presence of even a single moral reason drawn from impartial morality within the agent’s practical reasoning is enough to destroy the integrity of the relationship; and that, consequently, the objection shows utilitarianism to be not merely psychologically unrealistic but conceptually incoherent, since it requires agents to occupy a standpoint that no embodied, historically situated self could actually maintain. She concludes that defenders of utilitarianism miss the point entirely when they reply that the husband and the impartial agent might converge on the same action, because for Williams the action’s moral status depends entirely on the absence of any impartial reasoning in its genesis.

This strikes me as capturing something important about Williams’s resistance to the moral system, yet I suspect it overstates both the psychological claim and the logical conclusion. Could you help me audit this summary? In particular, please distinguish which elements are faithful to Williams’s position—especially regarding the structure of agent-relative reasons and the specific function of one thought too many—which parts are standard pedagogical simplifications that still preserve the core argument, and which parts are subtle but significant distortions, perhaps conflating Williams’s view with a more radical form of particularism or with an ethical theory that rejects all universal moral claims. I am also interested in whether the conclusion that utilitarianism is conceptually incoherent actually follows from the accurate premises, or whether it relies on the misrepresentations you identify. Please be explicit about what a careful defender of utilitarianism—say, one who accepts the psychological realism of ground projects but denies that they generate moral permissions—would say in response to the corrected version of the objection.
