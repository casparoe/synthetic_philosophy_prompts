I have been working with a colleague to map the precise fault lines between two positions in the debate over the hedonistic imperative—the view that biotechnology ought to be used, including via germline intervention, to eliminate suffering. One of us defends what might be called an abolitionist bioengineering program, holding that negative affect is a tractable target for neuromodulatory and genetic intervention; the other defends a valence-structuralist position, holding that suffering and positive affect are not merely opposite poles but structurally coupled dimensions of conscious experience, so that suppressing the former without collapsing the latter may be conceptually impossible or ethically catastrophic. Rather than continue arguing past one another, we want to draft a joint adversarial-collaboration statement that both of us could sign. The document should contain: first, a numbered section of agreed claims about the current neuroscience of negative valence (including the role of specific neuromodulatory circuits such as dynorphin-mediated kappa-opioid signaling and its interaction with broader default-mode dynamics), about the present feasibility of targeted genetic or pharmacological suppression, and about the normative boundary conditions of such intervention; second, a disagreements table in which each camp's core commitments are formulated in wording that camp itself would accept—particularly regarding the coherence of "valence decoupling," the idea that suffering and well-being can be independently modulated rather than moved along a single axis, and whether a "gradient-only" affective architecture (lacking sharp negative peaks but retaining positive variation) could sustain the contrast mechanisms necessary for preference, moral judgment, and personal identity; third, the cruxes, which we suspect include whether suppression of kappa-opioid pathways can occur without disrupting reward-learning architectures, whether beings with decoupled valence could possess genuine moral agency, and whether the epistemic risks of germline affective engineering outweigh prospective welfare gains; and fourth, for each crux, one concrete empirical finding, hypothetical clinical scenario, or conceptual test-case that both sides accept as genuinely bearing on the question, with each side's explicit prediction or assessment recorded in advance. Where our disagreement is partly verbal—especially around terms like "suffering," "naturalness," or "enhancement"—the document should identify the exact ambiguity and propose a disambiguated formulation that separates substantive from terminological conflict. The aim is not adjudication but a text precise enough that either of us could publish it with our name attached without misrepresenting our own view.
