You are delivering the opening remarks—about twelve minutes—at a university-community ceremony marking the opening of an archive on state violence across regimes. The audience is divided: half are comparative historians of memory culture familiar with Germany's Vergangenheitsbewältigung, Japan's postwar institutional silence, Turkey's denialist state frameworks regarding the Armenian genocide, and American Southern Lost Cause mythologies; the other half are descendants, local activists, and students without specialist training. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Structure the speech around one conceptual distinction: remembrance as retrieval of a fixed past versus remembrance as a present-tense performative commitment that necessarily excludes other voices. This distinction should do real structural work: it should determine which comparisons among the four cases you draw, which you deliberately suppress, and what form of collective silence you ask the room to share. Write for the experts without jargon that loses the non-specialists; avoid treating any national model as the universal template. Conclude not with a general exhortation but with a specific, concrete invitation to the audience. The philosophical idea must shape what can and cannot be said, not serve as decoration.
