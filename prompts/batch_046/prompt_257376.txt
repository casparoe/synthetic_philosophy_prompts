I was arguing with my roommate about our school's gen-ed rules — you can take 'Big History: From the Big Bang to the Internet' to satisfy the history requirement, but you cant take a course on just, like, the Ming dynasty. The syllabus frames agriculture, writing, and industrialization as 'thresholds' where energy flows suddenly reorganize, and it uses words like emergence and complexity for both galaxy formation and the French Revolution. Meanwhile critics like Graeber and Wengrow (and Harari in a different register) want to keep human-scale politics and choice in the picture. What view of historical causation does letting this course count as history actually presuppose? Does it require that macro-level physical patterns are the real explanations and human intentions are basically noise? Or can you defend the practice without buying that reductionist picture — maybe with some kind of pluralism where both scales are real but incommensurable? I'm also wondering if it's consistent with the philosophy departments usual committment that human-science explanations should be interpretive rather than law-like. If we actually took that interpretive stance seriously, would Big History have to drop its cosmic framing entirely, or could it be rebuilt without losing what makes it attractive?
