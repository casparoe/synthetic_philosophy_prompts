Having retired from three decades of work in experimental physics, I have recently begun reading philosophy of rational choice and find myself with strongly held but apparently inconsistent verdicts about backward induction and the centipede game. I would like to lay out five concrete cases, state my judgments plainly, and ask whether there is a coherent principle that captures the pattern—or whether I must abandon one or more of my intuitions.

Consider first the standard six-node centipede game: at each decision point the active player may take a slightly larger share of an accumulating pot, ending the interaction, or pass and allow the pool to grow. Under perfect information, common knowledge of rationality, and one-shot anonymous play, I judge that backward induction applies without exception: rational agents should take at the very first node. I do not regard experimental data showing continued cooperation as philosophically decisive; in my view, such behavior is an error of reasoning or a failure of common knowledge, not a genuine counterexample to the induction argument.

Now modify the epistemic conditions without altering the payoffs. In Case 2, player 2 knows that player 1 is rational, but player 1 is genuinely uncertain whether player 2 knows that player 1 knows this—there is a precise one-level gap in the hierarchy of common knowledge. Here my verdict shifts: I think rational play should reach approximately the third or fourth node, because the induction chain is broken at the point of mutual uncertainty, not merely weakened.

Case 3 keeps the standard payoff structure but introduces what I call an explicit reasoning cost: at every round, both players must solve a progressively more complex logical verification to continue, so that performing the full backward induction itself consumes measurable resources. My intuition is that rational agents ought not to perform the induction if its computational cost exceeds the marginal gain from taking early; consequently, they may cooperate through several nodes not out of irrationality, but because reasoning is physically expensive and should be budgeted like any other resource.

Case 4 replaces the centipede structure with a game I will call reverse accumulation: both players begin with a large shared pool that shrinks if either takes an individual payout, but if both refrain through all nodes they split the original pool equally. The logical structure appears identical to the centipede—each player has an incentive to defect at every stage—yet my philosophical verdict is that reaching the cooperative endpoint is fully rational, not foolish. I do not think this judgment depends on repetition or reputation; even in a one-shot, anonymous setting, I feel the cooperative outcome is defensible.

Case 5 is Case 4 played repeatedly with the same partner and fully observable histories. Here cooperation seems clearly rational, but I want to know whether my verdict in Case 4 was already smuggling in an implicit expectation of repetition, or whether there is an independent feature—perhaps the framing of payoffs as preservation rather than extraction—that distinguishes Case 4 from Case 1 on purely structural grounds.

Is there a single principle—perhaps involving the transparency of rationality hierarchies, the framing of gains versus losses, or the feasibility conditions on reasoning itself—that delivers all five verdicts? Please state it precisely, assign each clause to the case that motivates it, and predict its verdict on a variant I have not considered: the same centipede game played by artificial agents whose decision algorithms are fully transparent to one another but who share no evolutionary or social history. I also ask you to be explicit about which of my five verdicts your principle cannot accommodate without contrivance, and whether saving the others requires me to drop it.
