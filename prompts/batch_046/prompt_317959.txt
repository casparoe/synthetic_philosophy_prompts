I've been thinking about a tension that standard comparisons of French laïcité and Anglo-American multiculturalism tend to flatten. The conventional account holds that laïcité demands assimilation into an abstract, universal citizenship—stripping visible particularity from the public sphere—while multiculturalism protects group difference through institutional recognition and accommodation. But suppose we accept the stronger critical claim that laïcité is not genuinely neutral but operates as an unacknowledged civil religion: republican schools function as liturgical spaces in which a specific historical culture—Enlightenment rationalism, a Cartesian style of universalist reasoning, and a particular affective discipline of bodily restraint—is ritualized as the very form of public reason, and the prohibition on conspicuous religious symbols in public education is best understood not as the removal of religion from politics but as the enforcement of a specific ontology of the citizen, one in which the body must be rendered transparent to the state.

If we take this reframing seriously, what are the most important conceptual implications for democratic legitimacy and social cohesion? Specifically, does recognizing laïcité as an assimilationist ontology rather than a neutral framework collapse the distinction between French republicanism and Anglo-American multiculturalism, or does it reveal that multiculturalism possesses its own hidden assimilationist logic—namely, that accommodating group identity requires the state to fix, categorize, and essentialize cultural boundaries, thereby manufacturing the very particularity it claims merely to recognize? I am especially interested in the consequences for the coherence of "public reason" in deeply plural societies: if neutrality is always the neutrality of some particular tradition made invisible, is the multiculturalist alternative genuinely more inclusive, or does it simply relocate the site of assimilation from the individual body to the collective category? Please develop these implications for democratic theory rather than policy, and address whether this analysis forces us to abandon the assimilation-versus-multiculturalism dichotomy altogether or to reconceive it along a different axis—perhaps around the visibility and legitimation of state power rather than around the presence or absence of cultural recognition.
