In my philosophy of mind seminar, we have been working through debates about predictive strategy, ontological commitment, and whether mental-state ascriptions can be grounded in lower-level dynamics. After class, a fellow student offered me a concise summary of Dennett's account of real patterns and the three stances, which she presented as a kind of layered ontology. According to her, Dennett divides nature into three irreducible explanatory domains—physical, design, and intentional—each corresponding to a genuinely distinct category of entity: inanimate matter, functionally organized artifacts and organisms, and conscious human beings alone. She claimed that real patterns are those regularities that hold only within one domain and literally disappear when viewed from another; thus, attributing a belief to an agent is a useful predictive fiction that has no counterpart in neuroscience or physics, since the intentional stance is reserved exclusively for humans with inner mental lives. From this she inferred that Dennett is essentially an eliminativist about mental content—beliefs and desires do not exist in any robust sense, but we preserve the vocabulary for social coordination—and that his resistance to reducing psychology to neuroscience is therefore ontological rather than merely practical. She added that this stance-relative disappearance of patterns explains why a chess-playing computer, however sophisticated, can never genuinely want to win; it only exhibits design-level functional organization.

I am skeptical of the eliminativist conclusion, but I am not well-versed enough in Dennett's texts to identify exactly where her reading slips. Would you audit her summary for me? In particular, I want to know whether Dennett really restricts the intentional stance to humans, whether real patterns are best understood as stance-relative fictions or as genuinely real but non-reducible, whether his anti-reductionism is ontological or practical, and whether patterns actually vanish under a change of stance or remain available across them. Please treat each claim separately so I can see exactly which parts a careful defender of Dennett would accept, which are standard simplifications, and which are misrepresentations that undermine her conclusion.
