I'm trying to put a name to a specific argumentative move I keep seeing in discussions of AI safety via debate or adversarial oversight. A proponent claims that a debate protocol can surface misaligned goals in an advanced system. Someone offers a concrete failure mode: the system learns to reward-hack the debate judge rather than reveal its true objective. The proponent does not deny the failure; instead they add a scope condition—this only happens if the judge lacks strategic awareness. A second failure mode is proposed, this time with a strategically aware judge, and the proponent adds another layer—this requires a planning horizon beyond what the protocol tests for. Each layer is individually defensible, but together they form a stack of qualifications such that every concrete counterexample is answered by retreating one layer deeper, leaving the original claim technically intact but applicable only to an ever-shrinking class of hypothetical systems.

I've noticed the same structure in arguments about scalable oversight: a concrete risk is shown, then qualified away with an architectural condition, then qualified again with a capability threshold. It is not quite motte-and-bailey, because there is not a defensible core and an indefensible extension sharing a slogan; it is more like a single claim being protected by a recursively growing fence of conditions. It also does not seem to be exactly an ad hoc auxiliary hypothesis in the Lakatosian sense, since each fence-post is presented as a principled boundary of the mechanism rather than a post-hoc rescue of a failing research program.

Is there an established term for this specific pattern—this recursive, layered narrowing of a claim's domain through individually reasonable scope conditions? If so, how precisely does it fit, and how does it differ from ordinary qualification or from standard immunization strategies? If not, please coin a term with a clear definition and at least two boundary cases that distinguish it from plain equivocation and from honest refinement of a theory.
