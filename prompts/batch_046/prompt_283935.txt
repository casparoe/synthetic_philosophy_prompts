You are a senior referee-consultant for the peer-review board of the Journal of Explicit Mathematics and Applied Logic. The board processes roughly twenty submissions monthly that invoke set-theoretic constructions; each evaluation must be completed within ninety minutes by a referee with graduate training in mathematics or philosophy of mathematics but not necessarily in set theory, and any decision must be defensible in two paragraphs signed by you, with escalation to the editor-in-chief reserved for questions of value rather than logic. The board has adopted the following editorial principle regarding the Axiom of Choice: a proof may invoke choice only if the selection it performs is either (i) replaceable by a deterministic rule using only data explicitly present in the problem statement, or (ii) confined to a subset of the domain that is well-orderable or canonically enumerable within the model, and in neither case may the theorem’s practical conclusion depend essentially on which particular object was selected. Before drafting anything, briefly brainstorm the distinctions that must survive operationalization—especially the gap between a redundant invocation and a load-bearing one, between a replaceable selection and an irreplaceable one, and between logical permissibility and methodological transparency. Then turn the principle into a reusable, ordered decision procedure: present it as a checklist of questions in sequence, specifying for each what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a clear reject or an accept-with-required-revision, and where it must halt and hand the case to human judgment because values, not logic, are at stake. Explicitly flag the misfire conditions—scenarios where the procedure will systematically over-accept or over-reject. Apply the procedure to three submissions: (A) a functional-analysis paper that selects a Hamel basis for a non-separable Banach space used in a quantum-field model; (B) a game-theoretic proof that uses a choice function to pick one Nash equilibrium from an uncountable continuum, where the equilibrium selection is essential to the welfare-comparison conclusion; and (C) a commutative-algebra proof that invokes Zorn’s Lemma to establish a maximal ideal in a Noetherian ring, even though the ascending-chain condition already supplies an explicit construction. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer you expect the board will dislike, and conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot do—where judgment remains irreducible regardless of how the checklist is refined. Write formally, think carefully at each step, avoid unnecessary formalism, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
