The Rietdijk-Putnam argument is standardly read as showing that special relativity eliminates an objective present, because inertial observers in flat Minkowski spacetime assign incompatible hyperplanes of simultaneity. Yet this reasoning appears to presuppose that every physical system can be treated as a collection of freely falling observers; for a rotating body such as Earth, the Sagnac effect selects a unique rotational axis and supports a globally defined time parameter that is not relative to arbitrary inertial frames. Consequently, presentism might survive for rotating systems, with the block-universe conclusion applying only to the idealized case of non-rotating observers spread through flat spacetime. Why, then, do philosophers of physics treat the inertial-frame argument as decisive against presentism rather than as a special-case idealization overturned by rotation?
