In my graduate seminar on metaethics, discussion of whether expressivism can accommodate the Frege–Geach problem has stalled in a way that feels deeper than the usual exchange of counterexamples. One camp maintains that the embedding problem dissolves once we distinguish the propositional content expressed from the attitude expressed—treating embedded moral sentences as expressing a complex attitude structured by logical operators—while the other insists that any such reconstruction either collapses expressivism into cognitivism or fails to preserve the inference patterns that originally generated the difficulty. Both sides accept the standard diagnostics: the need to explain valid inferences involving moral sentences in unasserted contexts, the requirement that embedded occurrences carry the same semantic contribution as unembedded ones, and the inadequacy of simple negation tests to settle the dispute. Yet the literature has become a series of parallel monologues, and neither position has moved in years. Is this impasse best diagnosed as a hidden disagreement about what counts as an adequate explanation of linguistic competence—specifically, a clash between those who treat compositionality as a constraint on psychological reality and those who regard it as a purely formal property of the language system? Or does it mask a substantive but unacknowledged divergence about whether moral discourse is genuinely truth-apt in embedded contexts, concealed by shared vocabulary? Given your diagnosis, what would actually constitute progress: a formal result about the limits of attitude-composition, an empirical study of how competent speakers process embedded moral judgments, or a reconception of the boundary between semantics and pragmatics? Please ground your answer in what each side actually says about inference preservation and compositional explanation, rather than delivering a verdict on which view is correct.
