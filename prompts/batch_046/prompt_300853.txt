I spent much of last night arguing with a friend about program equilibrium—whether it reveals a genuine structure of rational cooperation or merely formalizes the trivial observation that agents can coordinate if they can read one another’s source code. We kept circling the same tension: in a program equilibrium, agents submit transparent, syntactically precise decision procedures, which allows them to correlate actions in one-shot dilemmas that standard Nash reasoning treats as inescapable, yet this correlation depends entirely on the absence of hidden state, interpretive ambiguity, or private annotations. My friend dismissed it as a formal curiosity; I think it captures a precise institutional fragility. I want a classroom activity that makes twelve graduate students in a philosophy-of-rational-choice seminar feel that fragility with their hands, not just hear it described.

The session is ninety minutes, with paper and pens, no laptops. I want participants to construct and exchange literal “programs”—decision rules written in a restricted conditional syntax—then play a modified cooperation game over three rounds with rotating partners, experiencing both how transparency enables coordination and how quickly it collapses when hidden interpretation is introduced.

Please design the full activity. Specify the payoff matrix (mutual cooperation 3/3, mutual defection 1/1, exploitation 5/0) and the three-round sequence. In round one, each participant writes a program of no more than three conditional lines in a restricted syntax—e.g., IF partner_program_contains_clause_X THEN I_cooperate ELSE I_defect—exchanges papers with a partner, reads the literal text, and then both choose simultaneously. In round two, each participant may add exactly one hidden annotation in invisible ink (representing private mental state or hidden implementation detail) that modifies how they interpret their own rule, but the partner sees only the original text; choices are made, then annotations are revealed. In round three, partners are rotated and annotations are banned again, but participants may now write programs that reference the round-two experience. Include explicit rules for syntax validation (who checks that rules are genuinely formal and contain no smuggled ambiguity), the rotation schedule, and the debrief.

The debrief should map each round onto a feature of program equilibrium: round one onto syntactic transparency as a correlation device; round two onto the destruction of equilibrium by hidden state despite unchanged surface rules; round three onto whether participants revert to Nash-style reasoning or attempt to reconstruct transparency institutionally. Predict what will go wrong—likely that participants will try to smuggle interpretive intent into the formal syntax in round one, or that they will treat the invisible-ink annotation as a legitimate move rather than a corruption of the mechanism—and say how the facilitator should handle it in the moment. Be frank about what this paper-and-ink simulation cannot reproduce: actual recursive computation of best responses, infinite-order reasoning about an opponent’s reasoning about one’s reasoning, and the computational cost of verifying source-code equivalence. Therefore specify what the facilitator must supply verbally to bridge the gap between the classroom experience and the formal concept.

The goal is that participants leave understanding program equilibrium not as “mind-reading made formal,” but as an institutionally specific arrangement that requires perfect syntactic disclosure, and that the introduction of even minimal private interpretation destroys the cooperative outcome without anyone having technically violated a visible rule. Write this as a runnable activity plan with rules, payoffs, and debrief structure, not as a lecture outline.
