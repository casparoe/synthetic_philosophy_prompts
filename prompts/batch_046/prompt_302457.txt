I'm considering whether to withhold funding from a medical-device startup after my cousin, a biomedical engineer, warned me that the device's core mechanism—relying on cyclic piezoelectric loading—fails above a specific frequency threshold. She insists this flaw won't appear in the public patents because the relevant failure mode only shows up under an industry testing protocol that isn't included in the filings, so reading the documents myself wouldn't reveal it. I have no engineering background and couldn't evaluate the mechanism without years of study; I could try to learn the field, but that seems unrealistic before a decision is needed. She has been accurate about technical matters before, though she also dislikes the founder personally. I'm caught between two intuitions: one says that relying on her word is perfectly rational, since we constantly depend on experts for knowledge we can't verify firsthand; the other says that if I have no path—through study, documentation, or independent expertise—to eventually check her claim myself, then her testimony alone shouldn't justify my belief, and I should remain agnostic. Which intuition tracks the right epistemic principle here, and does the fact that direct verification is practically out of reach change whether her word can stand alone as a reason to act?
