As a novelist concerned with how cities narrate their pasts, analyze the 2020 toppling of the Edward Colston statue in Bristol and its subsequent museum display—damaged, contextualized with explanatory text—through the ethics of commemoration. Take as your central lens the distinction between honorific presence and documentary preservation, together with the claim that monumental objects act as narrative anchors that enforce moral closure on collective memory regardless of venue. Frame the core tension as one between mnemonic sovereignty—the authority to determine which moral arc a public object asserts—and archival neutrality—the idea that relocation and contextualization can fully strip an object of its endorsing force. What does this framework illuminate about the protesters' act, the museum's framing, and the parallel disputes over Bristol street names? Where does it break down, particularly in accounting for the fact that the statue's physical damage is now part of the exhibited object itself? And does the episode ultimately force commemorative ethics into a binary between erasure and endorsement, or does it point to a third possibility that the theory has not yet adequately named?
