You serve as the ethics compliance officer for a municipal public-health department. Every quarter you must approve or reject proposals for community-health-worker incentive schemes, with the mandate that any approved scheme be designed so workers act for morally worthy reasons rather than primarily for pay, reputation, or audit avoidance. You have fifteen minutes per proposal, no philosophy consultant on call, and you must personally sign off. Turn the philosophical distinction between moral worth and acting for the right reasons—drawing on the Kantian tradition and its post-Kantian refinements—into a reusable decision procedure you can run on a proposal's design documents without interviewing workers.

To keep the procedure from collapsing into generic motivation-talk, build it around three registers of reason-tracking that the literature distinguishes but rarely operationalizes together: (a) de dicto tracking (the worker holds the belief that the action is morally right); (b) de re tracking (the worker is responsive to the right-making feature itself, e.g., a patient's vulnerability or dignity, rather than to the abstract classification of the act); and (c) structural reason-dominance (the institutional architecture makes the right reason causally dominant over competing motives, regardless of what the worker introspectively reports). For each register, specify exactly one operational test applied to the proposal's text—budget lines, workflow descriptions, evaluation rubrics, or reporting structures—stating what counts as a sufficient answer, what triggers an immediate stop with rejection, and where the test ends in a judgment call that must be referred to a values-based human decision rather than resolved by design analysis.

Present the procedure as an ordered flow: the questions to ask in sequence, the answers that move the proposal forward, the answers that halt it, and the points where the procedure explicitly hands authority back to you because the issue is normative rather than factual. Apply it to three proposals: (1) a bonus tied strictly to vaccination-rate targets with no narrative component; (2) a flat stipend paired with mandatory peer-reflection sessions in which workers discuss which specific patients they found hardest to assist and articulate why those patients' situations mattered; (3) a hybrid in which bonuses still exist but are administratively masked behind a co-authored "quality narrative" that workers must file. In one of the second or third applications, the procedure should yield an approval or rejection that conflicts with your intuitive judgment, and you must explain why the procedure delivers that answer and whether you should override it.

Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot do—especially whether it can verify an actual worker's present mental state, whether structural dominance is sufficient for moral worth if de re tracking is absent, and whether the three-register framework itself should be collapsed into one. Then provide a brief structured summary in JSON format with the fields: "procedure_name" (string), "registers_tested" (array of strings naming the three registers), "hard_cases_flagged" (array of strings describing the judgment-call boundaries), "least_sure_point" (string identifying the specific claim or boundary you are least confident about), "confidence_statement" (string describing your overall confidence in the procedure's validity), and "application_outcomes" (array of objects each with "proposal" and "procedure_verdict").

Throughout, say how confident you are in what the procedure claims and single out the point about which you are least sure—whether that is the operationalization of de re tracking in institutional design, the claim that structural dominance can substitute for de dicto awareness, or the boundary between design analysis and values judgment. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; the model evaluating this prompt will have no web access.
