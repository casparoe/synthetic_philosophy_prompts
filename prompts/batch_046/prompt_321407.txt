I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, though I read in philosophy of language and interpretation occasionally. I've noticed a specific interpretive pattern that keeps appearing both in philosophical texts and in technical specifications, and I don't know whether it has an established name.

Here are two concrete instances. First, consider a philosophical essay that advances a strong universal claim—say, that all mental states are identical to physical brain states. Within the same essay, the author discusses several apparent counterexamples: phenomenological experience, intentionality, or subjective qualitative feel. The author never explicitly marks these cases as falling outside the thesis's scope. Nevertheless, interpreters routinely resolve the tension by attributing to the author an unstated domain restriction: they propose that the thesis was only intended to cover "third-person observable states," or "states describable in neurophysiological vocabulary," and that the counterexample discussions were included precisely so that this boundary could be reconstructed. The restriction is not grammatically or structurally marked in the text; it is inferred from the prior conviction that a competent author would not present obvious counterexamples without intending them to be bracketed.

Second, I see an isomorphic move in software documentation. A specification states flatly that "all inputs are validated against the schema," but then includes an extensive error-handling section describing behavior when invalid inputs are received. Rather than reading this as a direct contradiction, maintainers routinely reconstruct an implicit exception clause—"except within the error-handling pathway"—that is not present in the document's formal structure. They then treat this reconstructed clause as part of the author's intended meaning and defend the original flat claim as technically correct within its reconstructed domain. In both cases, the interpreter uses the presence of internal undermining material not as evidence of error or ambiguity, but as evidence that a hidden scope condition must exist.

The pattern seems to be this: an interpreter pairs a broad claim with material inside the same text that undermines it; rather than weakening the claim, rejecting the undermining material, or attributing confusion to the author, the interpreter posits an unstated boundary that absorbs the counterexamples. The boundary is justified not by explicit textual markers, but by a prior assumption that the author is coherent and that the counterexamples were included deliberately to be set aside. Once reconstructed, the boundary becomes part of what the text "really means," and is used to defend the original claim.

Is there an established concept for this specific move? I am familiar with broader ideas like the principle of charity, the hermeneutic circle, and Gricean implicature, but those seem different: charity is a general presumption of rationality, the hermeneutic circle concerns understanding parts through wholes and vice versa, and implicature involves recovering unstated meaning from conversational context rather than from internal textual tension. This feels narrower—it is specifically the reconstruction of an unstated scope restriction to neutralize internal counterexamples, using those very counterexamples as the primary evidence for the restriction's existence. How exactly does it differ from finding an implicit premise, from applying a charitable interpretation, and from identifying a tacit ceteris paribus clause? If there is no precise established term, could you coin one, with a clear definition and explicit boundary cases—distinguishing, for example, cases where the restriction is genuinely textually implicit (as in an unmarked but conventional exception) from cases where it is projected onto the text solely by the interpreter?

Please separate what is relatively settled about textual interpretation in these situations from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of language, interpretation theorists, and scholars of authorial intention. In particular, do not present any single framework—whether intentionalist, anti-intentionalist, or pragmatic—as the consensus view if it is in fact disputed.
