Write the opening remarks for a one-day workshop titled "Evidence, Stakes, and Collective Judgment," delivered to an audience split between epistemologists, legal scholars, and policy analysts. The remarks should take pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism as their organizing tension—not as decoration, but as a structural problem for the day's agenda: participants will need to decide whether raising the practical stakes of error should raise the evidential bar for knowledge attribution, and whether disagreements about "knowing" across contexts are semantic shifts (contextualism) or invariant norms applied under different pragmatic pressures. Make the distinction concrete for non-philosophers—use a case where a laboratory finding and a regulatory decision involve the same data but radically different stakes—without losing the experts in the room. The speech should not take a definitive side, but it should frame why the distinction matters for collective decision-making. Keep it to about four minutes, do not include citations or a reading list, and do not quote specific texts; paraphrase positions freely. To avoid generic treatment, build in one specific, somewhat niche complication: the transmission of knowledge attributions up a hierarchical chain—when a low-stakes research team attributes knowledge to a finding that is then used in a high-stakes regulatory context—so that the remarks must address whether that attribution should travel with the data or be renegotiated at each level.
