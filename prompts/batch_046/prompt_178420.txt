I was observing a postgraduate seminar on social choice theory last week, where two participants — Rowan and Sam — became locked in a disagreement about Amartya Sen's liberal paradox. I kept out of the argument entirely, but I would value an impartial adjudication of the points they actually made, step by step.

Rowan began by stating that the paradox reveals an unavoidable structural tension: if every individual is granted a minimal sphere of self-determination over a pair of alternatives (the liberal condition), and if no social state can be changed to one that is Pareto-inferior (Pareto efficiency), then for certain preference profiles no consistent social decision rule can satisfy both. Rowan used the familiar two-person illustration involving a single book and contrasting attitudes toward reading it, but then advanced a more specific claim: the contradiction only emerges because we permit preferences that concern others' choices within those others' protected spheres to enter the aggregation. Rowan argued that if we restrict the domain to exclude such preferences, treating them as illegitimate inputs to social choice, the paradox dissolves entirely, leaving both minimal liberal rights and Pareto efficiency intact.

Sam replied that this restriction is not a neutral technical amendment but a substantive moral judgement disguised as formal housekeeping. Real-world preferences are rarely self-contained; choices about labour, consumption, or environment inevitably affect others. More fundamentally, Sam insisted, Sen's liberal condition is intended as a non-consequentialist veto power, not as one weight among others in an aggregative calculus. By excising preferences about others' spheres, Rowan smuggles in a perfectionist distinction between legitimate and illegitimate preferences — precisely the kind of substantive theory of the good that liberal neutrality is meant to avoid. Sam concluded that the paradox therefore survives as a genuine demonstration that unrestricted Pareto efficiency and minimal liberalism pull in opposite directions.

Rowan then proposed a two-tier resolution. In tier one, each agent possesses a protected sphere of self-regarding alternatives guarded by an individual veto. In tier two, preferences that cross personal boundaries are aggregated according to standard efficiency rules. Rowan maintained that this preserves the spirit of the liberal condition — no agent is forced into an alternative they reject within their sphere — while allowing Pareto improvements in the interpersonal domain. Rowan added that the boundary between tiers is defined procedurally, not by any particular conception of human flourishing, so the model avoids perfectionism.

Sam objected that the boundary problem is fatal. Determining which alternatives belong in tier one and which in tier two is itself a social choice problem that requires prior aggregation of preferences about the boundary itself. This generates either circularity or an infinite regress. Sam further noted that in Sen's formal statement, the liberal condition applies pairwise across the full domain of alternatives; pre-partitioning the domain violates the structural logic of the condition. Consequently, Rowan's model does not resolve the paradox but redefines liberal rights out of existence, saving efficiency only by abandoning the unconditional nature of the veto.

Rowan conceded that boundary disputes are genuinely difficult, but countered that every institutional order must draw such lines, and the practical difficulty does not invalidate the two-tier architecture. Rowan then suggested that both of them might be overlooking a deeper ambiguity: perhaps the paradox shows not that liberalism and efficiency are strictly incompatible, but that "minimal liberalism" is ambiguous between an outcome-based guarantee (each person receives their preferred option within a protected pair) and a procedural constraint on the decision mechanism (the mechanism must contain veto points regardless of the outcome). If interpreted procedurally, Rowan argued, the conflict with Pareto might soften, because the mechanism can respect veto structures without demanding that every preference profile yield a Pareto-optimal social state.

Please go through this exchange point by point and adjudicate who is right at each step. Is Rowan correct that excluding preferences about others' spheres dissolves the paradox? Is Sam right that this exclusion is perfectionist and violates liberal neutrality? Does the two-tier model genuinely resolve the tension, or does Sam's regress objection undermine it? Is Sam correct about the formal structure of Sen's condition? And is Rowan's closing distinction between procedural and outcome-based liberalism a genuine resolution, or does it alter the subject? Say what, if anything, both parties are missing — perhaps regarding how preference intensity or institutional design interacts with veto structures — and present your answer in British English.
