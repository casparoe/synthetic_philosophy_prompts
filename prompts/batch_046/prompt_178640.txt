Having spent thirty-five years in experimental condensed-matter physics before turning to philosophy in retirement, I have been struck by how demarcation problems in the natural sciences differ from those in historiography. In a recent reading group on pseudohistory, we examined Fomenko's New Chronology—whose statistical arguments about duplicated historical records borrow probabilistic methods familiar to me from statistical mechanics—alongside Atlantis narratives that invoke allegedly suppressed archaeological data to claim prehistoric technological sophistication. Both seem to fail not merely because they are empirically false, but because they transpose evidential norms across domains in a conceptually dubious way. Give me a precise example of a conceptual issue in pseudohistory and demarcation—one that does not require bibliographic citation or reference to obscure secondary literature—that isolates this transposition problem: specifically, an issue that asks whether a pseudohistorical claim can be criticized for applying a legitimate disciplinary criterion (such as replicability or probabilistic independence) outside the domain in which that criterion has conceptual authority, and that illustrates the tension using either Fomenko's chronological statistics or the Atlantis claim of suppressed material evidence. I want the example formulated clearly enough that it could guide a discussion among philosophers of science who have no specialized training in ancient history, yet nuanced enough to show why simple empirical refutation is insufficient to capture what goes wrong conceptually.
