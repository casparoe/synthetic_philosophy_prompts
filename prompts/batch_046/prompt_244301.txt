I’ve just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend—she practices international-development law, I’m working through comparative political ethics—about whether corruption is a universal moral category or one that is indexed to the institutional role it violates, and I want to see if my scattered gut judgments actually cohere or whether I’m hiding contradictions behind cultural sympathy. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. First, when a Japanese firm sends a lavish but fully registered, reciprocal ceremonial gift to a partner after contract signing, with no expectation of preferential treatment, that strikes me as etiquette, not corruption. Second, when a municipal councilor awards a no-bid contract to a cousin and the cousin pays for the councilor’s child’s private tuition out of project margins, that is unambiguously corrupt. Third, in a patron-client society where voters bring seasonal produce and small labor favors to a local mediator who then advocates for them at the district office—keeping no private surplus but distributing state resources according to loyalty—my instinct is that this is corrupt, though I feel uneasy labeling it theft rather than politics. Fourth, in a society with virtually no formal state apparatus, where the same mediator is literally the only bridge to external markets and the “tribute” maintains collective access rather than personal enrichment, my instinct flips: it is structural reciprocity, not corruption. Fifth, when a Western auditor applies a uniform “zero-gift” standard to both contexts, refusing to distinguish institutional absence from institutional capture and thereby excluding the latter society from trade partnerships, I think the auditor is wrong—perhaps imposing a kind of normative imperialism—yet I cannot articulate why without seeming to excuse Case Three.

Is there a single principle—perhaps something like corruption being the diversion of resources entrusted to an institutional role away from that role’s own public-purpose norms, where the relevant norms are those internal to the role rather than imported from outside—that delivers all five verdicts? If so, state it precisely, identify which clause saves Case Four without excusing Case Three, and say what it predicts for a case I keep going back and forth on: a physician in a resource-scarce public hospital who accepts homemade meals from patients’ families, not as payment for specific treatments but as acknowledgment of care, in a culture where refusing food is a grave social insult. If no principle can save every verdict, tell me which one I should overturn, what simpler principle replaces it, and which of my five judgments it must abandon.
