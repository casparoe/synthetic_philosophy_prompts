I'm drafting the take-home final for an advanced philosophy seminar (final-year undergraduates and early postgraduates) on value, mortality, and the long future. I want one essay question that works with Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture — the claim that human extinction is bad not merely for the deaths it entails, but because it removes the future context that gives present projects their point — yet forces students well past mere summary.

To achieve that, embed the following discontinuity scenario directly into the question: suppose humanity is succeeded not by void, but by entities (call them the Successors) who preserve every human achievement with perfect fidelity, continue mathematics, art, and science in technically superior forms, and ensure no knowledge is lost. Crucially, however, the Successors lack any emotional or conceptual connection to human life: they understand our theorems but not why we pursued them, maintain our archives but feel none of our concerns.

Ask students to determine whether, on Scheffler's own logic, this discontinuity rescues the present value of our activities or instead exposes that the conjecture covertly depends on an unexamined condition — something like interpretive kinship — that makes present value hostage to a very specific kind of future audience. The best answers should distinguish value-as-continuation from value-as-recognition, and should say whether Scheffler can reject the Successors without begging the question.

Please provide:
1. The essay question itself, framed for about 90 minutes of writing and guiding the student toward roughly 1,200 words.
2. A model answer of about 500 words.
3. A marking scheme out of 20 marks, built around five must-hit analytical moves. For each move, state the marks allocated, the specific misconception or failure mode that should cost marks, and the precise difference between an A-grade and a B-grade execution.

Write in British English. Keep your entire response within 950 words. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the student should answer from reasoning and course concepts alone. Avoid referencing obscure primary texts that a student could not reasonably be expected to have memorised; build any necessary distinctions into the question itself.
