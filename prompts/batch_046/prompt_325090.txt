In my graduate seminar on comparative metaphysics, a student recently resisted a realist objection by invoking anekāntavāda: she said that reality is irreducibly many-sided, so no single theoretical framework can be more than partly correct, and therefore two apparently incompatible models can both be “syāt-true” — true in some respect or from some standpoint. The slogan “truth has many sides,” together with its technical cousin syādvāda, was doing very heavy argumentative lifting, but I am unsure which precise philosophical thesis she meant to defend. Could you lay out the distinct readings that appeal could have, treating them as a menu rather than selecting one?

Specifically, I want you to distinguish: (1) a metaphysical reading, according to which reality literally contains contradictory properties distributed across irreducible standpoints or aspects; (2) a semantic/logical reading, according to which every predication is implicitly qualified by a contextual parameter, so that apparent contradictions dissolve into conditional judgments of the form “syāt P”; (3) an epistemic reading, according to which human cognition is structurally partial and perspectival, but reality itself is fully consistent and in principle capturable without contradiction; (4) a normative reading, according to which intellectual humility or tolerance requires us to grant every framework equal assent regardless of differential evidential support; and (5) a deflationary reading, according to which “many-sidedness” is merely a reminder that descriptions vary with interest, scale, or vocabulary, carrying no deep metaphysical or logical commitment.

For each reading, please indicate whether it is a platitude, whether it is philosophically defensible, whether it is false, and — crucially — whether it actually supports her move against the realist, who holds that when two theories make genuinely incompatible predictions about the same entities, they cannot both be literally true. Please also say which readings, if any, commit her to the claim that the law of non-contradictity is only locally or contextually valid, and whether that commitment is essential to classical Jain doctrine or an interpretive overreach imported from elsewhere.

Finally, apply what you take to be the best-supported reading to the case I keep returning to: quantum mechanics and Bohmian mechanics appear to make contradictory claims about whether particle positions have determinate values prior to measurement, yet both reproduce the same empirical predictions. Would the anekāntavāda-inspired pluralist be forced to say both are syāt-true, and if so, in precisely what sense? If not, what does her position actually predict about this standoff?
