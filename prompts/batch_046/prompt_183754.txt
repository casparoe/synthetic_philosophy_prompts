Please give an intuitive but conceptually precise explanation of how approximate assertion challenges unified norms of assertion—specifically, whether a knowledge norm can accommodate assertions that are deliberately and transparently false in a strict literal sense yet true within an intended margin of approximation. Consider concrete cases: a physicist stating that gravitational acceleration is 9.8 m/s² while knowing the more precise value is 9.80665 m/s²; a physician saying that a treatment succeeds in 90% of cases when the trial data show 89.2%; or an engineer reporting that a bridge can bear ten tons when the certified limit is 9.7 tons and the rounding convention is understood by all parties. In each case, the speaker knows the literal propositional content is false, the deviation is calibrated, and the audience is expected to recover the accurate figure or operate within the tolerated range.

Some philosophers treat these as pragmatic violations of a knowledge norm that are excused by contextual convention; others take them to show that the norm is not knowledge of literal truth but something like warranted belief in communicative adequacy, or a graded norm of approximate truth. Explain whether there is a coherent way to preserve a single knowledge norm—perhaps by distinguishing the propositional content literally asserted from the communicative license actually granted, or by arguing that the speaker knows a suitably related approximate proposition—without collapsing into an ad hoc exception. Alternatively, explain whether approximate assertion genuinely forces a split between a strict knowledge norm governing literal content and a weaker belief-based or pragmatic norm governing practical transmission.

In particular, address whether the speaker’s explicit awareness of the precise deviation undermines knowledge that the literal claim is false, and whether the audience’s capacity to correct for the approximation changes the normative status of the act. If the knowledge norm survives, show how it avoids implausibly condemning ordinary scientific and practical communication; if it does not, specify what replaces it and whether that replacement can still distinguish approximate assertion from reckless exaggeration or deceptive rounding.
