I am taking a course in practical ethics and have become convinced that the standard philosophical distinction between corruption—where introducing a price degrades the intrinsic value of a good—and crowding-out—where a price displaces non-instrumental motivation—is too abstract to guide actual regulatory decisions. I sit on a regional review board that evaluates proposals to extend market mechanisms into domains previously governed by gift, duty, or prohibition: specifically, direct cash payments for living kidney donation, surge pricing for essential goods during declared emergencies, and the bundling or trading of referendum votes. We meet monthly with no legal staff, must issue recommendations within ninety minutes, and need a reusable procedure rather than case-by-case intuition. To that end, please construct a decision procedure that operationalizes the corruption/crowding-out distinction together with a third filter I want formalized: the exit-cost asymmetry, which asks whether the party who provides the good can reverse or exit the transaction with a cost comparable to the buyer's exit cost. For each filter, specify the exact diagnostic question, what evidence counts as a sufficient answer, the point at which a negative answer stops the procedure with a recommendation to reject, and the point at which the question becomes irreducibly normative—where the board must vote rather than apply a factual test.

The procedure should take the form of a step-by-step checklist that a non-philosopher board member can run in under thirty minutes, with explicit stop points and clear flags marking judgment calls disguised as procedure. Apply it to three proposals: a scheme allowing direct cash compensation for living kidney donors; a proposal permitting dynamic pricing of bottled water and fuel within forty-eight hours of a natural-disaster declaration; and a mechanism allowing citizens to sell their referendum votes to an aggregator who casts them as a bloc. Include one case where the procedure produces a result that conflicts with common moral intuition—either permitting a practice the board would likely find repugnant or prohibiting one it would likely find harmless—and identify which filter drives the counterintuitive outcome. Finally, state precisely what the procedure cannot settle: for example, whether it presupposes a particular account of human motivation, whether it applies to goods that are not bodily or civic in nature, or whether it can resolve underlying disputes about distributive justice. Be explicit about where the translation from theory to practice forces a simplification that risks misfiring, and do not ask me to consult any bibliography or external citation.
