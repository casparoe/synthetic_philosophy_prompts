I have been working through the St. Petersburg paradox — the coin-flipping wager in which the expected payout is infinite, yet people consistently refuse to pay very much to play. Most discussions I encounter resolve it by appealing to risk aversion or to a utility function that grows slowly. I am less convinced that these settle the matter, because the paradox keeps reappearing in long-horizon planning problems where the stakes are genuinely unbounded. I want to know which open lines of work actually deserve priority. Consider three directions: (A) whether the paradox shows that expected-value maximization is normatively wrong and should be replaced by a different decision rule; (B) whether it is better explained as a descriptive failure — real agents use heuristics or face computational limits that prevent them from evaluating infinite expectations; and (C) whether its real importance lies in how we ought to weigh extremely unlikely, catastrophically large outcomes over multi-generational time scales, such as in climate or institutional design. If you had three years and a solid grasp of probability but no specialized economics background, which direction should you pursue first? Defend an ordering: identify which question is upstream of the others, which is tractable with present formal tools, and which would change your view least if resolved differently.
