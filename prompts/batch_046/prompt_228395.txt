I'm a software engineer leading deployment of a loan-approval support tool used by regional banks. We've finalized two model versions. Version Alpha achieves 96% predictive accuracy across the full applicant pool. However, there is a specific interaction pattern—joint applications with non-standard documentation—where it incorrectly rejects qualified borrowers with complete consistency. This pattern occurs in roughly 0.02% of cases. Version Beta includes a hard guardrail: any application matching that profile is automatically routed to human review. This eliminates the false rejection, but drops overall accuracy to 91% and adds an average three-day delay for those cases.

The disagreement on my team feels like more than an engineering preference. One side treats the choice as a comparison of total outcomes: the aggregate harm from 0.02% false rejections is smaller than the harm caused by reducing accuracy and delaying decisions for everyone else. The other side says that Version Alpha contains a failure mode we can precisely describe and prevent, and that knowingly shipping a system with a deterministic, avoidable error—one that always strikes the same kind of applicant—is unacceptable regardless of statistical rarity.

There is an added layer that complicates both positions. Under Alpha, a false rejection is discoverable within our audit window; the borrower can appeal, and the error is fully corrected within a week, so the harm is temporary. Under Beta, the three-day delay and human inconsistency cause some routed applicants to miss time-sensitive mortgage rate locks—harms that are permanent and cannot be undone. Thus the option that eliminates the targeted error introduces irreversible damage distributed across a broader group, while the "riskier" option creates only temporary damage for a tiny, identifiable group.

Can you help me understand the conceptual structure here? I want to know whether there is a recognized distinction between optimizing for the best aggregate result and guaranteeing against specific, identifiable failures, and whether the reversibility of harm should change how that distinction applies.
