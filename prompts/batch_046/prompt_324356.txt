I'm working through the tension between William James's defense of the will to believe and Clifford's evidentialist ethics in a seminar on philosophy of religion, and I'm trying to determine whether my gut reactions to concrete cases track a single principle or just an inconsistent patchwork. To push past the standard religious examples, I've been framing the problem in terms of what I call high-leverage beliefs—stances where adopting the belief is not merely representational but functions as a causal input into the very state of affairs believed. Here are my verdicts. First, when a community organizer believes a long-disinvested neighborhood can be revitalized despite the absence of current economic indicators, and that belief itself mobilizes the capital and labor that make revitalization happen, I judge the belief permissible and even admirable. Second, when a cancer patient rejects a clear biopsy result and believes she is healthy, thereby avoiding treatment, I judge it impermissible and reckless. Third, when a philosopher working in normative ethics maintains commitment to a particular framework despite serious unresolved objections, on the ground that abandoning it would leave her without practical guidance for her advisory work, my judgment oscillates: sometimes it feels like a legitimate Jamesian adoption of a living option, sometimes like Cliffordian self-deception. Fourth, when a citizen believes in the integrity of an election despite statistical anomalies, because accepting fraud would erode democratic participation, I lean toward permissibility, yet this feels structurally similar to the cancer case—both involve suppressing evidence for a socially important outcome—so I am genuinely unsure. Fifth, when a parent believes her child possesses exceptional artistic talent despite consistently mediocre external reviews, and this belief fosters the child's confidence and sustained effort, I think it is not merely permissible but perhaps obligatory. Sixth, when a CEO believes his firm is solvent despite explicit auditor warnings, suppressing disclosure to prevent market panic, I think it is clearly not fine, though the social-stabilizing rationale resembles the election case.

Is there a principle—perhaps involving what I want to call doxastic leverage asymmetry, according to which belief-formation is licensed only when directed at a future-contingent state that the belief itself helps constitute, and forbidden when directed at an already-fixed state that belief cannot alter—that captures cases one, four, and five while excluding two and six? Or does case three require a separate clause about practical identity maintenance? I want to know whether the principle can reconcile my conflicting intuitions about case three and case four without collapsing into the trivial maxim that one should believe whatever is useful, and what it predicts for a case I have not listed: a climate scientist who believes a particular mitigation technology will succeed despite ambiguous preliminary data, not because the belief mobilizes resources, but because it sustains her motivation to continue research that might otherwise seem futile. If no single principle saves all six verdicts, please say which ones should be overturned and why, and state the principle precisely with the motivating clause for each case it accommodates.
