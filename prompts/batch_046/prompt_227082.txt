I chair the Regional Emergency Therapeutics Committee, which must vote next week on Amendment 14-C to our post-exposure triage protocol. The amendment addresses what our engineers call an interruption-cost infusion: a portable antidote pump must deliver a continuous 72-hour infusion, and once initiated with one patient, disconnecting it to redirect the pump not only kills the first patient but also risks calibration failure that renders the pump permanently unusable. The reference case involves one victim who is 48 hours into infusion; five additional victims have just been located nearby, untreated. Our current protocol directs responders to redistribute all available pumps to maximize lives saved, which in this geometry requires disconnecting the first patient and attempting to initiate five new infusions—though only four pumps remain available after accounting for the lost unit. Amendment 14-C would permit responders to complete the single ongoing infusion if they judge that the interruption cost makes redistribution futile, thereby allowing them to save one life rather than attempt to save five.

The committee is split. One faction treats this as a standard continuity-of-care obligation: once treatment is underway, the clinician-patient relationship generates a special duty that is not erased by aggregate calculations. The other faction regards the interruption-cost argument as a practical pretext for refusing to let numbers count, effectively privileging an identifiable individual over the greater good without a principled basis.

Draft the committee's formal decision memo—approve Amendment 14-C, reject it, or approve it subject to specific conditions—with reasoning that researchers and field responders could actually engage with and reply to. The memo should examine whether the technical interruption-cost feature genuinely alters the moral calculus or merely disguises a deeper commitment about special obligations versus aggregate welfare. It must also treat the strongest consideration on the losing side with intellectual honesty: if you recommend approval, explain precisely why the aggregate-counting objection fails to be decisive in this institutional context; if you recommend rejection, explain why continuity-of-care does not license disregarding the greater number of lives at stake. The document should not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the argument should stand entirely on its own reasoning.
