As a novelist constructing a Black avant-garde painter in Harlem circa 1926, I have given her a literal, almost metaphysical reading of Du Bois's Criteria of Negro Art. She understands his declaration that all art is propaganda and must ever remain so not as a cultural observation but as an ontological constraint: she believes that any canvas lacking explicit representational content about Black life—say, a non-objective study in chromatic relation—fails to qualify as art at all, since propaganda requires a message-bearing form directed at an audience. From this she infers that formal experimentation without direct depiction is not merely irresponsible but conceptually impossible within Du Bois's framework, amounting to a retreat into the pure aestheticism he rejects. I want her dialogue to be philosophically serious, yet I suspect I have smuggled in a false premise. Does Du Bois's concept of propaganda actually require representational subject matter, or have I conflated his broader claim—that all art inevitably expresses a group's conditions and values—with the narrower thesis that every work must be deliberately didactic? And if the latter is indeed a misreading, what becomes of her conviction that beauty and political purpose are not merely allied but strictly identical?
