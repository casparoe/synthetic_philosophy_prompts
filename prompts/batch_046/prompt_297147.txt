As a novelist developing a work of literary fiction, I am seeking a short story that dramatizes a specific conceptual problem in the philosophy of science: the criteria by which we determine that an anomalous system instantiates the same causal mechanism rather than an entirely different one. The narrative should be composed in polished American English.

The setting is a remote deep-ocean research station in the Mariana Trench, where two scientists—Dr. Elena Voss, a philosopher of mechanistic explanation, and Dr. Marcus Chen, a marine systems ecologist—have observed a vast, dispersed colony of extremophile organisms. These entities perform a biochemical cycle isomorphic to the Calvin cycle, yet the entities and activities differ radically: there are no chloroplasts, no enclosed cells, and no continuous tissue. Instead, discrete bacterial nodes separated by miles of abyssal water are linked by conductive mineral filaments, and the energy source is geochemical rather than solar. The causal sequence—fixation, reduction, regeneration—maps precisely onto the known mechanism, but every physical realization is different.

The story should center on their disagreement over classification. Voss should argue that without shared entities and direct spatial contiguity, the interdependence of parts and activities required for mechanism identity is absent, so the anomaly is not the same mechanism but a functionally analogous yet ontologically distinct process. Chen should counter that mechanism identity is fixed by the stability of causal relations and organizational patterns, not by substrate or location, and that treating the finding as different risks obscuring a unified law of deep biosphere metabolism. Their debate must carry real stakes: the station's funding board requires them to file a report labeling the discovery either as "known mechanism, anomalous environment" or "novel mechanism, new theoretical framework," and the choice will determine whether the project is folded into an existing grant line or terminated for falling outside scope.

Please write a short story—approximately one thousand to fifteen hundred words—that renders this dispute through scene, dialogue, and sensory detail rather than through exposition or lecture. Avoid bibliographies, citations, or named philosophers unless they arise naturally from the characters' own voices. Do not rely on the reader knowing any particular technical literature; embed the conceptual tension fully in the narrative action, using the physical texture of the underwater environment and the pressure of the report deadline to drive the plot. The tone should be restrained, precise, and literary. Write the answer in American English.
