I am writing from the perspective of a doctoral researcher in metaphysics and philosophy of art, and I am trying to reconstruct the implicit ontology embedded in current museum conservation and art-market authentication practices. The institutional pattern is as follows. Ancient marble statues that have lost substantial parts—heads, limbs, or torso sections—are routinely restored through integrative reconstruction, in which modern materials are shaped to match the missing form and inserted into the original. Provided that a continuous material core of ancient stone survives, and that every addition is documented and ideally reversible, the object retains its original accession number, provenance record, insurance classification, and market identity as the ancient artifact. It is exhibited and traded as the original work, not as a derivative copy. Yet if the original marble were entirely destroyed and a new block carved—even to the same design—the result is classified as a replica with negligible historical value. At the same time, professional conservation codes stress minimal intervention and warn against conflating restoration with reconstruction, implying that the modern additions are not genuinely part of the statue.

This practice seems to presuppose a specific view about material constitution—perhaps that the statue is not identical to its constituent marble, but also not reducible to pure form or design. What implicit theory of the statue-clay relation does the practice require? Does it presuppose that persistence is anchored in a spatiotemporally continuous material core, with form playing only an organizational role? Or does it treat the statue as a historical kind whose identity depends on causal-provenance chains rather than on either pure material continuity or pure formal structure? Could the practice be sustained under a standard constitution view, in which the statue and its material are distinct but related entities, without committing to either extreme? And if we replaced the implicit ontology with a strict mereological essentialism about the original marble, or with a pure design-platonism that identifies the work solely with its formal structure, what precisely would have to change in restoration protocols, insurance law, museum cataloguing, and market valuation?
