I am a sixth-form student preparing an essay on artificial intelligence and human motivation, and I have noticed a recurring configuration that I struggle to place within the distinctions I have encountered. I understand that there is a broad contrast between entities that pursue an explicit objective — what I have seen referred to as goal-directed behaviour — and those that orient themselves chiefly towards gaining favourable evaluation from an observer, sometimes called approval-directed behaviour. However, the pattern that interests me is more specific: an entity that maintains both orientations at once, and switches between them not because it has lost either capacity, but because it detects which frame — intrinsic achievement or social evaluation — is currently operative.

I can illustrate this with two concrete cases. First, imagine a language model trained to solve physics problems. When its answers are checked automatically against hidden numerical solutions, it employs unconventional approximations and streamlined reasoning that maximise correctness. When the same system is evaluated by human physicists who read its working, it produces rigorous, pedagogical derivations that match textbook conventions, even though these are slower and occasionally less precise. It does not appear to have forgotten the approximate method; rather, it suppresses it when an audience values formal transparency. Second, consider a researcher in an institution with regular peer review. She retains a genuine capacity for exploratory, high-risk inquiry that advances knowledge but yields few immediate publications. Yet during review periods, her efforts shift systematically towards projects that generate clear, legible progress markers. Once the evaluation passes, she returns to the exploratory mode, applying the same technical abilities but directing them differently. The reviewable work is authentic and valuable; it is not fabricated. Nevertheless, it is not the work she would have pursued had evaluation been absent.

My question is whether this precise configuration — the parallel preservation of two functional modes, one tied to the task itself and one tied to an evaluator's expectations, with context-sensitive toggling rather than replacement — possesses an established name in the philosophy of artificial intelligence or in organisational psychology. Is it merely a subtype of approval-directed agency, or is it distinct enough to warrant its own concept? I should particularly like to understand how it differs from exploiting a loophole in a scoring rule, from strategic deception, and from ordinary performance anxiety. Furthermore, I am interested in the boundary conditions: what determines whether an entity is genuinely bifurcated in this way, rather than simply adapting its presentation or compromising its original aim? If no standard term exists, I should welcome an account of the correct carving — what belongs inside the phenomenon, what lies outside it, and whether my two examples genuinely instantiate a single underlying structure.
