After a long argument with a friend about whether human rights groups dilute the term “genocide” by stretching it beyond its legal boundaries, I need to formalize how my organization actually makes that call. I am the senior legal analyst for a small international documentation group; every quarter I must review emerging atrocity situations and, within a 48-hour window, recommend whether our public findings should frame a pattern as genocide, as crimes against humanity, or as neither. The recommendation goes to our general counsel for sign-off, and the stakes are high: the label can activate diplomatic and funding mechanisms, but overuse risks credibility collapse and strategic dismissal by states. My friend insisted that if the moral reality is genocidal, the legal label should follow automatically; I think the gap between moral description and legal naming is precisely where disciplined work happens, and I want a reusable procedure that non-specialist reviewers can run without becoming genocide scholars.

The principle to operationalize is the tension between Lemkin’s original sociological concept—which treated genocide as the coordinated destruction of a group’s social and cultural existence through multiple techniques, not only mass physical killing—and the narrower 1948 Convention framework, which requires specific intent to destroy a protected group in whole or in part through enumerated acts. Added to this is the politics of naming: in advocacy practice, “genocide” functions not merely as description but as a speech act that triggers legal obligations and shapes audience response. I need a step-by-step decision procedure—an ordered checklist or flowchart in words—that separates factual assessment, legal categorization, and communicative judgment; includes explicit failure points; and marks exactly where judgment cannot be eliminated.

Please write this in American English. Deliver a reusable procedure a reviewer can apply in under an hour. It should include: (1) a preliminary filter for whether a protected group is present and whether state or organizational policy is coordinated; (2) an act-assessment step comparing observed conduct against both the Convention’s enumerated acts and Lemkin’s broader techniques of group destruction; (3) an intent-assessment step with specific evidentiary indicators, distinguishing direct evidence from structural inference; (4) a group-scope determination addressing “in whole or in part” and the qualitative significance threshold; (5) a communicative-risk step weighing strategic credibility, stakeholder reception, and category-inflation danger, with explicit criteria for when moral urgency should or should not override legal caution. For each step, provide the exact test questions, flag the judgment calls disguised as procedural checks, and specify required documentation before advancing.

Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical cases. Case A should present explicit official rhetoric, mass killings, and clear group targeting, yielding a straightforward recommendation. Case B should involve systematic cultural destruction, forced assimilation, economic strangulation, and displacement without large-scale physical killing—here Lemkin’s broader framework applies strongly, but the Convention’s physical-biological focus is weak; this is the case where I expect the procedure to advise against using the genocide label in our legal-facing report despite my own moral intuition that the destruction is genocidal in character, and you should explain what the procedure cannot resolve about that tension. Case C should present scattered violence, ambiguous organizational command, and conflicting group identifications, causing the procedure to stall and identifying what further evidence is needed.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure is not designed to do: it is not a substitute for legal counsel, not a predictive model for international criminal prosecution, not an ethical framework for speaking out regardless of evidentiary weakness, and not a historical classification tool for poorly documented past events. Include a brief note on where reviewers must stop and escalate rather than complete the checklist.
