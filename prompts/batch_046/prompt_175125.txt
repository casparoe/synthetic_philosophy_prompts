You are asked to extrapolate the judgments of a colleague—Dr. K., a researcher in inverse reinforcement learning—who has ruled on five cases about whether a learning system correctly inferred a human agent's preferences from observed behavior.

Her rulings are these:

1. A household robot observes its owner consistently carrying recyclable materials to a distant outdoor bin despite closer indoor trash cans; the system infers a preference for environmental responsibility. Dr. K. accepts the inference.

2. In a behavioral study, participants choose an immediate small reward over a larger delayed one, but only after being falsely told that the delayed reward might be canceled; in debriefing they state they would have waited had they received accurate information. Dr. K. rejects the inferred preference for immediacy, treating it as an artifact of a false intermediate belief.

3. A surgical AI learns from demonstrations in which an expert surgeon employs minimally invasive techniques in well-equipped hospitals and open procedures in under-resourced settings for identical pathologies; the system infers a context-indexed preference for less invasive care when technology permits. Dr. K. accepts the inference but insists the preference must be parameterized by available institutional resources.

4. A recommendation engine records frequent clicks on sensational news headlines, yet concurrent eye-tracking and post-hoc interviews reveal the user was only testing interface responsiveness and reports no actual interest in the content. Dr. K. rejects any inferred preference for sensational news.

5. A child refuses vegetables at home but consumes them eagerly at school; parents confirm the child privately expresses enjoyment of vegetables. The system infers a social-context-modulated preference for vegetables that is suppressed by family dynamics. Dr. K. accepts the inference with an explicit contextual parameter.

Dr. K. will now review a proposal concerning a clinical decision-support system that observes an experienced physician prescribing conservative watchful-waiting protocols for low-risk patients exclusively in jurisdictions with high malpractice liability, while prescribing aggressive intervention for clinically identical patient profiles in jurisdictions with tort reform and minimal liability. The physician is fully informed about both legal environments and the medical conditions, expresses no conscious preference for either protocol, and regards both as defensible. The system asks whether to infer (a) a genuine preference for conservative medicine that is revealed only in legally safe contexts, (b) a compound preference that weights patient welfare against career risk and shifts systematically with jurisdiction, or (c) no stable medical preference at all, with behavior driven solely by a context-independent risk-avoidance trait.

Predict Dr. K.’s verdict—acceptance, qualified acceptance, or rejection—regarding the system’s proposed inference. Identify the implicit interpretive rule (for example, informational-autonomy, revealed-preference coherence, counterfactual robustness, or a hybrid) that best organizes her five prior rulings. Specify which of those prior cases remain genuinely ambiguous between the two strongest competing rules. Finally, propose one clearly described hypothetical case—not drawn from her existing list—that would decisively discriminate between those leading interpretations. Present your assessment point by point rather than as a loose general impression.
