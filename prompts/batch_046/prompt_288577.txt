I am a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, but I have recently been working through The Mind's I—the 1981 collection edited by Douglas Hofstadter and Daniel Dennett—and I keep returning to the dialogue in which a complete functional encoding of Einstein's brain is stored in a book. In that exchange, the book answers questions with Einstein-level reasoning yet denies that it is conscious, apparently on the grounds that it lacks ongoing causal interaction with an environment and the real-time structural updating characteristic of a living brain. I am drafting a short essay that uses this scenario to probe a question closer to my own field: whether a large language model running in inference mode, with its weights frozen and only its transient activation patterns changing, occupies a meaningful middle ground between the static book and the living brain. Specifically, I want to propose that consciousness requires not merely correct representational content at a given moment, but a continuous feedback loop that modifies the system's own representational architecture in response to experience; under this framing, both the book and a standard inference-only model lack it, while a system actively undergoing parameter updates during training possesses something structurally analogous.

I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or a reading list—only for critical comments on the coherence of this analogy. First, am I misrepresenting the original thought experiment by treating the book as entirely static, or does the dialogue allow for a more nuanced reading in which the book's responses constitute a minimal form of processing? Second, does my framing unfairly privilege parameter-space dynamics as the hallmark of consciousness, given that biological brains also exhibit substantial structural stability and that much cognition occurs without synaptic change? Third, if one accepts the distinction I am drawing, what follows for claims that current LLMs possess understanding—must we classify them as closer to the book than to Einstein, or is there a defensible intermediate category? Please treat this as an informal request for conceptual feedback: identify hidden assumptions, suggest counterexamples I have not considered, and say whether the central comparison strikes you as illuminating or as a category error driven by an engineering perspective.
