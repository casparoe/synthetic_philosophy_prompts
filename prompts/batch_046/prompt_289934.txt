In a seminar on epistemic logic last week, a colleague insisted that Williamson's anti-luminosity argument and the KK principle are essentially two sides of the same transparency thesis, and I'm trying to see whether that identification holds up. Luminosity demands that whenever a condition—say, feeling cold—obtains, one is in a position to know it obtains; the KK principle demands that knowing p entails knowing that one knows p. Both appear to rest on the idea that mental states are transparent to their subjects, just applied to phenomenal conditions in one case and to propositional attitudes in the other. So if Williamson's margin-for-error reasoning defeats luminosity for gradual thermal or perceptual states, shouldn't the same reasoning undermine KK whenever knowledge concerns properties that come in degrees?

I'm attempting to formalize a defense for my notes: perhaps KK can be preserved by restricting it to conditions modeled by discrete accessibility frames, while luminosity fails because phenomenal access requires continuous, gradient-sensitive relations. But I worry this distinction is ad hoc unless there's an independent structural feature of propositional knowledge that insulates it—something about the fixity of content or the logic of belief revision—that doesn't apply to raw feeling. Could you clarify whether rejecting luminosity for graded phenomenal conditions genuinely threatens the epistemic transparency captured by KK, and whether there's a principled, non-ad-hoc way to separate them?
