I am currently enrolled in a seminar on philosophy of mind and long-range governance, and in our discussions of AI alignment and intergenerational ethics I have kept encountering a pattern that I do not know how to classify. The pattern is this: a researcher updates her predictive model to include a new class of entities—say, potential synthetic consciousnesses, or a radically altered future biosphere—yet the normative apparatus she applies remains anchored to the original set of moral patients. She revises her beliefs about who or what will exist, but she does not revise her framework of obligations, rights, or welfare weights accordingly, not because she has argued that the new entities fall outside the scope of morality, but because the descriptive revision and the normative revision are treated as belonging to separate modules that do not automatically communicate. I have seen this with climate modelers who incorporate novel ecosystem configurations without adjusting conservation priorities, and with theorists of digital minds who expand their population forecasts without revisiting their criteria for moral status. The failure is structural rather than argumentative: no one defends the omission; it simply does not occur as a required step.

Does this configuration have an established name in philosophy, cognitive science, or AI ethics? I am aware of the classical frame problem in artificial intelligence, where an agent must determine which beliefs to update after an action, and I can see a family resemblance; but the classical version concerns factual belief revision, whereas here the stasis is specifically normative and concerns the domain of entities owed consideration. I am also aware that this might look like ordinary status quo bias or like a variant of Parfit's non-identity problem, yet in these cases there is no comparison between harming one group versus another—there is only a silent failure to extend the existing moral calculus to a newly acknowledged population. If there is no precise term, I would like to understand how to delineate this phenomenon: what distinguishes it from simple moral inertia, how it relates to the frame problem when applied to axiological rather than doxastic frames, and whether it deserves an independent conceptual label. I want the carving, not just a bare name: what exactly belongs inside this failure mode, what clearly lies outside it, and where its boundaries sit relative to adjacent concepts.
