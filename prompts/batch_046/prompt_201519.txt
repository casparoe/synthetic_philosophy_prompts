I'm a PhD student working on formal epistemology and I got stuck in a loop while prepping for a workshop on self-defeat in rational belief revision. My study partner cooked up this argument that seems to push any agent with a little self-doubt straight into full-blown dogmatism, and I can't spot where it slips.

(1) Rational updating is just conditonalization on your total evidence.
(2) Now suppose you assign some non-zero prior weight to U: the hypothesis that you're epistemically unreliable about some domain D.
(3) You observe counterevidence E against your belief P in D. If U is false, E lowers your credence in P; but if U is true, E is likely misleading, so observing E also raises your credence in U.
(4) Once U picks up enough weight, you start reflexively conditioning on your own unreliability — every future counterevidence gets discounted through that filter, and you converge to a fixed point where P stays near 1 no matter what data comes in.
(5) So any rational agent who doesn't assign credence exactly 0 to U ends up structurally locked into dogmatism about P.
(6) But assigning credence 0 to U is just assuming you're infalible, which no reasonable agent should do.
(7) Therefore rationality literally requires dogmatism, which can't be right because we think open-minded revision is a virtue, not a failure.

This feels like it proves way too much — it's not the classic paradox where you start out knowing P for sure, since here you only need a little self-doubt. I really want to hold onto (6) if possible, because assuming infallibility sounds worse than being a bit stubborn. Which step should I give up, and what's the real cost? Is there an equivocation between updating on U and using U to discount E, or is the fixed-point claim in (4) just not guaranteed? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then lay out the reasoning, with no preamble and don't restate the question.
