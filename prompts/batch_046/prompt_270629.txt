I run a 75-minute seminar with about 16 advanced students on political memory and national identity. Design a structured in-class activity that makes participants feel the structural impossibility of commemorating a morally compromised lost cause without either endorsing its ideology or betraying its dead. Divide them into groups of four; each group receives brief paraphrased descriptions—no quoted primary sources—of one case: the Confederacy’s Lost Cause mythology, Masada’s zealot martyrdom, the Kosovo Field of Blackbirds narrative, or the Dunkirk “spirit of deliverance” that masks strategic defeat. Their task is to draft a single commemorative statement (a museum plaque or school-lesson framing) that must simultaneously honor the suffering and courage of ordinary participants, explicitly reject the ideology of the defeated leadership, and maintain continuity with the present nation. After they present their drafts, reveal a hidden structural constraint: any statement that praises the rebels’ courage without naming the cause’s moral failure is classified as endorsement; any statement that names the failure without honoring the dead is classified as betrayal. Force them to revise under this rule, arranging the rules so that every revision only displaces the contradiction onto a different clause rather than resolving it. Include specific roles, a minute-by-minute timeline, any needed materials, and a debrief that maps each failure onto what we might call the commemorative trilemma—the idea that fidelity to the dead, moral clarity, and collective identity form a genuinely unstable triad for such cases, not merely a rhetorical difficulty. Be explicit about which feature of this structural tension the game cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Also predict what will go wrong the first time you run it—likely a group finding an apparent verbal escape hatch—and explain how to block it without lecturing. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; present all historical material through paraphrase only.
