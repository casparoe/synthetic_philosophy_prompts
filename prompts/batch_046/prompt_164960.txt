I have been thinking about evolution on my own, without formal training, and I keep returning to a critique I encountered in passing: that biologists often treat every trait as if it were engineered for survival, when in fact many features might be unavoidable byproducts of an organism's underlying architecture—like the triangular spaces between arches in San Marco that happen to hold mosaics, not because anyone planned them as frames for art, but because the arches themselves create them. This idea, that something can look meticulously purpose-built yet be a spandrel, strikes me as both obvious and hard to fully accept.

Write a villanelle about this critique of pan-adaptationism. Use exactly nineteen lines with two refrains that do structural work: one refrain should return to the architectural image (the arch, the triangular space, the mosaic set into it), and the other should return to the error of reading design into presence alone. Move from the basilica analogy through the biological claim—that traits seeming tailored for fitness may be structural leftovers—then turn in the closing tercet to acknowledge that even byproducts can later be used. Render the idea precisely enough that someone familiar with Gould and Lewontin's objection to just-so stories would recognize the specific argument, rather than seeing a generic poem about nature being imperfect. You may allude to the later concept of exaptation, but do not conflate it with the original spandrel claim.

Present the response in two parts. First, the poem itself, labeled The Villanelle, with the two refrains as specified. Second, a section titled Commentary on liberties and contested readings, roughly 150 to 200 words, in which you explain where the poem takes creative license—such as whether spandrels are strictly non-adaptive, how exaptation complicates the picture, or whether modern evolutionary developmental biology has softened the critique—and also state clearly what specific finding or reasoning would change your mind about the interpretation offered in the poem's closing tercet. What would change my own view is a convincing demonstration that nearly all spandrel-like traits are rapidly co-opted into adaptive functions, or a historical clarification that the original critique targeted only the most extreme forms of adaptationism rather than the broader program.
