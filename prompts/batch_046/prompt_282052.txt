A colleague in my philosophy reading group presented the following argument, which she claims shows that the standard epistemicist reply to the sorites paradox is self-defeating. She writes:

(1) The sorites for baldness depends on a tolerance principle: if a person with n hairs is not bald, then a person with n+1 hairs is not bald.

(2) The epistemicist denies (1) for first-order predicates, insisting there is a sharp, unknown cutoff—say, exactly 4,237 hairs—that separates bald from non-bald.

(3) But the predicate "has a sharp boundary" is itself vague: there is no obvious fact of the matter whether a cutoff at 4,237 hairs counts as "sharp" in the relevant sense, as opposed to a cutoff at 4,236 or 4,238.

(4) So the epistemicist must apply her anti-tolerance strategy either consistently (to meta-level predicates) or inconsistently (only to object-level ones).

(5) If applied consistently: she must claim there is a sharp boundary in the space of predicates separating those with sharp boundaries from those without. But then vagueness is not eliminated; it is merely relocated to the meta-level, and a second-order sorites can be run on "having a sharp boundary," yielding the same paradox.

(6) If applied inconsistently: she accepts sharp boundaries for first-order predicates while allowing vagueness at the meta-level. But then her defense of sharp boundaries for baldness rests on a distinction—between object-level and meta-level—that itself lacks a sharp boundary, undermining the motivation for treating the two levels differently.

(7) Therefore, epistemicism either fails to solve the sorites (it pushes vagueness upstairs) or relies on an unmotivated asymmetry.

(8) The only stable alternatives, she concludes, are universal tolerance—which, if extended to mathematical and logical predicates, seems absurd—or a global denial of vagueness altogether, which contradicts our pre-theoretic data.

She asks whether this is a fair diagnosis of the epistemicist's costs. Which step should be rejected, and what does giving it up entail? In particular, is there a principled reason to treat the meta-predicate "has a sharp boundary" differently from "is bald," or does the argument correctly show that sharp-boundary solutions are either unstable or arbitrarily restricted? If you think the argument proves too much, identify the weakest premise and explain why an epistemicist can keep her core commitment without accepting (8).
