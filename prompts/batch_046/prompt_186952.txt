Write a dialogue for my graduate seminar on the mechanics of moral change, one that avoids summary-level rehearsal and instead stages a live disagreement between two equally sharp interlocutors—call them Dr. Aris Thorne and Dr. Lena Vasquez—over why practices such as chattel slavery or dueling eventually collapse. Thorne should defend a modified honor-based thesis in the spirit of Bernard Williams: he holds that norms dissolve not when rational arguments become decisive but when a practice ceases to function as viable “status choreography”—the ritual through which an elite class performs its own dignity and distinguishes itself from social subordinates. Once the choreography reads as vulgar or pathetic rather than noble, the practice dies of internal embarrassment, and moral argument arrives only as post-hoc rationalization. Vasquez should defend an interest-convergence view closer to Kwame Anthony Appiah’s analysis but pushed further: she treats honor-codes as coordination mechanisms that persist only so long as the collective benefits of exclusion outweigh the costs of enforcement; moral rhetoric is decorative, and revolutions occur when material incentives realign, not when hearts change.

The dialogue must contain genuine back-and-forth: each speaker must answer the specific point just made rather than advancing a prepared monologue. They should test their competing explanations against a single contrived scenario—an island aristocracy in which dueling with firearms has become lethally inefficient, yet refusal still signals cowardice and invites social death—and determine whether honor-collapse or interest-convergence is doing the real explanatory work. Both should also confront the meta-objection that their accounts reduce sincerely held moral commitments to cynical instruments of class interest or self-presentation. Neither side should be a strawman; both should be philosophically competent. Maintain a formal academic tone, aim for roughly 600 to 800 words, and avoid citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts and page numbers—the conceptual machinery should stand on its own. Finally, build the “status choreography” idea with enough internal specificity—its dependence on audience recognition, its vulnerability to parody, and its distinction from mere fashion—so that the exchange does not collapse into generic social-science platitudes.
