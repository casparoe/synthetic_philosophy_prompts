As a doctoral student working at the intersection of philosophy of religion and metaphysics, I have been tracking a long-running stalemate over how to read Buddhist anattā in relation to personal identity, and I am trying to determine whether the disagreement is genuinely metaphysical or structurally misaligned. For decades, interpreters have divided between those who read the denial of a permanent self as an early reductionist bundle theory—holding that only skandhas, causal dependencies, and momentary mental events remain, with no further fact of personal persistence—and those who insist that Buddhist practice and doctrine presuppose a conventionally real, functionally unified agent (pudgala) capable of undergoing moral development and bearing the fruits of karma across rebirths. The reductionist camp emphasizes impermanence and dependent origination; their critics respond that mere causal contiguity among mental streams cannot ground the normative continuity required for moral responsibility, since without some principle of diachronic unity the sufferer of a karmic result bears no meaningful connection to the agent who produced it. Defenders of a two-truths framing then propose that conventional designation (sammuti-sacca) tracks a practical self adequate for ethics and rebirth, while ultimate analysis (paramattha-sacca) reveals its emptiness; yet opponents reply that this merely relocates the problem, leaving unexplained why conventional designation should align with moral agency rather than with arbitrary causal chains, and failing to specify the metaphysical status of the mental factors that carry continuity.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a dispute about ontology—about what entities exist—but about explanatory adequacy within different disciplinary aims. Specifically, I suspect that Western analytic philosophers are asking a metaphysical question about diachronic identity conditions ("what makes me the same person over time?"), while Buddhist soteriology is asking a transformative question about what must be altered for the cessation of suffering, and that importing the former framing forces Buddhist metaphysics into an ontology of substance and persistence that it explicitly rejects. On this view, the sides are not disagreeing about the same fact but are evaluating different explanatory demands under one name: one requires a principle of personal persistence, the other requires only causal-psychological continuity sufficient for practice. If that is right, then no new textual discovery, formal model of persistence, or Abhidharmic analysis of mental factors (cetasika) will resolve the debate, because the disagreement concerns which questions are legitimate rather than which answers are correct.

Is this diagnosis accurate, or is there a hidden substantive disagreement I am overlooking—perhaps about whether causal dependence can ground normative commitments, about the metaphysics of mental continuity in rebirth, or about whether conventional designation requires an implicit substrate? I want an honest assessment of whether the impasse is verbal, whether it reflects differing standards of what counts as explaining moral continuity, or whether one framework is misapplying its categories to the other. Furthermore, if my hypothesis holds, what should count as progress? Would it require a conceptual distinction that separates soteriological continuity from metaphysical identity, a formal account of how conventional designation operates without reification, or should we conclude that the framing itself is ill-posed and that the debate should be dissolved rather than solved?
