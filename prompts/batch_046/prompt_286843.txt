As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun auditing seminars in philosophy of science, I have been attempting to formalize the instructor's implicit rules for confirmation. Over the past term she has ruled consistently on whether particular observations support the hypothesis that all ravens are black. Her judgments on natural cases are these: observing a wild black raven counts as direct confirmation; observing a wild raven that is not black counts as direct disconfirmation; observing any clearly non-raven object in nature—a white laboratory glove, a green leaf, a black crow—carries no confirmatory or disconfirmatory weight whatsoever; and when a wild bird's taxonomy is initially ambiguous but subsequently resolved by genetic analysis to Corvus corax, the observation is retroactively assigned the same epistemic status as an unambiguous raven-observation. She has never endorsed the unrestricted logical-equivalence view that every non-black non-raven equally supports the claim, yet she has also resisted reducing confirmation to purely morphological appearance.

We will next discuss whether synthetic biology extends or breaks natural-kind generalizations, and I have proposed the following case: a laboratory-engineered organism possessing the complete Corvus corax genome except for a single pigmentation mutation, producing crimson feathers and phenotypically indistinguishable from a wild raven except for color; it is viable, reproduces in captivity, and is observed in an aviary with no wild conspecifics present.

Please predict whether she will treat an observation of this crimson synthetic organism as disconfirmation of the hypothesis that all ravens are black. Identify whether her prior track record is best explained by a wild-type natural-kind essentialism that restricts the generalization to naturally occurring instances, a taxonomic essentialism that includes engineered genome-matches regardless of origin, or a morphological Nicod criterion that relies on visible kind-membership alone. Finally, specify the single further observation—perhaps a wild albino raven observed in parallel with a synthetic black dove engineered to possess perfect raven morphology but a Columba livia genome—that would most clearly distinguish these three interpretations from one another. I am less interested in the standard textbook resolution of Hempel's paradox than in a rigorous extrapolation of her specific pattern of rulings into this engineered-domain extension.
