I sit on my university's Committee on Epistemic Governance, and we vote Thursday on whether to pilot an "idealized deliberation" protocol for cross-disciplinary seed grants. The pilot replaces our open-floor faculty debates with anonymized project briefs, structured reasoning checklists, and a mandatory 72-hour cooling-off period before representatives cast written, justified votes. Advocates ground the proposal in indirect normativity—the view that binding institutional norms are those we would endorse under conditions of full information and rational coherence—arguing that stripping away status, identity, and spontaneous disagreement reveals what we actually have reason to fund rather than what power dynamics select. Opponents counter that this conflates the epistemic conditions for discovering correct norms with the political conditions for legitimate authorization, and that removing contestation and visibility undermines the very democratic goods that justify shared governance of research priorities. Draft the decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting: recommend approval, rejection, or conditional approval with a concrete timeline and implementation check, treat the authorization objection as serious rather than dismissible, and present the memo as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
