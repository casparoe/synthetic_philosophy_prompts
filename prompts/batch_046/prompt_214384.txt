I'm the clinical governance lead at a 400-bed hospital, and every quarter my team reviews staff proposals to retire "legacy" clinical-administrative protocols—redundant sign-offs, isolation rules, paper consent steps—that clinicians claim waste time without benefit. I need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in Burkean conservatism (treating long-standing rules as carrying "prescriptive" wisdom accumulated through practice) and Chesterton's fence (do not remove a barrier until you understand why it was erected), that my team can run in about thirty minutes per proposal. The procedure must be explicit about how to reconstruct a protocol's original purpose when the risk it targeted never manifested—so-called "silent failures" that leave no incident record—and it must flag "normative drift," where the values that originally justified the rule (e.g., strong paternalism) no longer align with current ethics, without collapsing into pure cost-benefit analysis. Give me the ordered checkpoints, what counts as a sufficient answer at each, the points where the procedure stops with "retain," where it stops with "remove," and where it must hand the decision to an ethics committee because the issue is values rather than reconstructible fact. Mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and flag the conditions under which the procedure will misfire—especially when institutional memory has decayed. Then apply the procedure to three proposals: retiring a mandatory second-physician sign-off for routine antibiotic renewals; removing a visitor-restriction rule for immunocompromised wards that was instituted during a past outbreak with no recurrence; and eliminating a handwritten witness step for surgical consent now that digital signatures are standard. Include one case where the procedure recommends retention despite unanimous staff consensus that the step is useless, and conclude by stating exactly what this procedure cannot decide.
