You serve as the senior standards editor at a safety-critical engineering firm, and every quarter you must review design specifications that will be signed off by regulators who have no philosophy background. You want a reusable, written decision procedure grounded in Hobbes’s geometric epistemology—specifically his claim that demonstrative knowledge is synthetic, constructed from precisely defined first principles that name the generating causes, and that analytic breakdown (resolution) is only preparatory, never by itself sufficient for certainty—to determine whether a specification document is “demonstratively clear” in that sense. The procedure must be something an editor can run in under thirty minutes, include an ordered sequence of tests with explicit pass/fail criteria, distinguish genuinely demonstrative steps from those that rely on analytic inference, empirical guesswork, or undefined terms, and flag the exact points where judgment cannot be eliminated and the editor must decide. Provide worked applications to three hypothetical specifications: one that passes cleanly, one that fails on definitional ambiguity in its opening terms, and one where the procedure yields an answer that conflicts with standard engineering intuition (explain what to do in that case). Be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle—such as whether an empirical claim about material behavior is true—and state its limits honestly. Do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; do not invoke niche modern theories unknown to small models; and write so that someone with no background in early modern philosophy can apply it.
