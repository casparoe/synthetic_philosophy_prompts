I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. I occasionally facilitate internal workshops for technical colleagues, and I want to run a 75-minute session for approximately 12 senior engineers — all with strong quantitative backgrounds but no training in philosophy or forecasting methodology. I would like you to design a participatory simulation, not a lecture, that makes them feel the tension between inside-view reasoning and outside-view reasoning, and that forces them to confront what epistemic modesty demands when the correct reference class is genuinely unknown.

Please include the following specific structure. The participants must forecast the delivery date of a fictional but realistic software module. Each engineer first constructs an inside-view estimate by decomposing the task into sub-components and summing estimated durations. They then receive an outside-view data sheet showing historical delivery times for past projects, but the reference classes are deliberately ambiguous: each participant is given a private 'similarity profile' that assigns the module to different historical categories with conflicting base rates, and they do not know which profiles the others hold. They must place bets — using a point-based scoring system rather than real money — on a delivery window, with payoffs calibrated to reward accurate calibration and penalise overconfidence.

The rules and incentives should be arranged so that participants first experience the precision and apparent authority of the inside view, then discover that the outside view produces overlapping, contradictory ranges because of the ambiguous reference-class membership, and finally face a forced choice: either widen their forecast interval so far that it becomes actionably useless, or retain a narrower interval and accept that they are ignoring part of the evidence. I want the moment where they feel that epistemic modesty might demand a kind of agnosticism that conflicts with practical decision-making to arise from the mechanics of the game, not from anything I say.

In your response, provide: the full rules and a timeline for the 75 minutes; the materials needed (cards, score sheets, or similar); the payoff scheme in detail; a debrief that maps each phase of the simulation onto the philosophical distinction between inside and outside view and onto the concept of epistemic modesty; an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time I run this (for example, engineers refusing to abandon task-level decomposition); and a frank note on which aspect of epistemic modesty — likely the normative question of when one is rationally required to suspend the inside view entirely — the simulation cannot reproduce and I will have to supply through direct discussion afterwards.

The prompt should be written in polished British English, should require no web access or citations, and should not depend on knowledge of obscure philosophical texts. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for further sources.
