Consider the view I will call “radical tranquilism”: that well-being is constituted solely by the absence of craving and the absence of suffering, with no positive contribution from pleasure, achievement, or meaning. This is stricter than negative utilitarianism, which still counts positive goods but weights suffering more heavily, and stricter than Buddhist-inspired accounts that treat the cessation of tanha as opening onto a distinct positive state (pīti).

Within this framing, several open questions arise. First, whether “the absence of craving” denotes a single state or a family—satiety, indifference, active renunciation—and whether these carry different moral or phenomenological weights. Second, whether suffering can be defined purely phenomenologically or necessarily includes a conative component (the desire that it cease), which bears on whether tranquilism is self-defeating if eliminating suffering requires a prior craving for its elimination. Third, whether the view implies an obligation to induce permanent anaesthesia or deep meditative equanimity, or whether there is a defensible distinction between “natural” and “induced” absence. Fourth, how tranquilism relates to the philosophy of death: if non-existence is the limit case of zero craving and zero suffering, does the view entail that bringing about non-existence is always best?

Of these lines of inquiry, which are upstream of the others, which are tractable without heavy empirical neuroscience, and which—if settled—would most change our assessment of whether radical tranquilism is coherent rather than merely austere? Give me a dependency-aware prioritisation: identify the conceptual cruxes that other questions implicitly depend on, note which are likely dead ends, and defend where limited philosophical effort ought to go.
