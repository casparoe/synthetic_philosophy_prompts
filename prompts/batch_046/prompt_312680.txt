I've been working through the moral status of local conventions for a blog post I'm drafting, and I find my own intuitions pulling in directions I can't quite reconcile. I read rationalist forums and decision-theory writing regularly, but I'm not a professional moral philosopher, so I'd like you to take the role of an expert in social philosophy and normative ethics: please think through this carefully, begin with some brief brainstorming about how to classify conventional obligations, and then lay out the reasoning precisely.

Here are my verdicts on six concrete cases. First, in a culture with strict queueing norms, both a visitor and a lifelong local who philosophically rejects queuing are wrong to jump the line—yet the wrongness feels different in each instance. Second, in Germany a pedestrian waits alone at a red light with no traffic present; a visitor crosses. I think this is wrong, but only in a thin, script-following way, with no direct harm done. Third, in the US a diner refuses to tip: wrong, apparently because the wage structure makes the gratuity part of compensation. Fourth, in Japan a visitor tips a waiter and causes visible discomfort: wrong, but here the failure seems to be imposing an alien normative frame rather than omitting a local one. Fifth, in a fixed-price culture a visitor haggles aggressively, misapplying a local custom they barely understand: wrong, though this feels more like an epistemic error than a moral breach. Sixth—this is the tension—if obligations come from opting into local coordination, it is unclear why the philosophical queue-refuser in the first case is bound at all, since they never consented; but if they come from some broader jurisdictional principle tied to mutual expectation rather than geography, I cannot explain why the second case (jaywalking with zero harm) is still obligatory.

Can you state one principle, or a small family of candidates, that delivers all six verdicts? For each clause, please identify the specific case that motivates it. Then tell me what the principle predicts for a case I have not listed: a temporary worker in a tipping culture refuses to tip on the grounds that the practice is structurally unjust, not merely that they are opting out of a coordination game. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate, and whether overturning that verdict or complicating the principle is the better repair.
