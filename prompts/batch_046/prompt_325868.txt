I listened to a podcast that tried to explain Imre Lakatos’s idea of scientific research programmes. The host said each programme has a hard core of central theories that scientists shield by methodological decision, surrounded by a protective belt of auxiliary assumptions that absorb contradictions with data. He said Lakatos divides programmes into progressive ones, which anticipate novel facts, and degenerating ones, which only explain failures after they happen. From this he concluded that Lakatos was essentially a conventionalist: because the hard core is protected, empirical evidence never actually forces scientists to change course, and picking between competing programmes comes down to taste or social agreement rather than reason. He also described the negative heuristic as an ironclad ban on criticizing the core, and the positive heuristic as basically a catalogue of permitted excuses for failed predictions. I have no formal background in philosophy of science, but this sounds like it might overstate the anti-empirical side. Is this summary fair? Please separate what captures Lakatos accurately, what subtly misrepresents him—especially about the role of predictive success and rational comparison between programmes—and whether the conclusion that programme choice is merely conventional actually follows from the accurate parts.
