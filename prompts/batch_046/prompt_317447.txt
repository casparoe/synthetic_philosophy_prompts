The claim is strict tranquilism: only the absence of craving and suffering possesses intrinsic value, while all apparently positive mental states, achievements, and relational goods derive their worth solely from instrumental contribution to that absence. My own credence in this claim is approximately 0.30; a colleague's is near 0.05. Give me your actual credence, but decompose the reasoning explicitly. Break the question into four sub-components: (a) whether positive affect can be conceptually and phenomenologically separated from the mere cessation of negative affect, or whether joy, satisfaction, and aesthetic pleasure are always reducible to relieved tension or satisfied expectation with no independent qualitative content; (b) whether 'craving' can be defined without circular reference to desired positive states, such that the absence of craving does not already presuppose a prior orientation toward goods that are not themselves defined negatively; (c) whether relational and moral goods — such as justice, love, or knowledge — can be fully reconstructed as mechanisms for mutual craving-reduction without leaving a normative or qualitative residue that demands independent valuation; and (d) whether a value theory that assigns intrinsic value exclusively to absences can generate coherent practical reasons, or whether it collapses into a form of practical quietism in which no change of state is positively required. Assign a rough probability to each sub-component, show how they combine into your overall number, and identify the single sub-component to which your final credence is most sensitive. Finally, specify what kind of argument, conceptual clarification, or empirical observation would move that particular sub-probability the most.
