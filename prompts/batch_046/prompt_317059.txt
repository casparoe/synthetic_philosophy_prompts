Write the opening remarks for a museum exhibition titled Lost Causes: Memory After Defeat, which gathers artifacts and narratives from four cases—Dunkirk, Masada, the Battle of Kosovo Polje, and the American Confederacy. The audience is split between academic historians of collective memory and members of local heritage groups for whom one or more of these causes still carries emotional weight. Structure your remarks around a clear conceptual distinction between commemorative loyalty (honoring the sacrifice, identity-formation, and human cost borne by the defeated) and normative endorsement (affirming the moral or political aims of the cause itself). This distinction should do real structural work: it should determine what can and cannot be said about each case, explain why the Confederacy demands a different rhetorical handling from Dunkirk despite both being defeats, and support a closing statement about why societies preserve the memory of defeat without glorifying the cause. The speech should last about five minutes, comfort and include the non-academic half without condescension, and avoid any false consolation that the speaker does not genuinely believe.
