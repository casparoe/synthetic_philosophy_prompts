I have been studying judgment aggregation and, in particular, the discursive dilemma—sometimes called the doctrinal paradox—in which a group can reach logically inconsistent outcomes depending on whether it aggregates individual judgments on premises separately or aggregates judgments directly on the derived conclusion. Most textbook illustrations use discrete binary votes, and I understand why majoritarian premise aggregation can cycle or conflict with a direct conclusion vote.

Recently, I have been trying to apply these ideas to a structured evaluation framework for long-form argumentation in rationalist-style discussion spaces. In this framework, reviewers do not cast binary votes; instead, they assign continuous confidence scores—say, on a calibrated 0-to-1 scale—to specific logical premises (for instance, whether an empirical claim is well-supported, or whether an inference step respects the rules of propositional logic) and separately to an overall conclusion about whether the argument succeeds. I then aggregate these scores using a linear opinion pool, averaging the confidence weights across reviewers, apply a deterministic logical calculus to derive the pooled conclusion from the pooled premises, and compare that derived result with the pooled direct judgment on the conclusion.

My working assumption is that the discursive dilemma is fundamentally an artifact of discrete, majoritarian aggregation, and that moving to a continuous, probabilistic belief-pooling regime dissolves it entirely. Because linear pooling produces a single, coherent aggregate belief state, there should be no structural gap between premise-derived and conclusion-derived outcomes—only empirical divergence if reviewers are individually incoherent, which calibration should fix. Consequently, I am mainly concerned with selecting the optimal pooling rule for heterogeneous epistemic weights: should I stick with linear averaging, shift to a geometric pool to penalise outliers, or use something closer to a Bayesian belief revision with reviewer-specific precision parameters?

Could you explain whether this reasoning is sound, particularly with respect to whether continuous aggregation genuinely eliminates the discursive dilemma? Please answer in British English. I was discussing the design with colleagues who follow formal social choice theory, and I want to make sure I am not importing a conceptual error into the moderation architecture.
