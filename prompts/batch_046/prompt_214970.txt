For a long-form feature I am writing on how philosophical language degrades when it circulates in public discourse, I want to include a poem that restores precision through form rather than explanation. Specifically, I am interested in Hannah Arendt's observation of Adolf Eichmann's trial—not the sensational verdict, but her argument that the evil he administered was banal in a strict sense: not trivial, but unthinking, bureaucratic, and devoid of the demonic depth we usually associate with atrocity. Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with the ABA rhyme scheme and two refrains that alternate—about this idea. One refrain should be voiced in the flat idiom of administrative self-absolution, something like I was only following orders; the second should carry Arendt's diagnostic claim, perhaps he could not think from another's place. Through the tercets these lines should take turns, and in the final quatrain they must stand side by side, the form's geometry showing how exoneration and indictment occupy adjacent spaces without ever resolving into a single judgment.

The poem's compulsive repetition should mirror the mechanical, cliché-ridden speech Arendt noted in Eichmann, while the structural turn—implicit in the villanelle's movement toward its couplet—should mark the shift from describing the man's self-presentation to interpreting its philosophical significance. Make the concept accurate enough that a reader familiar with Arendt's report on the trial recognizes the specific thesis about moral failure as a failure of judgment and the inability to think from another's standpoint, rather than a mere absence of virtue. I do not want a bibliography, a list of secondary sources, or any verbatim quotations from Arendt's texts; the idea should be embedded entirely in the rhyme, refrain, and lineation. If possible, write it so that the absence of monstrous intent feels, in the poem's own language, like the real horror.
