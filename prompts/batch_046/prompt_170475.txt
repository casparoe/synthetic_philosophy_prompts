student answer:

'The Pasadena game is basically St Petersburg but with penalties mixed in: payoffs go 1, -2, 4, -8, ... with prob 1/2, 1/4, 1/8... Each term in the expectation is ±1/2, so the series is conditionally convergent but not absolutely convergent. That means the expectation doesn't exist as a real number. In St Petersburg you get infinity, which is at least a definite value, so you can apply risk-averse utility bounding. Here there's literally no value, so EU theory can't compute anything.

Nover and Hájek propose using Abel summation to assign an expectation of 1/4, which makes the game fair if you pay 1/4 to enter. But that's wrong because Abel summation is really just the same as Cesàro averaging of partial sums, and that gives 0, not 1/4. Also, since the expectation is undefined, dominance reasoning fails completely—there's no ranking of strategies—so agents should be indifferent.

If we truncate after 10 tosses the expectation is exactly 0, and letting the horizon go to infinity recovers the Abel value of 1/4, so truncation solves the paradox cleanly. Finally, Savage's sure-thing principle requires every act to have a well-defined expectation, so in Pasadena we just have to reject that principle entirely rather than tweaking utilities.'
