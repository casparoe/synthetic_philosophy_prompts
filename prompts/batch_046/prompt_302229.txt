I have been asked to design and lead a single ninety-minute workshop for a small group of public-health policy analysts—practitioners who are analytically sharp but have never taken a philosophy course—on the conceptual distinction between internal and external reasons for action. The request became personally urgent after a recent disagreement with a senior colleague about whether a burned-out epidemiologist can genuinely be said to have a reason to complete a critical outbreak report once all personal motivation has evaporated; I recognized that we were talking past each other because we had not agreed on what conditions must be satisfied for a consideration to count as that particular agent's reason. I do not want you to explain the distinction to me; I want a structured teaching plan that respects the dependencies among the ideas.

Please specify the exact order in which the conceptual components must be introduced—what must come first, what relies on it, and why—so that the audience builds the framework rather than receiving it as a finished definition. Identify the one concrete, carrying scenario that should anchor the entire session, describing what features it must possess and which familiar philosophical templates—such as the standard desire-belief pairing or appeals to unconditional moral law—should be explicitly avoided or deferred at this stage, and why. Map the predictable misconceptions I should expect: I anticipate they will slide from “internal” to “selfish,” from “external” to “authoritarian,” and from “no internal motivation” to “no reason at all”; tell me precisely where in the sequence each should be intercepted before it hardens. Include one check-for-understanding question I can pose at the end that reveals whether they have grasped the dependency structure rather than merely recalled the labels. State clearly what adjacent material—historical figures, technical literature, or metaethical debates—should be left out at this level and openly acknowledged as beyond the session’s scope. Write your response in American English.
