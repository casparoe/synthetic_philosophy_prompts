Benjamin Franklin’s Autobiography outlines a lifelong project of moral perfection organized around thirteen virtues—temperance, silence, order, resolution, frugality, industry, sincerity, justice, moderation, cleanliness, tranquility, chastity, and humility—tracked in a personal ledger intended to make improvement visible and habitual. He conceded that he never reached perfection, and he treated the late addition of humility with special suspicion, observing that mastering pride could easily become its own form of pride.

Assume Franklin survived into the present and encountered two bodies of thought unavailable in the eighteenth century: first, the psychological literature on ego depletion, including its later replication crisis, which calls into question whether self-control draws on a finite, expendable resource; second, the concept of moral licensing, in which a prior virtuous act can psychologically permit a subsequent lapse.

Predict Franklin’s considered response. Would he abandon his ledger method if willpower turned out not to be a depletable reservoir but rather a belief-dependent capacity? How might his particular difficulty with humility shape his view of moral licensing—would he treat licensing as a failure of moderation, or as an inevitable distortion introduced by quantifying virtue? And given his empirical, habit-oriented pragmatism, would he regard these modern findings as reasons to revise his thirteen-virtue scheme, or as distractions from the practical work of gradual improvement?
