I'm working through a difficult passage in David Hume's Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, specifically the section in which he examines how we come to believe that the future will resemble the past. Hume argues that reason by itself cannot justify any inference from observed regularities to unobserved cases; instead, he appeals to custom, or habit, as the actual principle that fixes the mind to project past patterns forward. He presents this as an explanatory account of human psychology rather than as a rational justification, and he does not appear to supply any criterion for distinguishing among competing patterns that are all equally consistent with the same history of observations.

I am struggling to understand how this account interacts with Nelson Goodman's later problem of grue—the predicate defined so that an object is grue if it is green before some arbitrarily chosen future time and blue thereafter, which yields predictions about emeralds that are incompatible with the predicate green even though both predicates have been equally satisfied by every prior observation. If Hume explains induction solely by saying that custom determines which associations the mind forms, does that mean he implicitly treats the projection of green-like properties as legitimate only because human mental habits happen to be structured that way, leaving grue-like projections as psychologically unnatural but not rationally excluded? Or does Hume's framework contain a hidden constraint—perhaps tied to his remarks on the uniformity or simplicity of nature—that could privilege one projectible predicate over its incompatible counterpart? Alternatively, might Goodman's puzzle be showing that Hume's appeal to custom was never meant to solve the problem of selecting among competing inductive hypotheses at all, but only to describe the psychological mechanism behind whatever selection we happen to make?

Could you help me understand the passage by clarifying whether Hume's treatment of custom offers any resources for resolving the selection problem Goodman raises, or whether the two thinkers are addressing fundamentally different questions about the grounds and limits of inductive inference?
