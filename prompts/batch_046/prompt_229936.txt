I am finalizing the assessment for my graduate seminar on the epistemology of historical prediction, and after a long discussion with colleagues last week about the limits of reference-class forecasting, I have become convinced that our students need to be tested on a distinction that standard accounts of the outside view tend to blur rather than clarify. Specifically, I want them to confront the difference between selecting a reference class according to structural analogies—shared constitutional architectures, economic integration patterns, and technological infrastructures—and selecting it according to phenomenological analogies—similar observable trajectories of legitimacy erosion, elite defection, or territorial overreach. This distinction matters because it determines whether rapid technological change can be treated as an internal variable within an existing class or whether it forces the forecaster onto an inside view disguised as statistics. Please write one essay examination question suitable for a three-hour sitting, aimed at doctoral-level candidates in philosophy or political theory, that asks them to evaluate how this structural-versus-phenomenological framing shapes the base-rate forecast for the long-run durability of a large democratic federation undergoing rapid technological disruption. The question must require students to show that the dispute over reference classes is conceptual rather than merely empirical—specifically, that it concerns what properties make two political systems instances of the same kind—and it must force them to address the particular risk that introducing contemporary technological variables collapses the outside view back into an actor-centered inside view.

Together with the question, provide a model answer of approximately seven hundred words, written entirely in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or other visual formatting, since I will be distributing this to graders who need to read it as a unified argument. The answer should not contain bibliographies, citations, or requests for further reading, and it should be fully evaluable from conceptual reasoning alone. Also provide a marking scheme that names five specific conceptual moves a top answer must make, assigns marks to each with a total of twenty points, and includes a brief one-line note for each move explaining how candidates typically fail to secure those marks. The difficulty should come from requiring genuine analysis rather than summary: students who have merely memorized the vocabulary of base rates and reference classes should not be able to score well.
