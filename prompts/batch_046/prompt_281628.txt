Consider David Hume, brought into the present with full knowledge of contemporary epistemology, and ask him to adjudicate a live case of higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia. A research collective issues each scientist an individualized “reasoning reliability forecast” calibrated to that scientist’s past errors in analogous judgments; several members receive forecasts indicating roughly a 35 percent error rate for the kind of judgment they now face, yet they retain strong first-order confidence in their current conclusion—some by dismissing the forecast as inapplicable, others by accepting it but treating this case as a genuine outlier. Take a committed position on what Hume would conclude: argue definitively whether these scientists are rationally retaining belief or exhibiting epistemic akrasia, and whether the forecast ought to function as a defeater, drawing specifically on Hume’s views about custom, belief, and the limits of reason rather than laying out options for the reader. Do not survey competing interpretations; commit to one reading and defend it. End with a single line in exactly this form: Verdict: [a brief label summarizing Hume’s predicted stance].
