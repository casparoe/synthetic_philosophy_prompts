My colleague serves on our museum’s acquisition and display committee, and her past rulings include: she blocked a recently discovered “Caravaggio” that was a 19th-century copy with artificially aged materials, calling it a historical curiosity but not the work itself; she approved acquiring a Roman marble copy of a lost Greek bronze, insisting it be labeled as an honest Roman transmission of the lost design; she rejected AI-generated images composed in Van Gogh’s style but depicting novel scenes, arguing they transmit no specific prior artistic decision; she supported displaying a Renaissance fresco in which 40% had been reconstructed by human conservators using documented period techniques, saying transparent human repair preserves the work’s identity; and she argued that a forged signature added later to an authentic Picasso sketch should not affect its display value, since the mark is a parasitic addition to an intact object.

The committee now must advise on a proposed display: a destroyed mural by Artist M is represented by 30% original fragments embedded in a substrate, with the remaining 70% reconstructed by an AI system trained exclusively on M’s verified works and executed robotically using M’s original pigments, every reconstructed section clearly marked. The museum asks whether to hang it in the main gallery alongside M’s intact works.

Predict her recommendation, identify at least two distinct principles that could equally explain her earlier rulings, say which she is most likely running and why, propose one further case that would best distinguish those principles, and take a definite stance with your reasoning rather than leaving the options open. Note specifically where her reasoning is strongest and where a critic could press her hardest.
