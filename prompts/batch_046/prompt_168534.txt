I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science consulting for a regional watershed authority's technical review board, which approves predictive-intervention proposals—early-warning systems, zoning rules, evacuation protocols—without running the underlying dynamical models themselves. The board has one engineer, two planners, and thirty minutes per proposal. I want you to operationalize the philosophical distinction between determinism, chaos, and practical predictability into a reusable decision procedure. The guiding principle is that deterministic dynamics do not entail actionable forecasts: exponential divergence from positive Lyapunov exponents can destroy long-range prediction even when governing equations are fully known, yet intermediate regimes—specifically shadowability (the property that approximate model trajectories stay near true ones over a horizon) and structural stability of the attractor—can license limited, conditional action. The procedure should also incorporate predictive bankruptcy, a threshold I define not by raw error magnitude but by the collapse of the decision-relevant gap between acting on the model and acting on a uniform prior; once reached, forecasting must be abandoned regardless of theoretical determinism.

Write a checklist or flowchart in words with the ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, the clear reject or approve-with-conditions stop points, and the explicit hand-off points where the remaining issue is normative rather than empirical—such as whether a small risk of catastrophic false negative justifies high-cost false positives. Flag which steps are judgment calls dressed as procedure, and state what the procedure cannot do. Apply it to three cases: (1) deploying a 48-hour flash-flood predictor for a mountain watershed where the model exhibits low-dimensional chaos but verified shadowability up to 36 hours; (2) imposing 30-year land-use zoning based on climate-trajectory projections for the same region, where dynamics are deterministic but topologically unstable and predictive bankruptcy is reached well before the zoning horizon; and (3) a politically urgent mandatory hurricane-evacuation trigger based on long-range path forecasts that the procedure will reject—show exactly where the unwelcome answer falls and what values-based decision the board must then make alone.

The response should lead with its bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Write in American English.
