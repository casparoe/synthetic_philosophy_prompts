In discussions of Locke's program for education, I often see it analogized to the calibration of a precision instrument: the tutor observes the pupil's innate temperament with clinical detachment, adjusts environmental inputs with exacting care, and avoids coercive force on the grounds that any external pressure introduces mechanical distortion. The analogy is deployed to suggest that Locke's pedagogy is essentially technocratic and value-neutral — an optimization of outputs given fixed initial conditions, with virtue treated as a by-product of correct tuning rather than an object of direct cultivation.

I want to audit how far this holds. Please construct the correspondence table: what in Locke's actual practice plays the role of the instrument, the calibrator, the calibration protocol, the source of distortion, and the target state? Then show where the mapping fractures: does Locke regard the child as a passive mechanism? How does the formation of moral character — particularly the habituation of self-denial and rational self-governance — fit within an instrumental model? Does the analogy silently import a modern, mechanistic conception of development that conflicts with Locke's insistence on the pupil's emerging rational agency?

Separate the disanalogies that undermine the technocratic reading from those that are merely decorative. Does the conclusion — that Locke's education is value-neutral and purely instrumental — survive once the breaks are exposed, or was the analogy carrying that interpretation? If the instrument model collapses, propose a better analogy, perhaps drawn from Locke's own metaphors, that can accommodate both the observational precision of the tutor and the normative, character-forming purpose of the education.
