I am a philosophy professor serving as a fellow at a museum opening a new gallery titled "Thresholds: When Legends Become History." I will deliver the opening remarks at the private launch this Thursday evening. The audience of roughly sixty will be split evenly between academic historians and archaeologists—specialists in Bronze Age Greece, medieval Britain, and ancient Near Eastern studies—and museum donors and community members with no professional background in these fields. The gallery juxtaposes artifacts and interpretive texts related to the Trojan War, the Arthurian cycle, and the Exodus narrative, arranged not chronologically but in a triangular layout that refuses to rank them.

I need remarks of about eight minutes, roughly 1,000 words. The philosophical problem must do structural work: the boundary between legend and history should determine what I can and cannot say about each case, rather than serving as decorative framing. To that end, please build the speech around the following two-criteria framework, which I am introducing for this occasion: one criterion demands independent material or documentary corroboration outside the narrative tradition itself; the other criterion looks to the narrative's functional role in a living community's identity-formation, regardless of external corroboration. Applying both criteria simultaneously to the three cases should yield not a neat classification but an epistemic tension—a triangulation rather than a timeline—and I must not resolve it. The speech should end with the audience holding both criteria in suspension, mirroring the gallery's triangular design.

Please adopt the persona of an expert in philosophy of history and science, writing for oral delivery: formal but conversational, precise without condescension, able to signal to specialists that you understand the evidentiary disputes without losing the non-specialists.

Before drafting, think step by step: briefly sketch how each case behaves under the corroboration criterion versus the identity-formation criterion, and identify where the tension is sharpest. Then draft.

Structure your response in the following sections, in this order, with approximate lengths:
1. Audience strategy and framing (about 150 words): explain how you will navigate the mixed audience and why leaving the tension unresolved serves the gallery's purpose.
2. The opening remarks (about 1,000 words): the full speech text, broken into paragraphs suitable for oral delivery, with clear transitions.
3. Philosophical architecture notes (about 250 words): explain exactly how the two-criteria framework shaped the structure of the remarks—which claims about Troy, Arthur, and Exodus were included, which were deliberately withheld, and how the triangular layout of the gallery is mirrored in the argument's form.

Important constraints: do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citation to named scholars. Do not quote any real text verbatim unless you provide the exact source; paraphrase freely. Do not treat any of the three cases as definitively resolved in either direction. The idea of the two criteria should be developed within the speech itself, not imported as an established named theory.
