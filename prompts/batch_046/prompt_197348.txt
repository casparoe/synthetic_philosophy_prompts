I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and after working through Berkeley’s master argument and the standard replies, I am struck by how long the disagreement has persisted without anyone apparently conceding ground. As I understand the argument, Berkeley claims that we cannot consistently conceive of a material object—say, a tree—existing entirely unperceived, because the moment we form that conception, the tree becomes an object of our mental awareness, hence an idea in the mind, not something independent of mind. Critics respond that this conflates the mental act of conceiving with the content of what is conceived, or that it begs the question by treating “being an object of conception” as identical to “being perceived” in the ontologically relevant sense. These exchanges have been refined for centuries, yet the debate feels frozen.

My own suspicion, shaped by how I think about computation, is that part of the stalemate comes from an unacknowledged ambiguity about how mental representation works—something like the difference between lazy evaluation, where a value is brought into existence only when observed, and a pointer reference, which can direct attention to an object that persists independently of whether the pointer is currently dereferenced. If Berkeley is implicitly treating mental content as observer-dependent in the first way, while his critics assume a referential model that permits representation without ontological instantiation, then the two sides may not be disagreeing about trees or minds at all, but about the semantics of representation, under the shared label of “the master argument.”

Is this diagnosis right? Is the impasse substantive—about metaphysics or the nature of mental representation—or is it largely verbal, driven by different uses of “conceive,” “perceive,” and “idea”? Are the opponents answering the same question, or is one side investigating the conditions for representational content while the other investigates the ontological status of material objects? What would actually count as progress: a formal distinction between representational vehicle and representational content, an empirical finding about cognitive architecture, a conceptual clarification that dissolves the apparent contradiction, or is the question itself ill-posed in a way that makes resolution impossible? I want a diagnosis grounded in what each side actually says, together with an honest assessment of whether any distinction, result, or reframing could move the debate forward.
