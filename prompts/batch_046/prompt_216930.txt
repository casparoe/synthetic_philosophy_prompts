Read Ralph Ellison's Invisible Man through W.E.B. Du Bois's claim that all Black art is propaganda — specifically the version in that well-known essay where he says artists have a duty to fight for rights and dignity, not just make pretty things. I want a reading that takes both the novel and Du Bois seriosly: look at the prologue's light-bulb scene and the ending where the narrator's underground with all those bulbs burning, and ask wether that's propaganda for visibility or actually a rejection of the idea that art needs a clear racial message to matter. There's a niche angle I think people miss — Du Bois seems to say propaganda only works if it's beautiful, not just didactic, and there's a real debate about whether he softened the hardline stance after seeing the Harlem Renaissance get commercialized and co-opted. Does Ellison's modernist messiness — all that jazz and surrealism — prove Du Bois right that propaganda without beauty fails, or prove that beauty without propaganda is the stronger political move? Please separate what's settled about Du Bois's position from what's genuinely contested — I keep seeing people cite his all-art-is-propaganda line like it's his final word, but scholars disagree on whether he walked it back. Don't present the contested view as consensus. And no reading lists, please — I just want the argument.
