Write a sestina of thirty-nine lines—six stanzas of six lines each, followed by a three-line envoi—using the six end-words space, time, motion, correction, substance, and deity in the standard rotational order. Compose it in the voice of Isaac Newton addressing Richard Bentley around 1693, treating his own mechanical philosophy not as a self-sufficient clockwork but as a system that cannot maintain itself. Let each return of space, time, and motion grow more unstable through the sestets, so that the cyclical form enacts the gravitational pull that threatens to collapse the orbits; then, in the envoi, compress all six words into three unrhymed lines that abandon the rhyme scheme entirely, representing the point where mechanical explanation fails and an actively intervening cause must impress motion according to the tangents of orbit. Reflect Newton’s specific claim in this correspondence that gravitation is not innate to matter but requires a continually acting agent, and reflect also his anti-Trinitarian theology: deity should appear singular, absolute, and omnipresent in infinite substance and space, never divided. Do not resolve the tension—end with the universe held together only by the unmechanical presence of its corrector. The poem should be accurate enough that a historian of early modern science recognizes the Bentley letters and Newton’s rejection of action-at-a-distance as an essential property of bodies. Present the poem first, with or without a title. Then, separated by a blank line, present a section titled Commentary on Form and Theology of approximately 150 to 200 words, explaining which specific claim from Newton’s correspondence or theological writings each rotated end-word tracks, and why the unrhymed envoi structurally enacts the failure of a purely mechanical account.
