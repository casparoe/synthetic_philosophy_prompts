I am a retired physicist who has recently begun studying logic and the philosophy of science. The following argument troubles me because it seems to show that Gödel's results doom any scientific aspiration to completeness, yet I suspect the conclusion is too sweeping. (1) Gödel's first incompleteness theorem holds for any consistent formal system that can represent primitive recursive arithmetic. (2) Any mature empirical theory—whether in climate dynamics, quantum field theory, or evolutionary biology—that employs numerical measurement, iterative algorithms, and quantified predictions contains an embedded arithmetic rich enough to satisfy the theorem's conditions. (3) Therefore each such theory, if consistent, contains true statements about its domain that are not derivable within the theory itself. (4) If a statement is not derivable within the theoretical framework, then no observation interpreted solely through that framework can establish its truth. (5) Consequently, every mature science harbors empirically inaccessible but genuine truths about its subject matter. (6) This limitation is recursive: any meta-theory constructed to capture those missing truths will itself be subject to the same formal constraint. (7) Hence the very idea of a complete scientific description—whether a final physics or a unified theory of nature—is not merely unattainable in practice but logically incoherent. I should like to retain (1) through (3), and possibly (5), but I reject (7). Where does the reasoning slip? Identify the step that fails, explain why it appeared sound, and specify what giving it up costs for the relationship between formal incompleteness and empirical inquiry.
