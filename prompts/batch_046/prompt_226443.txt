I hold a gradient view of secession and self-determination that rejects the binary state/non-state framing. Rather than treating democratic mandate as the decisive factor, I claim that the recognition and success of secessionist claims in Scotland (2014), Catalonia (2017), and Quebec depend primarily on institutional path dependency: whether the sub-state unit inherited administrative, fiscal, and legal architectures that allow the parent state to treat separation as administratively continuous rather than structurally disruptive. Scotland's failure to secede, on this view, stems not mainly from the referendum margin but from deep fiscal and legal integration with UK institutions; Catalonia's crisis arose because its autonomous structures remained constitutionally embedded within Spain with no disembedding pathway; Quebec's enduring union, conversely, is stabilized by high autonomy (civil-law tradition, tax powers) that reduces the urgency of formal exit. What would count against this position? I want observations, arguments, or historical analogies that would genuinely undermine it—cases where high institutional separation still produced secession (or low separation was overcome by democratic will alone), theoretical objections that democratic mandate carries independent normative weight my view ignores, and whether the framework risks unfalsifiability by redescribing every outcome as a function of embeddedness. Rank the defeaters from fatal to awkward, indicate which ones defenders of mandate-based theories would concede versus dismiss, and assess honestly whether my view has been insulated into a position that risks nothing empirically.
