Two coworkers of mine got into it over coffee about whether Sartre's radical freedom thing holds up once you take predictive processing seriously. (1) Jordan said Sartre claims we're condemned to be free even tied to a chair, but now we know the brain runs interoceptive predictive models that generate action and affect predictions before conscious awareness. So the "choice" Sartre describes is just post-hoc narration of deterministic neural dynamics, and freedom is basically dead. (2) Riley said that's conflating causal determinism with phenomenalogical structure. Sartre isn't denying brain processes; he's describing consciousness (being-for-itself) as always exceeding its facticity. Even if an interoceptive model predicts your panic or your movement, the for-itself still has to assume that state as its own — that's the gap between being and nothingness. (3) Jordan pushed back: but Sartre uses "bad faith" to diagnose people who deny freedom. If anguish is really just an interoceptive prediction error — a homeostatic signal — then Sartre's claim that anguish reveals freedom is just romanticizing a brain glitch. Someone with panic disorder who says "I'm trapped" isn't lying; they're reporting a malfunctioning model. So Sartre can't distinguish genuine pathology from self-deception, which makes bad faith either useless or cruel. (4) Riley replied that the panic patient is still free: they can seek therapy, reinterpret the signal, or resist letting it define them. Calling it "bad faith" when they say "I'm trapped" isn't saying they can magically stop; it's saying they treat their body as defining their essence, ignoring the transendence that remains. The predictive brain is facticity, not destiny. (5) Jordan said this makes bad faith unfalsifiable. Any behavior — submitting, resisting, going to therapy — can be read as authentic or inauthentic based on the "attitude," with no observable difference except Riley's moral preference. So if you want to keep Sartre's freedom, you're paying the price of an untestable framework dressed up in neuroscience. (6) Riley countered that the difference is right there in self-reference: saying "I am my panic" collapses transcendence into facticity, while "I experience panic in a situation I can act on" preserves it. You can hear it in language and see it in whether they seek help as an agent or as a passive victim. It's not unfalsifiable; it's structural.
