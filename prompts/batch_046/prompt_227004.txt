I am a high school student working on an independent study in philosophy of mind and institutional design. I have been trying to understand the Löbian obstacle—the difficulty that a formal system powerful enough to represent its own reasoning cannot consistently prove its own soundness without undermining its credibility—and I want to see whether it actually applies to a concrete governance practice rather than only to abstract logic.

Consider this proposed design protocol for autonomous systems capable of self-modification: the system must contain an internal “trust loop,” a sub-module whose sole function is to certify that the system’s current reasoning about its own safety constraints is sound. If the sub-module returns a negative verdict, the system must halt. Crucially, the sub-module is not given privileged access to the system’s source code or logical architecture; it must evaluate the system’s reasoning using exactly the same formal tools available to the system itself. The framework presents this internal partition as sufficient to overcome self-certification worries, yet it also treats the sub-module as genuinely assessing the system’s beliefs rather than merely computing a mechanical correlation.

What philosophical commitments about rational self-trust, belief, and intentional agency does this protocol presuppose? Does it assume that dividing an agent into evaluator and evaluated can dissolve the Löbian obstacle, or does it quietly depend on a distinction between original and derived intentionality—between a system that genuinely believes its own outputs are safe and a sub-routine that only derives a logical registration of them? Reconstruct the implicit theory of agency and proof that makes the practice seem coherent, and say whether the protocol strictly needs that theory or is compatible with rival views—such as a view that denies internal self-trust altogether or one that treats all belief-attribution as a pragmatic stance. Then tell me whether the practice is internally consistent, where it is philosophically underdetermined, and what would have to change in the institutional design if we replaced the internal loop with an external audit conducted by human operators who stand outside the system’s logical architecture entirely.
