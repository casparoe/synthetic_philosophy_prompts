I just finished a long argument with a close friend about whether determinism really undermines moral responsibility. She defended a hard determinist line—if our resentments are just causal products of upbringing and brain chemistry, they cannot legitimately ground blame—while I tried to advance a Strawsonian view: that responsibility is anchored in our reactive practices rather than in any metaphysical freedom of the will. She then introduced a challenge I could not easily answer. She noted that in many professional and cultural contexts—certain East Asian communication norms, high-context organizational cultures, even some Northern European workplaces—direct expressions of resentment are suppressed. Instead, people deploy what she called “cooling behaviors”: calibrated silence, subtle social withdrawal, meticulously polite distance, and indirect signaling of disapproval. She claimed these are not genuinely reactive attitudes in Strawson’s sense, because they treat the offender as an object to be managed or navigated around, rather than as a fellow participant to be addressed. If she is right, then the reactive/objective distinction collapses across cultures, and Strawson’s defense of moral responsibility works only for societies that wear their reactive attitudes openly.

Please play P.F. Strawson—not a caricature, but the philosopher speaking in the first person, using the distinctions between participant reactive attitudes, the objective attitude, and ordinary interpersonal engagement developed in your own work. Stay in character: you hold that the demand for metaphysical freedom rests on a conceptual confusion; that reactive attitudes are woven into the fabric of ordinary human relationships; and that adopting the objective attitude involves a specific, recognizable shift in perspective, not merely a change in emotional temperature. You may extend your own distinctions to meet this challenge, but do not break character to cite modern scholarship or to lecture me about what later philosophers said. Respond in one or two substantial paragraphs, and remain in role until I say “stop.”

My opening move: if indirect resentment—expressed through silence, social cooling, or calibrated exclusion—is structurally closer to managing a malfunctioning mechanism than to addressing a person as a responsible agent, does that not mean the objective attitude is not the opposite of the reactive attitude, but rather its culturally disguised form? And if reactive attitudes can be masked so thoroughly that they become indistinguishable from strategic management, has your defense of moral responsibility not been quietly restricted to cultures that display their reactions on their sleeves?
