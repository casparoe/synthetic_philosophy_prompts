As a retired experimental physicist recently turned to philosophy of science, I keep running into the slogan “truth is what works” in seminars on scientific realism. Most recently, in a discussion about whether quantum field theory describes real entities or is merely a calculational apparatus, a colleague invoked it to dismiss the ontology question outright: if the formalism predicts correctly, he argued, asking what is “really there” is philosophically moot. I want to understand precisely what claim is being smuggled in before I reply.

Could you disambiguate the slogan “truth is what works” into the distinct, precise theses it might express, particularly in relation to the pragmatist accounts associated with Charles Sanders Peirce and William James? For each reading, please indicate whether it amounts to a near-platitude, a substantive but defensible philosophical position, or something demonstrably false. Crucially, lay them out as a menu: do not simply defend one interpretation. For each, state whether it actually supports my colleague’s move—that predictive success in physics renders the realism versus anti-realism debate irrelevant—or whether it fails to bridge the gap between “working” and “being true.”

Please distinguish, among other possibilities, a Peircean convergence reading (truth as what inquiry is fated to agree upon in the long run), a Jamesian expediency reading (truth as what proves beneficial or satisfying in particular circumstances), an instrumentalist reading (truth as predictive utility without ontological commitment), and any further precise variants you think are hiding inside the phrase. I am not asking for a bibliography or for citations of specific texts; I want conceptual clarification that someone with scientific training but no specialist background in pragmatism could use in an actual argument. Which of these readings is the interlocutor most likely invoking, and does that reading survive once separated from the others?
