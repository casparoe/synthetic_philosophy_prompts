As a novelist constructing a character who suffers an unusual form of dissociation, I have kept encountering a structural pattern in clinical accounts and in the architecture of certain thought experiments—one that seems to involve two opposite ways a mental content can fail to align with the rest of the mind.

In the first case, a person can discriminate stimuli, navigate space, produce accurate verbal reports, and integrate information across tasks—everything that looks like full awareness from the outside—while insisting sincerely that nothing had any qualitative character for them, that there was no inner feel or subjective presence at all. The content guides behavior and enters language, yet it is not, as they describe it, inhabited from within. In the second case, there is an undeniable, vivid inner presence—perhaps a saturated color, an intense bodily sensation, or an emotional tone—that nevertheless seems locked away from reasoning, report, and voluntary action. It is there subjectively, but it does not participate in the person's thinking or doing; one might almost say it is present without being available for use.

These two failures feel like mirror images, and I wonder whether philosophy of mind or cognitive science treats them as manifestations of a single distinction, or whether they belong to entirely separate categories. Is there an established concept for this gap between what is usable in thought and action and what is present to the subject from the inside? If such a concept exists, how precisely is its boundary drawn—around reportability, around integration with reasoning, around some mechanism of widespread broadcasting? And do the clinical and experimental instances genuinely support the same carving, or do they mix together failures that should be separated?

I am not asking for a bare name. I want to know exactly what the phenomenon comprises, what it excludes, and where its edges become unclear—particularly whether the two configurations I have described are genuinely symmetric, or whether one is conceptually parasitic on the other. So please address not only whether a term exists and what it denotes, but also how it relates to nearby distinctions that might be mistaken for it.

Please also say what would change your mind: which argument or empirical finding would convince you that this split is not coherent, or that one of these configurations is impossible? And indicate where your own reasoning is most likely to fail—whether in interpreting the clinical material, in drawing the analogy between the two cases, or in framing the underlying philosophical debate.
