Read Edwin Abbott Abbott's Flatland through the lens of Milton Friedman's "F-twist" — the methodological claim, central to his essay on positive economics, that the descriptive realism of a model's assumptions is irrelevant to its scientific value, which should be judged solely by predictive accuracy. Flatland depicts a two-dimensional society whose geometric inhabitants literally embody a rigid social hierarchy: men are polygons whose status rises with the number of sides, women are line segments rendered nearly invisible when seen edge-on, and social mobility is blocked by taboos against irregular shapes and chromatic alteration. Treat the novella as if it were a scientific model of human society: do its physically impossible premises — flat existence, shape-determined caste, the suppression of proposals to allow lower shapes to paint themselves into higher ranks, and the eventual prohibition against preaching a third dimension — function like Friedman's unrealistic idealizations, yielding insight precisely because they strip away descriptive clutter? Or does Abbott's satire depend on insisting that a model of society must be descriptively adequate to be morally or intellectually legitimate? Examine the Square's encounter with the Sphere, the official decrees that silence witnesses of Spaceland, and the narrator's imprisonment, asking in each case whether the scene operates as a predictive idealization or as a refusal of idealization. Where does Flatland resist being read through the F-twist — particularly in its moral outrage at the fragility and invisibility of women and the violence done to irregular shapes — and what does that resistance suggest about whether unrealistic assumptions can ever be neutral in social explanation? Conclude by saying what, if anything, Abbott's geometric fable adds to the philosophical debate over scientific idealization that a direct argument about model-building cannot.
