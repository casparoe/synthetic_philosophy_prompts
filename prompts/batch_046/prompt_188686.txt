Medical law lets you write an advance directive saying if you're ever severely demented don't keep you alive with tubes, and doctors usually treat that as binding even though the patient might smile and seem content. But the same legal system usually won't enforce a Ulysses contract where an addict says "if I beg for opioids in rehab, don't give them to me" — courts call that unenforcable coercion. What theory of self-authority is hiding in that split? Does it presuppose that the earlier self only governs when the later one lacks some specific mental capacity, or is there a hidden view about which kinds of preference-change are authentic? Reconstruct the implicit commitment, say wheather it's actually consitant with negligence law and contract doctrine, and tell me what a policy that took the addict's future refusal seriously would have to change — don't just say "respect autonomy" either way.
