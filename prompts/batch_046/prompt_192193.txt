As a journalist writing on the philosophy of quantum mechanics, I have been struck by how rarely Erwin Schrödinger's Vedantic metaphysics—his conviction, drawn from the Upanishads, that consciousness is fundamentally one and that individual minds are only apparently plural—is taken seriously as a constraint on interpreting the formalism he helped create. Most treatments treat this view as a biographical curiosity, yet I want to press it as a rigorous philosophical premise. Please argue that Schrödinger's commitment to the unity of consciousness is not merely ornament but actually undermines the Everettian many-worlds interpretation, despite the latter's preservation of wave-mechanical unitary evolution. Your argument should proceed as follows. First, distinguish between the Vedantic idea that physical bodies and worlds are pluralized only as maya—appearance or illusion—from the much stronger claim that conscious subjects themselves are multiplied; the former is compatible with branching physical histories, but the latter, you should maintain, is not. Second, show that if consciousness is genuinely singular, then decoherence can account for the appearance of separate observers (each apparently associated with a different branch) only as an epistemic limitation, not as an ontological generation of new subjects; in other words, the same one consciousness is associated with each apparent outcome, and there is no copying of observers. Third, argue that treating observer-states as physically branching therefore commits a category error: it takes an epistemic pluralization (the many perspectives from which the single consciousness can view outcomes) for an ontological one (the creation of many conscious beings). Fourth, address the objection that Schrödinger's own wave mechanics, with its continuous evolution, is precisely what allows branching; reply that formal compatibility is not metaphysical coherence, and that a single-world interpretation—perhaps one in which measurement is the point at which the unitary description gives way to a singular actualized outcome experienced by the one consciousness—better respects the ontological priority of mind. Finally, explain what this implies for psychophysical parallelism: if consciousness is one and the physical world is apparently many, the correlation must be one-to-many rather than one-to-one, which avoids the parallelism Schrödinger criticized but requires us to reject any interpretation that assigns a distinct conscious state to each branch. I am not asking for a historical report on what Schrödinger said about Hugh Everett; I am asking for a normative reconstruction of his metaphysical premises and their interpretive consequences. Be precise about the identity conditions for the observer across outcomes, and do not invoke bibliographic references or citation lists.
