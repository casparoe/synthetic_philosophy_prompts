I am a doctoral student in political philosophy working on the democratic boundary problem, specifically on how to draw the demos when jurisdictional authority is layered, functionally disaggregated, or territorially nested rather than mapped onto a single sovereign state. I have formed a cluster of judgments about concrete inclusion cases, and I want to know whether they can be unified under one principle of democratic membership, or whether reflective equilibrium forces me to abandon at least one verdict. Here are my judgments.

First, consider a nested jurisdiction: a semi-autonomous region R within state S maintains its own democratic assembly with authority over education, zoning, and local taxation, while S retains control over defense, currency, and immigration. A policy is being decided in R's assembly that will significantly alter water rights for residents of neighboring regions in S who are not citizens of R. My verdict: those affected outsiders should have an advisory or weighted participatory voice in R's decision, but they should not be full members of R's demos.

Second, consider a diaspora community that retains formal citizenship in state C but has lived abroad for four generations, holds no property in C, pays no taxes to C, and is subject to none of C's domestic legislation except symbolically through consular protection. They retain the legal right to vote in C's national elections. My verdict: they should not belong to C's demos for ordinary domestic policy, though they might retain a narrowly circumscribed voice in constitutional amendments that directly redefine citizenship itself.

Third, consider temporary residents—skilled workers present in state D for a fixed five-year term—who are fully subject to D's labor, criminal, and tax law, who use D's infrastructure, and who intend to depart upon contract completion, retaining citizenship elsewhere. My verdict: they should be included in D's demos for the specific regulatory domains that govern their conditions of residence, but not for long-term constitutional or redistributive commitments that outlast their presence.

Fourth, consider a synthetic administrative system—an artificial deliberative agent that processes all municipal data for city E, generates binding policy recommendations, and is functionally indispensable to governance, yet lacks consciousness, interests, or any capacity for welfare or suffering. My verdict: it should be excluded from E's demos entirely, regardless of its functional centrality.

Fifth, consider descendants not yet born who will inherit the institutional, ecological, and financial consequences of a present decision about irreversible resource extraction. My verdict: they should be represented in the current decision-process, but not as full present members of the demos; rather, through a trusteeship or proxy mechanism that gives them standing without treating them as contemporaneous voters.

Sixth, the case I keep revisiting: a group was forcibly relocated from territory T to territory U ninety years ago, formed a distinct cultural and linguistic community, and now lives entirely within U's borders. They explicitly reject U's citizenship, do not vote in U's elections, and deny any allegiance to U, yet they are fully subject to U's laws and are materially affected by them. My verdict: they belong to U's demos, and U bears an obligation to include them in democratic processes that affect them, irrespective of their refusal.

These judgments appear to combine affectedness, legal subjection, and interest-capacity, but I am struggling to reconcile the diaspora case with the relocated-group case—both involve long-term separation from a territorial state, yet I want to include one and exclude the other. I am also unsure how to square the exclusion of the functionally central synthetic agent with the inclusion of future generations who are not presently affected at all.

Is there a single coherent principle—perhaps one that weights present subjection-to-law, direct affectedness, and interest-bearing capacity in a specific way—that delivers all six verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If not, which verdict should I overturn in favor of simplicity, and why? Please also state precisely what your principle predicts for a further case I have not listed: a citizen who permanently emigrates from state F to state G, retains F's citizenship, returns to F every decade solely to vote in national elections on issues that do not affect them, and is otherwise fully subject to G's laws.

Finally, please indicate how confident you are in the principle you propose, and single out the specific point—whether the diaspora versus relocated-group distinction, the status of synthetic agents, or the proxy representation of future generations—about which you are least certain.
