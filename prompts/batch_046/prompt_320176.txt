I sit on the grant review panel for a small environmental science foundation -- we meet twice a month, have about 20 minutes per proposal, three of us vote and I have to write the justification. Lately every other grant claims they've "confirmed" a new predictive model using historical records that have been sitting in public archives for decades -- river flow logs from the 70s, soil samples, census blocks, whatever. I need a reusable decision procedure my panel can actually run: a checklist in order, with clear stop points where the answer is "this is just bootstrapping, reject", and explicit flags for where we're making a value call rather than applying logic. The procedure has to deal with the standard Bayesian problem -- if evidence E was already known, P(E) should be 1, so how does it update belief? -- but also this niche distinction I'm injecting called "epistemic accessibility tiers": was the data technically public but effectively invisible until the new theory introduced the specific coding scheme or calibration that makes it readable?

Please include steps for when we don't have formal priors or numbers, be honest about which parts are just intuition dressed up, and apply it to three cases. Case one: a proposal using 1980s rainfall logs to confirm a drought model that was literally paramaterized on those same logs -- I want a clear no. Case two: same logs but the theory predicted a specific non-obvious interaction that wasn't part of the model-building -- I want a conditional yes with caveats. Case three: the logs were in a regional archive that wasn't digitized until last year, so technically old but not epistemically accesible before -- I want the procedure to give us an answer we'll hate, probably that it counts as old evidence but with a discount factor, and we still have to vote on it. Don't ask for bibliographies or citations, we don't have library access in the meeting room, and don't quote specific philosophers by name unless you're 100% sure of the attribution -- I'm not testing your recall, I'm testing whether the logic holds up when you translate it into something a tired panel can use.
