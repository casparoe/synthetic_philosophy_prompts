In my graduate seminar on philosophy of history, two advanced students—call them M and N—recently debated whether historical explanation can ever claim objectivity in the face of Carr’s and Hayden White’s well-known scepticism about Ranke’s aspiration to represent the past as it actually was. I am reporting their exchange as a bystander and do not take sides; please adjudicate it move by move.

M opened by defending a restrained empirical ideal: even if perfect reconstruction is impossible, archives impose hard constraints—documents can falsify claims, and intersubjective verification allows historians to converge asymptotically on what actually occurred. The goal is regulative, not empty. N replied that archives do not select themselves; the historian’s choice of evidence is already shaped by narrative interest. More radically, White’s analysis of tropological prefiguration—romance, tragedy, comedy, satire—shows that the very constitution of facts depends on an emplotment that operates before cognition. M, N argued, is not writing history but compiling an unstructured chronicle, which White sharply distinguishes from history proper.

M then charged N with self-refutation: if all historiography is tropologically predetermined, then White’s own theory is merely another romantic or satirical narrative, not a structural analysis. Furthermore, Carr allows that debate among historians can refine interpretation, which presupposes shared empirical ground. N countered that this tu quoque misses the point—structural claims about discourse need not be self-exempting like empirical ones. N added that M’s chronicle-versus-history distinction actually proves N’s case, since any meaningful ordering requires emplotment; the Rankean mirror is therefore not an unattainable ideal but a category mistake—history without narrative is not impoverished history, but no history at all.

Please rate each of these four moves—M’s empirical-constraint argument, N’s archive-and-tropology reply, M’s self-refutation charge, and N’s category-mistake defense—from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Say also what both parties miss: is there a conceptual distinction, perhaps between procedural objectivity and metaphysical mirroring, that explains why they talk past each other? Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Please conclude with a final line beginning Verdict: that states in one sentence which interlocutor is closer to the truth overall and what single distinction both neglect.
