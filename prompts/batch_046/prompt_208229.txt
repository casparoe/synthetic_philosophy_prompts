I'm leading the closing session of a philosophy of mind seminar on experimental philosophy, and our group has spent the past two weeks working through the literature on the side-effect effect—the finding that people are far more willing to call a harmful foreseen but undesired consequence "intentional" than they are to apply the same label to an identical beneficial consequence, even when the agent's mental states and causal role are held constant. Rather than ending with a conventional discussion, I'd like to open with a short villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a quatrain, with two refrains—that treats this asymmetry not as a cognitive quirk to be explained away but as a structural feature of how ordinary language parses agency. The poem's two refrains should be lines that remain identical except for a single evaluative reversal, so that the rhyme scheme physically enacts the redescribing of the same causal sequence: one version anchored to harm, one to benefit. The narrative situation should involve an agent pursuing an uncontroversial end—say, maximizing corporate returns—while fully anticipating but not aiming at an environmental side effect. I want the poem to dramatize the tension between two readings of our judgment: on one reading, we track something genuinely mental (foresight coupled with indifference), and on the other, the term "intentional" is being pulled toward "blameworthy," so that normative appraisal is doing the classificatory work. Rather than resolving this, the villanelle should end with its refrains still in opposition, leaving the audience in the same unsettled place our seminar reached. It should be precise enough that someone familiar with the debate recognizes the specific conceptual pressure point—the entanglement of descriptive psychology and normative evaluation—without relying on citations, author names, or bibliographic references, and it should stand as good verse for anyone encountering the problem for the first time.
