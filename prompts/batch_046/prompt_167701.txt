For a research project on autonomous systems and moral accountability, I have been examining a recurring institutional pattern: regulations governing lethal autonomous weapons and high-risk AI—such as European risk-classification frameworks and U.S. Department of Defense directives—require that a human operator remain the legally accountable party, even when the system's sensor-to-actuator loop completes in milliseconds, leaving the human with no practical opportunity to intervene or veto. The result is often described as a moral crumple zone, in which the human is structurally positioned to absorb blame despite lacking the temporal or epistemic conditions for genuine control. This design strikes me as philosophically puzzling because it appears to conflict with standard negligence doctrine, which generally refuses to hold individuals responsible for harms they could not have prevented through reasonable care. What implicit theory of moral responsibility does this institutional practice presuppose? Must it assume that responsibility can be assigned on purely structural or role-based grounds, independent of causal control, or can a modified control-based view accommodate it by appealing to institutional authorization? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, then distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely aligns well with it. Is the design genuinely philosophically underdetermined—compatible, for example, with a relational accountability view, a forward-looking regulatory view, or even a form of command-level strict liability? Finally, if we replaced the presupposed view with a straightforward control-based theory that assigns blame only for outcomes within an agent's practical reach, what specific changes would be required in the technical architecture, the legal liability framework, and the command structure?
