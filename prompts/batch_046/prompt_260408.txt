After a long and somewhat circular argument with a friend about whether secularism is genuinely neutral or just another ideological posture, I want to audit an analogy she kept returning to: she said that a secular state relating to religious bodies is like a referee relating to competing sports teams—present, enforcing common rules, but never playing for one side. She used this to defend Germany's Kirchensteuer system and to claim that England's established church is essentially the same mechanism in ceremonial clothing, while insisting that France's laïcité is the referee leaving the stadium entirely, which she considers unstable. I think the analogy is carrying more normative weight than she admits, but I need the mapping made explicit.

Can you lay out a correspondence table—what in the source domain (the referee, the rulebook, the teams, the stadium, the spectators) maps onto what in each of the four target regimes (England's Crown-in-Parliament establishment with bishops in the Lords, Germany's state-collected church tax with its formal exit mechanism, France's 1905 separation law together with its subsequent public-order restrictions on religious expression, and the US Establishment Clause framework with its tax-exemption and non-establishment jurisprudence)? Then identify the disanalogies that actually matter for her argument: does the referee picture smuggle in a false symmetry among churches (treating them all as voluntary associations of equal standing), does it obscure the coercive element when the state collects religious taxes, and can it accommodate the fact that in England the state literally appoints church leadership while in Germany it merely processes payments? Does her conclusion—that neutrality is coherent, that establishment is just a different uniform for the same referee, and that France's strict separation is therefore an unstable outlier—survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does the analogy collapse into a defense of one particular institutional form?

If the referee picture fails, is there a better analogy—perhaps a licensing authority, a public-utility regulator, or an escrow agent—that captures the actual structural differences among these four regimes without importing hidden commitments about whether religious association is voluntary, whether the state may enforce membership criteria, or whether neutrality requires identical treatment rather than proportional accommodation?
