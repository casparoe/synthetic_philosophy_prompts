Consider the following reconstructed verdicts that Blaise Pascal implicitly endorses in the fragments conventionally grouped under justice and power. First, a legal regime that is substantively arbitrary—grounded in historical accident rather than moral reason—is nevertheless treated as functioning justice in practice, provided it is consistently enforced by human authority and universally obeyed through custom. Second, a reform that is morally sound but can be sustained only through perpetual violent suppression of widespread dissent is rejected, on the grounds that justice lacking the support of stable custom and social order becomes either tyrannical or impotent. Third, a revolutionary overthrow achieved by violence is initially condemned; however, once the new order achieves stable enforcement and widespread acquiescence without ongoing coercion, it is accepted as just, with the original violence treated as a transitional aberration. Fourth, a moral principle that commands universal assent in the abstract but is entirely unenforced and ignored in practice is denied the status of justice altogether.

Now suppose a novel institutional form: an automated governance architecture that enforces behavioral norms through algorithmic penalties—financial restrictions, access limitations, and algorithmic exclusion—applied with perfect consistency and without human discretion. The norms enforced are substantively arbitrary, derived from contingent historical data rather than moral argument. There is no physical violence, no human agent wielding coercive power, and no independent social custom preceding or supporting the norms; compliance is near-universal solely because the penalties are technically unavoidable. The system is stable, produces no social disorder, and is instrumentally accepted by the population.
