I have just emerged from a long and rather exhausting argument with a close friend about the conditions that ought to govern institutional reform. We were debating whether a change should ever be permitted unless it satisfies what I called a strict safe-Pareto standard: every affected party must be left no worse off according to their own ex-ante preference ordering, and this non-harm condition must hold even if some agents have strategically misrepresented those preferences to block change. My friend defended this standard as the only ethically defensible safeguard; I argued that it is either epistemically unattainable or covertly conservative, and that insisting upon it privileges those best served by the status quo.

Please write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—A, who advocates the strict safe-Pareto position, and B, who rejects it—so that they respond to each other’s actual arguments rather than talking past one another. Their exchange should examine, in particular: whether an anonymous preference-threshold registration mechanism can genuinely distinguish sincere risk aversion from strategic inflation intended to veto reform; whether the requirement that no one be made worse off implicitly assigns greater weight to the welfare of those who benefit most from the current arrangement; and whether a coherent intermediate position—perhaps a conditionally safe reform that permits bounded, reversible temporary disutility for some parties, provided aggregate risk remains capped—is conceptually stable or merely collapses back into one of the two extremes.

The dialogue should be written in British English, should maintain formal and precise diction throughout, and should not appeal to named authors, canonical texts, or external bibliographies. I want the logic worked out from first principles, as my friend and I failed to do.
