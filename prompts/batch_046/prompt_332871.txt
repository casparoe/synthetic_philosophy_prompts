I'm in a philosophy of well-being seminar, and I tried to extend Nozick's experience-machine reasoning further than he does and now have an argument that feels like a reductio of objective-list theories. Here is the chain I presented in our discussion section:

(1) If hedonism or pure desire-satisfaction is correct, then plugging into the experience machine should be rational, since the machine perfectly replicates the subjective profile of an excellent life.
(2) Most people reject plugging in, which suggests well-being requires something beyond subjective experience: actual contact with reality, genuine achievement, real relationships.
(3) Call this the Reality Constraint: for any objective good G on an objective-list theory, actually possessing G is necessary for well-being, and merely seeming to possess G is not sufficient.
(4) But there is a further condition that makes "actually possessing" robust: an achievement only counts as genuinely yours if it is modally stable—if your success is not just a fluke or the result of hidden deception about your causal role. Call this the Anti-Fluke Condition.
(5) So if achievement is on the objective list, then contributing to well-being requires not only actually achieving, but achieving in a way that is not Gettiered by hidden luck or deception.
(6) Apply this consistently: anyone whose success depends on an unnoticed external scaffold—a hidden collaborator, a rigged environment, a brain-in-vat simulation they never detect—fails the Anti-Fluke Condition and therefore lacks the achievement-component of well-being.
(7) But since no ordinary agent has epistemic access to the full causal history of their successes, everyone is in the position of possibly being deceived about whether they satisfy the Anti-Fluke Condition.
(8) Therefore, on objective-list theories that include achievement, well-being requires a kind of epistemic omniscience about one's causal embeddedness that no human possesses.
(9) That is absurd—objective-list theories should not entail that well-being is impossible for ordinary people.
(10) So either we reject the Reality Constraint (1–3) and return to hedonism, or we reject the Anti-Fluke Condition (4–5).

I want to keep (2) and (3)—I think Nozick is right that there is something wrong with living in the machine. But I am unsure whether (4) smuggles in an illegitimate epistemic requirement on top of a metaphysical one, or whether the slide from (6) to (7) confuses actually achieving with knowing that one has achieved without hidden help. There may also be an equivocation between "actually writing poetry" and "knowing one actually wrote poetry in a causally un-deceived manner." Where exactly does the argument overreach, and if I must choose between giving up the Anti-Fluke Condition or accepting that well-being is radically epistemically fragile, which option is less costly?
