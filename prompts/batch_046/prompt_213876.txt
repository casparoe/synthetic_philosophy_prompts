I'm currently taking a seminar on practical reason and moral psychology, and we've been working through the literature on what philosophers call the "wrong kind of reasons" problem. A summary circulated in our course reader—prepared, I believe, by one of the TAs—offers the following gloss, which several of us have been arguing about in study group. It says that the problem arises whenever a consideration rationalizes an attitude without being the kind of consideration that justifies it in the relevant normative domain. For actions, fear of punishment can rationalize returning a lost wallet, yet because the moral rightness of returning it depends on respect for ownership rather than on avoiding sanctions, the fear is a wrong-kind reason for the moral act—even though it may still be a perfectly good reason to perform the act all things considered. For belief, the practical comfort of believing that one's health is good might rationalize that belief, but since health is not made true by comfort, such considerations are wrong-kind reasons for belief. The summary then claims that the standard philosophical resolution distinguishes a reason's psychological efficacy from its normative authority: wrong-kind reasons possess the former but lack the latter within the domain in question. It further suggests, attributing the point to Scanlon's contractualist framework, that in morality this distinction becomes especially sharp, because any reason that could not be reasonably rejected by others automatically carries normative authority, whereas self-interested considerations are structurally domain-external and therefore always the wrong kind for moral justification. Finally, the summary introduces a two-tier taxonomy drawn from recent work: domain-internal wrong-kind reasons target features within the normative domain but the wrong ones—such as evaluating a poem by its rhyme scheme rather than its expressive coherence—while domain-external reasons, like financial incentives, come from entirely outside. From this, the summary concludes that once the taxonomy is in place, all apparent tensions between moral demands and self-interest dissolve, since self-interest is by definition the wrong kind of reason for moral justification.

Something about this feels both illuminating and suspicious. The distinction between internal and external wrong-kind reasons seems like a useful injection, but I'm not sure whether Scanlon's view really excludes self-interest in the structural way described, or whether the summary is conflating wrong-kind reasons for justifying morality with wrong-kind reasons for acting morally. I also wonder if the conclusion that conflicts dissolve is too quick—surely there can still be cases where the right kind of reason and the self-interested reason pull in opposite directions, even if only one has normative authority. Could you audit this summary sentence by sentence? Please separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, what subtly misrepresents a philosopher's view, and whether the final conclusion actually follows from the premises given. In particular, I want to know whether the two-tier taxonomy is a recognized distinction in the literature or an artificial addition, and whether treating self-interest as always domain-external to morality is fair to the views being summarized. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in each part of your assessment, and explicitly identify the point about which you are least certain—especially regarding the attribution to Scanlon and the status of that internal/external distinction.
