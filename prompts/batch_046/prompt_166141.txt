I just had a long and exhausting argument with a friend about whether everyday conventions—tipping, queueing, and not jaywalking—are actual moral obligations or just local habits, especially for visitors who come from cultures where the norms are different or nonexistent. We kept talking past each other: I insisted that obligations require some form of tacit consent or opt-in, my friend insisted that predictable harm is enough regardless of consent, and neither of us could explain why a purely symbolic violation—like skipping a line when no one is visibly disadvantaged—should carry any moral weight. I want to turn this dispute into a rigorous assessment rather than another circular conversation.

Please draft a 75-minute final-exam essay question for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in social and political philosophy. The answer should run about 1,200 words and be worth 25 marks total. The question must not be answerable by summarizing standard accounts of moral relativism, social contract theory, or legal positivism. Instead, it should require students to distinguish and apply three precisely defined positions: (A) conventions generate obligations only for participants who have given tacit consent by engaging with the practice; (B) conventions generate obligations for anyone—including visitors—whenever non-compliance produces predictable harm, regardless of consent or cultural membership; and (C) conventions are purely strategic coordination devices with no intrinsic moral weight unless they protect rights that exist independently of the convention itself. The prompt should present a constructed scenario: a tourist visiting a country with strict queue norms, a 15% tipping expectation enforced only by social pressure, and rigorous jaywalking prohibitions, all lacking legal penalties. The tourist’s home culture treats queue-jumping as harmless when unnoticed, considers tipping purely voluntary, and regards jaywalking as acceptable on empty streets. Ask students to apply all three positions to the visitor’s obligations across all three domains, and to defend one position with an explicit theory of harm that excludes purely symbolic or expressive offenses.

Then provide a model answer of about 400 words that takes a committed stance—specifically, defend position B, but concede that it depends on a harm-based threshold that rules out obligations in cases where violation is only symbolically offensive and causes no predictable damage. Provide a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, each worth 5 marks (total 25). For each point, name the specific philosophical move required and describe the typical error that should cost marks. Write the entire response in American English. Do not include requests for bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific scholars’ texts; the difficulty should come from identifying the conceptual crux, not from recalling labels. Ensure the question and answer are meaningful without web access and do not depend on obscure theories that require specialist memorization.
