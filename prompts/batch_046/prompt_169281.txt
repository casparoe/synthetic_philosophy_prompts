Here are my intuitions about Bernard Williams’s integrity objection — specifically about when a justification involves one thought too many. I want to know whether a single principle can capture all four verdicts.

Case 1. A surgeon must choose between saving her child and saving a stranger, both needing the same scarce resource. She saves her child solely because he is mine; she does not weigh outcomes. Verdict: her integrity is intact; no alien thought has entered.

Case 2. The same surgeon saves her child, but her reason is that parents who save their own children show lower long-term PTSD rates, which preserves her surgical capacity. Verdict: this is precisely one thought too many; strategic self-management has replaced her ground project.

Case 3. A refugee advocate abandons direct, face-to-face solidarity work to design an algorithmic triage system for camps, convinced it will save more lives. She makes the shift after a personal crisis of meaning that reshapes what standing with the displaced means to her, not after a moral calculation. Verdict: integrity is intact; her ground project has evolved organically.

Case 4. The same advocate makes the identical career shift, but she does so because she has adopted an external quantitative-ethics framework — one she learned in graduate study — that demands maximising lives saved regardless of personal meaning. Verdict: one thought too many; an alien framework has displaced her ground project.

My question: is there one account of integrity that delivers all four verdicts? If not, which verdict should be overturned and why? Please also state what your principle predicts for a case I have not listed: a soldier refuses an unlawful order to destroy a village, not because he feels personal revulsion, but because he has internalised a professional military code that categorically forbids harming non-combatants, treating that code as part of his identity rather than an external rule.
