I am a senior software engineer at a firm that has maintained the same core platform for over fifteen years. The original architecture was designed by a small founding team, and over time we have accumulated not only deprecated modules and extensive internal documentation, but also an institutional folklore that treats the early build as a kind of golden standard against which all current work is implicitly measured.

As we prepare for a ground-up rewrite, our team has split into three distinct positions regarding how to treat this legacy. One group argues that we should keep the original system running in a protected, isolated environment, study its design patterns continuously, and use it as the primary training reference for new hires. They believe that without this living reference point, we will lose the foundational vision that made our product coherent and will drift into arbitrary redesign.

A second group insists that we should decommission the active system but preserve every artifact—every commit log, every deprecated module, every internal memo—in a meticulously curated, read-only archive. They do not want the old code executed, but they maintain that destroying or neglecting these records would erase the accumulated errors and insights that explain why our current system behaves as it does. For them, preservation is an obligation to the people who built it.

A third group, which I find myself closest to, argues that we should aggressively delete the deprecated code, shut down the archive maintenance, and stop using the original architecture as a benchmark. They claim that carrying the past—technically, culturally, and psychologically—prevents us from defining a genuinely new direction. I share this impulse, yet I worry that my preference for clearing everything away may be driven less by practical engineering needs and more by an emotional desire to reject an authority I never chose.

The debate was recently complicated by a discovery: deep in the legacy system, we found undocumented functional modules—code that processes live data, has no identifiable authors in our version history, and is not referenced in any documentation. No one on the current team remembers writing it, yet it is actively doing work. This has made the preservationists more insistent that the past contains hidden wisdom we do not understand, while the deletionists see these ghost modules as proof that the legacy is an ungovernable burden that should be eliminated entirely.

How should I evaluate the right relationship between our past engineering work and our present capacity to innovate? Specifically, is there a principled distinction between honoring what came before and being trapped by it, and how should the existence of these unknown, active remnants change that evaluation? I am not looking for project-management advice; I want to understand whether the tension between preservation, veneration, and destruction is resolvable in principle, or whether it reflects an unavoidable conflict in how a collective relates to its own history.
