As a high school student working through Kant's moral philosophy, I have been trying to map my own judgments about human obligations to animals onto his framework of indirect duties, and I keep running into cases where the standard indirect-duty explanation—that cruelty to animals hardens our character toward other humans—seems to miss something I think is genuinely wrong. To test whether there is a coherent middle position, I want to propose a concept I am tentatively calling relational proxy dignity: the idea that animals lack direct moral rights in the Kantian sense, but they acquire a derivative, non-instrumental standing through the specific dependency and care relationships they enter with moral agents, so that harming them can be wrong in virtue of violating that relational standing rather than merely corrupting the agent's character. Here are my verdicts on five cases, and I want to know whether any established principle—strict Kantian indirect duty, direct rights theories, or my own relational proxy dignity—can capture all of them without forcing me to abandon one.

First, large-scale industrial livestock operations in which workers are kept entirely separate from the animals and experience no emotional exposure to suffering: I think this is gravely wrong, but the indirect-duty account seems silent, since no human agent's character is being hardened by direct cruelty. Second, abandoning a healthy, domesticated pet when one moves to an apartment that does not allow animals: I think this is wrong, and the wrongness seems partly grounded in the specific dependency the animal has developed, not only in the owner's future callousness. Third, cosmetic testing on primates conducted by researchers who are emotionally detached and experience no moral discomfort: I still think it is wrong, yet if the agent is genuinely unaffected, Kant's indirect-duty view appears to have no complaint. Fourth, painlessly euthanizing a suffering stray animal that has no owner or established relational network: I think this is permissible, perhaps even required, which suggests that mere biological life is not protected by whatever principle governs the other cases. Fifth, training a guide dog using aversive shock methods that are effective but cause the animal distress, where the direct benefit to a disabled human is substantial: I think this is wrong, and the wrongness seems to reside in what is done to the dog itself, not in any corruption of the trainer, especially since the trainer is serving a noble end.

Given these five verdicts, is there a single principle that delivers them all? If strict Kantian indirect duty cannot accommodate the first, third, and fifth cases without stretching the notion of character corruption beyond recognition, should I reject those verdicts, or should I reject the framework? Does relational proxy dignity actually do better, or does it collapse into either indirect duty on one side or full direct rights on the other? Finally, apply whatever principle you think best captures my judgments to a case I have not listed: a farmer who raises cattle with excellent care, forms recognizable bonds with them, and then slaughters them painlessly for profit. Would your principle condemn this, permit it, or leave it ambiguous, and does that result reveal an instability in the principle itself?
