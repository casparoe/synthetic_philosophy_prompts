In my senior-year literature seminar, our professor has consistently ruled on whether an author's private intentions should fix the meaning of a text. Her verdicts so far are these. First, a contemporary poet told an interviewer that her sonnet's imagery of melting ice and rising tides was meant to evoke climate anxiety rather than personal grief; the poem never mentions climate, politics, or collective crisis. Our professor ruled that we should read the imagery as referring to climate anxiety, since an author's intended referent governs genuinely ambiguous symbolic content. Second, a novelist wrote in his memoir that the protagonist of his best-known book was modeled directly on his father; the novel's descriptions of that character, however, differ in every significant biographical detail from the father. Our professor ruled that we should not import the autobiographical claim, because the text's explicit descriptions establish the character independently of the author's private associations. Third, an anonymous eighteenth-century political pamphlet was recently attributed to a known republican writer through stylistic analysis; the pamphlet's rhetoric is ambiguous between moderate constitutional reform and radical democratic revolution. Our professor ruled that, given the attribution, we should interpret the rhetoric in light of that author's documented republican commitments, since establishing authorship resolves ambiguity about political reference. Fourth, a modern translator's preface explains that she systematically replaced gendered pronouns with gender-neutral equivalents throughout her version of an ancient epic, even though the source language's gendered dynamics are clearly present in the original; our professor ruled that, for this translated text, the translator's intended gender-neutral framework determines meaning, not the original author's framework.

Recently, a digitized archive released a voice memo in which a well-known modernist essayist listens to a recording of her widely studied essay being read aloud. In the memo, she remarks that a particular paragraph—one that explicitly defends "unmediated rational progress" with no tonal shifts, no contradictory context, and no previously noted ironic framing—is "obviously ironic," though she never mentioned irony in any written correspondence or published commentary. The paragraph reads as a straightforward philosophical defense.

If our professor wants to remain consistent with her own precedents, how should she rule on whether that paragraph should be read as ironic? Which of her earlier rulings is the closest analog—the climate-anxiety poem or the autobiographical protagonist? More importantly, her past rulings seem jointly consistent with at least two different principles: one holding that authorial intention resolves interpretive ambiguity only where the text itself is genuinely underdetermined, and another holding that an author's retrospective statement about her own work is authoritative whenever it does not require contradicting an explicit textual fact. What does each principle predict about the voice-memo paragraph? Which prediction do you think is best, and why? If her precedents genuinely underdetermine her position here, what principle would she be adopting with each choice? Finally, describe one further hypothetical case—not among her precedents—that would best distinguish these two principles from each other.
