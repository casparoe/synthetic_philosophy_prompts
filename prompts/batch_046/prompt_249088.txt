My opening move: I want to test your myth thesis against what I will call the double-track problem of psychiatric explanation — a form of causal overdetermination specific to mental health. Consider severe melancholic depression. On one track, there is reproducible pharmacological efficacy: agents that modulate monoamine transmission produce measurable symptom remission in a subset of patients, with dose-response relationships that look like standard medical intervention. On a second, apparently independent track, structured psychotherapeutic interventions (cognitive restructuring, behavioral activation) produce comparable remission rates, without altering the same biological parameters, and often work in patients who do not respond to drugs. The two tracks are not reducible to one another; neither is merely placebo; both are necessary for optimal outcomes in many cases, yet neither explains why the other succeeds when it does. This creates an explanatory overdetermination: the symptomatic outcome seems to have two sufficient but heterogeneous causal pathways, with no unified underlying disease mechanism that selects between them or binds them together.

If "depression" named a single biological disease, we might expect one pathway to be derivative or epiphenomenal; if it named only a social or moral problem, we might expect the pharmacological track to be illusory or purely symptomatic. Yet both tracks are real, robust, and clinically indispensable. I challenge you: does this double-track pattern undermine your claim that mental illness is a myth? Or does it instead reveal that "depression" is not a disease entity at all, but a heterogeneous outcome label that gathers together biologically and socially distinct conditions under one mistaken medical category? Or is there a third Szaszian response?
