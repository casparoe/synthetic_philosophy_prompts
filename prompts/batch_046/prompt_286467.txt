I am preparing a seminar paper for my philosophy of law course on the legitimacy of post-war international criminal tribunals, and I am struggling with a specific conceptual tension in the Nuremberg and Tokyo proceedings. Both tribunals prosecuted defendants for crimes against peace and crimes against humanity—categories that lacked clear definition in positive international law prior to 1945—while simultaneously maintaining rigorous procedural safeguards, including full defense counsel, formal rules of evidence, and structured deliberation, precisely to distinguish the trials from mere victors' justice. When defendants and critics invoked the principle of nullum crimen sine lege, the tribunals and their defenders appealed instead to a universal moral law that transcends positive statute. Given this institutional design, what implicit view of legal authority, moral responsibility, and the rule of law does it presuppose? Please reconstruct the underlying philosophy: does it commit us to a form of moral realism about criminal wrongs, or can it be sustained by a thinner, purely procedural conception of justice? Is the combination of substantive retroactivity and procedural fairness philosophically coherent, or does it depend on quietly separating the ontology of crime from the epistemology of adjudication? Which rival positions—for example, a strict separation of law and morality, or a purely retributive framework that disregards procedural constraints—does this practice implicitly exclude? Finally, if we replaced its underlying view with an unqualified legal positivism, what precisely would have to change in the tribunal's jurisdiction, justification, or institutional structure?
