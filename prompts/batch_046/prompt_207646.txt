Instead, the agent should treat its decision procedure as a deterministic function—essentially a program with a fixed source code—and compute expected utility across all counterfactual worlds in which that same function outputs each possible action, weighted only by the prior distribution over worlds consistent with the agent's initial state. By refusing to condition on the actual output, UDT avoids the updating error that causal decision theory allegedly commits, namely treating one's own choice as fixed evidence about the external world. This is why UDT recommends one-boxing in Newcomb's problem. However, the summary added a further claim: because UDT rejects conditioning on its own outputs, it must also reject conditioning on any sensory inputs received after initialization, since those inputs are just further outputs of the world that would break the symmetry required for counterfactual evaluation. Consequently, according to the summary, a UDT agent is effectively a stateless process that evaluates its complete input-output mapping once at startup and then executes blindly, unable to learn from experience.

Part of this sounds like a plausible analogy to formal verification—evaluating a function over its specification—but the conclusion that the agent ignores all subsequent observations strikes me as wrong, since surely one can observe the world without treating one's own decision as evidence about it. I do not have the conceptual tools to identify exactly where the slide occurs. Could you audit this summary? Please distinguish what is accurate, what is a subtle misrepresentation or conflation, and whether the claim that UDT requires ignoring post-initialization sensory evidence actually follows from the theory's premises.
