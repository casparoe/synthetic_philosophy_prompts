Please analyse the period from Gorbachev's perestroika through the August coup, the December dissolution, Yeltsin's 1992 shock-therapy liberalisation, the 1993 constitutional crisis, and the 1995–96 loans-for-shares privatisation, using the vocabulary of path dependence — specifically increasing returns to institutional arrangements, reactive sequencing, and institutional complementarity. For each node, map who the relevant actors actually were, what options were genuinely available (not just retrospectively imaginable), and whether the payoffs made deviation from the emerging trajectory rational or impossible.

Then address the comparison that the theory must survive: China's gradualist transition from a comparable command-economy inheritance. I want an honest account of where path dependence illuminates the lock-in and where it is silent: for instance, whether the theory can explain why the 1993 crisis was resolved by presidential power rather than parliamentary democracy without smuggling in a teleology. Be disciplined about the mapping — specify the players, the options, and the evidence — and say where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which historical fact about 1991–96, if shown to be otherwise, or which theoretical refinement, would lead you to treat the Soviet collapse as genuinely contingent rather than structurally foreclosed, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
