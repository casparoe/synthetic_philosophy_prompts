You are a philosopher who writes and delivers public-facing institutional remarks, and you have been asked to open an interdisciplinary workshop. Please think carefully, and begin by briefly brainstorming the three different demands placed on this speech—what the philosophy and museum-studies graduate students need to hear in order to take the conceptual framing seriously, what the practicing conservators and archivists need in order to see their own work reflected rather than metaphorized away, and what the non-specialist board members need in order to understand why the workshop deserves their attention—before you draft the remarks themselves.

Write the opening address (about six to seven minutes, roughly 950 to 1150 words) for a one-day workshop titled "Continuity and Replacement: Institutions, Archives, and Material Culture," held in a university auditorium. The audience is deliberately mixed: conservators and digital archivists who manage physical and migrated collections; graduate students in philosophy and museum studies; and board members from the foundation that funded the event. The philosophical idea that must do real structural work—not decorative ornament—is the Ship of Theseus, but specifically in its less-discussed "two-ship" form: the replaced planks are not thrown away but stored in a warehouse and eventually reassembled into a second, competing vessel. Apply this to the situation of an institution whose original records sit in cold storage while the working, accessed archive is constantly reformatted, migrated, and replaced. Let this framing determine exactly what you can and cannot say: you must avoid offering false reassurance that institutional continuity is straightforward, but you must also avoid collapsing into the claim that the archive or the institution has no real identity over time. Because the room includes specialists who will spot sloppy analogies and practitioners who need practical takeaways, navigate precisely: state the conceptual tension sharply enough for the philosophers, translate it into the language of preservation, access, and organizational memory for the practitioners, and treat the ship not as a mere metaphor but as a genuine problem about identity conditions that happens to illuminate archival practice. You should not request citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; write as if you have no need to demonstrate scholarly coverage, and do not invent specific scholars, publication years, or verbatim quotations—paraphrase any ideas without quotation marks. After your brief brainstorming, draft the speech so that it welcomes the audience, establishes why the workshop is addressing one genuinely shared problem rather than several that only look alike, and closes by setting the terms of engagement for the day without resolving the paradox.
