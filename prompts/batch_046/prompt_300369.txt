I'm preparing a unit for my metaphysics course on optimism and the problem of evil, and I want to use Groundhog Day not as a cute illustration but as a serious interlocutor for Leibniz's claim that this world is the best of all possible worlds—not because it lacks suffering, but because any alteration that removed a particular evil would require a greater loss of compossible good. The film's structure, in which Phil Connors is trapped in a repeating February 2nd, seems to invite a modal reading: each loop looks like a possible world, and Phil moves through them like a selector testing combinations. I want a reading that takes both the film's narrative logic and Leibniz's actual metaphysics seriously.

Specifically, I'm interested in whether the loop dramatizes Leibniz's principle of compossibility—the idea that God does not choose the best individual things in isolation, but the best total combination of things that can exist together. In the early loops Phil treats the day as a sandbox for isolated optimizations: he robs a truck, seduces strangers, perfects his piano playing, all without lasting consequence. Later he attempts suicide repeatedly, as if trying to exit a world that contains too much local evil. Only in the final movement does he stop trying to change the day's fixed events and instead transforms his own responses to them—learning ice sculpture, saving a falling child, speaking kindly to everyone—until the loop finally breaks. Does this arc map onto the Leibnizian idea that the rational creature's freedom consists not in altering the pre-established harmony but in aligning one's internal complete concept with the fixed relations of the world? Or does the film smuggle in a Pelagian assumption—that Phil earns his release through moral self-improvement—thereby distorting Leibniz's view that liberation is never a reward for virtue but part of the optimal combination itself?

At the same time, I want to be honest about where the analogy strains. Leibniz holds that each substance has a complete concept that includes all its predicates and relations; if Phil truly changes his complete concept, then in a strict Leibnizian framework he should become a different individual, and the people he interacts with—Rita, Ned, the mayor—might also have to change, since their concepts include relations to him. The film, however, treats Rita and the townspeople as constant across loops, available for Phil to eventually "win" through accumulated knowledge of them. Does this reveal that Groundhog Day operates with a popular, "possible-worlds" modal realism—where worlds share individuals across variations—rather than with Leibniz's doctrine that individuals are world-bound? And does the film's happy ending, in which the loop breaks only after Phil achieves near-perfect virtue, suggest that the "best world" is one without repetition and without the suffering that repetition caused, or does it imply that the repetition was always a pedagogical instrument within the best world, like the permitted evil that makes greater goods possible?

I'm writing this for students who know the film well but haven't read Leibniz's writings on theodicy in detail, so I don't need a bibliography or a survey of scholarship. What I need is a careful, two-directional reading: one that shows what the film genuinely illuminates about compossibility, sufficient reason, and the place of evil in an optimal combination, and one that shows where the film's narrative logic resists the philosophical framework and forces the honest critic to say so—particularly regarding whether moral improvement can ever be the cause, rather than the symptom, of living in the best world.
