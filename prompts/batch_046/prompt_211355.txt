My audience will be heterogeneous: the faculty advisor is an environmental philosopher with broadly Kantian commitments who treats ecological integrity as possessing intrinsic value; two classmates are well-read effective altruists who are fluent in the replaceability argument and in suffering-weighted calculus for farmed animals; and three members of the ecology club are present who tend to view wild-animal suffering as outside the moral frame altogether and who are deeply skeptical of any metric that equates insect or rodent harm with mammalian harm.

Please lay out the objections I should expect, in the rough order they are likely to arrive, phrased in the vocabulary and conceptual framework each faction would actually use. For each objection, tell me whether I should concede it outright, hold firm with a qualification, or partially absorb it into a revised claim; identify which reply is weakest and where I risk being pushed into an empirical corner about mortality-rate estimates or about the moral weighting of invertebrate versus vertebrate suffering; and flag the one objection—perhaps about the land-use efficiency of plant agriculture or about the non-identity problem applied to wild animals—that I am secretly hoping nobody raises, together with the briefest defensible reply I could give if it comes up. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need a speaker’s defense map calibrated to this specific room.
