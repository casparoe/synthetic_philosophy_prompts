I've been trying to sketch an essay on voting ethics and I'm a bit stuck on the framing. The usual story is either civic duty (you ought to vote because democracy needs participation) or pure expression (your ballot is like stating who you are). I want to push a third angle: voting as a collective damage-limitation ritual. The core idea is that you don't vote positively for someone; you vote to shrink the expected harm from the worst viable winner. So the moral weight sits in the statistical gap between bad and worse, not in endorsing a platform.

I've got rough notes saying this explains why people feel okay backing the 'lesser evil' without actually endorsing them, and why abstaining feels negligent even in safe seats — you're still contributing to the ritual threshold that legitimises the result. But I'm worried this just collapses into consequentialism with extra steps, or that I'm ignoring the expressive value people clearly attach to symbolic representation. Also, theres a tension: if voting is purely harm-reduction, why do voters get angry about 'wasted' protest votes? Shouldn't they be neutral if harm is minimised either way?

Could you comment on these notes? I'd like your take on whether the framing holds water, whether I've missed an obivous objection about expressive value, and if there's a cleaner way to seperate ritual participation from ordinary moral duty. No need for citations, just your honest reaction.
