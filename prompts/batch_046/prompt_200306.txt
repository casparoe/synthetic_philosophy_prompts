I want the full specification for what a theory of rational choice in conditions of genuine preference absence actually needs to cover. Give me the requirements list: the cases it must handle (perfect symmetry, near-perfect symmetry, repeated identical choices, and a scenario where delaying the choice incurs an accelerating penalty while the options remain indistinguishable in every describable feature except location), the distinctions it must preserve (between arbitrary selection and compelled selection, between lacking a preference and being actively indifferent, and between a resolution mechanism and a post-hoc rationalization), and the results from psychology or decision science about paralysis and computation cost that it cannot contradict. For each requirement, explain why it is necessary and whether it is negotiable. Tell me which single requirement does the most damage to the common accounts — hidden-preference models, pure randomization accounts, and will-based resolution theories — and if you score those briefly against the list, keep the weight on the criteria themselves, since adequacy here should be judged relative to explaining choice without preference rather than preserving any particular metaphysics of the will.
