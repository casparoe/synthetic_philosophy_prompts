I am preparing questions for a podcast interview I will host next month, and the subject has become personally unavoidable. Six months ago, while serving on an institutional review board assessing high-risk cognitive-enhancement protocols, I found myself locked in a sustained disagreement with two senior colleagues about acceptable risk thresholds. I had first-order arguments that a particular protocol was ethically permissible, supported by what I took to be sound comparative reasoning; yet I also possessed robust peer-feedback—backed by my own documented error rates in structurally similar judgments—indicating that my risk-assessment heuristic was specifically unreliable in exactly this domain. I could not resolve the tension by straightforward deference without abandoning my first-order reasoning altogether, and I could not dismiss the peer feedback without seeming irrationally stubborn. This experience of holding a belief while simultaneously holding that one ought not to hold it—epistemic akrasia in a practical reasoning context—is why I invited my guest.

She is a philosopher who defends a conciliatory but bounded account of higher-order defeat. On her view, when you acquire domain-specific higher-order evidence of your own reasoning failure, rationality requires you to reduce confidence in your first-order belief proportionally to the strength of that evidence, but only within a stability window defined by the structural coherence of your first-order argument; outright suspension of judgment is required only when the unreliability is domain-general or when your first-order confidence already lies near the boundary of the window. She distinguishes her position from steadfastness, which she says ignores the higher-order evidence entirely, and from full conciliationism, which she argues collapses into self-undermining skepticism because the higher-order judgment about unreliability is itself produced by the very faculty under suspicion.

I want eight to ten interview questions that turn on the actual mechanics of her view rather than asking for a general introduction. First, I need at least one question about how her stability window avoids the self-reference problem: if the cognitive faculty generating the higher-order belief that I am unreliable is the same faculty generating my first-order belief about the protocol, why does the defeat not apply reflexively to the higher-order belief itself? For that question, include a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that the higher-order belief is formed through a distinct metacognitive monitoring process rather than through the primary reasoning pathway, pressing her on how we identify that monitoring process without circular appeal to its own outputs.

Second, I need at least one question about the boundary between epistemic and practical irrationality: if I reduce my confidence in the protocol's permissibility in light of the peer feedback but still cast my vote to approve it because institutional rules demand a decision, am I exercising a rational division of labor between belief and action, or am I exhibiting a kind of practical akrasia? Include a follow-up that anticipates her saying that practical contexts license acting on residual confidence and presses her on whether that license survives cases where the practical stakes involve irreversible harm and the residual confidence is precisely what the higher-order evidence targeted.

Third, I want at least one question about a pattern I have started calling calibration drift—the gradual, usually unnoticed shift in an expert's internal confidence scale caused by repeated exposure to edge cases, such that peer disagreement functions not as direct counter-evidence to the first-order claim but as an external calibration signal revealing that the scale itself has shifted. Ask whether her view treats calibration drift as a genuine form of higher-order evidence about reasoning reliability, or rather as a background interpretive condition that changes how first-order evidence should be weighted, and include a follow-up keyed to her likely claim that drift is best handled by institutional checks rather than by individual belief revision, pressing her on what individual rationality requires when institutional checks are absent or themselves contested.

Please write the questions in a tone appropriate for serious philosophical conversation: precise, respectful, but unafraid to press on the weak points where her distinctions might blur. Do not include generic biographical questions, and do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
