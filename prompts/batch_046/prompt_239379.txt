The poverty-of-stimulus argument for linguistic innateness and its rejection by statistical-learning and constructivist camps have run since roughly the 1960s without either side conceding. The pattern is now ritualized: one side cites children’s rapid acquisition of syntax despite sparse, noisy input and the absence of explicit negative feedback; the other responds that domain-general statistical learning, distributional analysis, and social-pragmatic cues supply sufficient data, with recent neural-network language models offered as existence proofs. My working theory is that the stalemate is not about learnability at all but about explanatory adequacy—whether approximating grammatical behavior in a statistical model explains the human capacity, or whether explanation requires representing abstract rules independently of performance data. Is that right? Or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about the actual information content of child-directed speech, about the representational format required for generative syntax, or about whether comparing children to large models is conceptually fair? I want a diagnosis of the disagreement’s structure: do the sides share one question and differ on evidence; do they talk past each other because “innateness” and “learning” carry different theoretical loads; or is the framing ill-posed because it assumes a false dichotomy between innate structure and statistical extraction? And given your diagnosis, what—if anything—would move it: a precise information-theoretic measurement of the input environment, a formal result on the sample complexity of specific syntactic constructions, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the framing, or should we expect permanent deadlock?
