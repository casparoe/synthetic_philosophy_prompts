I'm an undergraduate philosophy major in an upper-level epistemology seminar, and for our closing session I've been asked to present a villanelle that makes its philosophical argument through form rather than exposition. I want the poem to stage the tension between Nozick's tracking conditions for knowledge and the principle of epistemic closure. Use the traditional villanelle structure—nineteen lines, iambic pentameter, ABA rhyme for the tercets and ABAA for the final quatrain—with two refrains that alternate through the poem and finally appear together in the last couplet. One refrain should voice the tracking idea, that knowing p requires that in nearby worlds where p is false the subject does not believe p; the other should voice closure, that if one knows p and knows p entails q then one knows q. The rhyme scheme should hold the two lines in parallel without resolving them, and the final couplet should bring them side by side only to show they pass each other without meeting.

To keep this from being a generic philosophy poem, ground it in a specific observational scenario: a naturalist in the field who tracks that an animal in an enclosure is a zebra, knows that being a zebra entails not being a mule painted with stripes, yet finds that the tracking condition for the entailment fails. The failure should turn on modal distance—the worlds where the deception holds are closer than the worlds where the species identity shifts—so that the breakdown is in the adherence condition measured across possibility space, not in logical entailment itself. The turn in the poem should occur where the reader realizes this: that closure looks intact from the inside of the belief, but tracking measures distance differently for p and for q.

Write it precisely enough that someone who has worked through Nozick's tracking theory and the closure debate would recognize the argument, not just the vocabulary. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or references to specific texts or page numbers; the poem should stand on its own for an audience that knows the debate. Make the form do the work: the returning lines should feel like conditions that keep reasserting themselves, and the closing couplet should leave the two principles adjacent but unreconciled.
