I'm writing from a constitutional-logic seminar I'm taking this term. We've worked through the classic self-amendment puzzles—Ross's paradox and the omnipotence problems raised by fully entrenched amendment rules—but I keep returning to a hypothetical design that seems to sit in the gap between them. Imagine a legal order with two tiers: ordinary statutes, and a higher tier containing the rules of recognition and amendment. The higher tier includes a self-entrenchment provision stating that no ordinary statute may repeal or suspend any higher-tier rule. Now introduce a temporal clause: every fifty years, all higher-tier rules automatically lapse unless they are explicitly renewed through a procedure defined within the higher tier itself. The catch is that the renewal vote is only valid if the self-entrenchment provision is in force at the moment of voting; yet once the sunset triggers, that provision is suspended, which appears to invalidate the very mechanism required to restore it. I've encountered discussions of intertemporal conflict in constitutional law, and of course the standard paradoxes of legal self-reference, but this particular loop—where temporal expiration and self-protective validity conditions create a constitutional deadlock—doesn't map neatly onto either. Does this configuration have an established name? How precisely does it differ from Ross's paradox of self-amendment and from the omnipotence paradox of constitutional design? And if there is no specific term, should it be treated as a distinct variant of legal self-reference, or as a problem belonging to a different category altogether—say, intertemporal jurisprudence?
