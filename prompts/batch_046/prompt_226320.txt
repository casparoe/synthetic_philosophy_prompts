I'm helping organize an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Binding Force and Past Decisions," and I've been asked to deliver the opening remarks. The audience is split evenly between practicing clinicians, who treat advance directives as binding expressions of patient autonomy, and philosophers, who question whether a later demented self is genuinely the same authority that signed the document. I want the remarks to do real structural work: they must take the philosophical doubt seriously by framing the directive not as an inherited command from a past self but as a delegated authorization that depends on the present clinical team's ability to reconstruct, rather than merely apply, the values behind it. This should let me reject the voice-from-beyond metaphor without undermining the clinicians, since I can claim that in most cases the reconstruction succeeds and the practical commitment holds, but I must also leave open that where reconstruction genuinely fails, the authority lapses rather than being overridden. The remarks should run about five minutes, be formal but accessible to non-philosophers, and contain no named philosophers, citations, or references to specific texts — the conceptual architecture alone should carry the argument. Could you draft them?
