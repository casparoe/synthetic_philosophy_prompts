I write as a retired experimental biologist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been working through philosophy of science and, more recently, Richard Rorty’s neopragmatism and antirepresentationalism. In my scientific career I assumed without question that a mature theory describes structures that exist independently of our descriptions of them; Rorty’s challenge to this representationalist picture strikes me as both attractive and slippery, because several quite different claims travel under the same label, and I am unsure which ones survive serious scrutiny when applied to actual physical theories rather than to ordinary language.

To help me disentangle the philosophically substantial core from the merely provocative restatements of familiar scientific practice, I ask you to evaluate the following five theses—each of which might be called “antirepresentationalist”—with respect to mature theoretical physics, say quantum electrodynamics or statistical mechanics. For each thesis, assign two scores on a scale of 1 to 10: (A) fidelity to a position Rorty actually holds or would endorse, and (B) defensibility when applied specifically to the practice of high-energy physics or field theory, not to ethics or everyday discourse. Then rank the five theses from strongest overall to weakest, presenting each entry with its paired scores and a concise integrated justification that explains where fidelity and defensibility diverge and why. I am especially interested in whether the biological analogy in thesis 4 is a genuine extension of Rorty’s view or an alien imposition from my own former discipline; address that point explicitly for that entry.

The five theses are:

1. That the aim of scientific inquiry is not to mirror an independent reality but to construct vocabularies that enable successful prediction and technological intervention, with no further metaphysical legitimation required.

2. That the scheme-content distinction, as applied in scientific reasoning, collapses because there is no raw, unconceptualized “given” against which theoretical schemes are tested—only further descriptions made within other schemes.

3. That when physicists debate whether an unobservable entity—say, a virtual excitation or a gauge-dependent potential—is “real,” the dispute is not genuinely ontological but concerns whether including that item in our vocabulary improves problem-solving capacity.

4. That scientific progress should be understood not as asymptotic convergence on a uniquely correct description of nature, but as an evolutionary increase in the diversity and flexibility of descriptive tools, analogous to adaptive radiation in biological lineages.

5. That the very notion of “representational accuracy” should be eliminated from the philosophy of science and replaced entirely by a pragmatic criterion of coherence with existing experimental practice—effectively treating theoretical physics as a branch of engineering discourse with no representational pretensions whatsoever.

Please present your answer as a ranked list, with brief but precise reasoning for each placement, and avoid bibliographic references or appeals to secondary literature; I want to see the evaluation carried out from first principles and from what can be said about Rorty’s view and about physical practice without consulting external sources.
