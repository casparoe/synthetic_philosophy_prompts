I'm a journalist who usually writes about science and policy overlap, and next Thursday I'm giving the opening remarks at a workshop in Berlin titled 'Who Pays for the Soul? Church Tax, Establishment, and Secular States.' The audience is going to be a weird mix — constitutional lawyers who can cite the Basic Law in their sleep, theologains who flew in from Oxford, and local activists who think secularism just means 'leave religion out of it.' I need about five minutes of spoken text that actually structures the day, not just welcomes people. I want to hang everything on this idea that secularism isn't the absence of religion but a specific fiscal and admnistrative arrangement: in Germany you don't just stop believing, you file a Kirchenaustritt and the state stops collecting Kirchensteuer for you, which means unbelief is literally an administrative category you have to declare. Meanwhile England keeps an established church with no direct church tax, France runs laïcité that bans state funding but also regulates religious expression in public, and the US hides its subsidy inside tax exemptions. The philosophical hook — and this is what I need help framing — is whether every secular state is actually running a hidden fiscal theology, deciding not whether God exists but what belonging costs and what leaving requires. It needs to land for experts without losing the activists, and it should ask the question without pretending I have the answer. I'm writing this late, probably with a typo or two in my draft, so keep the tone like a smart reporter talking — conversational, a little messy, definately not a lecture. No citations, no bibliography, just the speech text.
