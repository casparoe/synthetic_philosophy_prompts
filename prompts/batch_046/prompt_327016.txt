I'm trying to identify a concept I keep encountering in suffering-focused ethics and population ethics debates, but I don't know whether it has an established name. Consider this pattern: an interlocutor treats risks of severe suffering very differently depending on whether the affected beings currently exist. For existing populations, they accept ordinary expected-value reasoning—if a policy carries a small chance of catastrophic suffering but large expected welfare gains, they weigh the probabilities in the usual way. But for potential populations—beings who would come into existence only if certain conditions are met, or whose existence is contingent on the policy being adopted—they invoke something closer to an absolute veto: if there's any non-negligible path to severe suffering for those contingent beings, the option is ruled out regardless of the expected value, even when the statistical risk profile is structurally identical to the existing-population case.

I've noticed this in two contexts. First, in debates about expanding high-risk animal agriculture: critics reject the expansion not merely because expected suffering is high, but because they frame it as generating "a lottery over suffering for beings who wouldn't otherwise exist," and they treat that lottery as categorically impermissible. Yet the same critics often support incremental welfare reforms for currently existing farmed animals that carry comparable statistical risks of suffering. Second, in longtermist discussions about existential risk and future generations, the same thinkers apply standard risk-benefit calculus to suffering risks faced by currently living people, but switch to a near-absolute precautionary stance when the suffering would befall future people whose very existence is contingent on the path taken.

This is not simply the standard procreation asymmetry—creating suffering is wrong, creating happiness is optional—because it operates even when the beings in question are not being deliberately brought into existence for the sake of the outcome; they are side-effect populations whose existence is probabilistically tied to the policy rather than intended by it. Nor is it ordinary risk aversion, since the aversion activates selectively based on the moral-status-contingency of the beings at risk rather than on the magnitude or probability of the suffering itself. Is there an established term for this specific configuration—an existence-contingent, suffering-specific veto on risk-taking? If there is none, how should it be distinguished from person-affecting restrictions, the procreation asymmetry, and domain-general precautionary reasoning? And does the pattern point to a coherent underlying principle, or is it best understood as an unstable hybrid that collapses under closer analysis?
