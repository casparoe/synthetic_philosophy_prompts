I'm a high school junior invited to deliver remarks at our town's Founders' Day ceremony, where there is active debate about retaining the name of the local library, named after an eighteenth-century regional founder who established the town's first free school but also held enslaved people. The audience is mixed: local historians, council members, parents, and fellow students. I need a four-minute speech that treats the "man of his time" defense not as a casual excuse but as a serious claim in moral epistemology—namely, that people in past eras lacked the conceptual resources to recognize certain wrongs. I want the speech to turn on one specific, somewhat unconventional implication: that if we apply this defense consistently, we must also apply it to our own present failures, which means the defense does not preserve our moral confidence in condemning the past but rather dissolves the asymmetry between then and now. This point should do real structural work: it should shape what I can say about the founder, about analogies to figures such as Jefferson or Churchill, and about our current obligations, without collapsing into a simple "rename" or "retain" verdict. The remarks should be precise enough that specialists in the audience do not wince, yet fully accessible to everyone else. Please write the speech. I do not need citations or a bibliography; I will supply any references myself.
