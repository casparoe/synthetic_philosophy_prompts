I am writing for my political philosophy course on democratic legitimacy and the boundary problem. Our readings have focused on the standard dilemma—whether the demos should be defined by subjection to coercive law, by affected interests, or by citizenship status—but I have become preoccupied with a concrete institutional case that seems to scramble these categories in ways the literature does not address directly. At my university, the graduate student senate grants full voting rights on curricular and fee-related matters to all enrolled doctoral candidates, including those on temporary student visas who will leave the country upon graduation; simultaneously, it denies any vote to postdoctoral researchers who hold multi-year contracts, teach required courses, and pay the same mandatory facility fees, on the grounds that they are employees rather than members of the student body. It also excludes part-time master's students registered through a professional-school division, even when they sit in the same seminars and are assessed by the same committees. The senate's formal justification appeals to a direct and continuing stake in the academic community, yet the boundary it draws tracks administrative employment codes rather than duration of presence, intensity of interaction, or exposure to institutional authority. What theory, if any, of democratic membership does this practice actually presuppose? Can an affected-interests view accommodate the exclusion of long-term postdocs who are clearly subject to departmental authority? Can a subject-to-authority view accommodate the inclusion of temporary international students who will depart before most policies take effect? Or does the practice reveal that institutional boundary-drawing is driven not by a coherent normative theory but by a prior commitment to preserving a particular organizational identity—namely, that the student demos must correspond to a registrarial category? And if so, what does this imply about the feasibility of applying philosophical boundary principles to real-world collective decision-making bodies?
