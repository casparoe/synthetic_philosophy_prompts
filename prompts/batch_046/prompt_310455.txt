I serve on my university’s Strategic Allocation Committee, which oversees a new $40 million interdisciplinary research endowment. By our March meeting we must adopt a formal evaluation framework for grant proposals. One faction demands that every application include quantified risk probabilities and expected-value calculations; another faction, invoking Keynes’s distinction between measurable risk and radical uncertainty, contends that for genuinely novel interdisciplinary work statistical frequencies are absent not because evidence is lacking but because the sample space itself is undefined, so imposing probabilistic metrics will systematically misweight proposals by treating an open epistemic field as if it carried zero weight. They further appeal to Keynes’s claim that the “weight” of an argument—its total body of relevant evidence—can increase even when no precise numerical probability can be assigned, and they propose that reviewers rate “argument weight” and “structural openness to revision” rather than expected value. The quantitative faction replies that without probabilistic discipline allocation becomes arbitrary or captured by disciplinary prestige, and that what is called “radical uncertainty” is often just incomplete information that rigorous modeling can progressively reduce.

The committee is deadlocked. Please draft the formal decision memo I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—whether to adopt the quantitative mandate, reject it for this fund, institute a dual-track evaluation system, or impose conditional safeguards—together with an explicit implementation time frame and a scheduled review date six months hence. The memo should engage Keynes’s risk/uncertainty distinction seriously, including the specific idea that argument weight can grow independently of any assigned probability, and it should treat the quantitative faction’s strongest objection honestly rather than dismissing it. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. End with a fixed bottom line on its own line: Verdict: [the recommended framework in a short phrase], followed immediately by a one-sentence summary of the core reasoning on the next line.
