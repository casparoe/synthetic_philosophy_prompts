I have been reflecting on the meta-philosophical debate about whether philosophy makes genuine progress, and I am struck by how little the opposing camps seem to move one another despite decades of exchange. On one side, critics observe that the central problems of metaphysics, epistemology and ethics remain structurally intact across centuries, with little predictive success or institutional convergence comparable to the sciences; they treat the persistence of disagreement about free will, consciousness or moral status as evidence of stagnation. On the other side, defenders argue that philosophy advances not by delivering definitive answers but by refining distinctions, dissolving confusions, and improving conceptual architecture — they point to the development of modal logic, decision theory, or the precise formulation of supervenience relations as clear instances of cumulative achievement. Yet the debate feels curiously static: critics demand benchmarks borrowed from empirical disciplines, while defenders redefine progress as increased articulation or problem-dissolution, and neither side appears to address the possibility that they are measuring different dimensions.

My working hypothesis is that this is not simply a substantive disagreement about historical facts, but a multi-layered impasse. Part of it may be verbal — a dispute over whether the word 'progress' ought to carry connotations of convergence and prediction, or whether it can denote the horizontal expansion of coherent conceptual space without vertical resolution. Part of it may involve different questions being answered under one heading: one camp asks whether philosophy approximates scientific knowledge-accumulation, while the other asks whether it improves our cognitive or normative frameworks, and these are not necessarily rival answers to the same query. I also suspect there is a hidden normative disagreement about whether progress must be an intrinsic good requiring cumulative, irreversible knowledge, or whether it can be a local, practical virtue satisfied by problem-solving within particular contexts, together with an unacknowledged question about the conditions for epistemic division of labour in a non-experimental field.

I would like you to diagnose the structure of this disagreement using what each side actually says. Do they differ about empirical evidence — for instance, about the sociology of philosophical practice or the actual trajectory of specific sub-disciplines? Do they differ about which cases count as data, such as whether the endurance of the mind-body problem outweighs the refinement of modal semantics? Or do they rest on starting intuitions about what counts as knowledge or improvement that neither side can independently justify? Is there a formal or empirical claim doing hidden work?

In addition, I want you to consider whether the stall signals that the question is ill-posed, or merely that it is hard. Given your diagnosis, tell me what, if anything, would count as progress in resolving the meta-debate itself: a conceptual distinction that separates competing metrics of vertical and horizontal achievement; an empirical study of how philosophical problems actually evolve; a formal result about convergence in non-empirical domains; or a change of question that renders the original dispute moot. Alternatively, argue that no resolution should be expected, and explain why the disagreement is structurally irresolvable. Please avoid giving a simple verdict on whether philosophy progresses; the deliverable is the anatomy of the disagreement and the conditions for its resolution or dissolution.
