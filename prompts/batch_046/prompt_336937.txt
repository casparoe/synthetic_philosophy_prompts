I am a high school student working on an independent study project about self-binding mechanisms in democratic theory. I have been trying to understand the analogy between individual Ulysses contracts—where a single agent restricts their future choices to avoid weakness of will—and collective constitutional precommitment, in which a founding generation entrenches rules that bind later democratic majorities.

What I want to clarify is a conceptual tension I am calling diachronic agency asymmetry. In the individual case, the agent who ties herself to the mast and the agent who later wants to untie herself are the same continuous person; the restriction can therefore be interpreted as one extended exercise of autonomy over time. In the collective case, however, the constituent power that writes the precommitment and the future citizens who live under it are not the same participatory agents. The future majority bears the costs of the binding—what we might call asymmetric exit costs—without having consented to it, yet if every generation retains full revisory power, the constitutional mechanism loses its point and becomes indistinguishable from ordinary legislation.

Please explain whether this asymmetry undermines the autonomy-based justification for constitutional precommitment. Specifically: must defenders of such precommitment rely on a notion of a single, diachronically continuous democratic "people" in order to preserve the analogy with individual Ulysses contracts? Or is there a conceptually coherent middle ground—perhaps a framework of conditional or layered precommitment that allows future revision only under specified procedural burdens—such that stability is preserved without treating future citizens as mere objects of the founding generation's will? I am looking for a conceptual analysis rather than historical examples or citations; in particular, I want to know what adjustments the autonomy defense requires to survive the objection that collective binding is not self-binding but other-binding across generations.
