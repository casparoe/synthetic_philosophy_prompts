I'm writing a paper for my seminar on normative ethics and the philosophy of artificial intelligence, and I've become convinced that coherent extrapolated volition—roughly, the view that we should determine collective outcomes by asking what we would want if we were more rational, better informed, and had reflected longer—offers the best framework for resolving deep normative disagreement, particularly in alignment contexts where we need to aggregate values across diverse populations. The view seems attractive because it respects autonomy in a non-trivial way: it doesn't impose an external standard of the good, but instead tries to recover what agents already implicitly endorse once cognitive and informational limitations are corrected. At the same time, I'm trying to assess whether this position is genuinely exposed to refutation or whether it has been insulated by qualifications, and I want an honest map of its vulnerability rather than a list of objections that defenders can dismiss with a wave.

In particular, I've been thinking about what I call the "depth limit problem" for recursive idealization. If coherence requires us to idealize not only our first-order preferences but also our meta-preferences about how far idealization should go—for instance, whether we want to remain the kind of agents who value authenticity or emotional attachment over fully corrected preference—then we seem to face either an infinite regress or an arbitrary stopping point. A defender might respond that coherence naturally terminates at a fixed point where further reflection produces no change, but I'm unsure whether that response assumes precisely what needs to be shown about the stability of second-order volition. So I'd like to know: what observations, arguments, formal results, or conceptual developments would actually count against the CEV framework? Which of these are live rather than merely logical possibilities—for example, instability under recursive self-modification, conflicts with results in social choice theory about the impossibility of aggregating diverse rational preferences into a coherent ordering, or clashes with contractualist accounts of reasonable rejection?

Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and distinguish what committed defenders of idealized-preference aggregation would themselves concede as damaging from what they would dismiss as missing the point. I also want to understand what distinctive predictions this framework makes that rival approaches—say, democratic proceduralism, contractualist reasonableness, or preference-satisfaction utilitarianism—do not make, and whether any of those distinctive predictions are empirically or conceptually risky. Finally, is the position well insulated in a way that makes it unfalsifiable, and is that insulation a philosophical strength or a methodological weakness?
