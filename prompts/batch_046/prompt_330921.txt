Here is an argument I built for a seminar on Dewey and now find suspicious because it seems to prove that his experimentalism undermines the very democratic authority he wants to preserve. (1) Dewey holds that democratic legitimacy depends on treating social policies as working hypotheses within an ongoing inquiry, not as fixed rules justified by prior authority or eternal principle. (2) A working hypothesis is warranted only provisionally: its value lies in directing action that transforms an indeterminate situation, and it must be revised or abandoned if the consequences show the situation is not being resolved. (3) So if a democratic law is genuinely adopted as a working hypothesis, then neither citizens nor officials are committed to its continued enforcement; they are committed only to the institutional process of testing and, if necessary, replacing it. (4) But democratic authority requires that citizens have an obligation to obey specific enacted laws even when they personally judge the preliminary results poor, because otherwise majoritarian coordination collapses into a collection of individual vetoes. (5) Therefore democratic authority requires treating laws as fixed commitments, while democratic legitimacy requires treating them as permanently revisable instruments. (6) These are incompatible: one cannot be both unconditionally bound to obey a law and provisionally testing it as an instrument that may be discarded. (7) Dewey nevertheless insists that democracy is both a mode of social intelligence and a form of community life that generates genuine shared obligations among citizens. (8) So Dewey’s position entails that democracy is legitimate only if it lacks the binding authority that makes it a form of government at all. I would most like to keep (1) and (7). Tell me which step to reject: is there an equivocation between applying “working hypothesis” to an institution versus to an individual belief, or a scope shift between revisability-in-principle and absence-of-obligation-in-practice? Explain why the step looked plausible, and price the alternatives—what I pay if I save democratic authority, and what I pay if I save pure experimentalism.
