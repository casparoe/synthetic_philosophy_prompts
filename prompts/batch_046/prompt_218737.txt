I was arguing with my roommate about whether Maimonides would be vegan if he lived now, but then I started thinking about his actual reasoning in the Guide about sacrifices being a kind of pedagogical bridge away from pagan slaughter rituals. So here's a weirder version: imagine Maimonides is around today and he has to advise a Jewish community that's trying to replace the entire concept of animal sacrifice with a ritual of "reverse offering" — where people donate lab-cultured tissue samples to a bioreacter that produces symbolic meat substitutes, and the "sacrifice" is actually the giving up of one's own cultivated cells rather than killing an animal. He'd have to figure out whether this fits his theory that commandments adapt to human psycholgical needs without being literally necessary. Would he see this as the logical endpoint of his concession theory, or as a category mistake because there's no living creature being offered?

I'm especially curious how he'd apply his distinction between the essential purpose of worship (directing the mind toward God) versus the accidental form (blood and smoke) to a situation where the form is biotechnology but the intent is still communal atonement. Would he think the need for tangable loss is still being met, or is giving up a petri-dish sample too abstract to do the moral work he thought sacrifices were meant to do? Also, since he was pretty clear that some commandments exist just because people in a particular historical moment need them, does that mean once factory farming and cultured meat make animal death unnecessary, the whole sacrificial framework should dissolve into pure prayer — or does the human craving for physical ritual mean we'd still need some version of this, just with human-derived tissue instead?
