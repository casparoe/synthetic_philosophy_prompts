I am preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for second-year philosophy undergraduates who have just finished an introductory ethics course but have not yet encountered population axiology, and I want a teaching plan for the repugnant conclusion presented entirely in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write in a long-form letter. Lay out the precise order in which the pieces must be introduced—specifically why average and total utilitarianism need to be separated before introducing any quality-of-life axis, where the mere-addition argument belongs in that sequence, and which concept depends on which—together with one concrete carrying example that avoids the standard abstract comparison between world A and world Z and instead uses a fixed municipal cultural budget distributed across successive generations with diminishing per-capita returns. Identify the misconceptions students will carry over from prior exposure to trolley problems and threshold deontology, especially the hidden assumption that there must be a non-arbitrary quality floor below which lives are not worth adding, and specify exactly where in the explanation each misconception should be defused before it hardens. Include a brief check for understanding that reveals whether students see why the conclusion is structurally forced rather than merely counterintuitive, and state explicitly what technical apparatus—such as critical-level utilitarianism, person-affecting restrictions, or formal axiomatic comparison—should be left out and openly acknowledged as beyond this level. Do not provide readings, citations, or a bibliography.
