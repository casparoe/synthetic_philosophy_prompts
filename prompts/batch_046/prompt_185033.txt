Draft a joint adversarial-collaboration statement between a philosopher who defends the epistemic closure principle for knowledge—holding that if S knows p and knows p entails q, then S knows q—and a philosopher who endorses Robert Nozick’s tracking theory and therefore rejects closure, on the grounds that knowledge of an entailed proposition can fail its counterfactual conditions even when knowledge of the base proposition succeeds. Structure the document in three parts: first, a numbered section of claims both sides can sign, including (i) the four counterfactual conditions Nozick places on S knowing p, (ii) the formal statement of closure under known entailment, and (iii) the structure of the skeptical argument that exploits closure to generate global skepticism from local doubts; second, a disagreements table that states, in wording each camp would accept for its own view, the exact divergence—specifically, whether the failure of tracking for q undermines S’s knowledge of p, or whether closure simply does not apply to propositions whose tracking conditions are unsatisfied, and whether knowledge is a unitary cognitive achievement or a proposition-relative relation; third, a cruxes section identifying at least two sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side, such as whether the skeptical argument is a reductio of closure or a correct description of tracking’s limitations, and whether “knowing” in ordinary language tracks closure-licensed inference or counterfactual-sensitive belief. For each crux, present a concrete case both sides agree is relevant: for example, a climate scientist who tracks the global temperature trajectory predicted by her model and knows that this entails specific regional drought probabilities, but whose model lacks the resolution to track those regional outcomes, and ask both camps to record in advance whether she knows the regional drought claim. Finally, include a brief conclusion assessing whether any portion of the dispute is verbal—e.g., whether the closure defender and the tracking theorist are using “knowledge” to denote different achievements—and, if so, identify precisely which part and why. Write in formal, neutral language; do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or references to specific page numbers.
