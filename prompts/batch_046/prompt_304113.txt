hey, i'm drafting a longform science-and-ideas column on why we keep misreading 1789, and i've gotten weirdly attached to this hybrid framing. basically, i think the french revolution wasn't driven by class struggle or some big ideological blueprint, but by a contigency cascade—specifically, a cluster of fiscal micro-decisions in 1787-88 that a slightly different court culture could have absorbed without collapse. and the whole revolutions-devour-their-children line? i think it's wrong to read it as radicalization dynamics. instead it's institutional digestion: the new state metabolizes its own creators into bureacratic machinery, not out of betrayal but because the administrative vacuum pulls them in. so the revolution eats its children the way an organism digests food—functional, not malicious.

what i need is a real stress-test. not the standard marxist objection or the revisionist pushback that it was all culture, but the actual defeaters: which comparative cases—say, 1848 or 1917—break the cascade pattern? which results about institutional path dependence does this view need to survive? which intuitions about agency and structure does it have to swallow? and if that fiscal cluster was really so fragile, why didn't similar absolutist crises elsewhere trigger the same collapse? rank these from fatal to merely awkward. also, what would a committed contingency-plus-digestion theorist actually concede as counting against them, versus what they'd dismiss as missing the point? does giving up the class-struggle and ideology lenses entirely cost us explanatory power we can't recover? i'm especially worried this view has been over-insulated by qualifications—does it risk anything real, or is it basically unfalsifiable?

sorry for the ramble—this is for an editor who wants to know the framing can take a punch before i pitch it properly. typo's are mine, not the copy desk's.
