I am moderating a panel interview for a philosophy-of-science podcast, and next week's guest is an unnamed philosopher of biology who defends a view she calls dynamic pluralism about the species problem. Her core claim is that reproductive isolation, ecological niche occupancy, and phylogenetic branching each define genuinely different groupings, and that the demand for a single unified species concept rests on an unjustified essentialist assumption that causal reality requires ontological uniformity. She extends this with a less common claim: evolutionary lineages are causally efficacious in the same way that meteorological fronts are—real enough to enter into explanations and predictions—even though there is no single correct criterion for drawing their boundaries. I want eight to ten interview questions that press her on the substance of this analogy. Specifically, I need questions that: (1) ask how lineage-level causation can work if the entities in question lack the kind of internal unity usually thought necessary for causal powers; (2) anticipate her likely reply that niche-relational properties solve this problem, and include a follow-up showing why that reply may just relocate the pluralism rather than resolve it; (3) ask whether her view avoids collapsing into anti-realism about species altogether, given that she denies there is one thing that all species are; (4) address the practical consequence for conservation biology—if legal protections apply to discrete species, and her view denies that discreteness is ontologically fundamental, what should policymakers do? For each question, please sketch the answer she is most likely to give and suggest a sharp follow-up keyed to that answer. Avoid generic opening questions about her career or about defining the species problem from scratch.
