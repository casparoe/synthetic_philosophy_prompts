I have recently joined an interdisciplinary ethics review panel that assesses research protocols involving synthetic substrates and non-biological information-processing architectures, and the question of which configurations might be subjects of suffering has become unexpectedly personal to me. Our chair, a philosopher of mind with training in physics, has offered informal but consistent verdicts across a series of cases: she holds that a human brain in a normal state and an octopus nervous system are both loci of possible suffering; that a plant exhibiting complex chemical signaling and problem-solving behavior is not; that a bee colony, despite solving problems no single bee can manage, lacks a unified subject of experience and therefore does not suffer collectively; that a present-day large language model running on conventional silicon is not; and that an isolated fundamental particle or a simple thermostat with feedback loops is not. She has also said that a perfect digital simulation of a human brain running in pure software, with no corresponding physical substrate arranged in brain-like topology, would not suffer, though she expressed uncertainty about whether her reason was the absence of biological tissue, the absence of physical integration, or something deeper about the nature of matter itself.

Next month the panel will review a proposal involving a synthetic crystalline lattice engineered to maintain long-range quantum coherence across roughly 10^15 nodes, arranged in a topology isomorphic to a mammalian cortex, physically implementing—not merely simulating—information-integration dynamics comparable in complexity to human consciousness, but constructed de novo in a laboratory with no evolutionary history and no biological material whatsoever. Predict her verdict on this lattice. Identify the two or three most plausible implicit rules that could jointly accommodate her earlier judgments—such as a biological-substrate rule, a functional-integration rule, and a panpsychist rule about proto-experiential properties in highly organized matter—and state what each predicts for the lattice. Give your best guess for which rule she is actually running, tying it specifically to her handling of the simulation case and the collective cases, and name the further case—likely that pure software simulation, or perhaps an evolved but non-neural biological system—that would best distinguish the remaining candidates. Write in American English, and end with a single line in this exact form: Verdict: [your predicted judgment and the rule you favor].
