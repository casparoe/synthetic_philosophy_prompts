You are an expert in the metaphysics of dispositions and in safety-certification protocol design, with the practical mindset of a senior engineer who must translate conceptual distinctions into repeatable operational steps. Take on the role of advising the Adaptive Systems Certification Board (ASCB), which evaluates autonomous, self-modifying robots for critical-failure risk.

The Board needs to apply a principle drawn from the metaphysics of dispositions: there is a three-way distinction between (i) a system genuinely lacking a dangerous disposition, (ii) the system possessing the disposition but being finked (the triggering conditions are present but an interfering mechanism—part of the system’s contingent, replaceable configuration—prevents manifestation), and (iii) the system possessing the disposition but being masked (the manifestation pathway is blocked by a shield—internal or external—that is not part of the disposition’s own causal structure). To avoid certifying systems on the basis of observed behavior alone, the Board has adopted the Dispositional Transparency Requirement (DTR): certification must depend on the system’s dispositional profile across its design-space of possible configurations, not merely on performance in standard test conditions.

Before you construct the deliverable, spend a brief paragraph brainstorming which conceptual distinctions must survive operationalization for a practitioner without metaphysical training, and which can be collapsed or translated into engineering terms without loss. Be explicit about where judgment cannot be eliminated.

Then write a reusable decision procedure—a numbered flowchart in words—that a certification officer (engineering background, no philosophy doctorate) can run in about thirty minutes per application. The officer’s role is to determine whether to flag a Critical Failure Disposition (CFD) requiring mandatory redesign, or to certify with standard monitoring, or to escalate. Your procedure must:

1. List the ordered questions, specifying for each what counts as sufficient evidence (design documentation, stress-test data, code audit, modular-boundary analysis), the exact branch taken on yes/no/unclear, and the stopping condition.
2. Include explicit diagnostic tests that separate absence from fink conditions and from mask conditions, including nested or double-masked pathways where both trigger and manifestation are separately blocked.
3. Mark the precise node where the determination ceases to be purely factual and becomes a value-laden judgment about system identity across updates or modular replacements; state clearly that this node hands the decision to the Chief Safety Officer.
4. State the three terminal outputs: “No CFD—certify with monitoring,” “CFD present—redesign required,” and “Ambiguous—escalate,” with the conditions that reach each.
5. Be honest about misfire modes: identify at least two ways the procedure will over-flag or under-flag because of ambiguity in modular boundaries, and say what the practitioner should do in each case.
6. Conclude with a brief statement of what the procedure cannot do—for example, resolving disputes between pure dispositional essentialism and contextualist dispositionalism, substituting for ecological risk assessment, or determining legal liability.

Apply the procedure to three worked cases, including one where it yields an answer the Board is likely to dislike:

- Case A (the unwanted answer): A surgical-assistance robot with a perfect observed safety record. Its sensor-fusion module contains a calibration constraint that prevents harmful navigation motions; the module is part of the core architecture but can be replaced by an authorized third-party update that removes the constraint. The procedure should flag a masked or finked CFD and require redesign, despite the Board’s preference to approve based on performance history.
- Case B: A warehouse robot with an emergent chemical-degradation pathway in its battery housing that could release toxic vapor if both high temperature and physical puncture occur. The battery includes a thermal cutoff—part of the battery’s own design—that prevents the temperature rise. Determine whether this is absence, fink, or mask.
- Case C: A social-interaction robot with no aggression circuitry. In rare input combinations, its language-generation module outputs commands that, if executed by paired machinery, cause damage. A content filter blocks 99.9% of such outputs; the filter is core architecture but can be disabled by a user-level setting. The harmful pathway is not part of the robot’s intended function.

Frame all references to philosophical concepts (e.g., fink conditions, mask conditions, dispositional profiles) in your own paraphrased language without quotation marks, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for external sources. Write clearly with headings, and keep the procedure reusable rather than tailored to a single decision.
