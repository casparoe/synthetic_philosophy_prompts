Design a classroom simulation for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on political philosophy (approximately 15 students, 90 minutes, no prior assigned texts required beyond general familiarity with modern state formation). The goal is to make participants experience the conceptual pressure of what I will call "retroactive continuity of the collective self": the mechanism by which a modern group lacking any deep institutional unity constructs a seamless ancient identity not primarily through outright fabrication, but by reorganizing recent bureaucratic innovations—census categories, language standardization, boundary cartography—into "restored traditions" and by reclassifying modern fractures as temporary foreign interruptions. Draw explicitly on the dynamics of invented tradition and imagined community, but embed the idea in the rules so that students build the narrative themselves before the facilitator names it.

Structure the simulation as follows. Divide the group into three "regional councils" of a fictional post-imperial archipelago that shares a language but has no shared pre-modern state. Give each council an identical set of recent artifacts: a 1950s land survey, a 1920s school curriculum in the shared language, and a folk ritual recorded in the 1930s. Their task: draft a joint "Declaration of Ancient Federation" that justifies a present-day tax union by proving the archipelago was always a single sovereign people, interrupted only by colonial administration. They must use only the provided artifact cards, assigning each either to "our ancient continuity" or to "colonial imposition," and they must agree on a single origin date prior to 1800.

Include explicit rules about what counts as legitimate evidence (no outside history books, only the artifact cards), a payoff structure that rewards unanimous agreement and penalizes internal disagreement (e.g., collective points lost for every region that refuses to sign), and a "skeptical tribunal" role played by 2-3 students who challenge anachronisms using only the same artifact cards.

Then provide a debrief plan (about 20 minutes) that maps specific moments in the negotiation onto Hobsbawm's analysis of invented tradition and Anderson's modular nationalism—particularly the way nationalist scripts can be imported and assembled regardless of local content. The debrief should also explain what will predictably go wrong in a first run: students importing real-world national myths into the fictional archipelago, the facilitator being tempted to reveal the paradox before the councils have committed to an origin date, and one council dominating the narrative because the payoff structure rewards speed over coherence. Be honest about which feature of retroactive continuity—the emotional attachment to continuity that persists even after participants recognize the fabrication—the simulation rules cannot fully reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator through guided reflection rather than game mechanics. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the prompt should be answerable by reasoning about the described dynamics alone.
