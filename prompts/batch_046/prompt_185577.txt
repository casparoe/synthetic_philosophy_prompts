I'm serving on our university's Committee for Institutional Voice — we review proposals for campus-wide communication tools. There's a funding request for a platform called The Aggregator that lets students and staff post anonymus opinions, then uses an LLM to synthesize them into a single "public position" on any policy question, which admin treats as democratic input. Half the committee thinks it's just a fancy survey with extra steps; the other half thinks it's a concrete example of the phantom-public problem Kierkegaard wrote about, where anonymity and statistical aggregation dissolve individual responsability and replace actual people with an abstraction nobody owns. I was discussing this with a friend who reads a lot of rationalist blogs and she pointed out that Kierkegaard's critique wasn't just anti-newspaper but about how the press manufactures a "public" that is nobody in particular, so everyone can hide behind collective opinion without owning it. That seems relevant here, but I'm also worried I'm overfitting philosophy onto a basic tech procurement issue.

We need to issue a decision memo — approve with conditions, reject, or approve with a disclaimer — with reasoning the applicants could actually respond to. If we reject on philosophical grounds they should understand whether it's about the anonymity, the synthesis step, or the institutional weight given to the output; if we approve with conditions they need to be implementable, not just "think about ethics." The memo should also honestly treat the strongest consideration on the losing side: probably that democratic participation is good even when messy, and that waiting for perfect individual responsibility means never building anything collective.

Can you draft it? Keep it formal enough for the dean, but don't turn it into a literature review — no citations or reading lists needed, and please don't quote anything verbatim unless you can check the text. I'd rather recieve paraphrase than fake quotes. Also, if you think I'm wrong that this connects to Kierkegaard, say so — I don't want to force the framework if it doesn't actually fit.
