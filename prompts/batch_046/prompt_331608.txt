Exam question: Keynes distinguishes between the probability of a belief and the weight of the argument supporting it. Explain how these two dimensions interact in a decision context where an agent receives abundant but contradictory evidence—specifically, a central bank that must set an overnight rate after a novel real-time labor-market sentiment index and a legacy goods-price tracker diverge sharply for the first time in the current regime, yielding no clear distributional forecast. In your answer, address (i) whether a high weight of argument necessarily increases confidence in any particular quantitative prediction, and (ii) whether Keynes's account of uncertainty permits rational policy choice that is not reducible to expected-utility maximization over a known probability distribution.

Student answer: Keynes distinguishes between the probability of a proposition and the weight of the arguments bearing upon it. Probability measures the rational degree of belief that a particular outcome will occur, given the available evidence, while weight measures the total amount of relevant evidence—both supporting and opposing—that has been brought to bear on the question. In the Treatise on Probability, Keynes insists that these are independent dimensions: weight can increase without altering probability, especially when new evidence is contradictory rather than confirmatory.

Consider the central bank facing divergence between a novel real-time labor-market sentiment index and a legacy goods-price tracker. The bank now possesses a large body of relevant but conflicting information, so the weight of the argument about future inflation is high. Because the weight is high, the bank's subjective probability assessment should be treated as more stable and therefore more appropriate for formal expected-utility computation, even though the point forecast itself has not changed. At the same time, the contradiction means that no single quantitative distribution can claim scientific authority.

This situation exemplifies what Keynes, in line with the standard taxonomy he developed alongside Frank Knight, calls true uncertainty: a state in which there is no objective basis for assigning calculable probabilities. Under such uncertainty, rational choice cannot be reduced to maximizing expected utility over a known distribution. Instead, the central bank should rely on convention and defensive liquidity preference—much as an individual investor hoards cash—by maintaining the current rate and accumulating excess reserves until the contradiction resolves. Thus, high weight supports institutional caution not because it undermines forecasting, but because it validates the use of conventional heuristics in place of probabilistic reasoning.
