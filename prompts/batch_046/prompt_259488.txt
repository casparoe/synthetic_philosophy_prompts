At a recent symposium on collective decision-making in science policy, I observed an extended disagreement between two advisory-board members—Dr. Aisha Okonkwo, a philosopher of science, and Dr. Julian Reed, a policy analyst—regarding how a museum board ought to aggregate judgments on a proposed synthetic-biology exhibit. The board had voted separately on three propositions: (A) the exhibit is historically accurate in its depiction of CRISPR development; (B) it satisfies the institution's educational-access criteria for non-specialist audiences; and (C) the museum should approve and fund the installation. A majority endorsed both A and B, yet a clear majority rejected C.

Okonkwo opened by defending premise-based aggregation. Because expertise is distributed—biologists assess accuracy, educators assess accessibility—the premises should be collected separately and the recommendation derived by logic. If both premises command majority support, she argued, coherence requires endorsing C; to reject it while accepting its premises is for the collective body to hold an incoherent belief set, which undermines its claim to rational agency.

Reed replied that this substitutes deduction for democratic will. Five of seven members explicitly rejected the recommendation, so deriving approval from premises they never collectively endorsed treats the board like a syllogism rather than a decision-making body. He added that C is not strictly entailed by A and B alone: unstated practical considerations—budgetary trade-offs, institutional risk, opportunity costs—may lead members to reject funding even while granting accuracy and educational value. Forcing a logical bridge, he said, imposes false precision on holistic practical judgment.

Okonkwo countered that allowing hidden premises to override explicit ones destroys transparency. Once the board frames its task as evaluating accuracy, education, and recommendation separately, coherence becomes a structural constraint, not an optional aesthetic preference. The contradiction exposed by the discursive dilemma is not a legitimate strategic complexity but a failure of collective reasoning; if members genuinely hold the premises, rationality binds them to the conclusion, and any deviation should be articulated openly rather than buried in an unexamined conclusion-vote.

Reed closed by arguing that applying individual cognitive rationality standards to groups is a category mistake. Boards and legislatures routinely maintain complex, non-logical attitudes—endorsing premises while rejecting outcomes for strategic or contextual reasons—without suffering cognitive defect. Moreover, either aggregation method violates something: premise-based aggregation ignores the direct democratic vote on C, while conclusion-based aggregation may endorse premises that a majority actually rejects. There is therefore no neutrally correct rule; selecting coherence over responsiveness merely smuggles in a normative preference for logical tidiness.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the four rounds above, indicate whether Okonkwo or Reed is more right, briefly justifying your rating. Then state who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both parties are missing about collective judgment in structurally connected decision problems like this one.
