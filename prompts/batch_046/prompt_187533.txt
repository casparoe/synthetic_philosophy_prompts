I serve on the Joint Governance Board of an international research consortium with Dutch and Japanese partner institutions, and we must vote by the end of next month on an amendment to our cross-national peer-review charter. The proposed amendment — called the Literal Translation Protocol — would mandate that all evaluative feedback in collaborative project reviews be rendered in maximally direct propositional form, and it explicitly prohibits protective understatement (for instance, describing substantially flawed work as promising or worthy of further development) regardless of the recipient’s cultural background. The Protocol also includes a Transparent Gloss Clause: whenever a reviewer employs indirect or face-saving language, they must file an explicit literal translation in the institutional audit record, though that gloss need not be shown to the recipient.

Dutch members of the board defend the amendment by appealing to a norm of professional discussability: interactional padding obscures errors and undermines collective epistemic accountability, and protective understatement functions as a deceptive practice that treats politeness as more important than truthful evaluation. Japanese members oppose it on the grounds that indirectness is not a private preference but a constitutive feature of respectful professional interaction; forcing direct negative feedback violates relational obligations, and the Transparent Gloss Clause institutionalizes a two-tier asymmetry in which face-management is recorded as a hidden deficiency rather than recognized as a legitimate communicative practice.

Please draft the formal written opinion I will circulate before the vote. The memo should recommend a concrete course of action — adopt the Protocol, reject it, or adopt it with specific conditions — together with a clear implementation time frame if adoption or conditional adoption is recommended. The reasoning should address whether protective understatement in this cross-cultural setting amounts to a wrongful white lie or to a permissible pragmatic calibration of propositional content, engaging seriously with both the directness position and the indirectness position. It should also assess the Transparent Gloss Clause specifically: does it resolve the tension between transparency and politeness, or does it instead encode an institutional separation between what is said to a colleague and what is said about them? Include an honest and substantive account of the strongest consideration on the side you do not ultimately endorse. The document should be actionable for a governance board, should avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations, and should not depend on access to specialized sources outside general philosophical reasoning about communication, deception, and institutional design.
