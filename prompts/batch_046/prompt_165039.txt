Write a deadpan parody of an internal government compliance manual, formatted as numbered bureaucratic sections (1 through 7) with one embedded mock form, issued by the fictional Federal Bureau of Doxastic Integrity. The manual audits whether civil servants form beliefs for the epistemically “right kind of reasons”—that is, it satirizes the philosophical problem that self-interested, moral, or social considerations can motivate belief without justifying it, and that belief formed for such “wrong-kind” reasons is a form of doxastic malpractice.

To avoid a generic treatment, invent and apply a specific administrative taxonomy not found in standard literature: R-Type (reality-tracking/evidential), P-Type (prudential), S-Type (social-solidarity), and C-Type (coercive/conformity). Center the satire on “Mixed-Motive Doxastic Events,” in which evidence and extrinsic incentives happen to align; propose an absurd bureaucratic protocol for testing “causal purity” of belief-formation (for example, a counterfactual separation procedure in which the believer must demonstrate that the belief would survive the removal of all non-R-Type incentives). Include the embedded form “DRD-104: Doxastic Reason Declaration,” requiring itemized reason-categorization, a contamination-risk matrix, and a signed certification of zero P-, S-, or C-Type intrusion.

Keep the voice rigorously deadpan and institutional—never wink—so the humor arises from forcing a fragile philosophical distinction through administrative machinery. Land the genuine conceptual tensions: overdetermination (believing for both right and wrong reasons simultaneously), opaque introspective access to one’s own motives, and the risk that demanding “pure” R-Type reasons might crowd out belief-formation altogether. The piece should be legible to a non-specialist but precise enough that a philosopher recognizes the target is being treated fairly. Maximum 650 words. Do not include citations, a bibliography, references to specific works, or named living philosophers.
