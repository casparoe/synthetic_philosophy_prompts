I'm currently in a seminar on classical Chinese philosophy, and I'm working through the passage in the Zhuangzi's "Perfect Happiness" chapter where Zhuangzi's wife dies. His friend Huizi finds him sitting with legs spread, beating a tub and singing; Huizi accuses him of heartlessness, and Zhuangzi replies that he initially mourned but then realized death is only a transformation like the turning of seasons, so she now rests in the vastness of nature.

Most interpretations treat this as straightforward Daoist acceptance of impermanence. I want to understand something more precise: whether Zhuangzi's singing indicates a complete absence of grief, or rather a grief that has been restructured rather than suppressed—an emotional response that takes the form of affirmation because its object is understood differently. Specifically, does the image of drumming on an ordinary tub (turned suddenly into an instrument of celebration) help us see that he is not denying loss but redirecting the energy of mourning into an embodied acknowledgment of continuous transformation? And if that is the right reading, how exactly does the metaphysical claim that death is "just another change" relate to the phenomenological fact that the person is no longer present in the form she was? I'm not looking for sources or further reading; I just need help clarifying whether the text supports a coherent distinction between emotional suppression and ontologically reframed affect.
