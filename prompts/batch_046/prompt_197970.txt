Design a 60-minute seminar activity for 10 philosophy undergraduates that makes them feel the live force of Buridan's ass—choice under perfect symmetry—rather than treating it as a historical anecdote. The core task: pairs of students must select one of two physically identical sealed envelopes (same weight, texture, temperature), knowing only that each contains a different hidden reward revealed after opening, and then justify their choice to the group without appealing to chance, external rules, or post-hoc rationalization. To inject a non-obvious structural twist, run two rounds: in round one they choose and justify; then reveal the rewards and immediately swap them between the unchanged envelopes for round two, forcing the same pairs to choose again and defend whether their reason tracks the envelope or the reward. Specify: the setting and materials; rules and stakes (a tangible but non-monetary payoff, e.g., selecting the group's shared snack); a step-by-step timeline with roles; the debrief mapping each moment onto the paradox's logical parts (indifference, sufficient reason, invented vs. discovered preference); what will predictably go wrong the first run and how to fix it; and one feature of the original paradox—such as infinite temporal extension or the absence of any mental state—that this simulation cannot reproduce and you must supply verbally. Keep it formal, include no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and organize the response in these sections in order: (1) Setup and Rules, about 120 words; (2) Timeline and Roles, about 150 words; (3) Debrief and Conceptual Mapping, about 180 words; (4) Predictable Failures and Fixes, about 100 words; (5) Limits of the Simulation, about 80 words.
