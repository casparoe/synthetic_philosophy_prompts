In debates about logical uncertainty, logical induction is frequently analogized to a decentralized prediction market for mathematical propositions: rational agents with bounded computational resources trade contracts whose prices converge to probabilities, arbitrage eliminates inconsistencies, and no participant needs logical omniscience to maintain coherent beliefs. Advocates invoke this comparison to suggest that rational confidence about unproven theorems can emerge from competitive, resource-limited interaction rather than from complete deductive closure. Audit this analogy in detail. First, lay out an explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the traders, capital constraints, market liquidity, arbitrage opportunities, price convergence, settlement mechanisms, and the absence of insider knowledge, mapped onto the elements of logical induction such as reasoning agents, computational budgets, consistency conditions, belief updates, proof-search resources, and the epistemic inaccessibility of logical truths outside bounded proof length. Next, isolate the fractures: in particular, examine whether the 'market maker' in logical induction is actually a single algorithm computing a universal sequence rather than an emergent network of independent agents; whether logical truths are atemporal and non-contingent in ways that render 'settlement' conceptually different from financial resolution; and whether the framework's allowance for retroactive belief revision upon discovery of a proof corresponds to anything legitimate in market mechanics, or instead introduces a form of hindsight correction that financial contracts prohibit. Then determine which of these disanalogies carry weight for the intended conclusion—that logical uncertainty can be rationally managed without omniscience through market-like dynamics—and which are merely decorative. Finally, say whether a different analogy, such as Bayesian updating constrained by a computable prior, avoids importing misleading economic intuitions about competition and liquidity, and whether the market framing smuggles in an unjustified assumption that logical beliefs should be priced by adversarial interaction rather than by direct evidential evaluation.
