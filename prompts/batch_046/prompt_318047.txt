I am the senior acquisitions editor at a public-policy quarterly that publishes historical explanations of institutional norm collapse; my board includes a historian, a moral philosopher, and a policy analyst, and we must decide within fifteen minutes per submission whether an author's central causal claim is credible enough to send out for peer review. We receive too many manuscripts arguing that some once-mandatory practice—medical paternalism, workplace smoking culture, professional deference—became unthinkable within a generation, and they almost always claim the change was driven by rational argument. I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the Appiah-Williams framework for moral revolutions, that my board can run without archive access. The framework distinguishes three engines: rational argument (the norm collapses because participants come to see it as unjust or irrational), material interest (enforcement costs, liability structures, or economic incentives shift for powerful groups), and honor (the practice becomes shameful or its refusal becomes a status badge). Your deliverable should be an ordered checklist in plain language: the exact questions to ask about the author's evidence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, the points where the procedure stops with a definite classification, and the points—explicitly flagged—where judgment cannot be eliminated and the board must vote. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and identify the conditions under which the framework misfires, such as when all three engines operate but winners retrospectively narrate only one.

Apply the procedure to three submissions currently on my desk. First, a manuscript arguing that the rapid disappearance of indoor workplace smoking in the 1990s was driven primarily by epidemiological argument convincing legislators, with minimal attention to insurance liability or social stigma. Second, a manuscript claiming that the collapse of medical paternalism—the norm that physicians should withhold terminal diagnoses—was driven by patient-rights litigation altering hospital liability structures, not by ethical argument or shifts in professional honor. Third, a manuscript arguing that the tech-sector norm of "move fast and break things" lost its prestige after 2018 solely because of internal ethical arguments about algorithmic harm; the author explicitly denies that reputational risk to founders or venture-capital preference shifts played any causal role.

Run all three through the procedure. In at least one case—particularly the third—the procedure should yield a classification that the author will reject, but that I, as an editor skeptical of pure rationalist histories, will actually prefer. Then state precisely what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of submissions it will systematically misclassify, what further historical inquiry it demands but cannot replace, and under what conditions the board should reject the framework entirely and rely on narrative judgment alone.
