Two members of my study group, Mara and Jon, got into a sustained argument about the scope of Dutch book arguments after we discussed coherence constraints on credence. I want an adjudication of their exchange, move by move.

Mara opened by saying that the classic Dutch book for probabilism is psychologically unrealistic: it requires that an agent be disposed to accept bets on every proposition at stakes exactly proportional to her credence, but real people have budget constraints, risk aversion, and refusal thresholds, so the argument proves nothing about actual rational belief.

Jon replied that this misses the normative structure of the argument. The Dutch book does not depend on psychological betting dispositions; it demonstrates that if an agent's credences violate the probability axioms, then there exists a combination of bets — defined in terms of her own valuation of outcomes — that she would accept and that collectively guarantee a net loss. The betting framework is just a representation of internal valuation commitments, not a claim about behavior.

Mara conceded the point for synchronic coherence but raised a diachronic objection. She argued that the standard Dutch book for conditionalization assumes that after learning B, the agent will still honor bets placed before learning B on A-given-B at her prior conditional credence. But learning B might alter not only her beliefs but also her values, risk attitudes, or the utility she assigns to stakes. The post-learning agent is not necessarily bound by the commitments of the pre-learning agent, so the diachronic book collapses.

Jon countered that this conflates belief revision with preference revision. If utility is held stable across the learning event, then updating by a rule other than Bayes's rule exposes the agent to a two-stage Dutch book: a bookie can construct a pre-learning bet on A-given-B using her stated conditional credence and a post-learning bet on A using her reported posterior, arranged so that she loses regardless of whether B occurs. Risk attitude is constant in both stages, so it cannot block the loss.

Mara then said that Jon was smuggling in an unstated premise — the stability of utility across belief change — that the Dutch book argument does not itself justify. The synchronic argument needs only current valuations, she noted, whereas the diachronic version requires an independent argument that preferences should not shift when beliefs do. Without that independent justification, she claimed, the diachronic Dutch book overreaches: it shows at most that coherence is required at each time slice, not that beliefs must be updated by a specific rule across slices.

Then say, for each specific point of disagreement, which party is closer to being right and why. Finally, identify what — if anything — both parties are missing: is there a further distinction (for example, between epistemic and pragmatic norms, or between precise and imprecise credences, or between different forms of conditioning) that would help resolve their stalemate, and how does it bear on their arguments?
