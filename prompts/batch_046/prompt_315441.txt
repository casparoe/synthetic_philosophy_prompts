I am not an academic; I have been reading on my own about why societies suddenly abandon practices that once seemed unshakeable, like dueling or the acceptance of slavery. I am preparing a podcast interview with a philosopher who holds a specific, somewhat unpopular view: that these moral revolutions are not driven primarily by moral argument or by economic self-interest, but by the collapse of the honor-system that made the practice a marker of elite status. This is related to Kwame Anthony Appiah's work on honor and moral change, and to a thesis associated with Bernard Williams that treating moral history as rational progress is a mistake. I want a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that do not let the guest simply restate this view. I want the questions to press where the philosophical literature actually presses.

Please include at least one question on each of the following angles, with a follow-up tailored to the reply the guest is most likely to give.

First, if moral argument is not the engine of change, why do we see such intense production of moral justifications—pamphlets, sermons, parliamentary speeches—precisely at the moment a practice collapses? Second, is honor genuinely different from interest or social capital? How could an observer in 1820 Britain have distinguished an abolitionist's appeal to honor from a straightforward economic calculation? Third, given the Williams-style skepticism about rational moral progress, how does this view avoid reducing every revolution to mere fashion or status competition? What is left of the idea that a practice was genuinely wrong rather than simply unfashionable?

Fourth, apply the view to a comparative case that is not usually discussed together: why did dueling disappear faster in Britain than in France despite both having aristocratic honor cultures, and why did footbinding collapse rapidly across rural China once urban elites redefined it as backward? Fifth, introduce and apply a niche conceptual distinction—call it an honor-debt asymmetry, where sustaining a norm eventually costs more in social standing than abandoning it—to a modern analog such as the near-instant disappearance of indoor smoking in Western workplaces after 1995. Does the asymmetry explain the speed, or does it expose a gap in the honor-based account?

Please begin with the interview questions themselves, with no preface, no restatement of this request, and no introductory commentary. The questions should be formal, polished, and accessible to an intelligent non-specialist audience, and they should anticipate the standard defenses so that the follow-ups can push past them.
