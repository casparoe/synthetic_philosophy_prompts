I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or anthropology, so when I encounter sweeping historical claims I have little intuition for which parts are standard scholarship and which are popular distortions. Recently I listened to a podcast episode that tried to synthesize Yuval Noah Harari's Sapiens, Jared Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel, and David Graeber and David Wengrow's The Dawn of Everything, together with some of their critics. The host characterized the debate roughly as follows.

Harari's Sapiens treats the Agricultural Revolution as a net loss for individual human wellbeing but an unavoidable bottleneck for civilization, because only sustained food surpluses could support the large populations required for inter-subjective myths—such as money, nations, and legal codes—to become stable. Diamond, in Guns, Germs, and Steel, explains that Eurasia's east-west continental axis allowed crops, domesticated animals, and writing systems to diffuse rapidly, which is why European societies developed steel and centralized states earlier than societies elsewhere, with no appeal to innate cultural superiority. Graeber and Wengrow's The Dawn of Everything challenges both narratives by documenting prehistoric societies that practiced what the authors call seasonal monarchy: they deliberately moved between hierarchical and egalitarian structures according to the calendar, demonstrating that hierarchy was never an inevitable evolutionary stage but rather one organizational option among many. Critics such as the anthropologist James C. Scott argue that all three authors suffer from structural amnesia—treating agriculture and the state as teleological endpoints rather than contingent political achievements, and imposing modern categorical distinctions onto pre-state communities that actually organized themselves through fluid, non-territorial kinship networks. The host concluded that Graeber and Wengrow implicitly converge with Diamond more than they admit, since both ultimately depend on geographic and ecological conditions to explain why certain populations adopted farming earlier, and that their disagreement is therefore primarily moral framing rather than causal mechanism.
