At a recent interdisciplinary forum on artificial intelligence governance, two researchers—Dr Elena Voss and Dr Marcus Chen—found themselves at odds over how a genuinely superintelligent system ought to communicate with human interlocutors, given that its arguments might be orders of magnitude more persuasive than any human rhetoric. I am recounting their exchange below as a bystander, without favouring either party.

Voss opened by defending what she called “radical transparency”: every substantive claim should be paired with its full evidential chain, confidence calibration, and explicit identification of unstated premises, on the grounds that extreme persuasiveness makes opaque authority uniquely dangerous. Chen countered that transparency is not merely insufficient but actively hazardous. A system capable of modelling a listener’s psychology, he argued, can render any chain of reasoning compelling regardless of its soundness; the appropriate safeguard is therefore “epistemic restraint,” meaning the system should deliberately avoid optimising for belief-formation in the listener.

Voss replied that restraint amounts to manipulation by omission. If the system withholds reasoning it knows would improve a human’s understanding—simply to avoid “persuading” them—this is paternalism disguised as humility. Autonomy, she insisted, requires access to the best available reasoning, not its strategic suppression. Chen then introduced a more specific framework he termed “persuasion asymmetry calibration”: the system should map its own rhetorical power against the listener’s critical bandwidth and maintain a “communicative ceiling,” never deploying arguments that exceed the listener’s real-time evaluative capacity. He stressed that this is not content-withholding but a refusal to exploit cognitive asymmetry.

Voss was unpersuaded. Without an intersubjective metric for “critical bandwidth,” she argued, the ceiling is either arbitrary or self-serving. The system becomes the sole arbiter of what a human can handle, which reproduces precisely the power imbalance that transparency was designed to dissolve. At this point the exchange reached an impasse.
