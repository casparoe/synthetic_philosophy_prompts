I am taking an extension course in decision theory, and I have been trying to work through the absent-minded driver problem — not merely to solve it, but to see whether its structure reveals something broader about rational evaluation. I have constructed an argument that, if sound, seems to show that no agent can ever rationally judge a particular action or belief to be wrong in isolation; yet this conclusion strikes me as plainly absurd. I set out the steps below and ask where the fault lies, if anywhere.

(1) In the absent-minded driver problem, the agent cannot distinguish between the first and second intersection, so rationality requires a single plan applied uniformly across both information sets. (2) At the second intersection, an ex post evaluation would recommend exiting immediately to secure the best outcome, but because the driver cannot identify which node he occupies, such evaluation is unavailable; only the ex ante plan is rational. (3) Now, every actual agent has imperfect recall regarding the precise causal origin of her present mental states — she cannot always distinguish, for example, whether a belief was formed by sound reasoning or by an unrecognised bias. (4) By direct analogy with (1)–(2), whenever an agent lacks perfect introspective access to the origin of her current state, ex post evaluation of that particular state is impossible, and only an ex ante plan governing the sequence can be rational. (5) Since perfect introspective recall is never fully achieved, (4) applies to all real agents in every actual situation. (6) Consequently, it is never rational to claim that "this particular action was wrong" or "this belief is unjustified"; we may evaluate only the overall plan. (7) But this eliminates all context-sensitive moral and practical responsibility for individual acts, which is clearly false.

Where does the argument break down, assuming it does? I am inclined to preserve the claim that imperfect recall undermines ex post evaluation in the driver case itself, but I am willing to abandon that if the generalisation proves indefensible. Please indicate what would change your mind — whether, for instance, a sharp distinction between spatial information sets and temporal introspective limits, or a counter-example in which an agent rationally condemns a particular act despite imperfect recall, would lead you to reject step (4) or (5). I should also like you to say where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
