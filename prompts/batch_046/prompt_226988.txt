I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, though I have recently become preoccupied with a practical puzzle that seems to sit right at the boundary of ontology and data design. In discussions with colleagues, I have noticed that the classification schemes we implement—categories for gender, race, or currency—are treated in code as if they possessed fixed, natural properties, even as I read that philosophers regard many of these as socially constructed. This led me to formulate a somewhat niche concern I call incremental ontological drift: the idea that a socially constructed kind can persist through gradual, distributed shifts in the practices that constitute it, without any explicit collective decision to redefine it. For example, as physical cash is replaced by digital tokens, or as gender categories are incrementally redesigned across software interfaces, the underlying collective acceptance changes bit by bit, yet we continue to refer to money or gender as the same kind. I am trying to understand whether this drift creates a genuine philosophical problem about persistence conditions, or whether it is merely an engineering inconvenience, and more broadly, where the important conceptual work lies in the social construction of kinds.

With that framing in mind, I want a defended agenda rather than a survey or reading list. Among the open questions in this area—whether socially constructed kinds can have mind-independent persistence conditions; how intersectional categories should be handled when they resist single-axis constructionist accounts; whether institutional kinds like money require a symbolic or physical base to maintain stability across practice shifts; what normative implications follow for anti-discrimination frameworks or database ontology; and whether collective intentionality itself imposes constraints on kind identity—how would you rank them for someone with limited time and no access to specialized archives? Please defend the ordering with explicit reference to importance, tractability, neglectedness, and dependency: which questions are upstream of the others, which are likely to be resolved with existing conceptual tools, and which should be deprioritized because answering them would not change much downstream? If you believe I am missing a crucial line of work—perhaps regarding the compatibility of constructionism with scientific realism about some social phenomena, or regarding how incremental drift affects normative entitlement—include it and justify its position. I am especially interested in the trade-offs: which issues are overrated relative to their practical or theoretical payoff, and what kind of evidence or conceptual clarification would cause you to reorder the priorities?
