I'm constructing a taxonomy for a graduate seminar in democratic theory that classifies normative positions on direct democracy and referendum mechanisms—drawing on the institutional contrast between Switzerland's frequent mandatory and optional referendums, the UK's Brexit referendum as an exceptional constitutional event, and California's ballot-proposition system for statutory and constitutional changes. I want a decision-tree taxonomy constructed from the logical space of commitments rather than from a roster of named theorists.

Please begin with a root diagnostic commitment: "There are legitimate circumstances in which a binding collective decision should be made by direct popular vote rather than left to elected representatives."

From there, build successive branch nodes, each a concrete case or normative claim that divides the remaining positions. Every node should be answerable with yes, no, or qualified without requiring knowledge of the view's label. The tree must include branches that separate positions on:

1. Domain scope: whether direct democracy is legitimate only for constitutional or fundamental-rights questions (e.g., a secession referendum or a rights amendment) or also for ordinary statutory policy (e.g., a California-style tax, zoning, or regulatory proposition).
2. Epistemic conditions: whether legitimacy requires some filter—deliberative precondition, competence threshold, supermajority requirement, or expert review—or whether universal adult suffrage with simple majority is sufficient even for technically complex issues.
3. Excluded domains: whether there are categorically inalienable subjects—such as minority rights protections, ecological externalities with intergenerational effects, or fiscal obligations to future generations—that should never be submitted to popular vote regardless of majority preference.
4. Temporal binding: whether a referendum result should bind permanently, be subject to periodic re-ratification, or be treated as advisory and revocable by subsequent representatives.

For each leaf, provide:
- A label (standard name if one exists, such as "Swiss-style direct democracy" or "Schumpeterian representative exclusivism"; otherwise "unnamed/underexplored").
- A one-sentence statement of the position's most awkward commitment—the consequence its defenders must accept that critics find hardest to tolerate.
- A brief diagnosis for any unoccupied branch combination (e.g., universal suffrage with no epistemic filter applied exclusively to constitutional questions with permanent temporal binding), explaining whether the emptiness stems from logical incoherence, normative unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

Please also flag any pair of leaves that your tree fails to distinguish—where different theoretical justifications yield identical answers across all four branch dimensions.

The audience is graduate students familiar with the basic institutional differences among Switzerland, Brexit, and California propositions, but do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Present the taxonomy in a clear hierarchical format with explicit branch labeling. Write the response in American English.
