I need help thinking through a decision that has left me genuinely stuck, and I suspect it touches on a well-known philosophical problem about choice without preference — though I do not want generic life advice; I want the underlying structure named and applied.

The situation: I am a freelance translator who has received two contract offers that are, for all practical purposes, identical. Same pay rate, same word count, same deadline, same client prestige, same remote-work conditions, same cancellation policy. The clients are in different industries — one in medical devices, one in renewable energy — but I have no prior expertise or emotional investment in either sector, and both projects sound equally interesting. The contracts arrived on the same day. The only distinguishable feature is that Client A's proposal came in an email with a blue header logo, while Client B's came in an email with a gray header logo. This is clearly irrelevant to the work.

For three days I have been unable to sign either. I have considered flipping a coin, but something resists that: if I delegate the choice to chance, I worry I will not feel that the resulting year belongs to me in the same way. At the same time, a colleague suggested I should deliberately construct a post-hoc reason — for example, deciding that blue signals reliability — and then commit to it, because human beings need a narrative of agency. When I imagine the coin landing on A, I feel a faint relief; when I imagine it landing on B, I feel a faint disappointment. So my emotional response after an imagined decision seems asymmetrical, even though my evaluation before any decision is perfectly balanced.

My questions are these: Is rationality genuinely silent when options are exactly equal, or is there a hidden rational principle that should break the tie? Does my need to own the outcome after the fact mean my indifference was illusory, or is it just a psychological artifact? And what does it cost me — conceptually, not practically — to adopt each of the three available stances: (i) flipping a coin and accepting the result, (ii) manufacturing a reason and treating it as decisive, or (iii) refusing to choose until some hidden difference reveals itself?
