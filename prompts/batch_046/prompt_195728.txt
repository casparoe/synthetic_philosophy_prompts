Consider the philosophical distinction between internal and external reasons for action—the view that a reason genuinely applies to an agent only if it connects to some element in that agent's subjective motivational set, versus the view that reasons can exist independently of the agent's actual motivations. Rather than analyzing this at the level of individual moral psychology, examine its implications for institutional authority.

Provide a concrete, widely recognized example of an institutional directive—issued by a state, corporation, or international body—whose public-interest justification is rationally sound but is not, and is not expected to become, part of the actual motivational economy of the subjects required to comply. Identify the authority, the directive, the subjects, and the specific gap between the directive's rationale and the subjects' existing motivations. Then articulate the conceptual problem: if the internalist constraint holds for collective agents, legitimate authority seems to require a prior restructuring of the subjects' motivational sets rather than mere compliance; if, however, authority can operate through purely external reasons at institutional scale, specify what this reveals about the scalability of internalism and where the analogy between individual and collective practical reasoning collapses.
