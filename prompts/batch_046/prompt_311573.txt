I serve on a university committee that reviews complaints about proposed public lectures, open-access publications, and course modules that critics claim will cause harm—not physical violence, but what we are tentatively calling “cumulative epistemic injury”: the erosion of shared evidentiary standards in a specific research community through repeated, high-visibility misrepresentation of data. We have no workable framework, and we reject both pure laissez-faire and prior restraint. I want you to turn John Stuart Mill’s harm principle—specifically the distinction between self-regarding and other-regarding actions, and the further distinction between direct harm and remote, diffuse effects—into an operational decision procedure that a non-philosopher committee member can actually run under time pressure: we meet biweekly, each case gets thirty minutes, and we must document a reasoned vote.

Please design the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions: for each, specify what counts as a sufficient factual answer, where the chain stops with a clear “do not restrict,” and where it must hand off to a normative judgment call marked as such. The procedure must handle hard cases: speech that is technically accurate but framed to maximize predictable misunderstanding; speech that targets a vulnerable subgroup without naming individuals; and speech whose harm, if any, is mediated through algorithmic amplification rather than direct audience reaction. Then apply it to three proposals: (1) an invited campus talk arguing that a widely used clinical screening tool is racially biased, where clinicians fear patient panic and abandonment of care; (2) an open-access data visualization that accurately reports crime statistics but uses a color scale critics say will be weaponized in policy debates; and (3) a graduate seminar module that teaches a contested interpretive method known to produce false positives in qualitative coding, where alumni report that graduates apply it recklessly in government contracts. In at least one of these applications, the procedure should yield a recommendation that a reasonable committee member who values free inquiry would dislike, and you should flag exactly where the value judgment entered. Also state explicitly what this procedure cannot do—what kinds of harm it will systematically miss or misclassify, and where Mill’s framework itself breaks down when translated into institutional rules. Write in American English. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence—namely, the core design choice that makes the procedure workable—then present the procedure and the applications, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
