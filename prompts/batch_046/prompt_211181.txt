A friend and I spent much of last evening arguing about whether Foucault’s account of disciplinary power—especially the fusion of knowledge and surveillance—can survive contact with a case where the observer comes to care for the observed. To settle the matter, I want you to read Florian Henckel von Donnersmarck’s film The Lives of Others (2006) through Foucault’s analysis of power, knowledge, and discipline, but with a specific twist that our debate turned on. Rather than treating Stasi officer Gerd Wiesler as a straightforward panoptic agent who internalizes state norms, consider whether his meticulous accumulation of knowledge about playwright Georg Dreyman and actress Christa-Maria Sieland produces something closer to a protective, almost pastoral attachment—an unintended redirection of the disciplinary apparatus toward shielding its subject rather than normalizing him. Does Wiesler’s transformation represent a breakdown of the power/knowledge circuit, or does it reveal a hidden modality in which care and control are indistinguishable?

In your reading, take both the film and Foucault seriously: identify the scenes where the mechanics of surveillance are most explicit—the installation of the listening devices, the transcription of private conversations, the filing of reports—and show how each could be read as an exercise in disciplinary normalization. Then examine the moments where that same knowledge is redirected: Wiesler’s decision to omit damaging information, his emotional response to Dreyman’s piano performance of the Sonata for a Good Man, and the final encounter in the bookstore. Where does the film resist being reduced to an illustration of Foucault? Where does it complicate the claim that power produces docile bodies by showing knowledge generating solidarity?
