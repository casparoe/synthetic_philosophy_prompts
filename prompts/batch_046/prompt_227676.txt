I am delivering the opening remarks this Friday at an interdisciplinary workshop I helped organize—half clinical neuroscientists with direct experience of corpus callosotomy patients, half philosophers of mind and cognitive scientists who have never read a surgical report. The occasion has become personally urgent rather than merely academic: three months ago my older brother underwent a partial corpus callosotomy for intractable epilepsy, and the clinical team’s disagreement over whether he remains one subject or has become functionally divided has collapsed the abstract debate about the unity of consciousness into a question I now live with. I need about five minutes of remarks that do structural work, not decoration: the philosophical framing must determine exactly what I can and cannot say about his agency, his selfhood, and our obligations toward him. I want to reject both the pop-science headline that split brains contain two minds and the equally hollow reassurance that he is unchanged, and instead propose—tentatively, as my own attempt to think clearly—a scalar view: that consciousness is unified not in an all-or-nothing fashion but along dimensions of integrative density, and that after partial callosotomy certain subcortical pathways may preserve a degraded but genuine form of cross-hemispheric access, so that the question is not whether he is one or two but which integrative functions remain intact and which have genuinely dissociated. The remarks should welcome both audiences without condescension, should make clear that this framing is speculative and driven by my situation rather than by settled clinical consensus, and should leave the audience with the sense that the unity of consciousness is a problem they now share rather than a puzzle they have solved. Please write the speech, and afterward—separately—state how confident you are in the philosophical framing you have adopted, and identify the single claim about subcortical preservation of integrative density that you are least sure about.
