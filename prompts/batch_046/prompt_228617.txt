I'm putting together a taxonomy for my philosophy of language seminar on the white horse dialogue and the School of Names — I want something that sorts students by what they actually think, not by which label they memorised. Start with a concrete case: you've got a horse that's white, then it's dyed black; does "this is not a horse" suddenly become true? From there build a tree. First split: is the non-identity extensional (class membership), intensional (sense/reference), ontological (sortal structure), or pragmatic (what the speaker is doing)?

If ontological, split between the usual "form vs form-plus-colour" reading and a more niche branch I'm calling "phase-sortality": "horse" is a substance kind that survives colour-change, but "white horse" is just a temporary phase, so they're different ontological categories and can't be identical. There's also a School-of-Names-flavoured split: does "horse" point to the actuality (shi) of horseness, while "white horse" points to an actuality that includes whiteness, so the names fail to co-refer?

For each leaf give the position name or "unnamed", one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and flag any pair your tree can't really seperate. Also flag empty branches — combinations that seem coherent but nobody occupies — and say briefly if it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Audience is grad students who've read the dialogue once but aren't Sinologists; keep it concrete, don't ask them to name interpretations they already know. About six or seven leaves should do. Oh, and please don't include a bibliography — I want them working it out themselves. I also don't want the tree to recieve that classic mix-up between intension and extension, so double-check the branches don't confuse the two.
