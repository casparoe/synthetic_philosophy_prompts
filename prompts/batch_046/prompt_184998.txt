As a retired physicist who has lately turned to philosophy of collective action, I have been reading about Bostrom's formulation of the unilateralist's curse and find it striking how often it is treated in policy discourse as an obvious argument for centralized gatekeeping, whereas philosophers of science tend to ask whether the curse survives once we move from quantifiable risk to deep model uncertainty and pluralist value aggregation. I want a joint adversarial-collaboration document between two camps. Camp One holds that the curse exposes a structurally unavoidable collective-action failure in domains where a single actor can impose irreversible, widely distributed externalities—such as open-source release of highly capable models or decentralized climate-intervention technologies—so that some form of mandatory coordination or veto mechanism is rationally compelled. Camp Two holds that the curse is largely an artifact of treating heterogeneous, incommensurable value judgments as comparable expected-utility calculations, and that replacing point-estimate risk aggregation with institutional redundancy and Knightian-uncertainty norms dissolves the apparent necessity of centralization without requiring coercion.

Please draft the statement both camps could sign. Use this structure: (1) a numbered list of agreed claims about the logical form of the curse—specifically about the conditions that make unilateral action probable, the role of information asymmetry, and the distinction between risk and Knightian uncertainty in measuring externalities; (2) a table of residual disagreements, each phrased in wording its own camp would accept, focusing especially on whether value incommensurability genuinely blocks aggregation and whether epistemic humility can lower unilateral-action probabilities without coercive institutions; (3) the cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side—including whether historical cases of distributed technological adoption (for example, the transition from centralized recombinant-DNA guidelines to later open-source biotechnology practices) support or undermine the curse's predictions; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree is relevant, together with each camp's explicit prior prediction about what that case shows. If any part of the dispute turns out to be verbal—say, about whether "coordination" necessarily implies coercion—identify precisely which part and why. Formulate everything precisely enough that neither camp can dismiss it as a strawman, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
