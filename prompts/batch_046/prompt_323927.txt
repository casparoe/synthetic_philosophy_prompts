For a consistency audit before a seminar presentation, here is my current package of commitments on the wrong-kind-reasons problem and adjacent issues in practical rationality. I want an evaluation of joint tenability, not a lecture: go through these, say which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, rank the tensions by severity, and for each specify which commitment must be revised and what that costs. Also identify the single revision that would clean up the most.

(1) For belief, evidential considerations are the only right-kind reasons; pragmatic considerations—e.g., that holding the belief will improve mental health—are always wrong-kind and cannot justify belief even pro tanto.

(2) The same structural distinction applies to admiration: only merit-related features are right-kind reasons, while self-interested benefits such as social advancement are wrong-kind.

(3) Wrong-kind reasons must be excluded entirely from the rational audit of any attitude or action; if an attitude or action is supported solely by them, it is not merely unjustified but rationally defective.

(4) Nonetheless, a wrong-kind reason can legitimately initiate an attitude that is later sustained by right-kind reasons, and in such cases the attitude is fully rational and its wrong-kind origin does not taint it.

(5) In Parfit's hitchhiker case, one should pay the driver upon arrival, but the operative reason is purely decision-theoretic—grounded in the correlation between one's choice and the driver's prior prediction—rather than a moral obligation arising from promise or implicit contract.

(6) The distinction is not metaphysically primitive; it derives from the constitutive norms of each practice—belief aims at truth, admiration aims at merit, and moral action aims at rightness.

Does this hang together? Be specific about which commitment conflicts with which, and do not treat every pair as equally problematic.
