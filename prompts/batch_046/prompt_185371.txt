I am preparing a single ninety-minute graduate seminar for philosophy students with no prior training in economics. The goal is not historical survey but conceptual anatomy: I want the group to see why Mises and Hayek treated socialist planning as an epistemic failure rather than merely an incentive failure, and why Lange's iterative price-adjustment mechanism was meant to answer that specific charge—not to understand the debate as an ideological stand-in. Please design the explanatory arc rather than deliver the explanation itself. First, lay out the dependency order of the ideas: which distinction—say, between tacit and articulable knowledge, between calculation in value terms and calculation in kind, or between static equilibrium and entrepreneurial discovery—must be secured before the others become intelligible, and in what sequence should they be introduced? Second, propose one carrying example—explicitly not the canonical Soviet five-year plan—that can sustain both Mises's objection and Lange's reply; specify what structural features it must possess (e.g., dispersed, context-dependent information) and what political or institutional distractions it must lack. Third, identify the misconceptions this audience will likely generate (e.g., reducing Hayek's argument to a claim about worker motivation, or treating Lange's algorithm as having fully dissolved the knowledge problem) and state the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. Fourth, provide one diagnostic question I can ask at the close to determine whether they have internalized the conceptual structure rather than simply memorized positions. Fifth, state clearly what should be left out at this level—whether formal general-equilibrium mathematics, the historical chronology of the debate, or biographical context—and say so openly. Finally, if this request rests on a confused premise—if, for example, the conceptual distinctions I have named are not in fact central to the debate, or if a single session cannot do this work without serious distortion—say that plainly instead of accommodating the frame politely. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the session must be self-standing.
