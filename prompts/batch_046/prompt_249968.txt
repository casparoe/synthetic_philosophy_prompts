I'm taking an advanced seminar on moral responsibility that uses P.F. Strawson's framework of reactive attitudes as its anchor, but our discussions have kept returning to two tensions that standard textbook treatments tend to treat as peripheral. The first is what we have been calling structural opacity: agents routinely act inside institutional, technological, or bureaucratic systems whose normative architecture they do not fully understand, yet reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, indignation—are still directed at them as individuals rather than being redirected entirely toward the system. The second is distributive asymmetry: reactive attitudes attach naturally to particular persons, but the morally decisive feature of many situations is precisely a collective structure or decision-procedure that distributed options in a way no single agent authored, leaving it unclear whether individual accountability survives intact or should be dissolved into some form of collective responsibility.

Against this background, I want you to lay out the desiderata that any adequate theory of moral responsibility—one that takes reactive attitudes as genuinely central rather than as surface symptoms of some deeper metaphysical condition—must satisfy. For each desideratum, specify the case or consideration that motivates it, indicate whether it concerns the conditions for being responsible, being held responsible, or being exempted, and flag any pairs you take to be in serious tension or even jointly unsatisfiable. In particular, address: which judgments about praise, blame, and exemption the theory must preserve and which it can revise; how it must distinguish moral responsibility from mere causal contribution; what it must deliver on the compatibility question with determinism, and whether that result is negotiable; how it should treat reactive attitudes directed at agents under conditions of structural opacity; and what it must say about distributive asymmetry—whether it can preserve individual-targeted attitudes without falsely isolating the agent from the structures that shaped the act.

Then, briefly, score the main contenders—Strawson's own reactive-attitude view, a standard compatibilist account, and a hard incompatibilist position—against your list, with particular attention to whether any of them can meet the structural-opacity and distributive-asymmetry requirements without collapsing into the others. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring.
