I’m putting together a module for my upper-level philosophy of mind course and I keep coming back to Szasz — specifically his idea that psychiatric categories are basically metaphors for problems in living, not literal diseases. (I tried explaining this to my neighbor over coffee and she just asked if I meant people should suffer alone, so clearly I need a better framing.) I want the students to compare that with a view they won’t have seen in intro courses: the predictive-processing account of mental disorder, where conditions like depression are treated not as broken brains but as rigid, high-precision priors that don’t update properly. Both reject the mental-illness-is-like-diabetes line, but for different reasons.

Please compare Szasz’s anti-medicalization thesis with the predictive-processing framework. Where do they overlap in criticizing the biomedical model, and where do they split — especially on whether there is an objective dysfunction that needs correction versus just a social label? Be careful to seperate what’s fairly settled in the literature from what’s genuinely contested; don’t present either Szasz’s social-construction claim or the predictive-processing model as if they’re the consensus view among philosophers of psychiatry. Also flag the misconception students usually have — that Szasz is just an anti-psychiatry crank — and explain where that misreading comes from. No citations needed, just the conceptual map and the contested/settled divide.
