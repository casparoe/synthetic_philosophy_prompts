hey, i'm a phd student in philosophy of history and i'm stuck on this argument my advisor threw at me about contingency. it started with the nursery rhyme about the lost nail and the lost kingdom, but then it turned into something that seems to prove historical explanation is either infinite or impossible. here's the chain:

(1) if a single missing nail can alter the outcome of a battle or a kingdom, then micro-causes carry macro-historical weight.
(2) but if C is part of the explanation for O, then every cause in the chain that produced C is also part of the explanation for O—otherwise O wouldn't have depended on C in the first place, since causal dependancy runs all the way back.
(3) that chain extends backward without limit: the blacksmith's apprenticeship, the geology of the iron vein, eventually quantum fluctuations in the very early universe.
(4) so any full explanation of a macro-historical event requires an infinitely long causal chain.
(5) no actual historian can produce an infinite explanation.
(6) therefore either small-scale contingency is an illusion (and history is deterministic at the macro level) or historical explanation is literally impossible.

i think the error is in (2), conflating causal dependence with explanatory relevance—there must be some truncation principle that lets us stop at the blacksmith without citing his mother. but i can't defend that line without it looking ad hoc. if i keep (1) and reject (5), do i have to accept that there is an "ideal historian" with infinite knowledge? what's the least costly step to drop, and what does dropping it do to our idea of contingency? i'd rather hang on to (1) than swallow (6).
