I am a PhD candidate in philosophy of science preparing to defend a position at an interdisciplinary workshop on rational forecasting and epistemic virtue. My thesis is that conventional appeals to epistemic modesty—particularly those that treat outside-view, reference-class reasoning as the default corrective to inside-view overconfidence—rest on an unstated assumption that reference classes are both stable and identifiable in the case at hand. I argue that in domains of structural novelty, where the causal architecture is genuinely unprecedented, forcing an outside view does not correct overconfidence but substitutes it with a different epistemic failure I call base-rate imperialism: the imposition of an inappropriate reference class that generates spurious precision and masks genuine uncertainty. Consequently, I defend a hybrid account in which epistemic modesty requires inside-view structural modeling not as a fallback for the uninformed but as the primary filter for determining whether any outside view is even applicable; if the model reveals that the case violates the conditions that made the reference class valid, the modest response is to withhold the base rate rather than apply it cautiously. 

The audience will include philosophers of science who specialize in reference-class reasoning and prediction-market design and are committed to the view that base-rate neglect is the central cognitive failure in expert judgment; two decision theorists who regard outside-view calibration as the gold standard of rational belief revision; an epistemologist of intellectual humility who worries that my position licenses arbitrary expert privilege and collapses into relativism; and applied ethicists working on AI governance who are likely to interpret my argument as special pleading for intuitive, non-quantifiable expert judgment in precisely the domains where forecasting is most needed. For each entry, please include a concise reply suitable for the workshop format, and indicate where my reply is softest.
