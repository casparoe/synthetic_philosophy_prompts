I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and next week I am presenting a thesis at an interdisciplinary university colloquium. The thesis is that the self—understood in the spirit of Hofstadter’s strange-loop account, where an "I" is a pattern of self-referential symbol-activation rather than a substance tied to one skull—need not be confined to a single biological brain. I argue that if two brains are linked by a continuous, bidirectional, high-bandwidth neural interface that sustains a unified stream of symbol-activation patterns, they do not merely form two communicating agents but can instantiate one distributed self, which I call inter-cranial strange looping. I am not claiming this is empirically realized today; I am defending its conceptual coherence and its implications for personal identity in distributed cognitive architectures.

The audience will be analytic philosophers of mind and cognitive neuroscientists. Most are committed physicalists with empirical leanings; several specialize in split-brain research, embodied cognition, or the neuroscience of consciousness. They are generally skeptical of treating literary or mathematical metaphors as ontological mechanisms, and they will care whether my claim is operationally definable, empirically distinguishable from two cooperating minds, and not merely a projection of software-engineering intuition onto neuroscience. They are not hostile, but they will press hard on mechanism, falsifiability, and normative identity.

Finally, include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks—the question that would expose the softest point in my position and that I do not have a satisfying answer to yet.
