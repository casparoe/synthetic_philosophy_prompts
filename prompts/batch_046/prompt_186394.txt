I am constructing the metaphysical architecture for a novel in which a protagonist’s consciousness is incrementally migrated from carbon-based neural tissue into a synthetic lattice that operates on quantum-coherent information processing, with no abrupt rupture in memory, agency, or first-person reference. To ground this narrative, I have adopted a functionalist account of mind, precisely because its commitment to multiple realizability appears to license substrate-independent persistence: the same psychological roles continue, only the physical performer changes. Yet I have encountered what seems to be an internal contradiction. If functionalism genuinely permits radically dissimilar physical systems to realize an identical mental state, then during the transitional interval—when the substrate is half biological tissue and half synthetic mesh—there ought to exist not one continuous consciousness but two simultaneously instantiated mental profiles, each equally legitimate according to the theory. The biological portion realizes the mental state in its native electrochemical medium, while the synthetic portion realizes the very same functional profile in its quantum-coherent medium. Thus, functionalism would seem to predict a temporary bifurcation of subjective experience within a single narrative subject, which strikes me as conceptually intolerable. Is this bifurcation genuinely entailed by multiple realizability, or have I conflated a claim about mental types with an erroneous assumption about mental tokens? If the latter, what constraint within functionalism prevents a single mental state from being multiply instantiated across a hybrid substrate?
