I work as a mediator at a local community resolution centre — we run about four sessions a week, mostly neighbour rows and small contract quarrels. Lately we've had cases where one side claims they never agreed to something because it was only implied, not said outright, and the other insists the meaning was obvious. Our centre now makes us fill out a "statement interpretation form" for any utterance that might go into a settlement agreement. I need a reusable decision procedure I can actually run in about ten minutes per statement, with no legal training, that I then sign off on. It should be grounded in Grice's cooperative principle and the distinction between what a speaker says and what they implicate, but translated into steps a non-philosopher can follow. The procedure needs to go in order: first check for literal content, then check for conversational implicature, then check whether that implicature was "ratified" by there reply so it counts as part of the deal, and then check for "pragmatic debt" — an earlier turn that left an implicature hanging that the current statement only makes sense if you pay off. At each step I need to know what counts as a sufficient answer and where the procedure stops with a "no, this doesn't count as binding" versus where it stops because it's a value judgement I have to make myself. Apply it to three cases: (1) "I suppose I could move the fence back a bit" said during a boundary dispute; (2) "That repair bill is certainly... interesting" said when discussing compensation; (3) "I'll see what I can do about the noise" said right after the neighbour promised not to play music after ten. One of these should come out in a way that feels wrong — the procedure says it's not part of the agreement but your gut says it clearly is, or the other way round. Say what the procedure definitly can't do, especially when people are being deliberately vague. Don't ask me for a bibliography or a reading list — I haven't got time.
