I am writing as a student in an upper-level seminar on Wittgenstein's later philosophy, and I am trying to determine whether a practical disagreement I have encountered in a committee meeting is genuinely an instance of the conceptual difficulties we have been studying, or whether I am misapplying them.

Our university is revising its general-education distribution requirements, and there is a dispute over whether a proposed interdisciplinary course—tentatively titled Reasoning, Play, and Design—should satisfy the philosophy requirement. The course teaches logical reasoning through puzzle-solving and strategic game design, includes brief units on Socratic questioning and Cartesian method, but assigns no primary texts by canonical philosophers and draws substantially on empirical cognitive science and design theory.

Members of the committee have advanced competing necessary-and-sufficient conditions: one holds that a philosophy course must involve direct study of writings by traditionally recognized philosophers; another claims the essence is sustained conceptual analysis, regardless of textual source; a third argues that philosophy is defined by critical evaluation of reasoning, which this course certainly includes. Each treats their criterion as capturing what the discipline fundamentally is.

My resistance is that the courses currently approved do not share any single feature present in every case—some emphasize historical texts without formal logic, others formal logic without history, others applied ethics without either. The resemblances appear to be overlapping and criss-crossing rather than unified by one common property. But this observation seems to generate a troubling conclusion for our task. If there is genuinely no hidden essence that all philosophy courses share, then the boundary between philosophy and neighboring fields—cognitive psychology, design studies—appears arbitrary. If it is arbitrary, then either this course should be admitted without further argument, or exclusion is merely conventional and lacks justification.

What I want to assess is whether this reasoning is philosophically sound, or whether I have illicitly moved from a descriptive point about classification to a normative claim about curriculum design. Specifically, can one consistently maintain that a disciplinary concept is sustained by overlapping practices and implicit norms—without any underlying essence—while still defending principled, non-arbitrary boundaries in an institutional context? Furthermore, does the committee's attempt to codify explicit, necessary-and-sufficient criteria for course approval fundamentally misunderstand the kind of practice-governed, implicit understanding that actually sustains disciplinary identity? In our seminar we have discussed how explicit rules never fully determine their own application, and I wonder whether that consideration applies here: is writing formal criteria for what counts as philosophy not merely difficult, but conceptually misaligned with how the category actually functions?

I am not asking for administrative advice on how to win the vote. I want to know whether my framing correctly applies the philosophical issues we have examined—particularly the relationship between explicit codification and implicit normative practice, and whether recognizing the latter undermines the legitimacy of the former.
