I'm a retired ecologst who started reading philosophy late, so forgive me if this is a category mistake — but here's an argument I built and now find suspicious. (1) The handicap principle in biology says a signal is honest only if it's expensive to fake; if it were cheap, every peacocks tail would be long and the signal collapses. (2) When I tell someone what I believe, I'm sending a socail signal about my mental state. (3) So for my belief-assertions to be credible, holding those beliefs should be costly to me. (4) The natural cost is risk: the more dangerous or implausable my belief is, the more I stand to lose if I'm wrong, so the more honest my signal. (5) That means I should be more credible saying something wild and risky — like denying well-established science — than saying something safe and tested, since the risky claim carries a bigger socail handicap. (6) Obviously that's backwards; we usually trust people more on safe, well-supported claims. (7) So one step is wrong, but I can't tell if I'm misapplying biology to minds, or if there's a hidden slide between risk and credibility. I want to keep (1) since it's good biology, and some version of (2) feels right because beliefs do have a socail side. Which premise should I drop, and what does dropping it cost me? Is this just a bad analogy, or is there a real conceptual distinction I'm missing?
