Consider the following argument, which I have been formalizing and now find suspicious because its conclusion is far stronger than I intended.

(1) Accuracy-first epistemology holds that the sole intrinsic epistemic value of a doxastic state is its global accuracy, measurable in principle by a proper scoring rule over the entire belief set.

(2) A bounded agent faces an opportunity-cost structure: any cognitive resources devoted to forming belief B are unavailable for other beliefs, so rational doxastic planning should maximize the expected accuracy contribution per unit of resource—call this the belief’s “accuracy-density.”

(3) Beliefs about remote domains—say, the chemical composition of a star in Andromeda, or a historical event mediated by long testimonial chains—have very low accuracy-density. They consume heavy inferential resources but yield only a single belief, with high accumulated error risk, whereas the same resources could generate hundreds of high-confidence beliefs about local regularities.

(4) Therefore, for any bounded agent there exists a rational cutoff: any belief whose expected contribution to global accuracy divided by its resource cost falls below the best available alternative portfolio should be suspended or never formed.

(5) Apply this cutoff consistently. Nearly every belief we actually hold about history, other minds, or scientific theory depends on inference chains longer and more resource-intensive than those supporting immediate perceptual beliefs. All such beliefs fall below the cutoff.

(6) So a rational bounded agent, operating under accuracy-first norms, should retain only a minimal core of perceptual and logical beliefs.

(7) But this entails a radical localist skepticism about science, history, and other minds—something accuracy-first epistemology was never meant to license.

(8) Hence one of the premises must be wrong.

I am inclined to keep (1) and (2) intact, and I want a committed answer rather than a survey of options: which single step is the weakest link, why did it look plausible on first inspection, and what exactly does rejecting it cost the accuracy-first view? Does giving up that step force us to admit non-accuracy values such as coherence or practical utility into epistemic rationality, or is there a way to preserve accuracy-first commitments by repairing the step without abandoning the framework?
