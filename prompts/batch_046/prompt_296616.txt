Take the My Lai massacre in March 1968 and run it through the Browning-Goldhagen debate about whether perpetrators are ordinary men shaped by situation and peer pressure or ideologically committed willing executioners. Dont just apply the labels — actually map the dynamics: Calley's company, the absence of clear orders, the role of Hugh Thompson's intervention, the fact that it happened in a remote hamlet with no journalists or cameras present. Here's the part I want you to really dig into: inject the idea of "institutional silence" — the idea that violence is licensed not by explicit command but by the absence of prohibitive signals from above. Does that concept belong more to the situational side, or does it collapse the distinction because it requires a cultural expectation that silence means permission? And then flip it: what does My Lai show about the debate's limits? Does the presence of Thompson — one soldier who refused — prove Browning right that situation is decisive (he was in a different situation, on a helicopter) or prove Goldhagen right that character/ideology matters because he had to have a different moral makeup? Be honest about where the theory is silent and dont force it to speak.
