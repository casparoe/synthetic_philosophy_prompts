I am working through a fragment from Blaise Pascal's Pensées—the posthumous seventeenth-century collection—concerning the relation between justice and coercive power. The passage, reconstructed from memory, argues roughly this: justice is perpetually disputable, since reasonable people disagree about its demands, whereas might or force is immediately recognized and not open to dispute. Because justice alone is too weak to prevail, and force alone is tyrannical, societies have merged them: they lend justice the support of coercion, and they lend coercion the appearance of justice. The result is that whatever is enforced comes to be called just, and obedience is given not because a norm is independently justified, but because it is backed by power.

I need help interpreting this at a level that standard expositions usually miss. First, is Pascal making a conceptual claim—that institutional justice necessarily contains a coercive element, so that the concept of justice in social life is definitionally inseparable from force? Or is he making a psychological claim—that people, unable to endure life under naked domination, construct moral narratives to rationalize the power they must obey?

Second, I want to test an analogy that may be anachronistic or strained, but that I cannot resolve on my own. In the Pensées, Pascal also argues that where reason is insufficient to determine whether God exists, the rational agent should make a practical commitment—wagering on belief—under conditions of radical epistemic limitation. Is the social acceptance of enforced justice structurally parallel? That is, because human beings lack access to an absolute, non-contingent standard of justice, do they effectively "wager" on the legitimacy of the existing power structure, treating its contingent arrangements as normatively binding precisely because those arrangements cannot be rationally grounded? Or does this comparison fail because the wager concerns metaphysical belief, whereas the justice-fragment concerns acquiescence to a social fact?

Please help me understand the passage with attention to these distinctions, and indicate precisely where the analogy between the wager and the justice-fragment is illuminating and where it collapses.
