I spent thirty years as an evolutionary biologist studying population dynamics and the long temporal arcs of species, and since retiring I have begun reading philosophy with the seriousness it demands rather than treating it as a hobby. One argument that has unsettled my old scientific habits is Samuel Scheffler's claim—often called the afterlife conjecture—that human extinction is catastrophic not mainly because of the deaths it entails, but because much of what gives our present lives meaning depends on the expectation of a continuing human future. Without that expectation, projects like raising children, pursuing knowledge, or sustaining institutions seem to lose their point. I am hosting a small philosophical salon on this theme and want to close the session with a poem that takes the argument structurally rather than merely decorating it.

Please write a Petrarchan sonnet: in the octave, build the intuitive, almost actuarial view that extinction's horror is the aggregate loss of individual lives; then turn sharply at the volta to Scheffler's stranger claim that the harm reaches backward, undermining the meaning of lives that end long before the extinction event itself; in the sestet, extend this into a somewhat niche interpretation I have been developing—namely that if present meaning is genuinely borrowed from strangers in a future we will never inhabit, then the "self" whose projects matter was never confined to biological lifespan but functions as a distributed diachronic node, so extinction severs not bodies alone but the temporal coherence that made individual lives nodes in a collective narrative. The closing couplet should crystallize, without resolving, the paradox: that we live as authors of a story whose ending we will never read, and that this dependence on an unread future is not an illusion but the very condition of meaning.

Please make the poem philosophically precise enough that someone familiar with Scheffler recognizes the actual structure of his argument rather than a vague gesture at "future generations matter," and poetically coherent enough that a non-philosopher finds it moving on its own terms. Finally, please append a brief note—perhaps a short paragraph—indicating where the poem takes liberties with Scheffler's view or relies on my somewhat idiosyncratic extension about diachronic identity, and please invite pushback plainly: if my framing of the conjecture as implying a distributed self is confused, or if the task as posed asks for something incoherent, say so directly rather than accommodating it politely.
