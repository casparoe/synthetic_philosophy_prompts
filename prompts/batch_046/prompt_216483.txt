Since I recently began coordinating a distributed academic working group, I have been observing how citation and attribution practices stabilize without any formal charter—behavior seems to drift into regularity through imitation and occasional sanction, yet few members could articulate the rule they are following. This has led me to revisit Lewis's account of convention, particularly the idea that a convention requires common knowledge of a regularity and a system of preferences that make conformity rational conditional on others' conformity. Give an example of a specific conceptual tension that arises when we try to apply Lewis's framework to a norm that appears to have evolved through gradual behavioral drift rather than through mutual rational expectation. Construct your example around a concrete collective practice—whether drawn from actual institutional life or from a sharply defined hypothetical—so that it clearly exhibits one Lewisian condition being genuinely unsatisfied, not merely apparently so. Then take a committed position: argue whether the failure shows that the evolved practice is not a Lewisian convention at all, or whether it reveals that Lewis's conditions are too demanding and should be relaxed in light of evolutionary dynamics. I am not asking for a survey of interpretations; I want one definite diagnosis, with the example serving as its proof case.
