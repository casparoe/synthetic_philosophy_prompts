I have recently been appointed a trustee of a small, closed-membership housing cooperative that is rewriting its charter to govern a shared workshop and residential commons. Our proposed system combines a double-blind entry lottery with a disadvantage-weighted maintenance-duty rotation, and I am trying to understand whether John Rawls’s veil of ignorance and the difference principle can illuminate this design. I am fluent in the vocabulary of Elinor Ostrom’s eight design principles for common-pool resource governance—monitoring, graduated sanctions, collective-choice arrangements, and so on—but I have no background in political philosophy. Please restate Rawls’s veil of ignorance and the difference principle entirely within Ostrom’s framework, treating the cooperative as the unit of analysis. For each Rawlsian concept, indicate what translates cleanly into an Ostrom principle or mechanism, what must be distorted to fit (for instance, whether the ‘original position’ can be expressed as a collective-choice rule without importing normative assumptions that Ostrom’s descriptive framework rejects), and what has no expression at all in the governance vocabulary—particularly whether the moral force of the difference principle survives when rendered as an allocation rule for maintenance burdens. Then provide a residue report: does the untranslatable remainder suggest that Rawls is smuggling in a universalist moral claim that cooperative governance cannot accommodate, or does it reveal that Ostrom’s framework lacks the normative resources to justify preferential treatment for the worst-off member? Please write your answer in British English, and avoid citing specific texts or page numbers; I am interested in the conceptual architecture rather than scholarly attribution.
