I am attempting to articulate the principle, if there is one, that governs my intuitive judgements on when a culturally embedded practice is morally condemnable rather than merely different, taking as my point of departure the comparison Montaigne draws in his essay on cannibalism between Tupinambá ritual and European cruelty, though I wish to test the pattern well beyond that text. Here are my judgements.

First, the Tupinambá practice of ritually consuming captured enemies, understood within a cosmology of honour, reciprocity, and the spiritual incorporation of the enemy’s valour, strikes me as not barbaric in the relevant sense; I do not think it provides grounds for condemning that society, even though it involves killing and eating human beings.

Second, the European practice during the Wars of Religion of burning religious dissenters alive, sanctioned by law and theology and performed as public spectacle, strikes me as more genuinely barbaric than the first case, despite occurring within a framework that claims legal and moral superiority.

Third, a modern industrial society that permits factory farming, in which extreme suffering is systematically hidden behind supply chains and rationalised by economic efficiency rather than by any shared cosmological meaning, strikes me as morally comparable to the second case, and perhaps worse in scale and hypocrisy, even though it involves no direct human killing.

Fourth, a small community that performs open, collectively narrated ritual scarification of adolescents to mark social belonging, causing significant pain but embedded in transparent communal meaning, strikes me as not condemnable, closer to the first case than to the second or third.

Fifth, a technologically advanced society that uses germ-line editing to eliminate the capacity for suffering in future generations, thereby also flattening deep emotional and aesthetic experience, and that imposes this without the consent of those affected, strikes me as condemnable, but the wrongness seems different in kind from that in cases two and three: it is a wrong of imposed uniformity and erased agency rather than of cruelty or concealment.

The pair that is hardest for me to reconcile is the fourth and a variant of it: if that same community made scarification mandatory for all citizens in order to maximise economic productivity and social control, with the ritual narrative retained merely as ideological cover, I would condemn it, even though the physical act, the communal setting, and the lack of individual consent are structurally identical. So my judgements depend on something other than consent, visibility, or physical harm alone.

Is there a single principle—perhaps distinguishing practices that express a coherent cosmological framework from those driven by instrumental rationality, concealment, or domination—that can capture all five verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If so, please state it precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, and say what it predicts for the following further case: a society in which elders voluntarily undergo ritual exposure to die, performed openly with full spiritual meaning and communal participation, but where social pressure makes refusal practically impossible and where the practice is defended as necessary for ecological balance.

If no single principle works, which of my verdicts should be overturned, and what simpler principle survives? Please also indicate whether your principle treats moral assessment as relative to the observer’s own cultural framework, or whether it claims cross-cultural validity, and explain how that choice affects the verdict on case three.
