I am designing a diagnostic instrument for a graduate seminar on institutional consent—specifically, on what makes consent valid when one party holds epistemic authority, such as a clinician, a bureaucrat, or an architect of digital platforms. Rather than asking students to self-identify by theory-name, I want a decision tree or taxonomy that sorts philosophical positions according to concrete verdicts on cases involving deception, coercion, and competence. The tree should begin from a shared commitment—that consent can be invalidated by more than an explicit “no”—and branch through questions a respondent can answer without already knowing which academic label they point to. To keep the exercise conceptually sharp but not encyclopedic, treat “epistemic authority asymmetry” as a cross-cutting dimension: at least one node should ask whether the presence of an expert authority changes the threshold for what counts as deceptive omission or coercive structural pressure, so that the instrument distinguishes views that treat institutional consent as continuous with ordinary interpersonal consent from those that treat it as governed by distinct norms. The named leaves must include at minimum: a strict voluntarist or libertarian position; a material-information medical-ethics position; a structural-coercion or feminist critique position; a Kantian rational-autonomy position; and a normative-powers or relational-autonomy position. Build the tree from the logical space of yes/no answers first, then locate each named position within that space; note any logically possible but unoccupied combinations and briefly diagnose why—whether incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Please do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not rely on obscure primary texts; the instrument should be administrable by someone without web access.

Format the response in the following named sections, with approximate lengths. First, “The Instrument” (about 400 words): present the decision tree or flowchart entirely as questions a respondent could answer cold, with each internal node phrased as a concrete case or claim—e.g., whether consent given after a clinician omits a low-probability but severe side-effect is invalid; whether economic necessity that eliminates all reasonable alternatives counts as coercive; whether competence is binary or graded by domain; whether a false belief about a non-material feature of the transaction (such as the brand of equipment used) can invalidate consent; and whether an expert’s silence about a risk that a layperson could not reasonably discover counts differently from an expert’s silence about a risk that a layperson could have investigated independently. Second, “Logical Space and Leaf Placement” (about 250 words): map the combinations of answers onto the five required named positions, indicate which combinations are empty, and diagnose each gap. Third, “Unresolvable Pairs” (about 150 words): flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate, identifying which diagnostic question fails to distinguish them and explaining whether the overlap is conceptual or merely practical. Fourth, “Usage Note” (about 100 words): provide brief instructions for administering the quiz—how many questions, how to score answers, and how to interpret an ambiguous or split result—without referencing external literature.
