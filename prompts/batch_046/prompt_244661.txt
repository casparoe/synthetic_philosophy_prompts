At a recent seminar on philosophy of history, a colleague repeatedly invoked the claim that the arc of history bends towards justice, first to dismiss concerns about temporary authoritarian backsliding and then to argue that long-term moral progress is structurally inevitable regardless of present setbacks. I should like to understand precisely what thesis this slogan might be expressing, since the work it was made to do seemed to shift between incompatible readings. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims hidden in that phrase — distinguishing, in particular, between a descriptive statistical claim about directional trends in political institutions, a normative teleology that treats justice as a historical attractor state, a metaphysical or providential guarantee that moral failures are always eventually corrected, and a purely rhetorical exhortation that sustained collective effort tends to succeed over time? For each reading, please indicate whether it is trivial, substantive yet defensible, or false, and specify which reading actually supports the dismissal of backsliding worries and which supports the stronger claim of structural inevitability. I am especially keen to know whether any non-trivial version can avoid committing to a thick teleological view of history in which local injustices are merely noise around an upward trajectory, or whether all substantive readings ultimately collapse into some form of historical providentialism. Please write your answer in British English, state how confident you are in the different parts of your analysis, and single out the specific point about which you are least certain.
