Here is the pattern I keep noticing, and I do not know whether philosophy has named it precisely. In the classic Death-in-Damascus scenario, a person learns that death awaits them in Damascus, flees to Samarra, and is met there by Death, who explains that the appointment was in Damascus all along—so the flight itself was merely the path to the inevitable endpoint. The argument is usually read as showing that if an outcome is truly inevitable, intermediate efforts to avoid it are futile, because the avoidance maneuver becomes part of the causal chain that delivers you to the result.

But I have observed that this "futility of avoidance" intuition is triggered very selectively. It activates strongly when the inevitable outcome has a specific, singular "address"—Damascus, a particular named event, a fixed coordinate—while the avoidance action changes only the route, not the endpoint. By contrast, nobody draws the same conclusion about outcomes that are equally inevitable but structurally disjunctive. For instance, everyone accepts that dying eventually is inevitable, yet no one treats visiting a doctor as futile on the grounds that "you will die somewhere regardless." Likewise, if the total vote in an election is in some sense predetermined, people do not conclude that campaigning in one district is pointless because the aggregate is fixed.

So the phenomenon is not general logical fatalism, which applies to any necessary future. It is specifically the tendency to treat path-independent, singular inevitable endpoints as nullifying the efficacy of intermediate actions that alter only the path, while leaving intact our ordinary commitment to the efficacy of actions that affect distributed or non-addressed inevitabilities. The Damascus story seems to exploit this selective vulnerability.

My questions: Does this exact configuration—this asymmetry between singular-address inevitability and disjunctive inevitability in generating futility intuitions—have an established name in the literature on fatalism, decision theory, or philosophy of action? If not, can you name and define it precisely, distinguishing it from standard logical fatalism, from the Stoic "idle argument," and from plain confusion about counterfactual dependence? I am particularly interested in whether the "singular address" feature is what does the philosophical work, or whether the illusion of futility can be generated without it. Please map the boundary cases: under what conditions does an inevitable outcome genuinely undermine intermediate agency, and under what conditions is that undermining merely a rhetorical effect of framing the endpoint as uniquely located?
