Here is an argument my study group built after discussing Douglas Hofstadter’s Letter Spirit program, in which letterforms are generated and recognized not by fixed geometric templates but by matching a flexible stylistic “spirit” composed of relational roles. (1) If recognizing an ‘a’ succeeds without any shared geometric feature present in all and only ‘a’s, then recognition of ‘a’ does not depend on necessary and sufficient conditions. (2) The same fluid, context-sensitive pattern-matching mechanism plausibly underlies all human categorization, from identifying chairs to identifying moral obligations. (3) Therefore no human category is defined by necessary and sufficient conditions. (4) But if a category lacks necessary and sufficient conditions, membership is not binary but graded and context-dependent. (5) So for any object X there can be contexts in which X both belongs and does not belong to category C without contradiction, since belonging is not governed by fixed rules. (6) This makes logical consistency itself context-relative: the law of non-contradiction applies only within a fixed categorization scheme, and schemes can shift. (7) Yet if consistency is context-relative, no argument—including this one—can be valid across contexts. (8) So the reasoning self-destructs, even though (3) seemed well supported by Letter Spirit’s actual behavior. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? I would prefer to keep (2), since the unity of cognition is attractive, but I want to know what I am paying if I abandon it instead.
