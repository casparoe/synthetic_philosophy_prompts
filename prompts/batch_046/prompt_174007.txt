After a long and somewhat heated argument with a friend last night about modal metaphysics, I tried to turn our disagreement into a structured essay. We began with the familiar dispute between Lewisian counterpart theory and Kripkean transworld identity, but the conversation kept returning to a problem neither of us could settle: what happens to obligations, rights, and jurisdictional claims when we imagine the same state or person existing under counterfactual circumstances? Rather than rehearse the standard Humphrey-could-have-won examples, I want to examine a narrower domain that I call institutional bindings—contractual obligations, property rights, and legal jurisdiction—that seem to require both the persistence of a specific party and the continuity of the institutional framework that constitutes the binding.

My tentative thesis is that counterpart theory fails here not because it violates essentialist intuitions about individuals, but because it severs what I call normative directionality: if my counterpart in another possible world enters a treaty, the obligation does not point back to me in the actual world in any philosophically robust sense. Transworld identity, by contrast, preserves that directional link, but only by treating the institutional framework as essential to the party, which seems implausible for entities like states or corporations that can survive constitutional revolution or organizational restructuring.

I sketch the argument through three cases. First, a sovereign debt obligation that survives a constitutional revolution in the debtor state: does the obligation bind the same state, or only its institutional counterpart? Second, a scientific collaboration agreement that persists when one partner is replaced by a numerically distinct but functionally equivalent research collective: here counterpart relations seem more appropriate, yet we still speak of the agreement as continuing rather than being replaced by a new one. Third, an abstract framing involving cross-world causal dependencies that affect actual-world victims—used not as science fiction but as a logical isolator—to ask whether a jurisdictional claim depends on the numerical identity of the offender or on the continuity of the legal system that defines the offense.

My provisional conclusion is that neither pure view suffices. I propose a hybrid: the normative subject is preserved through transworld identity, but the content and enforceability conditions of the binding are determined by counterpart relations among institutional roles. Before I develop this fully, I would welcome critical comments on the essay plan. Specifically: (a) Is the distinction between normative directionality and institutional continuity coherent, or am I conflating metaphysical and normative considerations that should be kept separate? (b) Do the three cases actually pull in different directions—does the first favor identity while the second favors counterpart relations—or have I arranged them tendentiously? (c) Is this hybrid structurally stable, or does it collapse into one of the two standard positions once pressed? (d) Is there an established position in legal philosophy or social ontology that already articulates something like this, such that I am reinventing a view I should be engaging with rather than presenting as original? I am not asking for specific citations or a reading list, but I do want to know whether I am working in ignorance of a well-defended alternative.
