Two workshop participants—call them A and B—debated a hypothetical rescue case to test the boundaries of supererogation. The scenario: a trained rescue worker saves ten people from a collapsing structure at substantial personal risk, then declines to attempt an eleventh rescue that would involve only a marginal increase in danger, choosing instead to rest and recover. (1) A argued that this is the paradigmatic domain of the supererogatory: having already performed acts well beyond ordinary duty, the worker is entitled to stop without moral blame, and there is no obligation to continue until physical collapse. (2) B replied that if the additional risk is genuinely marginal, then the case for stopping is not about duty but about an unprincipled psychological cutoff—if saving ten was either required or heroic, and saving eleven is only slightly harder, then either all eleven fall within the same normative category or the boundary between required and heroic is arbitrary. (3) A countered that B ignores the phenomenon of accumulated moral expenditure: heroic acts deplete a kind of normative resource, and once a substantial surplus of good has been produced, the agent may legitimately preserve herself rather than maximally extend her contribution. (4) B objected that “moral expenditure” is at best a metaphor without independent normative force; without a metric for how much surplus counts as “enough,” the position collapses into the claim that agents may stop whenever they feel sufficiently virtuous, which undermines the very idea of a moral requirement that can be exceeded.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate the strength of each of the four moves, indicate where one party is clearly right or clearly mistaken, and say what, if anything, both are missing—particularly regarding whether supererogation requires a principled boundary or can accommodate a subjective threshold of moral fatigue. If this framing rests on a confused premise—for instance, if the notion of “moral expenditure” is incoherent in normative ethics, or if the aggregation of risk in the case mischaracterizes how supererogation works—please say so plainly rather than accommodating the premise. Also note any points where your assessment depends on contested interpretations of supererogation rather than on settled reasoning.
