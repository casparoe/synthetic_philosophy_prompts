me and my friends were arguing about people who do way more than they have to, like volunteering every weekend or donating huge chunks of pay. i was mostly just listening. maya said there's a clear line between what you gotta do and what's just nice extra — she called the extra stuff "supererogation" i think? — and that judging people for not doing extra is messed up because then morality turns into a competition. jake said if there's really a line, then staying exactly on the "must do" side all the time is kind of a failure of character, even if you never actually do anything wrong. he said we should think of virtue like a bank account where good deeds build credit, but people who never deposit anything extra are living in moral overdraft. maya said that's a weird way to talk about it and that "moral credit" isn't a real thing philosophers take seriously, it's just psychology pop science. jake said maybe it's not in textbooks but people clearly act like it matters — like if you see a billionaire giving away money you feel less guilty about not donating yourself. they went back and forth:

maya: the whole point of saying something is "above and beyond" is that you can't be blamed for skipping it. if you start ranking people for not skipping it, you've erased the line.

jake: but then why do we admire heroes? admiration isn't just neutral. if i admire someone for running into a burning building, i'm implicitly saying it's better than not running in. so not running in is worse, even if it's not wrong.

maya: admiring someone doesn't mean everyone else sucks. you can admire a marathon runner without saying people who jog are bad. and people have different capacities — some have more time, money, energy.

jake: capacity explains why someone can't do extra, not why they shouldn't be seen as falling short if they have capacity and still don't. and about the "bank account" thing — i'm not saying philosophers invented it, i'm saying people treat virtue like that, and maybe they should. if you do one huge good thing, maybe that gives you leeway to skip smaller ones without being a bad person.

maya: that's just moral licensing, and studies show it makes people do worse overall. so if anything, recognizing "credit" encourages people to do the bare minimum more often, not less.

jake: those studies are about feeling good after donating, not about a real philosophical structure. but even if licensing is real, doesn't that prove my point? people intuitively think they've earned slack.

they asked me to referee. can you go through each move and say who's right, and also say who's more right overall? please also seperate what's pretty settled about supererogation from what's genuinely contested among people who actually study this, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
