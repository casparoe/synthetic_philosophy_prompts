Samuel Scheffler’s “afterlife conjecture” holds that human extinction is catastrophically bad not only because of the deaths it entails, but because the total disappearance of future generations undermines the point and value of present commitments, relationships, and projects that implicitly presuppose an ongoing human future. Now introduce a revision: replace Scheffler’s condition of biological and cultural continuity with “axiological continuity,” according to which what sustains the value of current projects is not the persistence of Homo sapiens specifically, but the continued realization of the values embedded in those projects—whether by human descendants, artificial successors, or radically transformed beings.

Identify and explain the three most important implications of this substitution for Scheffler’s argument. In particular, address: (1) whether the revised conjecture can still distinguish the special badness of sudden, total extinction from a scenario of gradual replacement in which values are preserved; (2) whether it strengthens or weakens the moral urgency of longtermist efforts to shape the far future, especially if those efforts might accelerate non-human succession; and (3) how the revision interacts with the non-identity problem—specifically, whether obligations to preserve axiological continuity can be grounded in concerns for particular future people, or whether they must be reconceived as impersonal, structural duties. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; reason directly from the conceptual structure of the conjecture and its revision.
