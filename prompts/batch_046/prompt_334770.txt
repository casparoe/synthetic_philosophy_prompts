I've been working through Keynes's distinction between absolute needs—those satisfied regardless of others' state—and relative needs, which depend on feeling superior or equal to a reference group, and I think I've derived a strange consequence. Here is the argument.

(1) Once absolute needs are satisfied in a society, further labor directed at relative needs does not increase genuine welfare; it is essentially positional competition, which Keynes treated as a kind of love-of-money pathology that survives the economic problem only as a moral error.

(2) If labor produces no welfare and consumes time that could be devoted to art, friendship, contemplation, or other goods of the good life, then such labor is not merely useless but actively subtractive: it reduces the total stock of human flourishing.

(3) In modern wealthy economies, most labor beyond a minimal baseline is directed at relative needs—career advancement, status goods, competitive consumption—rather than at absolute necessity.

(4) Therefore, each hour worked beyond that baseline is a net loss to flourishing.

(5) But relative needs are positional, so my extra labor raises the reference point for others, tightening the competitive loop: they must work more to maintain status, which in turn raises the pressure on me. This creates what I will call a leisure externality—my overwork imposes a hidden cost on everyone else by deepening the positional trap.

(6) So the individual decision to work extra hours harms not only oneself but also others, by making the good life structurally harder for them to reach.

(7) From (2) and (6) it follows that anyone working beyond the absolute-need baseline is not merely wasting their own life but actively harming others.

(8) Hence, in a wealthy society, ordinary full-time employment is morally wrong, and we have a collective obligation to minimize labor to the baseline and redistribute the freed time toward non-instrumental goods.

(9) Yet this seems absurd: it condemns teachers, nurses, and artists who work long hours out of dedication or vocation, not out of status competition.

Where does this argument fail? I am inclined to defend (1) and (2), and I can see that (3) might be overstated, but I want to know whether the real damage is done by (5)—the leisure externality—or by some hidden slide between individual and collective obligation. If you had to reject one step, which would it be, and what does rejecting it cost for someone who wants to keep Keynes's critique of positional needs intact?
