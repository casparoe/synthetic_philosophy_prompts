I work as a senior investigator for a national regulatory body overseeing autonomous diagnostic and therapeutic devices, and I have been discussing with colleagues in engineering and compliance how to revise our adverse-event clustering protocol. At present we fall back either on simplistic part-matching—if the broken component has the same SKU, we cluster—or on an unexamined “expert judgment” that varies by investigator. I need a reusable, thirty-minute decision procedure my team can run using only the engineering failure reports we already possess, without consulting outside literature or philosophers. The stakes are concrete: misclustering triggers unnecessary recalls and erodes manufacturer trust; failing to cluster conceals systemic risk. The procedure must produce a recommendation that a senior investigator can sign off without requiring a philosophy consultant, but it must also mark the exact points where judgment cannot be eliminated and where the answer should be handed upward rather than resolved mechanically.

The conceptual problem is that “same underlying mechanism” is not identical to “same broken part” or “same clinical error message.” I want the procedure grounded in the philosophy of mechanistic explanation—though without asking me to cite anyone—while remaining usable by investigators who have never read that literature. To that end, the procedure should distinguish three layers in sequence: structural correspondence (the physical components and their organizational relations in each device), functional equivalence (the causal pathway that actually produces the failure), and contextual stability (whether that pathway persists across relevant variations in environment, user behavior, and production batch). Crucially, I want you to inject a specific conceptual distinction that standard checklists miss: the difference between a mechanism-preserving failure and a mechanism-mimicking failure. The latter reproduces the observable failure signature through an organizational pathway that would not survive a targeted component-substitution test—if you swapped the suspected component from Device A into an otherwise healthy Device B, the anomaly would not migrate in the mimicking case. Build this distinction in as a hard gate, not as an afterthought.

Write the procedure as an ordered checklist: for each step state the precise question, what counts as a sufficient yes or no answer given the contents of a typical engineering report, and where the procedure terminates with a definitive “do not cluster,” “cluster,” or “escalate to judgment.” Explicitly flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure—particularly risk tolerance for near-miss structural similarity, interpretation of substitution-test results when batch variability is high, and the weighting of clinical harm versus mechanistic similarity. Then apply it to three hypothetical case pairs. First, two portable dialysis units suffering seal rupture in an identical polymer gasket due to thermal cycling—this should be straightforward. Second, the case I most need you to show me: two different retinal-scanner models both begin producing false-negative classifications for a specific lesion type. In Unit A the cause is calibration drift in the CCD sensor array; in Unit B a software update inadvertently lowers a classification threshold, yielding statistically identical false-negative rates through a purely algorithmic pathway with no hardware degradation. The clinical outputs are indistinguishable. Run the procedure and show that it gives me the unwelcome answer—that these should not be clustered—while identifying exactly which gate stops them. Third, a borderline case: two infusion pumps from different batches both show flow-rate deviations involving the same stepper-motor model, but only in facilities above 2,500 meters elevation and only when a specific cleaning agent is present, leaving contextual stability genuinely ambiguous. Keep everything practical: no bibliography, no citation of specific works or authors, and no steps that require access to material my investigators do not have.
