In a graduate philosophy-of-science reading circle I attend, a participant recently blocked an attempt to compare pre- and post-relativistic mechanics by invoking the idea that after a scientific revolution scientists live in different worlds, treating the phrase as a conversation-ender that makes cross-paradigm evaluation conceptually impossible. Rather than arguing past each other, I want to return with a structured disambiguation that separates the different claims hiding inside that slogan.

Could you lay out the precise readings as a menu, ordered from weakest to strongest? Specifically, distinguish: (a) the near-trivial claim that scientists use different vocabularies, problem-sets, and standards; (b) Kuhn's taxonomic incommensurability, in which local conceptual networks are restructured but a shared background ontology remains largely intact; (c) a metaphysical world-change thesis closer to Quinean ontological relativity, in which the entities genuinely postulated by competing theories differ in kind; and (d) a phenomenological gestalt-switch reading, in which practitioners' perceptual experience is so altered that no common experiential ground exists. For each reading, indicate whether it is defensible on Kuhn's actual texts, whether it genuinely prohibits rational evaluation of earlier theories or merely complicates it, and which version is most likely being invoked when someone uses the slogan to dismiss comparison. Also clarify whether the phrase smuggles in a separate thesis about the absence of a neutral observation language, and identify the interpretation on which world is merely a metaphor for a disciplinary matrix—saying whether that makes the slogan a platitude or a substantive claim about the limits of scientific rationality.
