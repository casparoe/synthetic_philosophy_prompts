I have been debating with a close friend about the transformation of our hometown's old agricultural cooperative into a thriving precision-manufacturing cluster over the last twenty years. She argues that the primary cause is not economic but cultural: the region inherited a religious ethos that treated disciplined labor, thrift, and reinvestment as moral duties rather than instrumental strategies — a secularized "calling" that made entrepreneurial risk-taking psychologically sustainable in ways pure profit-maximization never could. I suspect this is circular reasoning: the culture praises hard work and frugality precisely because economic success selected for people who displayed those traits, while the real drivers were an accidental tax exemption, the collapse of the old steel plant that released skilled labor, and early venture capital that happened to locate nearby. Our disagreement feels like it touches something larger — whether values can genuinely cause structural economic change, or whether invoking "culture" always risks explaining an outcome by redescribing it in moral language after the fact.

What makes her case harder to dismiss is the specific organizational history she points to: the cooperative's founders explicitly rejected wealth accumulation as a legitimate goal, yet they developed meticulous accounting practices, refused consumer debt on principle, and treated business failure as a kind of moral failing rather than a market signal. These practices were not designed for capitalist expansion, yet they created institutional conditions — transparency, low leverage, high trust — that later made the cluster unusually attractive to outside investment, almost as if there were an unplanned compatibility between an originally non-economic ethos and subsequent commercial growth. I am not looking for a reading list or citations, but I want to understand whether there is a philosophically coherent account that allows culture to operate as a cause without collapsing into circularity or ignoring material conditions, and whether her narrative of a religious calling being gradually translated into entrepreneurial discipline counts as a genuine explanation or merely a just-so story. Please distinguish between points that are broadly settled in the relevant scholarship and those that remain genuinely contested among specialists, and avoid presenting any one disputed interpretation — especially about the direction of causation between values and economic structures — as if it were the established consensus.
