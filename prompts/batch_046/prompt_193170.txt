I hold the view that democratic backsliding is an analytically separable institutional process rather than a partisan label: it consists in directional erosion of horizontal accountability—specifically the weakening of judicial independence, legislative oversight, media pluralism, and electoral integrity—together with executive aggrandizement, and it can be applied consistently across Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States without reducing to disagreement over policy outcomes. What would have to be true for this to be wrong? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they should be: whether the indicators themselves smuggle in liberal-democratic normative commitments that make the concept circular; whether there are clear cases where these institutional metrics improved while liberal outcomes deteriorated, or where they deteriorated without corresponding democratic harm; whether the baseline problem—who decides what counts as the democratic standard—makes cross-national comparison incoherent; and whether applying the framework to the United States forces it to treat ordinary constitutional conflict as erosion, revealing that the concept has no stable boundary. I also want an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—such that any institutional change can be interpreted either as backsliding or as democratic deepening depending on prior commitments—and if so, whether that insulation is a problem for a concept intended to guide empirical comparison rather than merely express political disapproval. Which of these objections would defenders of the institutional-process view themselves concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss?
