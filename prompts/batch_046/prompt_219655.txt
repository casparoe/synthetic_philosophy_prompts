I just finished a long and exhausting argument with a close friend about whether human beings persist through time as enduring three-dimensional wholes or as four-dimensional entities composed of successive temporal parts—the familiar endurantist and perdurantist pictures. We kept circling the same abstract examples, so I want to force the issue into a concrete institutional decision. I serve on the National Cognitive-Liberty Review Panel, and we must issue a formal recommendation by next month on Case 734: a patient with progressive neurodegeneration underwent a fifteen-year cortical prosthesis cascade, in which every biological neuron was gradually replaced by a functionally identical synthetic analogue. The patient retains full psychological continuity—memories, personality, self-reference—but shares no original biological material with the pre-operative individual. The family petitions that the post-cascade individual is legally continuous with the original patient, retaining all property rights, medical decision-making authority over the original body-plan, and liability for pre-cascade contracts. The opposing brief argues that because material substrate has been entirely replaced, the original patient ceased to exist and was succeeded by a numerically distinct entity, which should inherit nothing and possess no prior consent rights.

Please draft the panel's decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—recommending a concrete legal and ethical course of action with a clear time frame for implementation. Your reasoning should apply the persistence debate directly: address whether psychological continuity alone suffices for identity under either an endurantist or perdurantist framework, and explain why the panel's recommendation holds even if the metaphysics remain unresolved. Include an honest, substantive treatment of the strongest argument for the opposing position—specifically, the intuition that complete material discontinuity breaks identity regardless of psychological stream—and show how your recommendation accommodates or overrides that intuition without dismissing it. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate for circulation to panel members and legal counsel.
