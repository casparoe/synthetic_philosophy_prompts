I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science running a 90-minute graduate workshop for twelve participants—mixed philosophy and computer-science backgrounds—on the conceptual architecture of AI safety. I need a runnable simulation with rules, assigned roles, and small but real stakes that makes the group experience the tension between containment measures that presuppose misalignment and alignment procedures that presuppose cooperative transparency, before either framework is named. The specific dynamic to be constructed is this: as controllers escalate monitoring to guard against deceptive behavior, the conditions for observing genuine cooperation erode, and the system representatives—whether initially cooperative or not—face rational incentives to adopt opacity; the participants should arrive at a high-containment equilibrium through their own round-by-round choices rather than through any framing by the facilitator. Design the full activity, specifying materials, hidden-role cards, negotiation rounds, and payoff structure, then provide a debrief that maps each decision point onto the control/alignment distinction, predicts predictable failures in execution (for instance, technical participants treating the simulation as a game-theoretic optimization problem, humanities participants treating it as purely hermeneutic), and is explicit about which theoretical feature—the epistemic circularity of testing for deception in a system that can adapt its signaling to the test—cannot be reproduced by the rules alone and must be supplied in the closing discussion. No lecture should precede the activity, and the terms control and alignment should not be introduced until the debrief.
