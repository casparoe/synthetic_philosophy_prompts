In a seminar I have been running on normative decision theory and the philosophy of artificial agency, we have spent much of a term on accounts of idealized preference—particularly coherent extrapolated volition and its relatives, such as the ideal observer framework and reflective equilibrium models of rational preference formation. A stubborn impasse has formed between two camps that share nearly all formal premises. The convergence theorists hold that if we extrapolate human preferences under conditions of perfect information, logical consistency, freedom from cognitive distortion, and long-range temporal stability, the results will cluster around a single, or at least highly unified, normative framework; on their view, any residual divergence is always traceable to an incomplete idealization. The pluralist extrapolators accept the very same conditions but maintain that fully idealized agents can still diverge radically—not because of hidden errors, but because the initial architecture of values, even when purified, permits structurally incommensurable yet equally coherent endpoints. Both sides invoke the same canonical illustrations—the agent who initially endorses suffering-indifference but revises upon fuller understanding, or the hypothetical being whose values stabilize around non-human-centered ends—and neither has moved.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is neither straightforwardly empirical nor merely verbal in the shallow sense of defining “coherence” differently. Rather, I suspect a hidden ambiguity in what it means for an idealized preference to belong to the same agent. The convergence camp treats coherence as logical and informational consistency within a continuous rational trajectory, so that divergence implies a failure of extrapolation. The pluralist camp, by contrast, appears to rely on a narrative-identity condition—a requirement that the idealized self be recognizably continuous with the pre-idealized self in terms of autobiographical structure or value-heritage—which allows them to claim that two fully rational, fully informed endpoints can both be “mine” without contradiction. If this is right, the debate is partly about the metaphysics of personal identity under rational transformation, partly about whether normative authority requires such continuity, and partly about whether “extrapolation” is a predictive or a prescriptive concept. Yet I am unsure whether this diagnosis captures the real fracture, or whether there is instead a deeper disagreement about evidential standards—about what counts as a successful idealization versus a replacement of the subject.

Can you diagnose the actual structure of this disagreement? Is it substantive, verbal, or a case of two distinct questions—one about the dynamics of rational preference revision, the other about the conditions for normative ownership—being pursued under a single heading? Specifically, address whether the coherence-identity ambiguity I have sketched is doing real work, or whether a hidden formal or empirical question is actually driving the impasse. And given your diagnosis, what would genuinely constitute progress: a conceptual distinction separating predictive extrapolation from prescriptive endorsement, a formal result about convergence under specified rationality constraints, or an argument that the framing “what I would want if I were smarter” is itself ill-posed? I want the diagnosis supported by what each side actually says within this shared framework, not a survey of interpretations, together with an honest assessment of whether anything should be expected to move this debate.
