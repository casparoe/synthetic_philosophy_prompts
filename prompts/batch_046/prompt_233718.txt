In philosophy of biology, the standoff between etiological and causal-role theories of biological function—whether a trait’s function is fixed by its evolutionary selection history or by its present contribution to a system’s capacity—has persisted since the 1970s without meaningful convergence. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not empirical but structural: the two camps are answering different explanatory questions—one about historical origin, the other about present organization—under the single word “function,” and they fail to notice because both invoke the same canonical examples (hearts pumping, kidneys filtering). Yet I am unsure whether this diagnosis holds, or whether a hidden normative dispute about what biological explanation ought to prioritize is doing the real work, or whether the question is simply ill-posed now that synthetic biology can design functional parts de novo with no evolutionary history at all. I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually claims, not a survey: is this a verbal disagreement about “function,” a substantive clash over explanatory standards, or two fields answering different questions under one heading? Specifically, consider whether engineered biological parts—designed from scratch and lacking any selection past—reveal a genuine conflict that only one theory can resolve, or whether they show we need two distinct concepts rather than one correct account. What, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a formal distinction among explanation-types, a conceptual separation of “selected function” from “systemic role,” or an argument that the debate should be dissolved?
