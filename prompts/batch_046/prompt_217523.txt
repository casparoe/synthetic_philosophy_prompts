I am working through a passage in Al-Ghazālī's Tahāfut al-falāsifa, in the discussion where he examines the philosophers' claim that fire necessarily burns cotton. In my paraphrase of the argument, he notes that human experience only reveals the constant conjunction of fire with burning, not an underlying necessary connection; he then asks why God could not directly produce the burning in cotton without the presence of fire, or prevent it despite the presence of fire, since we have observed no agent other than the divine will acting in particular instances. From this he concludes that philosophers have not demonstrated that created substances possess causal efficacy, but only appealed to observed regularity.

My difficulty is in determining whether this reasoning should be read as an epistemic critique—asserting that human reason cannot access hidden causal powers—or as an ontological denial that any created thing is an agent at all, with God being the direct cause of every particular event. More specifically, if one accepts Al-Ghazālī's view that God produces events according to a habitual sequence that resembles natural law, is there conceptual room for an intermediate pattern or custom (ʿāda) that operates with stable regularity by divine authorization, without being a true causal power? Or does the very idea of such an intermediate regularity collapse back into direct occasionalism, in which God creates each event ex nihilo with no structural connection between successive moments? I would value help in interpreting the passage through this distinction, particularly in identifying whether Al-Ghazālī's rejection of necessary connection can be reconciled with a view that allows for consistent, divinely instituted patterns that are not merely coincidental but still fall short of genuine secondary causation.
