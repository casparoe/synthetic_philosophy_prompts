I'm a journalist covering philosophy of science and conceptual design, and I'm assembling a dataset of pedagogical prompts that sit in the weeds of specific debates—far enough from standard textbook treatments that they resist rote reproduction, yet grounded enough that a well-read model can construct a rigorous answer without searching the web. For this entry, I'm looking for an embodied classroom design rather than an expository essay.

Please design a ninety-minute seminar activity for about ten graduate students focused on causation by omission, but framed through a niche conceptual lens that forces participants to feel the problem rather than merely discuss it. Rather than the familiar switch-flipping or rescue-failure scenarios, stage a collective "negative intervention" game: each participant holds an independent veto token that can halt a gradually destabilizing process, and the rules reward restraint—participants accumulate points for maintaining equilibrium—while the system is engineered so that universal inaction produces a predictable cascading failure. The twist should be that the failure state is causally traceable to the pattern of omissions themselves, not to any single actor's absence; participants should discover, only after the collapse, that their shared predictive knowledge of one another's restraint functioned as a coordinating mechanism that made the omission collective and structurally causal. Include explicit roles, a simple paper-token payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline, and rules that prevent any one person from being singled out as the sole cause. Then provide a debrief that maps the lived experience—particularly the moment when participants realize their inaction was not passive but actively sustaining—onto the metaphysical question of whether omissions can be genuine causes, and onto the normative problem of selecting which omissions count as culpable. Be explicit about which philosophical feature—say, the counterfactual dependence analysis of omission versus the normative selection of relevant omissions—this physical staging necessarily fails to reproduce and must be supplied in the facilitator's closing remarks. Do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or reading lists; the design should stand entirely on its internal conceptual architecture.
