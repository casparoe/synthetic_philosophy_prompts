I'm assembling a set of fiction prompts for an interdisciplinary philosophy seminar, and I want one that moves past standard AI-upload narratives to engage Buddhist metaphysics directly. Write a short story centered on an invented technology—an "aggregate-weaver"—used in a remote Himalayan archive to reconstruct a deceased teacher's mental continuum from separated traces corresponding to form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness. The protagonist, a junior archivist, must decide whether to activate the weaver for a revered master dead for decades. The reconstructed stream claims it is not the "same person" but a continuation of causal patterns, yet it lacks the karmic weight—no guilt, no fear of rebirth—that seemed to bind the original teacher's ethical life. Include concrete sensory details of the weaving: the tactile sorting of emotional residues, the mapping of volitional traces onto thread-like visualizations, the silence of the archive hall. Stage a confrontation among the archivist, the reconstructed entity, and a skeptical elder who insists that recognizing anatta means refusing to grant the stream any privileged moral status. The narrative should ask whether the absence of a permanent self dissolves ethical obligation or transforms it into something stranger—perhaps care without possession. End ambiguously: the archivist makes a choice about the weaver's activation, but the story leaves unresolved whether that choice was an act of compassion, an error of attachment, or both. Avoid supernatural resolution; let the tension reside in the lived experience of treating a being as if it matters when there may be no "one" there to matter.
