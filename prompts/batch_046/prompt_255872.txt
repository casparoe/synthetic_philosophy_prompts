After stepping away from a career I had structured my entire adult life around—without the institutional role, the daily rituals, or the narrative of progress that once anchored me—the question of whether life can be meaningful has become personally urgent rather than merely academic. I have come to hold a view I will call sustained liminal commitment: meaning is not a global property of a life as a whole, but arises only in discrete episodes in which one is fully engaged in a project while simultaneously maintaining explicit awareness that the project lacks any ultimate justification. The absurd—understood not as a logical contradiction but as the persistent tension between our demand for significance and the silence of the universe—is not an obstacle to meaning but its necessary background condition; without that tension, engagement collapses into either unreflective routine or self-deceptive dogma. This view borrows from the absurdist tradition the idea that we must live without appeal, but it rejects the common inference that we should therefore seek subjective fulfillment or objective value outside ourselves. Specifically, it denies Susan Wolf’s claim that meaning requires both subjective attraction and objective value, and it denies Thaddeus Metz’s fundamentality theory, which holds that meaning depends on being causally connected to goods of fundamental importance beyond oneself—because both attempts resolve the absurd tension rather than inhabit it. Meaning, on this account, is local, fragile, and sustained precisely through what I call double consciousness: acting as if one’s commitments matter deeply, while never allowing oneself to forget that they do not matter ultimately.
