I'm a journalist who writes on science and ideas. Next Thursday I am delivering the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop titled Lives in Parallel: Biography, Hagiography, and Moral Learning from Plutarch to the Present. The audience is split between classicists and philosophers of education who know Plutarch's Parallel Lives in detail, and biographers of scientists and activists who do not. I want the remarks—roughly five minutes—to treat Plutarch's comparative pairing of lives not as ancient hagiography but as a practice of moral particularism: by setting virtue and vice in parallel across different cultures and eras, the reader is compelled to judge rather than to imitate blindly. This framing should structure what I can and cannot say: I must not claim that biography automatically improves character, nor should I present our workshop subjects as secular saints; instead, I should argue that modern single-subject biography has lost Plutarch's comparative counterweight and therefore tends toward either idolization or debunking cynicism, both of which shut down judgment. To make the point concrete, please weave in Plutarch's concern—expressed in his essay on how young people should study poetry—that exposure to vicious conduct without a framing comparison risks mimetic corruption, and ask whether a biography of one life can guard against that risk without the parallel life Plutarch always provides. The speech must land for the scholars without losing the non-philosophers. At the end, please state how confident you are in attributing this anti-hagiographic, particularist reading to Plutarch, and identify the specific claim you are least sure of—whether mimetic corruption is a genuine risk for adult readers, or whether the comparative method produces moral judgment rather than mere juxtaposition.
