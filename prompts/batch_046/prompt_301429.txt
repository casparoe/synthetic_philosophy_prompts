Assess the work: assign a letter grade and justify it with point-by-point comments on reasoning, not a general impression. In particular, say what would change your verdict—which specific correction or distinction would raise or lower the grade, and identify the step in your assessment most vulnerable to error. Use these sections in order with approximate lengths: (1) Brief summary of strengths and weaknesses (about 100 words); (2) Point-by-point assessment of specific claims (about 250 words); (3) Grade and justification (about 100 words, stating the letter grade); (4) What would change my mind (about 80 words).

Question: A logistics algorithm reroutes emergency supplies away from a zone protected by contractual service-level agreements to a high-casualty disaster zone, saving lives but breaching contracts. Critics claim the system acts for "the wrong kind of reasons": it treats contractual obligations as fungible costs and lives saved as optimization targets rather than as authoritative demands. Explain the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem as it applies here, distinguishing it clearly from (i) acting for bad reasons and (ii) acting with no reasons at all. Then evaluate whether adding a "deontic layer"—a hard-coded rule forbidding contract breach except under explicit human override—solves the problem or merely relocates it through normative outsourcing.

Student answer:
The wrong kind of reasons problem arises when an agent performs the right action but is moved by considerations that fail to justify it in the proper normative way—such as giving to charity for tax benefits rather than for the good of others. It differs from acting for bad reasons (e.g., harming others out of malice) and from arational behavior that lacks any reason-giving structure altogether.

Applied to the logistics algorithm, the system reroutes supplies because its utility function assigns a higher weight to lives saved than to contract fulfillment. This is not a bad reason—no one programmed it maliciously—nor is it reasonless, since the rerouting is fully determined by the optimization objective. The problem is that the algorithm treats contractual obligations as commensurable costs and lives as quantitative inputs. This mirrors the Kantian concern that rational beings are treated as means when their value is captured by a metric. Consequently, the algorithm lacks the reason-responsive agency described in hierarchical models of the will, where an agent must endorse its motives at a higher-order level.

Installing a deontic layer that prohibits contract breach without human authorization appears to solve the issue by introducing an authoritative side-constraint. Once the constraint is hard-coded, the system no longer calculates breach as permissible; compliance is enforced structurally. This amounts to normative outsourcing: the right kind of reason—respect for institutional obligation—is embedded in the architecture rather than computed. Therefore, the deontic layer does not merely relocate the wrong-kind problem; it resolves it by removing the misaligned reason-structure entirely. The only residual difficulty is practical: ensuring the override mechanism is not itself captured by the optimization logic.
