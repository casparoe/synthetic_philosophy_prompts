I'm a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, but I've been trying to organize my own views on determinism, chaos, and predictability for a discussion with a technically minded colleague. Before I defend the package, I want a consistency audit. Here are my commitments:

1. I accept macroscopic physical determinism: if one knew the exact state and the governing laws, future macroscopic states would be fixed, with no metaphysical openness.
2. I think classical chaos—positive Lyapunov exponents, exponential divergence of trajectories—is purely an epistemic failure. The dynamics themselves are fully deterministic; long-term forecasting fails only because we cannot measure initial conditions with infinite precision.
3. I also hold that quantum mechanics involves genuine ontological indeterminacy, not hidden variables or mere epistemic uncertainty.
4. I believe that in open macroscopic chaotic systems, such as weather or turbulence, quantum fluctuations are amplified rather than fully decohered away. Consequently, practical unpredictability in these domains is partly driven by ontological randomness, not solely by deterministic sensitivity to initial conditions.
5. I take "predictability in principle"—not "computable with available resources"—to be the correct criterion for determinism. I further think some deterministic systems are computationally irreducible: even with perfect data, there is no algorithmic shortcut to their future states, and one must run the system step by step.
6. I am a compatibilist about free will: determinism does not undermine moral responsibility, because responsibility requires the absence of coercion, not metaphysical openness.
7. In my engineering work, I run deterministic chaotic simulations that diverge across runs due only to floating-point rounding differences. I treat this divergence as deterministic in origin and not as evidence of any underlying indeterminism.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to conflict? I particularly suspect friction between (2) and (4), and possibly between (1) and (3) once (4) is included. At the same time, I think some combinations—perhaps (6) with (1), or (7) with (2)—may dissolve on closer inspection rather than requiring revision. Rank the real tensions by severity. For each genuine conflict, specify the cheapest revision that restores consistency and what it costs me conceptually. Do not treat every combination as equally problematic; discriminate sharply between real contradictions and apparent ones.
