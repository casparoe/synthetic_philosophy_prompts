I'm treating Avicenna's flying-man argument not as historical exegesis but as a live constraint on theories of minimal selfhood. The argument establishes self-awareness without sensory mediation, but this leaves three open conceptual branches: whether the awareness implies a substantial soul distinct from body; whether it is an irreducible indexical grasp (de se knowledge that needs no bodily anchor); or whether it is a formal self-referential structure that could in principle be realized in non-organic systems. Give me a defended prioritization of these branches for someone whose goal is a coherent account of first-person authority and phenomenal ownership, not a commentary on Avicenna.

For each branch, state the conceptual work it must do—such as accommodating embodied-cognition findings without collapsing into physicalism, explaining error about one's own mental states, or preserving the normative force of first-person authority—identify which other branches depend on it being settled first, and specify what result would cause you to reorder them. I want an agenda, not a survey: which upstream question, if resolved, does the most downstream work, and what are the tractability trade-offs for purely conceptual philosophy versus empirically informed philosophy of mind?
