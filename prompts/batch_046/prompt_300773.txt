Please consider the following series of verdicts held by my colleague, which she derives from her interpretation of Camus's reflections on capital punishment and rebellion. She maintains that the state execution of a convicted murderer is impermissible, because it destroys the prisoner's capacity for future revolt or moral transformation; that the execution of a political dissident is impermissible, because it reduces the state to an absurd, mechanical instrument; that the execution of a soldier for desertion is impermissible, because it treats a human being solely as a means of disciplinary control; that the revolutionary execution of a tyrant is permissible, because it expresses an act of collective rebellion rather than state authority; that the execution of an innocent person is impermissible, though primarily because it reveals the state's fallibility rather than because execution is inherently wrong; and that life imprisonment without possibility of parole is also impermissible, albeit less severely, since the prisoner retains the possibility of living and of future rebellion. Now consider a case she has not yet judged: a state-sanctioned execution of a convicted war criminal who has shown sustained remorse and become an international symbol of reconciliation, performed not out of retribution but in fulfilment of a binding international treaty obligation. Predict her verdict. Then identify at least two competing principles that could consistently accommodate all six prior verdicts—one grounded in the preservation of the possibility of future rebellion, and one grounded in whether the killing is administered by state power or by an act of rebellion—state which principle she is more likely applying, and specify the further case (for example, a voluntary execution requested by a terminally ill prisoner, or the execution of a non-violent offender for an act now regarded as morally neutral) that would best discriminate between these two interpretations. Please answer in British English.
