I am leading a single ninety-minute seminar for philosophy graduate students who have completed introductory logic but have taken no formal decision theory, and I need a teaching plan rather than an explanation delivered to me. The session should treat superrationality through the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery, with particular attention to the asymmetry between the Platonia dilemma's symmetric abstention structure and the Luring Lottery's one-sided commitment mechanism, and specifically to what that asymmetry reveals about whether rational correlation requires a shared reasoning procedure that is itself exploitable rather than merely predictive. Lay out the exact order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced, noting which idea must be firmly in place before the next can be intelligible—for example, whether the students need a preliminary distinction between evidential and causal reasoning before encountering either puzzle, or whether that distinction should be drawn out organically from the puzzles themselves. Identify the one carrying example that should thread through the entire discussion, describing precisely what it must include, such as the payoff structure, the information available to each agent, and the strict absence of communication or repetition, and also what it must lack, such as any appearance of an external enforcement mechanism or a repeated interaction, so that the scenario does not collapse into a familiar prisoner's dilemma or a standard coordination game. Anticipate the misconceptions these students are likely to bring or form, including the assumption that rational agents necessarily defect in one-shot interactions, the conflation of evidential correlation with causal influence, and the belief that if two agents reason identically they are automatically locked in a zero-sum conflict; specify the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be preempted rather than corrected after it has taken root. Provide one diagnostic question I can ask near the end that would reveal whether they have grasped the underlying principle of superrationality rather than merely memorized the outcomes of the two puzzles. State openly what should be left out at this level and acknowledged as such—perhaps the formal calculus of evidential decision theory, the full taxonomy of Newcomb-like problems, or the metaphysical debate over whether superrationality presupposes a particular ontology of possible worlds—rather than being silently simplified. Present all of this in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, written in American English, as one might compose a detailed letter of instructions.
