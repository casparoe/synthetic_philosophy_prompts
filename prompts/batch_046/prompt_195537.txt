I am assessing competing accounts of how concrete particulars persist through change, but I want the evaluative standard fixed before I examine specific theories in detail. Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of diachronic persistence must deliver — the pre-theoretic judgments about survival and identity over time it must accommodate; the metaphysical puzzles it must dissolve or explain (such as the compatibility of intrinsic change with numerical identity, the ontological status of an object after complete part-replacement, and the conditions under which one object can become two); and the formal distinctions it must respect (for instance, between being wholly present at each time and being spread across times as a four-dimensional entity) — with the concrete case or scenario motivating each requirement. Pay particular attention to two pressure points that standard examples often obscure: first, gradual functional or biological replacement in which no individual part is essential but overall architecture is preserved through redundant, interchangeable subsystems; second, branching scenarios in which one post-fission trajectory retains direct spatio-temporal continuity with the original while another does not, yet both seem to inherit its properties and psychological profile. Flag any pair of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable, especially where accommodating both gradual replacement and branching forces a choice between treating persistence as an irreducible primitive and reducing it to some continuity relation. Finally, briefly score endurantism and perdurantism against the list, noting for each view which desiderata it can satisfy straightforwardly and which it must treat as negotiable or reinterpret.
