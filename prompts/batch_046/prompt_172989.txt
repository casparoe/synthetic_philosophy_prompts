For an advanced seminar I am convening on the epistemology of philosophical conviction, I would like a dialogue—approximately fifteen to twenty exchanges—between two equally capable philosophers: Professor Alina Reyes, who advocates for the inside view in matters of first-order philosophical judgment, and Professor David Okonkwo, who insists that the outside view and the reference class problem should govern our confidence in our own theoretical commitments. The occasion is specific: Reyes has just published a bold argument that consciousness is not substrate-dependent, and she is now confronting data from a longitudinal study of philosophers of mind indicating that scholars who defend non-substrate-dependent theories of consciousness within ten years of tenure recant or substantially weaken their position at a rate of roughly sixty-five percent. Okonkwo presses her to treat her own reasoning as an instance drawn from that reference class, arguing that without an independently identifiable feature—something she can point to that is not itself part of the reasoning she is assessing—she has no non-circular justification for assigning herself a lower failure rate than the base rate predicts. Reyes, in turn, must defend the idea that the step-by-step accessibility of her reasoning chain, its coherence with specific empirical findings in neuroscience, and her awareness of her own cognitive biases constitute such distinguishing features, without merely asserting that she feels different from the statistical cohort. The dialogue should avoid a strawman on either side: Okonkwo should concede that if Reyes can genuinely isolate a non-circular discriminator, modesty permits her to update; Reyes should concede that if she cannot, then epistemic modesty demands she lower her credence. I want the tension left open, since the pedagogical aim is to show students that the conflict between rational self-trust and statistical humility is not easily dissolved by appeal to intuition. Please do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to specific published texts; the arguments should stand on their own conceptual footing.
